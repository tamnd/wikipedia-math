# Prime reciprocal magic square

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Prime reciprocal magic square"}

A **prime reciprocal magic square** is a [magic square](wikipedia://en/52202) using the decimal digits of the [reciprocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_%28mathematics%29) of a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666).

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

Consider a [unit fraction](wikipedia://en/491316), like 1/3 or 1/7. In base ten, the remainder, and so the digits, of 1/3 repeats at once: 0.3333.... However, the remainders of 1/7 repeat over six, or 7−1, digits: 1/7 = 0·142857142857142857... Examining the multiples of 1/7, each is a [cyclic permutation](wikipedia://en/449166) of these six digits:

$$\begin{aligned} 1/7 & = 0.1 4 2 8 5 7\dots \\ 2/7 & = 0.2 8 5 7 1 4\dots \\ 3/7 & = 0.4 2 8 5 7 1\dots \\ 4/7 & = 0.5 7 1 4 2 8\dots \\ 5/7 & = 0.7 1 4 2 8 5\dots \\ 6/7 & = 0.8 5 7 1 4 2\dots \end{aligned}$$

If the digits are laid out as a [square](wikipedia://en/659939), each row and column sums to $1 + 4 + 2 + 8 + 5 + 7 = 27.$ This yields the smallest base-10 non-normal, prime reciprocal [magic square](wikipedia://en/52202):

::: {#o3 .table cue=table}

| 1 | 4 | 2 | 8 | 5 | 7 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 8 | 5 | 7 | 1 | 4 |
| 4 | 2 | 8 | 5 | 7 | 1 |
| 5 | 7 | 1 | 4 | 2 | 8 |
| 7 | 1 | 4 | 2 | 8 | 5 |
| 8 | 5 | 7 | 1 | 4 | 2 |

:::

In contrast with its rows and columns, the *diagonals* of this square do not sum to 27; however, their [mean](wikipedia://en/612) is 27, as one diagonal adds to 23 while the other adds to 31.

All prime reciprocals in any [base](wikipedia://en/701207) with a $p - 1$ period will generate magic squares where all rows and columns produce a [magic constant](wikipedia://en/546969), and only a select few will be **full**, such that their diagonals, rows and columns collectively yield equal sums.

## Decimal expansions {#Decimal_expansions}

In a full, or otherwise prime reciprocal magic square with $p - 1$ period, the even number of $k$−th rows in the square are arranged by multiples of $1/p$ — not necessarily consecutively — where a magic constant can be obtained.

For instance, an [even](wikipedia://en/143135) repeating [cycle](wikipedia://en/1672489) from an odd, prime reciprocal of $p$ that is divided into $n$−digit strings creates pairs of [complementary sequences](wikipedia://en/358754#Numeric_complements) of digits that yield strings of nines (9) when added together:

$$\begin{aligned} 1/7 = & \text { } 0.142\;857\dots \\ + & \text { } 0.857\;142\ldots = 6/7\\ & ------------ \\ & \text { } 0.999\;999\ldots \\ \\ 1/13 = & \text { } 0.076\;923\;076\;923\dots \\ + & \text { } 0.923\;076\;923\;076\ldots = 12/13\\ & ------------ \\ & \text { } 0.999\;999\;999\;999\ldots \\ \\ 1/19 = & \text { } 0.052631578\;947368421\dots \\ + & \text { } 0.947368421\;052631578\ldots = 18/19\\ & ------------ \\ & \text { } 0.999999999\;999999999\dots \end{aligned}$$

This is a result of [Midy's theorem](wikipedia://en/4915939).[^o5][^o6] These complementary sequences are generated between multiples of [prime reciprocals](wikipedia://en/54501684) that add to 1.

More specifically, a factor $n$ in the numerator of the reciprocal of a prime number $p$ will shift the [decimal places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_place) of its decimal expansion accordingly:

$$\begin{aligned} 1/23 & = 0.04347826\;08695652\;173913\ldots \\ 2/23 & = 0.08695652\;17391304\;347826\ldots \\ 4/23 & = 0.17391304\;34782608\;695652\ldots \\ 8/23 & = 0.34782608\;69565217\;391304\ldots \\ 16/23 & = 0.69565217\;39130434\;782608\ldots \end{aligned}$$

In this case, a factor of 2 moves the [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447) of $\tfrac{1}{23}$ by eight places.

A uniform solution of a prime reciprocal magic square, whether full or not, will hold rows with successive multiples of $1/p$. Other magic squares can be constructed whose rows do not represent consecutive multiples of $1/p$, which nonetheless generate a magic sum.

Magic squares based on reciprocals of primes $p$ in bases $b$ with periods $p - 1$ have [magic sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_sum) equal to

$$M = (b-1) \times \frac {p-1}{2}.$$

The first few prime numbers in decimal whose reciprocals can be used to produce a non-normal, full prime reciprocal magic square of this type are[^o12]

{19, 383, 32327, 34061, 45341, 61967, 65699, 117541, 158771, 405817, ...} .

The smallest prime number to yield such magic square in [binary](wikipedia://en/238686) is [59](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/59_%28number%29) (111011<sub>2</sub>), while in [ternary](wikipedia://en/62950) it is [223](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/223_%28number%29) (22021<sub>3</sub>); these are listed at [A096339](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OEIS:A096339), and [A096660](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OEIS:A096660).
