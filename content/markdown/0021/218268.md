# Characteristic polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Characteristic polynomial"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the **characteristic polynomial** of a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) which is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under [matrix similarity](wikipedia://en/341149) and has the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) as [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial). It has the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) and the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of the matrix among its coefficients. The **characteristic polynomial** of an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) of a finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) is the characteristic polynomial of the matrix of that endomorphism over any basis (that is, the characteristic polynomial does not depend on the choice of a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420)). The **characteristic equation**, also known as the **determinantal equation**,[^o2][^o3][^o4] is the equation obtained by equating the characteristic polynomial to zero.

:::

In [spectral graph theory](wikipedia://en/534914), the characteristic polynomial of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is the characteristic polynomial of its [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463).[^o5]

## Motivation {#Motivation}

[Eigenvalues and eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429) play a fundamental role in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), since, given a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation), an eigenvector is a vector whose direction is not changed by the transformation, and the corresponding eigenvalue is the measure of the resulting change of magnitude of the vector.

More precisely, suppose the transformation is represented by a square matrix $A.$ Then an eigenvector $\mathbf{v}$ and the corresponding eigenvalue $\lambda$ must satisfy the equation

$$A \mathbf{v} = \lambda \mathbf{v},$$

or, equivalently (since $\lambda \mathbf{v} = \lambda I \mathbf{v}$),

$$(\lambda I - A) \mathbf{v} =\mathbf 0$$

where $I$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), and $\mathbf{v}\ne \mathbf{0}$ (although the zero vector satisfies this equation for every $\lambda,$ it is not considered an eigenvector).

It follows that the matrix $(\lambda I - A)$ must be [singular](wikipedia://en/245528), and its determinant

$$\det(\lambda I - A) = 0$$

must be zero.

In other words, the eigenvalues of $A$ are the [roots](wikipedia://en/264210) of

$$\det(xI - A),$$

which is a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) in $x$ of degree $n$ if $A$ is a $n\times n$ matrix. This polynomial is the *characteristic polynomial* of $A$.

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Consider an $n \times n$ matrix $A.$ The characteristic polynomial of $A,$ denoted by $p_A(t),$ is the polynomial defined by[^o9]

$$p_A(t) = \det (t I - A)$$

where $I$ denotes the $n \times n$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

Some authors define the characteristic polynomial to be $\det(A - t I).$ That polynomial differs from the one defined here by a sign $(-1)^n,$ so it makes no difference for properties like having as roots the eigenvalues of $A$; however the definition above always gives a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730), whereas the alternative definition is monic only when $n$ is even.

:::

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

To compute the characteristic polynomial of the matrix

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} 2 & 1\\ -1& 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the following is computed:

$$t I-A = \begin{pmatrix} t-2&-1\\ 1&t-0 \end{pmatrix}$$

and found to be $(t-2)t - 1(-1) = t^2-2t+1 \,\!,$ the characteristic polynomial of $A.$

Another example uses [hyperbolic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) of a [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) φ. For the matrix take

$$A = \begin{pmatrix} \cosh(\varphi) & \sinh(\varphi)\\ \sinh(\varphi)& \cosh(\varphi) \end{pmatrix}.$$

Its characteristic polynomial is

$$\det (tI - A) = (t - \cosh(\varphi))^2 - \sinh^2(\varphi) = t^2 - 2 t \ \cosh(\varphi) + 1 = (t - e^\varphi) (t - e^{-\varphi}).$$

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The characteristic polynomial $p_A(t)$ of a $n \times n$ matrix $A$ is [monic](wikipedia://en/314730) (its leading coefficient is $1$) and its degree is $n.$ The most important fact about the characteristic polynomial was already mentioned in the [motivational paragraph](wikipedia://en/218268#Motivation): the eigenvalues of $A$ are precisely the [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of $p_A(t)$ (this also holds for the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667107) of $A,$ but its degree may be less than $n$). All coefficients of the characteristic polynomial are [polynomial expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_expression) in the entries of the matrix. In particular its constant coefficient of $t^0$ is $\det(-A) = (-1)^n \det(A),$ the coefficient of $t^n$ is 1, and the coefficient of $t^{n-1}$ is $\operatorname{tr}(-A) = -\operatorname{tr}(A)$, where $\operatorname{tr}(A)$ is the [trace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace_%28matrix%29) of $A.$ (The signs given here correspond to the [formal definition](wikipedia://en/218268#Formal_definition) given in the previous section; for the alternative definition these would instead be $\det(A)$ and $(-1)^{n - 1}\operatorname{tr}(A)$ respectively.[^o14])

For a $2 \times 2$ matrix $A,$ the characteristic polynomial is thus given by

$$t^2 - \operatorname{tr}(A) t + \det(A).$$

Using the language of [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537), the characteristic polynomial of an $n \times n$ matrix $A$ may be expressed as

$$p_A (t) = \sum_{k=0}^n t^{n-k} (-1)^k \operatorname{tr}\left(\textstyle\bigwedge^k A\right)$$

where $\operatorname{tr}\left(\bigwedge^k A\right)$ is the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of the $k$th [exterior power](wikipedia://en/221537#Functoriality) of $A,$ which has dimension $\binom {n}{k}.$ This trace may be computed as the sum of all [principal minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_minor) of $A$ of size $k.$ The recursive [Faddeev–LeVerrier algorithm](wikipedia://en/49598229) computes these coefficients more efficiently .

When the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of the coefficients is $0,$ each such trace may alternatively be computed as a single determinant, that of the $k \times k$ matrix,

$$\operatorname{tr}\left(\textstyle\bigwedge^k A\right) = \frac{1}{k!} \begin{vmatrix} \operatorname{tr}A & k-1 &0&\cdots &0 \\ \operatorname{tr}A^2 &\operatorname{tr}A& k-2 &\cdots &0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & & \ddots & \vdots \\ \operatorname{tr}A^{k-1} &\operatorname{tr}A^{k-2}& & \cdots & 1 \\ \operatorname{tr}A^k &\operatorname{tr}A^{k-1}& & \cdots & \operatorname{tr}A \end{vmatrix} ~.$$

The [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547) states that replacing $t$ by $A$ in the characteristic polynomial (interpreting the resulting powers as matrix powers, and the constant term $c$ as $c$ times the identity matrix) yields the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888). Informally speaking, every matrix satisfies its own characteristic equation. This statement is equivalent to saying that the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667107) of $A$ divides the characteristic polynomial of $A.$

Two [similar matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_matrices) have the same characteristic polynomial. The converse however is not true in general: two matrices with the same characteristic polynomial need not be similar.

The matrix $A$ and its [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) have the same characteristic polynomial. $A$ is similar to a [triangular matrix](wikipedia://en/374222) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its characteristic polynomial can be completely factored into linear factors over $K$ (the same is true with the minimal polynomial instead of the characteristic polynomial). In this case $A$ is similar to a matrix in [Jordan normal form](wikipedia://en/312877).

:::

## Characteristic polynomial of a product of two matrices {#Characteristic_polynomial_of_a_product_of_two_matrices}

If $A$ and $B$ are two square $n \times n$ matrices then characteristic polynomials of $AB$ and $BA$ coincide:

$$p_{AB}(t)=p_{BA}(t).\,$$

::: {#o16 .proof cue=math-proof}

If $\lambda$ is a non-zero generalized eigenvalue of $AB$ of algebraic multiplicity $k$, and $v$ belongs to the kernel of $(BA -\lambda)^k$, then $Av$ belongs to the kernel of $(AB-\lambda)^k$, so the non-zero generalized eigenspaces of $AB$ and $BA$ have the same dimension. Therefore, since $AB$ and $BA$ are both $n\times n$, the remaining generalized eigenspaces, with eigenvalue 0, have the same dimension. Therefore $AB$ and $BA$ have the same characteristic polynomial, because all generalized eigenvalues are the same, with the same algebraic multiplicities.

:::

More generally, if $A$ is a matrix of order $m \times n$ and $B$ is a matrix of order $n \times m,$ then $AB$ is $m \times m$ and $BA$ is $n \times n$ matrix, and one has

$$p_{BA}(t) = t^{n-m} p_{AB}(t).\,$$

To prove this, one may suppose $n > m,$ by exchanging, if needed, $A$ and $B.$ Then, by bordering $A$ on the bottom by $n - m$ rows of zeros, and $B$ on the right, by, $n - m$ columns of zeros, one gets two $n \times n$ matrices $A^{\prime}$ and $B^{\prime}$ such that $B^{\prime}A^{\prime} = BA$ and $A^{\prime}B^{\prime}$ is equal to $AB$ bordered by $n - m$ rows and columns of zeros. The result follows from the case of square matrices, by comparing the characteristic polynomials of $A^{\prime}B^{\prime}$ and $AB.$

## Characteristic polynomial of *A*<sup>*k*</sup> {#Characteristic_polynomial_of_Ak}

If $\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of a square matrix $A$ with eigenvector $\mathbf{v},$ then $\lambda^k$ is an eigenvalue of $A^k$ because

$$A^k \textbf{v} = A^{k-1} A \textbf{v} = \lambda A^{k-1} \textbf{v} = \dots = \lambda^k \textbf{v}.$$

The multiplicities can be shown to agree as well, and this generalizes to any polynomial in place of $x^k$:[^o18]

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let $A$ be a square $n \times n$ matrix and let $f(t)$ be a polynomial. If the characteristic polynomial of $A$ has a factorization

$$p_A(t) = (t - \lambda_1) (t - \lambda_2) \cdots (t-\lambda_n)$$

then the characteristic polynomial of the matrix $f(A)$ is given by

$$p_{f(A)}(t) = (t - f(\lambda_1)) (t - f(\lambda_2)) \cdots (t-f(\lambda_n)).$$

:::

That is, the algebraic multiplicity of $\lambda$ in $f(A)$ equals the sum of algebraic multiplicities of $\lambda'$ in $A$ over $\lambda'$ such that $f(\lambda') = \lambda.$ In particular, $\operatorname{tr}(f(A)) = \textstyle\sum_{i=1}^n f(\lambda_i)$ and $\operatorname{det}(f(A)) = \textstyle\prod_{i=1}^n f(\lambda_i).$ Here a polynomial $f(t) = t^3+1,$ for example, is evaluated on a matrix $A$ simply as $f(A) = A^3+I.$

The theorem applies to matrices and polynomials over any field or [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346).[^o20] However, the assumption that $p_A(t)$ has a factorization into linear factors is not always true, unless the matrix is over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) such as the complex numbers.

::: {#o21 .proof cue=math-proof}

This proof only applies to matrices and polynomials over complex numbers (or any algebraically closed field). In that case, the characteristic polynomial of any square matrix can be always factorized as

$$p_A(t) = \left(t - \lambda_1\right) \left(t - \lambda_2\right) \cdots \left(t - \lambda_n\right)$$

where $\lambda_1, \lambda_2, \ldots, \lambda_n$ are the eigenvalues of $A,$ possibly repeated. Moreover, the [Jordan decomposition theorem](wikipedia://en/312877) guarantees that any square matrix $A$ can be decomposed as $A = S^{-1} U S,$ where $S$ is an [invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122) and $U$ is [upper triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular) with $\lambda_1, \ldots, \lambda_n$ on the diagonal (with each eigenvalue repeated according to its algebraic multiplicity). (The Jordan normal form has stronger properties, but these are sufficient; alternatively the [Schur decomposition](wikipedia://en/372514) can be used, which is less popular but somewhat easier to prove).

Let $f(t) = \sum_i \alpha_i t^i.$ Then

$$\begin{aligned} f(A) &= \textstyle\sum \alpha_i (S^{-1} U S)^i \\ &= \textstyle\sum \alpha_i S^{-1} U S S^{-1} U S \cdots S^{-1} U S \\ &= \textstyle\sum \alpha_i S^{-1} U^i S \\ &= S^{-1} (\textstyle\sum \alpha_i U^i) S \\ &= S^{-1} f(U) S. \end{aligned}$$

For an upper triangular matrix $U$ with diagonal $\lambda_1, \dots, \lambda_n,$ the matrix $U^i$ is upper triangular with diagonal $\lambda_1^i,\dots,\lambda_n^i$ in $U^i,$ and hence $f(U)$ is upper triangular with diagonal $f\left(\lambda_1\right), \dots, f\left(\lambda_n\right).$ Therefore, the eigenvalues of $f(U)$ are $f(\lambda_1),\dots,f(\lambda_n).$ Since $f(A) = S^{-1} f(U) S$ is [similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_matrix) to $f(U),$ it has the same eigenvalues, with the same algebraic multiplicities.

:::

## Secular function and secular equation {#Secular_function_and_secular_equation}

### Secular function {#Secular_function}

The term **secular function** has been used for what is now called *characteristic polynomial* (in some literature the term secular function is still used). The term comes from the fact that the characteristic polynomial was used to calculate [secular perturbations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular_phenomena) (on a time scale of a century, that is, slow compared to annual motion) of planetary orbits, according to [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange)'s theory of oscillations.

### Secular equation {#Secular_equation}

*Secular equation* may have several meanings.

- In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) it is sometimes used in place of characteristic equation.
- In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) it is the algebraic or numerical expression of the magnitude of the inequalities in a planet's motion that remain after the inequalities of a short period have been allowed for.[^o25]
- In [molecular orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_orbital) calculations relating to the energy of the electron and its [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) it is also used instead of the characteristic equation.

## For general associative algebras {#For_general_associative_algebras}

The above definition of the characteristic polynomial of a matrix $A \in M_n(F)$ with entries in a field $F$ generalizes without any changes to the case when $F$ is just a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346). (Garibaldi 2004) defines the characteristic polynomial for elements of an arbitrary finite-dimensional ([associative](wikipedia://en/2112), but not necessarily commutative) algebra over a field $F$ and proves the standard properties of the characteristic polynomial in this generality.

## Theoretical complexity: calculation by fast matrix multiplication {#Theoretical_complexity:_calculation_by_fast_matrix_multiplication}

It is possible to calculate the characteristic polynomial in a fast way with the use of [fast matrix multiplication algorithms](wikipedia://en/24107400) in the time $O({n^\omega })$ for $\omega$ slightly above 2.37. Respective algorithms is given by[^o29].
