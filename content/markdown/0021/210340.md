# Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi"}

**Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi** (; ; 10 December 1804 – 18 February 1851) was a German [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who made fundamental contributions to [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939), [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28mechanics%29), [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309), [determinants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determinants) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Jacobi was born of [Ashkenazi Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashkenazi_Jew) parentage in [Potsdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potsdam) on 10 December 1804. He was the second of four children of a banker, Simon Jacobi. His elder brother, [Moritz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moritz_von_Jacobi), would also become known later as an engineer and physicist. He was initially home schooled by his uncle Lehman, who instructed him in the classical languages and elements of mathematics. In 1816, the twelve-year-old Jacobi went to the Potsdam [Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28Germany%29), where students were taught all the standard subjects: classical languages, history, philology, mathematics, sciences, etc. As a result of the good education he had received from his uncle, as well as his own remarkable abilities, after less than half a year Jacobi was moved to the senior year despite his young age. However, as the University would not accept students younger than 16 years old, he had to remain in the senior class until 1821. He used this time to advance his knowledge, showing interest in all subjects, including Latin, Greek, philology, history and mathematics. During this period he also made his first attempts at research, trying to solve the [quintic equation](wikipedia://en/303368) by [radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_solution).[^n1][^n2]

In 1821, Jacobi went to study at [Berlin University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_University), where he initially divided his attention between his passions for [philology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philology) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). In philology, he participated in the seminars of [August Böckh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/August_Böckh), drawing the professor's attention with his talent. Jacobi did not follow a lot of mathematics classes at the time, finding the level of mathematics taught at Berlin University too elementary. He continued, instead, with his private study of the more advanced works of [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler), [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange) and [Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace). By 1823 he understood that he needed to make a decision between his competing interests and chose to devote all his attention to mathematics.[^n3] In the same year he became qualified to teach secondary school and was offered a position at the [Joachimsthal Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachimsthal_Gymnasium) in Berlin. Jacobi decided instead to continue to work towards a university position. In 1825, he obtained the degree of Doctor of Philosophy with a dissertation on the [partial fraction decomposition](wikipedia://en/247288) of [rational fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_fraction) defended before a committee led by [Enno Dirksen](wikipedia://en/62908345). He followed immediately with his [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation) and at the same time [converted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_conversion) to Christianity. Now qualifying for teaching university classes, the 21-year-old Jacobi lectured in 1825/26 on the theory of [curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry_of_curves) and [surfaces](wikipedia://en/15513875) at the University of Berlin.[^n3][^n4]

In 1826, Jacobi became a [private lecturer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdozent), in the next year an [extraordinary professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academic_ranks_in_Germany), and in finally 1829, a tenured professor of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) at [Königsberg University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Königsberg_University), and held the chair until 1842. He suffered a [breakdown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_breakdown) from overwork in 1843. He then visited [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy) for a few months to regain his health. On his return he moved to Berlin, where he lived as a royal pensioner, apart from a very brief interim, until his death.[^n5] During the [Revolution of 1848](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revolution_of_1848) Jacobi was politically involved and unsuccessfully presented his parliamentary candidature on behalf of a [Liberal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liberalism) club. This led, after the suppression of the revolution, to his royal grant being cut off –but his fame and reputation were such that it was soon resumed, thanks to the personal intervention of [Alexander von Humboldt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_von_Humboldt).

Jacobi died in 1851 from a [smallpox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smallpox) infection. His grave is preserved at a cemetery in the [Kreuzberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kreuzberg) section of Berlin, the [Friedhof I der Dreifaltigkeits-Kirchengemeinde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Trinity_Church_%28Berlin%29#Cemeteries) (61 Baruther Street). His grave is close to that of [Johann Encke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Encke), the astronomer. The crater [Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_%28crater%29) on the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) is named after him.

Jacobi's birth name was **Jacques Simon**, a French-style name (his father was Simon Jacobi).[^o12] Later, his name was Germanized to Carl Gustav Jacob Jacobi and published in its Latinized form as Carolus Gustavus Jacobus Jacobi. He is sometimes referred to as C. G. J. Jacobi.

## Scientific contributions {#Scientific_contributions}

One of Jacobi's most impactful accomplishments was his theory of [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939) and their relation to the elliptic [theta function](wikipedia://en/449745). This was developed in his influential treatise *[Fundamenta nova theoriae functionum ellipticarum](wikipedia://en/37312651)* (1829), and in later papers in [Crelle's Journal](wikipedia://en/973547). Theta functions are of great importance in mathematical physics because of their role in the inverse problem for periodic and [quasi-periodic flows](wikipedia://en/1590390). The [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) are [integrable](wikipedia://en/1713939) in terms of [Jacobi's elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi's_elliptic_functions) in the well-known cases of the [pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum), the [Euler top](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange,_Euler,_and_Kovalevskaya_tops), the symmetric Lagrange top in a [gravitational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_field), and the [Kepler problem](wikipedia://en/2410571) (planetary motion in a central gravitational field).

He also made fundamental contributions in the study of differential equations and to [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), notably the [Hamilton–Jacobi theory](wikipedia://en/897539).

It was in algebraic development that Jacobi's particular power mainly lay, and he made important contributions of this kind in many areas of mathematics, as shown by his long list of papers in Crelle's Journal and elsewhere from 1826 onwards.[^n5] He is said to have told his students that when looking for a research topic, one should 'Invert, always invert' (German original: *"man muss immer umkehren"*), reflecting his belief that inverting known results can open up new fields for research, for example inverting [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960) and focusing on the nature of elliptic and theta functions.[^n6]

In his 1835 paper, Jacobi proved the following basic result classifying periodic (including elliptic) functions: If a univariate single-valued function is multiply [periodic](wikipedia://en/76848), then such a function cannot have more than two periods, and the ratio of the periods cannot be a real number.

He discovered many of the fundamental properties of theta functions, including the functional equation and the [Jacobi triple product](wikipedia://en/1475657) formula, as well as many other results on [q-series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-series) and [hypergeometric series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_series).

The solution of the [Jacobi inversion problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_inversion_problem) for the hyperelliptic [Abel map](wikipedia://en/10756143) by [Weierstrass](wikipedia://en/93459) in 1854 required the introduction of the [hyperelliptic theta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperelliptic_theta_function) and later the general Riemann theta function for [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260) of arbitrary [genus](wikipedia://en/61891). The [complex torus](wikipedia://en/3055042) associated to a genus $g$ algebraic curve, obtained by quotienting ${\mathbf C}^g$ by the [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) of periods is referred to as the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734). This method of inversion, and its subsequent extension by Weierstrass and [Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) to arbitrary algebraic curves, may be seen as a higher genus generalization of the relation between elliptic integrals and the Jacobi or Weierstrass elliptic functions.

Jacobi was the first to apply elliptic functions to [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), for example proving [Fermat's](wikipedia://en/7576966) [two-square theorem](wikipedia://en/1850216) and [Lagrange's four-square theorem](wikipedia://en/326395), and similar results for 6 and 8 squares.

His other work in number theory continued the work of [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125): new proofs of [quadratic reciprocity](wikipedia://en/25272), and the introduction of the [Jacobi symbol](wikipedia://en/97313); contributions to higher reciprocity laws, investigations of [continued fractions](wikipedia://en/739199), and the invention of [Jacobi sums](wikipedia://en/3142215).

He was also one of the early founders of the theory of determinants.[^n7] In particular, he invented the [Jacobian determinant](wikipedia://en/195351) formed from the *n*<sup>2</sup> partial derivatives of *n* given functions of *n* independent variables, which plays an important part in changes of variables in multiple integrals, and in many analytical investigations.[^n5] In 1841 he reintroduced the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) ∂ notation of [Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695), which was to become standard.

He was one of the first to introduce and study the symmetric polynomials that are now known as [Schur polynomials](wikipedia://en/3147062), giving the so-called [bialternant formula](wikipedia://en/3147062#Definition_%28Jacobi's_bialternant_formula%29) for these, which is a special case of the [Weyl character formula](wikipedia://en/5011916), and deriving the [Jacobi–Trudi identities](wikipedia://en/3147062#Jacobi−Trudi_identities). He also discovered the [Desnanot–Jacobi formula for determinants](wikipedia://en/1740225#Desnanot–Jacobi_identity_and_proof_of_correctness_of_the_condensation_algorithm), which underlie the [Plücker relations](wikipedia://en/1190270) for [Grassmannians](wikipedia://en/373810).

Students of [vector fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29), [Lie theory](wikipedia://en/17944), [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319) and [operator algebras](wikipedia://en/455987) often encounter the [Jacobi identity](wikipedia://en/294370), the analog of [associativity](wikipedia://en/1335) for the [Lie bracket](wikipedia://en/10282799) operation.

[Planetary theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_theory) and other particular dynamical problems likewise occupied his attention from time to time. While contributing to [celestial mechanics](wikipedia://en/102182), he introduced the [Jacobi integral](wikipedia://en/1713939) (1836) for a [sidereal coordinate system](wikipedia://en/48381). His theory of the *last multiplier* is treated in *Vorlesungen über Dynamik*, edited by [Alfred Clebsch](wikipedia://en/1369957) (1866).[^n5]

He left many manuscripts, portions of which have been published at intervals in Crelle's Journal. His other works include *Commentatio de transformatione integralis duplicis indefiniti in formam simpliciorem* (1832), *[Canon arithmeticus](wikipedia://en/37342010)* (1839), and *Opuscula mathematica* (1846–1857). His *Gesammelte Werke* (1881–1891), or collected works, were published by the [Berlin Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussian_Academy_of_Sciences).[^n5]
