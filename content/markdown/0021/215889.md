# Permanent (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Permanent"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the **permanent** of a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) is a function of the matrix similar to the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468). The permanent, as well as the determinant, is a polynomial in the entries of the matrix.[^o2] Both are special cases of a more general function of a matrix called the [immanant](wikipedia://en/6466838).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The permanent of an $n\times n$ matrix $A = (a_{i,j})$ is defined as

$$\operatorname{perm}(A)=\sum_{\sigma\in S_n}\prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$

The sum here extends over all elements *σ* of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) *S*<sub>*n*</sub>; i.e., over all [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) of the numbers 1, 2, ..., *n*.

For example,

$$\operatorname{perm}\begin{pmatrix}a&b \\ c&d\end{pmatrix}=ad+bc,$$

and

$$\operatorname{perm}\begin{pmatrix}a&b&c \\ d&e&f \\ g&h&i \end{pmatrix}=aei + bfg + cdh + ceg + bdi + afh.$$

The definition of the permanent of *A* differs from that of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of *A* in that the [signatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28permutation%29) of the permutations are not taken into account.

The permanent of a matrix A is denoted by per *A*, perm *A*, or Per *A*. Minc uses Per(*A*) for the permanent of a rectangular matrix, and per(*A*) when *A* is a square matrix.[^o5] Muir and Metzler use the notation $\overset{+}{|}\quad \overset{+}{|}$.[^o6]

The word "permanent" originated with Cauchy in 1812 as "fonctions symétriques permanentes" for a related type of function,[^o7] and was used by Muir and Metzler[^o8] in the more specific modern sense.[^o9]

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

If one views the permanent as a map that takes *n* vectors as arguments, then it is a [multilinear map](wikipedia://en/221525) and it is symmetric (meaning that any order of the vectors results in the same permanent). Furthermore, given a square matrix $A = \left(a_{ij}\right)$ of order *n*:[^o12]

- The permanent is invariant under arbitrary permutations of the rows and columns. This property may be written symbolically as perm(*A*) = perm(*PAQ*) for any appropriately sized [permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/238849) *P* and *Q.*
- Multiplying any single row or column of *A* by a [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331) *λ* changes perm(*A*) to *λ*⋅perm(*A*).
- The permanent is invariant under [transposition](wikipedia://en/173844); that is, perm(*A*) = perm(*A*<sup>T</sup>).
- If $A = \left(a_{ij}\right)$ and $B=\left(b_{ij}\right)$ are square matrices of order *n*, then $$\operatorname{perm}\left(A + B\right) = \sum_{s,t} \operatorname{perm} \left(a_{ij}\right)_{i \in s, j \in t} \operatorname{perm} \left(b_{ij}\right)_{i \in \bar{s}, j \in \bar{t}},$$ where *s* and *t* are subsets of the same size of {1, 2, ..., *n*} and $\bar{s}, \bar{t}$ are their respective complements in that set.[^o13]
- If $A$ is a [triangular matrix](wikipedia://en/374222), i.e., $a_{ij}=0$ whenever $i>j$ or, alternatively, whenever $i<j$, then its permanent equals the product of the diagonal entries: $$\operatorname{perm}\left(A\right) = a_{11} a_{22} \cdots a_{nn} = \prod_{i=1}^n a_{ii}.$$

:::

## Comparison to determinants {#Comparison_to_determinants}

Laplace's [expansion by minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_by_minors) for computing the determinant along a row, column or diagonal extends to the permanent by ignoring all signs.[^o15]

For every $i$,

$$\mathbb{perm}(B)=\sum_{j=1}^n B_{i,j} M_{i,j},$$

where $B_{i,j}$ is the entry of the *i*th row and the *j*th column of *B*, and $M_{i,j}$ is the permanent of the submatrix obtained by removing the *i*th row and the *j*th column of *B*.

For example, expanding along the first column,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{perm} \left ( \begin{matrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\2 & 1 & 0 & 0\\3 & 0 & 1 & 0\\4 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{matrix} \right ) = {} & 1 \cdot \operatorname{perm} \left( \begin{matrix} 1&0&0\\ 0&1&0\\ 0&0&1 \end{matrix}\right) + 2\cdot \operatorname{perm} \left(\begin{matrix}1&1&1\\0&1&0\\0&0&1\end{matrix}\right) \\ & {} + \ 3\cdot \operatorname{perm} \left(\begin{matrix}1&1&1\\1&0&0\\0&0&1\end{matrix}\right) + 4 \cdot \operatorname{perm} \left(\begin{matrix}1&1&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{matrix}\right) \\ = {} & 1(1) + 2(1) + 3(1) + 4(1) = 10, \end{aligned}$$

while expanding along the last row gives,

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{perm} \left ( \begin{matrix} 1 & 1 & 1 & 1\\2 & 1 & 0 & 0\\3 & 0 & 1 & 0\\4 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{matrix} \right ) = {} & 4 \cdot \operatorname{perm} \left(\begin{matrix}1&1&1\\1&0&0\\0&1&0\end{matrix}\right) + 0\cdot \operatorname{perm} \left(\begin{matrix}1&1&1\\2&0&0\\3&1&0\end{matrix}\right) \\ & {} + \ 0\cdot \operatorname{perm} \left(\begin{matrix}1&1&1\\2&1&0\\3&0&0\end{matrix}\right) + 1 \cdot \operatorname{perm} \left( \begin{matrix} 1&1&1\\ 2&1&0\\ 3&0&1\end{matrix}\right) \\ = {} & 4(1) + 0 + 0 + 1(6) = 10. \end{aligned}$$

On the other hand, the basic multiplicative property of determinants is not valid for permanents.[^o16] A simple example shows that this is so.

$$\begin{aligned} 4 &= \operatorname{perm} \left ( \begin{matrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{matrix} \right )\operatorname{perm} \left ( \begin{matrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{matrix} \right ) \\ &\neq \operatorname{perm}\left ( \left ( \begin{matrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{matrix} \right ) \left ( \begin{matrix} 1 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{matrix} \right ) \right ) = \operatorname{perm} \left ( \begin{matrix} 2 & 2 \\ 2 & 2 \end{matrix} \right )= 8. \end{aligned}$$

Unlike the determinant, the permanent has no easy geometrical interpretation; it is mainly used in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), in treating boson [Green's functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green's_function_%28many-body_theory%29) in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), and in determining state probabilities of [boson sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson_sampling) systems.[^o17] However, it has two [graph-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph-theoretic) interpretations: as the sum of weights of [cycle covers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_cycle_cover) of a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986), and as the sum of weights of perfect matchings in a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431).

::: {#o19 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

### Symmetric tensors {#Symmetric_tensors}

The permanent arises naturally in the study of the symmetric tensor power of [Hilbert spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_spaces).[^o21] In particular, for a Hilbert space $H$, let $\vee^k H$ denote the $k$th symmetric tensor power of $H$, which is the space of [symmetric tensors](wikipedia://en/23087738). Note in particular that $\vee^k H$ is spanned by the [symmetric products](wikipedia://en/23087738#Symmetric_product) of elements in $H$. For $x_1,x_2,\dots,x_k \in H$, we define the symmetric product of these elements by

$$x_1 \vee x_2 \vee \cdots \vee x_k = (k!)^{-1/2} \sum_{\sigma \in S_k} x_{\sigma(1)} \otimes x_{\sigma(2)} \otimes \cdots \otimes x_{\sigma(k)}$$

If we consider $\vee^k H$ (as a subspace of $\otimes^kH$, the *k*th [tensor power](wikipedia://en/2728973) of $H$) and define the inner product on $\vee^kH$ accordingly, we find that for $x_j,y_j \in H$

$$\langle x_1 \vee x_2 \vee \cdots \vee x_k, y_1 \vee y_2 \vee \cdots \vee y_k \rangle = \operatorname{perm}\left[\langle x_i,y_j \rangle\right]_{i,j = 1}^k$$

Applying the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128), we find that $\operatorname{perm} \left[\langle x_i,x_j \rangle\right]_{i,j = 1}^k \geq 0$, and that

$$\left|\operatorname{perm} \left[\langle x_i,y_j \rangle\right]_{i,j = 1}^k \right|^2 \leq \operatorname{perm} \left[\langle x_i,x_j \rangle\right]_{i,j = 1}^k \cdot \operatorname{perm} \left[\langle y_i,y_j \rangle\right]_{i,j = 1}^k$$

### Cycle covers {#Cycle_covers}

Any square matrix $A = (a_{ij})_{i,j=1}^n$ can be viewed as the [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) of a weighted [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) on vertex set $V=\{1,2,\dots,n\}$, with $a_{ij}$ representing the weight of the arc from vertex *i* to vertex *j*. A [cycle cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_cycle_cover) of a weighted directed graph is a collection of vertex-disjoint [directed cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_cycle) in the digraph that covers all vertices in the graph. Thus, each vertex *i* in the digraph has a unique "successor" $\sigma(i)$ in the cycle cover, and so $\sigma$ represents a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) on *V*. Conversely, any permutation $\sigma$ on *V* corresponds to a cycle cover with arcs from each vertex *i* to vertex $\sigma(i)$.

If the weight of a cycle-cover is defined to be the product of the weights of the arcs in each cycle, then

$$\operatorname{weight}(\sigma) = \prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)},$$

implying that

$$\operatorname{perm}(A)=\sum_\sigma \operatorname{weight}(\sigma).$$

Thus the permanent of *A* is equal to the sum of the weights of all cycle-covers of the digraph.

### Perfect matchings {#Perfect_matchings}

A square matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ can also be viewed as the [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) of a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) which has [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) $x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n$ on one side and $y_1, y_2, \dots, y_n$ on the other side, with $a_{ij}$ representing the weight of the edge from vertex $x_i$ to vertex $y_j$. If the weight of a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) $\sigma$ that matches $x_i$ to $y_{\sigma(i)}$ is defined to be the product of the weights of the edges in the matching, then

$$\operatorname{weight}(\sigma) = \prod_{i=1}^n a_{i,\sigma(i)}.$$

Thus the permanent of *A* is equal to the sum of the weights of all perfect matchings of the graph.

:::

## Permanents of (0, 1) matrices {#Permanents_of_(0,_1)_matrices}

::: {#o26 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Enumeration"}

### Enumeration {#Enumeration}

The answers to many counting questions can be computed as permanents of matrices that only have 0 and 1 as entries.

Let Ω(*n*, *k*) be the class of all (0, 1)-matrices of order *n* with each row and column sum equal to *k*. Every matrix *A* in this class has perm(*A*) > 0.[^o27] The incidence matrices every [finite projective plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_projective_plane) is in the class Ω(*n*<sup>2</sup> + *n* + 1, *n* + 1) for some integer *n* > 1. The permanents corresponding to the smallest projective planes have been calculated. For *n* = 2, 3, and 4 the values are 24, 3852 and 18,534,400, respectively.[^o27] Let *Z* be the incidence matrix of the projective plane with *n* = 2, the [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404). Remarkably, perm(*Z*) = 24 = |det (*Z*)|, the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the determinant of *Z*. This is a consequence of *Z* being a [circulant matrix](wikipedia://en/889936), as well as the theorem:[^o28]

If *A* is a circulant matrix in the class Ω(*n*, *k*) then if *k* > 3, perm(*A*) > |det (*A*)| and if *k* = 3, perm(*A*) = |det (*A*)|. Furthermore, when *k* = 3, by permuting rows and columns, *A* can be put into the form of a direct sum of *e* copies of the matrix *Z* and consequently, *n* = 7*e* and perm(*A*) = 24<sup>e</sup>.

Permanents can also be used to calculate the number of [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) with restricted (prohibited) positions. For the standard *n*-set {1, 2, ..., *n*}, let $A = (a_{ij})$ be the (0, 1)-matrix where *a*<sub>*ij*</sub> = 1 if *i* → *j* is allowed in a permutation and *a*<sub>*ij*</sub> = 0 otherwise. Then perm(*A*) is equal to the number of permutations of the *n*-set that satisfy all the restrictions.[^o15] Two well known special cases of this are the solution of the [derangement](wikipedia://en/162132) problem and the [ménage problem](wikipedia://en/21068755): the number of permutations of an *n*-set with no fixed points (derangements) is given by

$$\operatorname{perm}(J - I) = \operatorname{perm}\left (\begin{matrix} 0 & 1 & 1 & \dots & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 1 & \dots & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & \dots & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & \dots & 0 \end{matrix} \right) = n! \sum_{i=0}^n \frac{(-1)^i}{i!},$$

where *J* is the *n*×*n* all 1's matrix and *I* is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), and the [ménage numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ménage_number) are given by

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{perm}(J - I - I') & = \operatorname{perm}\left (\begin{matrix} 0 & 0 & 1 & \dots & 1 \\ 1 & 0 & 0 & \dots & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 0 & \dots & 1 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 1 & 1 & \dots & 0 \end{matrix} \right) \\ & = \sum_{k=0}^n (-1)^k \frac{2n}{2n-k} {2n-k\choose k} (n-k)!, \end{aligned}$$

where *I**is the (0, 1)-matrix with nonzero entries in positions (*****i*,*i*\+ 1) and (*n*, 1).***

For an *n*×*n* (0, 1)-matrix, the permanent may be equivalently described as the number of ways of placing *n* mutually non-attacking [rooks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rook_%28chess%29) on the *n*×*n* chessboard so that no rook is at a position where the matrix has a 0 entry. (These numbers arise in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) as leading coefficients of [rook polynomials](wikipedia://en/14031635).)[^o29]

:::

### Bounds {#Bounds}

The [Bregman–Minc inequality](wikipedia://en/48413286), conjectured by [H. Minc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henryk_Minc) in 1963[^o31] and proved by [L. M. Brégman](wikipedia://en/39180704) in 1973,[^o32] gives an upper bound for the permanent of an *n* × *n* (0, 1)-matrix. If *A* has *r*<sub>*i*</sub> ones in row *i* for each 1 ≤ *i* ≤ *n*, the inequality states that

$$\operatorname{perm} A \leq \prod_{i=1}^n (r_i)!^{1/r_i}.$$

::: {#o34 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Van der Waerden's conjecture"}

## Van der Waerden's conjecture {#Van_der_Waerden's_conjecture}

In 1926, [Van der Waerden](wikipedia://en/91390) conjectured that the minimum permanent among all *n* × *n* [doubly stochastic matrices](wikipedia://en/2906180) is *n*!/*n*<sup>*n*</sup>, achieved by the matrix for which all entries are equal to 1/*n*.[^o35] Proofs of this conjecture were published in 1980 by B. Gyires[^o36] and in 1981 by [G. P. Egorychev](wikipedia://en/56738296)[^o37] and D. I. Falikman;[^o38] Egorychev's proof is an application of the [Alexandrov&ndash;Fenchel inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandrov&ndash;Fenchel_inequality).[^o39] For this work, Egorychev and Falikman won the [Fulkerson Prize](wikipedia://en/1425916) in 1982.[^o40]

:::

::: {#o42 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Computation"}

## Computation {#Computation}

The naïve approach, using the definition, of computing permanents is computationally infeasible even for relatively small matrices. One of the fastest known algorithms is due to [H. J. Ryser](wikipedia://en/20825197).[^o43] [Ryser's method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ryser's_formula) is based on an [inclusion–exclusion](wikipedia://en/342684) formula that can be given[^o44] as follows: Let $A_k$ be obtained from *A* by deleting *k* columns, let $P(A_k)$ be the product of the row-sums of $A_k$, and let $\Sigma_k$ be the sum of the values of $P(A_k)$ over all possible $A_k$. Then

$$\operatorname{perm}(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} (-1)^{k} \Sigma_k.$$

It may be rewritten in terms of the matrix entries as follows:

$$\operatorname{perm} (A) = (-1)^n \sum_{S\subseteq\{1,\dots,n\}} (-1)^{|S|} \prod_{i=1}^n \sum_{j\in S} a_{ij}.$$

The permanent is believed to be more difficult to compute than the determinant. While the determinant can be computed in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) by [Gaussian elimination](wikipedia://en/13035), Gaussian elimination cannot be used to compute the permanent. Moreover, computing the permanent of a (0,1)-matrix is [\#P-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp-P-complete). Thus, if the permanent can be computed in polynomial time by any method, then **[FP](wikipedia://en/663347) = [\#P](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp-P)**, which is an even stronger statement than [P = NP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_=_NP_problem). When the entries of *A* are nonnegative, however, the permanent can be computed [approximately](wikipedia://en/563105) in [probabilistic](wikipedia://en/495383) polynomial time, up to an error of $\varepsilon M$, where $M$ is the value of the permanent and $\varepsilon > 0$ is arbitrary.[^o45] The permanent of a certain set of [positive semidefinite matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_matrix) is NP-hard to approximate within any subexponential factor.[^o46] If further conditions on the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206136) are imposed, the permanent can be approximated in probabilistic polynomial time: the best achievable error of this approximation is $\varepsilon\sqrt{M}$ ($M$ is again the value of the permanent).[^o47] The hardness in these instances is closely linked with difficulty of simulating [boson sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson_sampling) experiments.

:::

::: {#o49 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="MacMahon's master theorem"}

## MacMahon's master theorem {#MacMahon's_master_theorem}

Another way to view permanents is via multivariate [generating functions](wikipedia://en/160993). Let $A = (a_{ij})$ be a square matrix of order *n*. Consider the multivariate generating function:

$$\begin{aligned} F(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n) &= \prod_{i=1}^n \left ( \sum_{j=1}^n a_{ij} x_j \right ) \\ &= \left( \sum_{j=1}^n a_{1j} x_j \right ) \left ( \sum_{j=1}^n a_{2j} x_j \right ) \cdots \left ( \sum_{j=1}^n a_{nj} x_j \right). \end{aligned}$$

The coefficient of $x_1 x_2 \dots x_n$ in $F(x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n)$ is perm(*A*).[^o50]

As a generalization, for any sequence of *n* non-negative integers, $s_1,s_2,\dots,s_n$ define:

$$\operatorname{perm}^{(s_1,s_2,\dots,s_n)}(A)$$

as the coefficient of $x_1^{s_1} x_2^{s_2} \cdots x_n^{s_n}$ in$\left ( \sum_{j=1}^n a_{1j} x_j \right )^{s_1} \left ( \sum_{j=1}^n a_{2j} x_j \right )^{s_2} \cdots \left ( \sum_{j=1}^n a_{nj} x_j \right )^{s_n}.$

**MacMahon's master theorem** relating permanents and determinants is:[^o51]

$$\operatorname{perm}^{(s_1,s_2,\dots,s_n)}(A) = \text{ coefficient of }x_1^{s_1} x_2^{s_2} \cdots x_n^{s_n} \text{ in } \frac{1}{\det(I - XA)},$$

where *I* is the order *n* identity matrix and *X* is the diagonal matrix with diagonal $[x_1,x_2,\dots,x_n].$

:::

## Rectangular matrices {#Rectangular_matrices}

The permanent function can be generalized to apply to non-square matrices. Indeed, several authors make this the definition of a permanent and consider the restriction to square matrices a special case.[^o53] Specifically, for an *m* × *n* matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ with *m* ≤ *n*, define

$$\operatorname{perm} (A) = \sum_{\sigma \in \operatorname{P}(n,m)} a_{1 \sigma(1)} a_{2 \sigma(2)} \ldots a_{m \sigma(m)},$$

where P(*n*,*m*) is the set of all *m*-permutations of the *n*-set {1,2,...,n}.[^o54]

Ryser's computational result for permanents also generalizes. If *A* is an *m* × *n* matrix with *m* ≤ *n*, let $A_k$ be obtained from *A* by deleting *k* columns, let $P(A_k)$ be the product of the row-sums of $A_k$, and let $\sigma_k$ be the sum of the values of $P(A_k)$ over all possible $A_k$. Then[^o16]

$$\operatorname{perm}(A)=\sum_{k=0}^{m-1} (-1)^{k}\binom{n-m+k}{k}\sigma_{n-m+k}.$$

### Systems of distinct representatives {#Systems_of_distinct_representatives}

The generalization of the definition of a permanent to non-square matrices allows the concept to be used in a more natural way in some applications. For instance:

Let *S*<sub>1</sub>, *S*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *S*<sub>*m*</sub> be subsets (not necessarily distinct) of an *n*-set with *m* ≤ *n*. The [incidence matrix](wikipedia://en/420919) of this collection of subsets is an *m* × *n* (0,1)-matrix *A*. The number of [systems of distinct representatives](wikipedia://en/897733) (SDR's) of this collection is perm(*A*).[^o56]
