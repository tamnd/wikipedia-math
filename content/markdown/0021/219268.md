# Population genetics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Population genetics"}

**Population genetics** is a subfield of [genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics) that deals with genetic differences within and among [populations](wikipedia://en/22949), and is a part of [evolutionary biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_biology). Studies in this branch of [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology) examine such phenomena as [adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptation_%28biology%29), [speciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speciation), and [population structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_stratification).[^o2]

:::

Population genetics was a vital ingredient in the [emergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emergence) of the [modern evolutionary synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_synthesis_%2820th_century%29). Its primary founders were [Sewall Wright](wikipedia://en/1700593), [J. B. S. Haldane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._B._S._Haldane) and [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824), who also laid the foundations for the related discipline of [quantitative genetics](wikipedia://en/297382). Traditionally a highly mathematical discipline, modern population genetics encompasses theoretical, laboratory, and field work. Population genetic models are used both for [statistical inference](wikipedia://en/27577) from DNA sequence data and for proof/disproof of concept.[^o3]

What sets population genetics apart from newer, more phenotypic approaches to modelling evolution, such as [evolutionary game theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_game_theory) and [adaptive dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_invasion_analysis), is its emphasis on such genetic phenomena as [dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominance_%28genetics%29), [epistasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistasis), the degree to which [genetic recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_recombination) breaks [linkage disequilibrium](wikipedia://en/681230), and the random phenomena of [mutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation) and [genetic drift](wikipedia://en/72016). This makes it appropriate for comparison to [population genomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_genomics) data.

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="History of population genetics"}

## History {#History}

Population genetics began as a reconciliation of [Mendelian inheritance](wikipedia://en/19595) and [biostatistics](wikipedia://en/3878) models. [Natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection) will only cause evolution if there is enough [genetic variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_variation) in a population. Before the discovery of [Mendelian genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mendelian_genetics), one common hypothesis was [blending inheritance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blending_inheritance). But with blending inheritance, genetic variance would be rapidly lost, making evolution by natural or sexual selection implausible. The [Hardy–Weinberg principle](wikipedia://en/230319) provides the solution to how variation is maintained in a population with Mendelian inheritance. According to this principle, the frequencies of alleles (variations in a gene) will remain constant in the absence of selection, mutation, migration and genetic drift.[^o6]

The next key step was the work of the British biologist and statistician [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824). In a series of papers starting in 1918 and culminating in his 1930 book *[The Genetical Theory of Natural Selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Genetical_Theory_of_Natural_Selection)*, Fisher showed that the continuous variation measured by the biometricians could be produced by the combined action of many discrete genes, and that natural selection could change allele frequencies in a population, resulting in evolution. In a series of papers beginning in 1924, another British geneticist, [J. B. S. Haldane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._B._S._Haldane), worked out the mathematics of allele frequency change at a single gene [locus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locus_%28genetics%29) under a broad range of conditions. Haldane also applied statistical analysis to real-world examples of natural selection, such as [peppered moth evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peppered_moth_evolution) and [industrial melanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Industrial_melanism), and showed that [selection coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selection_coefficient) could be larger than Fisher assumed, leading to more rapid adaptive evolution as a camouflage strategy following increased pollution.[^o7][^o8]

The American biologist [Sewall Wright](wikipedia://en/1700593), who had a background in [animal breeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal_breeding) experiments, focused on combinations of interacting genes, and the effects of [inbreeding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inbreeding) on small, relatively isolated populations that exhibited genetic drift. In 1932 Wright introduced the concept of an [adaptive landscape](wikipedia://en/310599) and argued that genetic drift and inbreeding could drive a small, isolated sub-population away from an adaptive peak, allowing natural selection to drive it towards different adaptive peaks.

The work of Fisher, Haldane and Wright founded the discipline of population genetics. This integrated natural selection with Mendelian genetics, which was the critical first step in developing a unified theory of how evolution worked.[^o7][^o8] [John Maynard Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Maynard_Smith) was Haldane's pupil, whilst [W. D. Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W._D._Hamilton) was influenced by the writings of Fisher. The American [George R. Price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_R._Price) worked with both Hamilton and Maynard Smith. American [Richard Lewontin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Lewontin) and Japanese [Motoo Kimura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motoo_Kimura) were influenced by Wright and Haldane.

### Modern synthesis {#Modern_synthesis}

The mathematics of population genetics were originally developed as the beginning of the [modern synthesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_synthesis_%2820th_century%29). Authors such as Beatty[^o10] have asserted that population genetics defines the core of the modern synthesis. For the first few decades of the 20th century, most field naturalists continued to believe that [Lamarckism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamarckism) and [orthogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogenesis) provided the best explanation for the complexity they observed in the living world.[^o11] During the modern synthesis, these ideas were purged, and only evolutionary causes that could be expressed in the mathematical framework of population genetics were retained.[^o12] Consensus was reached as to which evolutionary factors might influence evolution, but not as to the relative importance of the various factors.[^o12]

[Theodosius Dobzhansky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodosius_Dobzhansky), a postdoctoral worker in [T. H. Morgan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._H._Morgan)'s lab, had been influenced by the work on [genetic diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_diversity) by Russian geneticists such as [Sergei Chetverikov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Chetverikov). He helped to bridge the divide between the foundations of [microevolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microevolution) developed by the population geneticists and the patterns of [macroevolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroevolution) observed by field biologists, with his 1937 book *[Genetics and the Origin of Species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetics_and_the_Origin_of_Species)*. Dobzhansky examined the genetic diversity of wild populations and showed that, contrary to the assumptions of the population geneticists, these populations had large amounts of genetic diversity, with marked differences between sub-populations. The book also took the highly mathematical work of the population geneticists and put it into a more accessible form. Many more biologists were influenced by population genetics via Dobzhansky than were able to read the highly mathematical works in the original.[^o13]

In Great Britain [E. B. Ford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._B._Ford), the pioneer of [ecological genetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_genetics),[^o14] continued throughout the 1930s and 1940s to empirically demonstrate the power of selection due to ecological factors including the ability to maintain genetic diversity through genetic [polymorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_%28biology%29) such as human [blood types](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood_type). Ford's work, in collaboration with Fisher, contributed to a shift in emphasis during the modern synthesis towards natural selection as the dominant force.[^o7][^o8][^o15][^o16]

### Neutral theory and origin-fixation dynamics {#Neutral_theory_and_origin-fixation_dynamics}

The original, modern synthesis view of population genetics assumes that mutations provide ample raw material, and focuses only on the change in [frequency of alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele_frequency) within [populations](wikipedia://en/22949).[^o18] The main processes influencing allele frequencies are [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection), [genetic drift](wikipedia://en/72016), [gene flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_flow) and recurrent [mutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation). Fisher and Wright had some fundamental disagreements about the relative roles of selection and drift.[^o19] The availability of molecular data on all genetic differences led to the [neutral theory of molecular evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_theory_of_molecular_evolution). In this view, many mutations are deleterious and so never observed, and most of the remainder are neutral, i.e. are not under selection. With the fate of each neutral mutation left to chance (genetic drift), the direction of evolutionary change is driven by which mutations occur, and so cannot be captured by models of change in the frequency of (existing) alleles alone.[^o18][^o20]

The origin-fixation view of population genetics generalizes this approach beyond strictly neutral mutations, and sees the rate at which a particular change happens as the product of the mutation rate and the [fixation probability](wikipedia://en/9185794).[^o18]

## Four processes {#Four_processes}

### Selection {#Selection}

[Natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection), which includes [sexual selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexual_selection), is the fact that some [traits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trait_%28biology%29) make it more likely for an [organism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organism) to survive and [reproduce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproduction). Population genetics describes natural selection by defining [fitness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fitness_%28biology%29) as a [propensity or probability](wikipedia://en/11351089) of survival and reproduction in a particular environment. The fitness is normally given by the symbol **w**=1-**s** where **s** is the [selection coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selection_coefficient). Natural selection acts on [phenotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotype), so population genetic models assume relatively simple relationships to predict the phenotype and hence fitness from the [allele](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele) at one or a small number of loci. In this way, natural selection converts differences in the fitness of individuals with different phenotypes into changes in allele frequency in a population over successive generations.

Before the advent of population genetics, many biologists doubted that small differences in fitness were sufficient to make a large difference to evolution.[^o13] Population geneticists addressed this concern in part by comparing selection to [genetic drift](wikipedia://en/72016). Selection can overcome genetic drift when **s** is greater than 1 divided by the [effective population size](wikipedia://en/1543960). When this criterion is met, the probability that a new advantageous mutant becomes [fixed](wikipedia://en/9185794) is approximately equal to **2s**.[^o23][^o24] The time until fixation of such an allele is approximately $(2 log(sN)+\gamma)/s$.[^o25]

#### Dominance {#Dominance}

[Dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominance_%28genetics%29) means that the phenotypic and/or fitness effect of one allele at a locus depends on which allele is present in the second copy for that locus. Consider three genotypes at one locus, with the following fitness values[^o27]

::: {#o28 .table cue=table}

| Genotype: | A<sub>1</sub>A<sub>1</sub> | A<sub>1</sub>A<sub>2</sub> | A<sub>2</sub>A<sub>2</sub> |
|---|---|---|---|
| Relative fitness: | 1 | 1-hs | 1-s |

:::

s is the [selection coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selection_coefficient) and h is the dominance coefficient. The value of h yields the following information:

::: {#o29 .table cue=table}

| h=0 | A<sub>1</sub> dominant, A<sub>2</sub> recessive |
|---|---|
| h=1 | A<sub>2</sub> dominant, A<sub>1</sub> recessive |
| 0\<h\<1 | incomplete dominance |
| h\<0 | [overdominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overdominance) |
| h>1 | [Underdominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underdominance) |

:::

#### Epistasis {#Epistasis}

*Image: Synergistic versus antagonistic epistasis.svg.* The [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of fitness as a function of the number of deleterious mutations. Synergistic epistasis is represented by the red line - each subsequent deleterious mutation has a larger proportionate effect on the organism's fitness. Antagonistic epistasis is in blue. The black line shows the non-epistatic case, where fitness is the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of the contributions from each of its loci.

[Epistasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistasis) means that the phenotypic and/or fitness effect of an allele at one locus depends on which alleles are present at other loci. Selection does not act on a single locus, but on a phenotype that arises through development from a complete genotype.[^o31] However, many population genetics models of sexual species are "single locus" models, where the fitness of an individual is calculated as the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of the contributions from each of its loci—effectively assuming no epistasis.

In fact, the [genotype to fitness landscape](wikipedia://en/310599) is more complex. Population genetics must either model this complexity in detail, or capture it by some simpler average rule. Empirically, beneficial mutations tend to have a smaller fitness benefit when added to a genetic background that already has high fitness: this is known as diminishing returns epistasis.[^o32] When deleterious mutations also have a smaller fitness effect on high fitness backgrounds, this is known as "synergistic epistasis". However, the effect of deleterious mutations tends on average to be very close to multiplicative, or can even show the opposite pattern, known as "antagonistic epistasis".[^o33]

Synergistic epistasis is central to some theories of the purging of [mutation load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_load)[^o34] and to the [evolution of sexual reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_sexual_reproduction).

### Mutation {#Mutation}

*Image: Drosophila melanogaster - side (aka).jpg.* *Drosophila melanogaster*

The genetic process of [mutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation) takes place within an individual, resulting in heritable changes to the genetic material. This process is often characterized by a description of the starting and ending states, or the kind of change that has happened at the level of DNA (e.g,. a T-to-C mutation, a 1-bp deletion), of genes or proteins (e.g., a null mutation, a loss-of-function mutation), or at a higher phenotypic level (e.g., red-eye mutation). Single-nucleotide changes are frequently the most common type of mutation, but many other types of [mutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation) are possible, and they occur at widely varying rates that may show systematic asymmetries or biases ([mutation bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation_bias)).

Mutations can involve large sections of DNA becoming [duplicated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_duplication), usually through [genetic recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_recombination).[^o36] This leads to [copy-number variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copy-number_variation) within a population. Duplications are a major source of raw material for evolving new genes.[^o37] Other types of mutation occasionally create new genes from previously noncoding DNA.[^o38][^o39]

In the [distribution of fitness effects (DFE)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation#Distribution_of_fitness_effects_%28DFE%29) for new mutations, only a minority of mutations are beneficial. Mutations with gross effects are typically deleterious. Studies in the fly *[Drosophila melanogaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drosophila_melanogaster)* suggest that if a mutation changes a protein produced by a gene, this will probably be harmful, with about 70 percent of these mutations having damaging effects, and the remainder being either neutral or weakly beneficial.[^o40]

This biological process of mutation is represented in population-genetic models in one of two ways, either as a deterministic pressure of recurrent mutation on allele frequencies, or a source of variation. In deterministic theory, evolution begins with a predetermined set of alleles and proceeds by shifts in continuous frequencies, as if the population is infinite. The occurrence of mutations in individuals is represented by a population-level "force" or "pressure" of mutation, i.e., the force of innumerable events of mutation with a scaled magnitude u applied to shifting frequencies f(A1) to f(A2). For instance, in the classic [mutation–selection balance](wikipedia://en/1674245) model,[^o41] the force of mutation pressure pushes the frequency of an allele upward, and selection against its deleterious effects pushes the frequency downward, so that a balance is reached at equilibrium, given (in the simplest case) by f = u/s.

This concept of mutation pressure is mostly useful for considering the implications of deleterious mutation, such as the mutation load and its implications for the evolution of the mutation rate.[^o42] Transformation of populations by mutation pressure is unlikely. Haldane[^o43] argued that it would require high mutation rates unopposed by selection, and Kimura[^o44] concluded even more pessimistically that even this was unlikely, as the process would take too long (see [evolution by mutation pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias_in_the_introduction_of_variation#Evolution_by_mutation_pressure)).

However, [evolution by mutation pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias_in_the_introduction_of_variation#Evolution_by_mutation_pressure) is possible under some circumstances and has long been suggested as a possible cause for the loss of unused traits.[^o45] For example, [pigments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pigment) are no longer useful when animals live in the darkness of caves, and tend to be lost.[^o46] An experimental example involves the loss of sporulation in experimental populations of *B. subtilis*. Sporulation is a complex trait encoded by many loci, such that the mutation rate for loss of the trait was estimated as an unusually high value, $\mu = 0.003$.[^o47] Loss of sporulation in this case can occur by recurrent mutation, without requiring selection for the loss of sporulation ability. When there is no selection for loss of function, the speed at which loss evolves depends more on the mutation rate than it does on the [effective population size](wikipedia://en/1543960),[^o48] indicating that it is driven more by mutation than by genetic drift.

The role of mutation as a source of novelty is different from these classical models of mutation pressure. When population-genetic models include a rate-dependent process of mutational introduction or origination, i.e., a process that introduces new alleles including neutral and beneficial ones, then the properties of mutation may have a more direct impact on the rate and direction of evolution, even if the rate of mutation is very low.[^o49][^o50] That is, the spectrum of mutation may become very important, particularly [mutation biases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutation_bias), predictable differences in the rates of occurrence for different types of mutations, because [bias in the introduction of variation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias_in_the_introduction_of_variation) can impose biases on the course of evolution.[^o51]

Mutation plays a key role in other classical and recent theories including [Muller%27s ratchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muller%27s_ratchet), [subfunctionalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subfunctionalization), Eigen's concept of an [error catastrophe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_catastrophe) and Lynch's [mutational hazard hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_biological_complexity#Mutational_hazard_hypothesis).

### Genetic drift {#Genetic_drift}

*Genetic drift* is a change in [allele frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele_frequency) caused by [random sampling](wikipedia://en/160361).[^o53] That is, the alleles in the offspring are a random sample of those in the parents.[^o54] Genetic drift may cause gene variants to disappear completely, and thereby reduce genetic variability. In contrast to natural selection, which makes gene variants more common or less common depending on their reproductive success,[^o55] the changes due to genetic drift are not driven by environmental or adaptive pressures, and are equally likely to make an allele more common as less common.

The effect of genetic drift is larger for alleles present in few copies than when an allele is present in many copies. The population genetics of genetic drift are described using either [branching processes](wikipedia://en/377202) or a [diffusion equation](wikipedia://en/645335) describing changes in allele frequency.[^o56] These approaches are usually applied to the Wright-Fisher and [Moran](wikipedia://en/21700432) models of population genetics. Assuming genetic drift is the only evolutionary force acting on an allele, after t generations in many replicated populations, starting with allele frequencies of p and q, the variance in allele frequency across those populations is

$V_t \approx pq\left(1-\exp\left\{-\frac{t}{2N_e} \right\}\right).$[^o57]

[Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) held the view that genetic drift plays at the most a minor role in evolution, and this remained the dominant view for several decades. No population genetics perspective have ever given genetic drift a central role by itself, but some have made genetic drift important in combination with another non-selective force. The [shifting balance theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shifting_balance_theory) of [Sewall Wright](wikipedia://en/1700593) held that the combination of population structure and genetic drift was important. [Motoo Kimura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motoo_Kimura)'s [neutral theory of molecular evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_theory_of_molecular_evolution) claims that most genetic differences within and between populations are caused by the combination of neutral mutations and genetic drift.[^o58]

The role of genetic drift by means of [sampling error](wikipedia://en/1955561) in evolution has been criticized by [John H Gillespie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_H_Gillespie)[^o59] and [Will Provine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_Provine),[^o60] who argue that selection on linked sites is a more important stochastic force, doing the work traditionally ascribed to genetic drift by means of sampling error. The mathematical properties of genetic draft are different from those of genetic drift.[^o61] The direction of the random change in allele frequency is [autocorrelated](wikipedia://en/2724) across generations.[^o53]

### Gene flow {#Gene_flow}

*Image: Gene flow final.png.* Gene flow is the transfer of [alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele) from one [population](wikipedia://en/22949) to another population through immigration of individuals. In this example, one of the birds from population A [immigrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immigrate) to population B, which has fewer of the dominant alleles, and through mating incorporates its alleles into the other population.

Because of physical barriers to migration, along with the limited tendency for individuals to move or spread (vagility), and tendency to remain or come back to natal place ([philopatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philopatry)), natural populations rarely all interbreed as may be assumed in theoretical random models ([panmixy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panmixy)).[^o63] There is usually a geographic range within which individuals are more closely [related](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficient_of_relatedness) to one another than those randomly selected from the general population. This is described as the extent to which a population is genetically structured.[^o64]

*Image: Greatwall large.jpg.* The [Great Wall of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China) is an obstacle to gene flow of some terrestrial species.[^o65]

Genetic structuring can be caused by migration due to historical [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change_%28general_concept%29), species [range expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_expansion) or current availability of [habitat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habitat). Gene flow is hindered by mountain ranges, oceans and deserts or even human-made structures such as the [Great Wall of China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Wall_of_China), which has hindered the flow of plant genes.[^o65]

[Gene flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_flow) is the exchange of genes between populations or species, breaking down the structure. Examples of gene flow within a species include the migration and then breeding of organisms, or the exchange of [pollen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollen). Gene transfer between species includes the formation of [hybrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hybrid_%28biology%29) organisms and [horizontal gene transfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_gene_transfer). Population genetic models can be used to identify which populations show significant genetic isolation from one another, and to reconstruct their history.[^o66]

Subjecting a population to isolation leads to [inbreeding depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inbreeding_depression). Migration into a population can introduce new genetic variants,[^o67] potentially contributing to [evolutionary rescue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_rescue). If a significant proportion of individuals or gametes migrate, it can also change allele frequencies, e.g. giving rise to [migration load](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_load#Migration_load).[^o68]

In the presence of gene flow, other [barriers to hybridization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reproductive_isolation) between two diverging populations of an [outcrossing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outcrossing) species are required for the populations to [become new species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speciation).

#### Horizontal gene transfer {#Horizontal_gene_transfer}

*Image: Tree Of Life (with horizontal gene transfer).svg.* Current tree of life showing vertical and horizontal gene transfers

Horizontal gene transfer is the transfer of genetic material from one organism to another organism that is not its offspring; this is most common among [prokaryotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prokaryote).[^o70] In medicine, this contributes to the spread of [antibiotic resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antibiotic_resistance), as when one bacteria acquires resistance genes it can rapidly transfer them to other species.[^o71] Horizontal transfer of genes from bacteria to eukaryotes such as the yeast *[Saccharomyces cerevisiae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saccharomyces_cerevisiae)* and the adzuki bean beetle *[Callosobruchus chinensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callosobruchus_chinensis)* may also have occurred.[^o72][^o73] An example of larger-scale transfers are the eukaryotic [bdelloid rotifers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bdelloidea), which appear to have received a range of genes from bacteria, fungi, and plants.[^o74] [Viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus) can also carry DNA between organisms, allowing transfer of genes even across [biological domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_%28taxonomy%29).[^o75] Large-scale gene transfer has also occurred between the ancestors of [eukaryotic cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eukaryote) and prokaryotes, during the acquisition of [chloroplasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chloroplast) and [mitochondria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitochondrion).[^o76]

## Linkage {#Linkage}

If all genes are in [linkage equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linkage_equilibrium), the effect of an allele at one locus can be averaged across the [gene pool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gene_pool) at other loci. In reality, one allele is frequently found in [linkage disequilibrium](wikipedia://en/681230) with genes at other loci, especially with genes located nearby on the same chromosome. [Recombination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recombination_%28biology%29) breaks up this linkage disequilibrium too slowly to avoid [genetic hitchhiking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_hitchhiking), where an allele at one locus rises to high frequency because it is [linked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_linkage) to an allele under selection at a nearby locus. Linkage also slows down the rate of adaptation, even in sexual populations.[^o78][^o79][^o80] The effect of linkage disequilibrium in slowing down the rate of adaptive evolution arises from a combination of the [Hill–Robertson effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hill–Robertson_effect) (delays in bringing beneficial mutations together) and [background selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Background_selection) (delays in separating beneficial mutations from deleterious [hitchhikers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_hitchhiking)).

Linkage is a problem for population genetic models that treat one gene locus at a time. It can, however, be exploited as a method for detecting the action of [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection) via [selective sweeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_sweep).

In the extreme case of an [asexual population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asexual_reproduction), linkage is complete, and population genetic equations can be derived and solved in terms of a travelling [wave](wikipedia://en/33516) of genotype frequencies along a simple [fitness landscape](wikipedia://en/310599).[^o81] Most [microbes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microorganisms), such as [bacteria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bacteria), are asexual. The population genetics of their [adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptation) have two contrasting regimes. When the product of the beneficial mutation rate and population size is small, asexual populations follow a "successional regime" of origin-fixation dynamics, with adaptation rate strongly dependent on this product. When the product is much larger, asexual populations follow a "concurrent mutations" regime with adaptation rate less dependent on the product, characterized by [clonal interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clonal_interference) and the appearance of a new beneficial mutation before the last one has [fixed](wikipedia://en/9185794).

## Applications {#Applications}

### Explaining levels of genetic variation {#Explaining_levels_of_genetic_variation}

[Neutral theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_theory_of_molecular_evolution) predicts that the level of [nucleotide diversity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleotide_diversity) in a population will be proportional to the product of the population size and the neutral mutation rate. The fact that levels of genetic diversity vary much less than population sizes do is known as the "paradox of variation".[^o85] While high levels of genetic diversity were one of the original arguments in favor of neutral theory, the paradox of variation has been one of the strongest arguments against neutral theory.

It is clear that levels of genetic diversity vary greatly within a species as a function of local recombination rate, due to both [genetic hitchhiking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_hitchhiking) and [background selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Background_selection). Most current solutions to the paradox of variation invoke some level of selection at linked sites.[^o86] For example, one analysis suggests that larger populations have more selective sweeps, which remove more neutral genetic diversity.[^o87] A negative correlation between mutation rate and population size may also contribute.[^o88]

Life history affects genetic diversity more than population history does, e.g. [r-strategists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R/K_selection_theory) have more genetic diversity.[^o86]

### Detecting selection {#Detecting_selection}

Population genetics models are used to infer which genes are undergoing selection. One common approach is to look for regions of high [linkage disequilibrium](wikipedia://en/681230) and low genetic variance along the chromosome, to detect recent [selective sweeps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selective_sweep).

A second common approach is the [McDonald–Kreitman test](wikipedia://en/30761750) which compares the amount of variation within a species ([polymorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_%28biology%29)) to the divergence between species (substitutions) at two types of sites; one assumed to be neutral. Typically, [synonymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonymous_substitution) sites are assumed to be neutral.[^o90] Genes undergoing positive selection have an excess of divergent sites relative to polymorphic sites. The test can also be used to obtain a genome-wide estimate of the proportion of substitutions that are fixed by positive selection, α.[^o91][^o92] According to the [neutral theory of molecular evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_theory_of_molecular_evolution), this number should be near zero. High numbers have therefore been interpreted as a genome-wide falsification of neutral theory.[^o93]

### Demographic inference {#Demographic_inference}

The simplest test for population structure in a sexually reproducing, diploid species, is to see whether genotype frequencies follow Hardy-Weinberg proportions as a function of allele frequencies. For example, in the simplest case of a single locus with two [alleles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allele) denoted **A** and **a** at frequencies *p* and *q*, random mating predicts freq(**AA**) = *p*<sup>2</sup> for the **AA** [homozygotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homozygote), freq(**aa**) = *q*<sup>2</sup> for the **aa** homozygotes, and freq(**Aa**) = 2*pq* for the [heterozygotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heterozygote). In the absence of population structure, Hardy-Weinberg proportions are reached within 1–2 generations of random mating. More typically, there is an excess of homozygotes, indicative of population structure. The extent of this excess can be quantified as the [inbreeding coefficient, F](wikipedia://en/683591).

Individuals can be clustered into *K* subpopulations.[^o95][^o96] The degree of population structure can then be calculated using [F<sub>ST</sub>](wikipedia://en/4196163), which is a measure of the proportion of genetic variance that can be explained by population structure. Genetic population structure can then be related to geographic structure, and [genetic admixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genetic_admixture) can be detected.

[Coalescent theory](wikipedia://en/3441831) relates genetic diversity in a sample to demographic history of the population from which it was taken. It normally assumes [neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_theory_of_molecular_evolution), and so sequences from more neutrally evolving portions of genomes are therefore selected for such analyses. It can be used to infer the relationships between species ([phylogenetics](wikipedia://en/3986130)), as well as the population structure, demographic history (e.g. [population bottlenecks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_bottlenecks), [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth)), [biological dispersal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_dispersal), [source–sink dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source–sink_dynamics)[^o97] and [introgression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introgression) within a species.

Another approach to demographic inference relies on the [allele frequency spectrum](wikipedia://en/47912905).[^o98]

### Evolution of genetic systems {#Evolution_of_genetic_systems}

By assuming that there are loci that control the genetic system itself, population genetic models are created to describe the [evolution of dominance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_dominance) and other forms of [robustness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robustness_%28evolution%29), the [evolution of sexual reproduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_sexual_reproduction) and recombination rates, the evolution of [mutation rates](wikipedia://en/1530353), the evolution of [evolutionary capacitors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolutionary_capacitance), the evolution of [costly signalling traits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signalling_theory), the [evolution of ageing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution_of_ageing), and the evolution of [co-operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-operation_%28evolution%29). For example, most mutations are deleterious, so the optimal [mutation rate](wikipedia://en/1530353) for a species may be a trade-off between the damage from a high deleterious mutation rate and the [metabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolism) costs of maintaining systems to reduce the mutation rate, such as DNA repair enzymes.[^o100]

One important aspect of such models is that selection is only strong enough to purge deleterious mutations and hence overpower mutational bias towards degradation if the selection coefficient s is greater than the inverse of the [effective population size](wikipedia://en/1543960). This is known as the drift barrier and is related to the [nearly neutral theory of molecular evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nearly_neutral_theory_of_molecular_evolution). Drift barrier theory predicts that species with large effective population sizes will have highly streamlined, efficient genetic systems, while those with small population sizes will have bloated and complex [genomes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genome) containing for example [introns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intron) and [transposable elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transposable_element).[^o101] However, somewhat paradoxically, species with large population sizes might be so tolerant to the consequences of certain types of errors that they evolve higher error rates, e.g. in [transcription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcription_%28genetics%29) and [translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_%28biology%29), than small populations.[^o102]
