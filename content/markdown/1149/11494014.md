# Strong product of graphs

*Image: King's graph.svg.* The [king's graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_graph), a strong product of two [path graphs](wikipedia://en/4587190)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Strong product of graphs"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **strong product** is a way of combining two graphs to make a larger graph. Two vertices are adjacent in the strong product when they come from pairs of vertices in the factor graphs that are either adjacent or identical. The strong product is one of several different [graph product](wikipedia://en/11492935) operations that have been studied in graph theory. The strong product of any two graphs can be constructed as the union of two other products of the same two graphs, the [Cartesian product of graphs](wikipedia://en/2856852) and the [tensor product of graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_graphs).

:::

An example of a strong product is the [king's graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_graph), the graph of moves of a chess king on a chessboard, which can be constructed as a strong product of path graphs. Decompositions of planar graphs and related graph classes into strong products have been used as a central tool to prove many other results about these graphs.

Care should be exercised when encountering the term *strong product* in the literature, since it has also been used to denote the tensor product of graphs.[^o2]

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and example"}

## Definition and example {#Definition_and_example}

The **strong product** $G \boxtimes H$ of [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ and $H$ is a graph such that[^o5] the vertex set of $G \boxtimes H$ is the Cartesian product $V(G) \times V(H)$; and distinct vertices $(u,u' )$ and $(v,v' )$ are adjacent in $G \boxtimes H$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922):

$u = v$ and $u'$ is adjacent to $v'$, **or**

$u' = v'$ and $u$ is adjacent to $v$, **or**

$u$ is adjacent to $v$ and $u'$ is adjacent to $v'$.

It is the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/2856852) and the [tensor product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_graphs).

For example, the [king's graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_graph), a graph whose vertices are squares of a [chessboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chessboard) and whose edges represent possible moves of a chess king, is a strong product of two [path graphs](wikipedia://en/4587190). Its horizontal edges come from the Cartesian product, and its diagonal edges come from the tensor product of the same two paths. Together, these two kinds of edges make up the entire strong product.[^o6]

:::

## Properties and applications {#Properties_and_applications}

Every [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is a subgraph of a strong product of a path and a graph of [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086) at most six.[^o9][^o10] This result has been used to prove that planar graphs have bounded [queue number](wikipedia://en/46183215),[^o9] small [universal graphs](wikipedia://en/1452141) and concise [adjacency labeling schemes](wikipedia://en/24109545),[^o11][^o12][^o13][^o14] and bounded nonrepetitive chromatic number[^o15] and centered chromatic number.[^o16] This product structure can be found in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time).[^o17][^o18] Beyond planar graphs, extensions of these results have been proven for graphs of bounded [genus](wikipedia://en/8149170),[^o9][^o19] graphs with a [forbidden minor](wikipedia://en/13547826) that is an [apex graph](wikipedia://en/28228942),[^o9] bounded-degree graphs with any forbidden minor,[^o20] and [k-planar graphs](wikipedia://en/38249632).[^o21]

The [clique number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clique_number) of the strong product of any two graphs equals the product of the clique numbers of the two graphs.[^o22] If two graphs both have bounded [twin-width](wikipedia://en/71380095), and in addition one of them has bounded degree, then their strong product also has bounded twin-width.[^o23]

A [leaf power](wikipedia://en/49639194) is a graph formed from the leaves of a tree by making two leaves adjacent when their distance in the tree is below some threshold $k$. If $G$ is a $k$-leaf power of a tree $T$, then $T$ can be found as a subgraph of a strong product of $G$ with a $k$-vertex cycle. This embedding has been used in recognition algorithms for leaf powers.[^o24]

The strong product of a 7-vertex [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) and a 4-vertex [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816), $C_7\boxtimes K_4$, has been suggested as a possibility for a 10-chromatic [biplanar graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biplanar_graph) that would improve the known bounds on the [Earth–Moon problem](wikipedia://en/72683786); another suggested example is the graph obtained by removing any vertex from $C_5\boxtimes K_4$. In both cases, the number of vertices in these graphs is more than 9 times the size of their largest [independent set](wikipedia://en/524501), implying that their chromatic number is at least 10. However, it is not known whether these graphs are biplanar.[^o25]
