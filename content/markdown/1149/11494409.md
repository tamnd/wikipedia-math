# Element (category theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Element"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the concept of an **element**, or a **point**, generalizes the more usual [set theoretic](wikipedia://en/27553) concept of an [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) to an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) of any [category](wikipedia://en/53759). This idea often allows restating of definitions or properties of [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) (such as [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) or [product](wikipedia://en/237868)) given by a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) in more familiar terms, by stating their relation to elements. Some very general theorems, such as [Yoneda's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoneda's_lemma) and the [Mitchell embedding theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_embedding_theorem), are of great utility for this, by allowing one to work in a context where these translations are valid. This approach to category theory – in particular the use of the Yoneda lemma in this way – is due to [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck), and is often called the method of the **functor of points**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose **C** is any [category](wikipedia://en/53759) and *A*, *T* are two objects of **C**. A *T*-valued point of *A* is simply a morphism *p* : *T* → *A*. The set of all *T*-valued points of *A* varies [functorially](wikipedia://en/10987) with *T*, giving rise to the "functor of points" of *A*; according to the [Yoneda lemma](wikipedia://en/34359), this completely determines *A* as an object of **C**.

:::

## Properties of morphisms {#Properties_of_morphisms}

Many properties of morphisms can be restated in terms of points. For example, a map *f* is said to be a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) if

For all maps *g*, *h*, if *f* ∘ *g* = *f* ∘ *h* then *g* = *h*.

Suppose *f* : *B* → *C* and *g*, *h* : *A* → *B* in *C*. Then *g* and *h* are *A*-valued points of *B*, and therefore monomorphism is equivalent to the more familiar statement

*f* is a monomorphism if it is an [injective function](wikipedia://en/45196) on points of *B*.

Some care is necessary. *f* is an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539) if the [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) condition holds:

For all maps *g*, *h* (of some suitable type), *g* ∘ *f* = *h* ∘ *f* implies *g* = *h*.

In set theory, the term "epimorphism" is synonymous with "[surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection)", i.e.

Every point of *C* is the [image](wikipedia://en/579311), under *f*, of some point of *B*.

This is clearly not the translation of the first statement into the language of points, and in fact these statements are *not* equivalent in general. However, in some contexts, such as [abelian categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_categories), "monomorphism" and "epimorphism" are backed by sufficiently strong conditions that in fact they do allow such a reinterpretation on points.

Similarly, [categorical](wikipedia://en/5869) constructions such as the [product](wikipedia://en/237868) have pointed analogues. Recall that if *A*, *B* are two objects of **C**, their product *A* × *B* is an object such that

There exist maps *p* : *A* × *B* → *A*, *q* : *A* × *B* → *B*, and for any *T* and maps *f* : *T* → *A*, *g* : *T* → *B*, there exists a unique map *h* : *T* → *A* × *B* such that *f* = *p* ∘ *h* and *g* = *q* ∘ *h*.

In this definition, *f* and *g* are *T*-valued points of *A* and *B*, respectively, while *h* is a *T*-valued point of *A* × *B*. An alternative definition of the product is therefore:

*A* × *B* is an object of **C**, together with projection maps *p* : *A* × *B* → *A* and *q* : *A* × *B* → *B*, such that *p* and *q* furnish a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between points of *A* × *B* and *pairs of points* of *A* and *B*.

This is the more familiar definition of the [product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two sets.

## Geometric origin {#Geometric_origin}

The terminology is geometric in origin; in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), Grothendieck introduced the notion of a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) in order to unify the subject with [arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177), which dealt with the same idea of studying solutions to [polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation) (i.e. [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808)) but where the solutions are not [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) but [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), or even elements of some [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615). A scheme is then just that: a scheme for collecting together all the manifestations of a variety defined by the same equations but with solutions taken in different number sets. One scheme gives a complex variety, whose points are its (Spec **C**)-valued points, as well as the set of (Spec **Q**)-valued points (rational solutions to the equations), and even (Spec **F**<sub>*p*</sub>)-valued points (solutions [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) *p*).

One feature of the language of points is evident from this example: it is, in general, not enough to consider just points with values in a single object. For example, the equation *x*<sup>2</sup> + 1 = 0 (which defines a scheme) has no [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) solutions, but it has complex solutions, namely ±*i*. It also has one solution modulo 2 and two modulo 5, 13, 29, etc. (all [primes](wikipedia://en/23666) that are 1 modulo 4). Just taking the real solutions would give no information whatsoever.

## Relation with set theory {#Relation_with_set_theory}

The situation is analogous to the case where **C** is the category **Set**, [of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) of actual elements. In this case, we have the "one-pointed" set $\{1\}$, and the elements of any set *S* are the same as the $\{1\}$-valued points of *S*. In addition, though, there are the $\{1, 2\}$-valued points, which are pairs of elements of *S*, or elements of *S* × *S*. In the context of sets, these higher points are extraneous: *S* is determined completely by its {1}-points. However, as shown above, this is special (in this case, it is because all sets are iterated [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889) of $\{1\}$).
