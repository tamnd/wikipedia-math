# Nicholson–Bailey model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nicholson–Bailey model"}

The **Nicholson–Bailey model** was developed in the 1930s to describe the [population dynamics](wikipedia://en/501466) of a coupled host-[parasitoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasitoid) system. It is named after [Alexander John Nicholson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_John_Nicholson) and [Victor Albert Bailey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Albert_Bailey). Host-parasite and [prey-predator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predator-prey) systems can also be represented with the Nicholson-Bailey model. The model is closely related to the [Lotka–Volterra model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka–Volterra_equation), which describes the dynamics of antagonistic populations (preys and predators) using [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309).

:::

The model uses (discrete time) [difference equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_equation) to describe the [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth) of host-parasite populations. The model assumes that parasitoids search for hosts at random, and that both parasitoids and hosts are assumed to be distributed in a non-contiguous ("clumped") fashion in the environment. In its original form, the model does not allow for stable coexistence. Subsequent refinements of the model, notably adding density dependence on several terms, allowed this coexistence to happen.

## Equations {#Equations}

### Derivation {#Derivation}

The model is defined in discrete time. It is usually expressed as [^o4][^o5]

$$\begin{array}{rcl} H_{t+1} & = & k H_t e^{-a P_t} \\ P_{t+1} & = & c H_t \left ( 1-e^{-aP_t} \right ) \end{array}$$

with *H* the population size of the host, *P* the population size of the parasitoid, *k* the reproductive rate of the host, *a* the searching efficiency of the parasitoid, and *c* the average number of viable eggs that a parasitoid lays on a single host.

This model can be explained based on probability.[^o6] $e^{-a P_t}$ is the probability that the host will survive $P_t$ predators; whereas $1 - e^{-a P_t}$ is that they will not, bearing in mind the parasitoid eventually will hatch into larva and escape.

### Analysis of the Nicholson–Bailey model {#Analysis_of_the_Nicholson–Bailey_model}

When $0 < k < 1$, $(\bar H, \bar P) = (0,0)$ is the unique non-negative fixed point and all non-negative solutions converge to $(0,0)$. When $k = 1$, all non-negative solutions lie on level curves of the function $z = H + P - \text{ln}(P)$ and converge to a fixed point on the $P$-axis.[^o8] When $k > 1$, this system admits one unstable positive fixed point, at

$$\begin{array}{rcl} \bar H & = & \frac{k\, \text{ln}(k)}{(k-1)\, ac} \\ \bar P & = & \frac{\text{ln}(k)}{a} \end{array}.$$

It has been proven[^o9] that all positive solutions whose initial conditions are not equal to $(\bar H, \bar P)$ are unbounded and exhibit oscillations with infinitely increasing amplitude.

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variations"}

### Variations {#Variations}

Density dependence can be added to the model, by assuming that the growth rate of the host decreases at high abundances. The equation for the parasitoid is unchanged, and the equation for the host is modified:

$$\begin{array}{rcl} H_{t+1} & = & H_t e^{r(1-H_t/K)}e^{-a P_t} \\ P_{t+1} & = & c H_t \left ( 1-e^{-aP_t} \right ) \end{array}$$

The host rate of increase *k* is replaced by *r*, which becomes negative when the host population density reaches *K*.

:::
