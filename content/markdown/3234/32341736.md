# Tau (mathematics)

*Image: Circle\_radians\_tau.gif.* An arc of a circle with the same length as the radius of that circle corresponds to an angle of 1 radian. A full circle corresponds to a full turn, or approximately 6.28 radians, which is expressed here using the Greek letter [tau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tau) ($\tau$).

*Image: Degree-Radian Conversion tau.svg.* A comparison of angles expressed in degrees and radians

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Tau"}

The number $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ (; spelled out as **tau**) is a [mathematical constant](wikipedia://en/48779551), equal to 2[$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601), that has been proposed as an alternative to [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) by various proponents, who argue that [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) would be simpler, clearer or pedagogically preferable if formulas and quantities (including [angles](wikipedia://en/1196)) were described in terms of $\tau$ instead of $\pi$. A constant named tau is implemented in some [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015) and calculator apps as an alias for 2$\pi$. $\tau$ is approximately equal to

:::

\6.28318

It is the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220)'s [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) to its [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815), whereas $\pi$ instead corresponds to the ratio of a circle's circumference to its [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007). Like $\pi$, $\tau$ can be defined in several other equivalent ways: notably, $\tau$ is equal to the [period](wikipedia://en/76848) of the complex [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678). Like $\pi$, $\tau$ is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689) and [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325).

While $\pi$ is used almost exclusively in mainstream mathematical education and practice, it has been proposed in recent decades, most notably by physicist [Michael Hartl](wikipedia://en/77109322) in his 2010 article *The Tau Manifesto*, that $\tau$ should be used instead. Hartl and other proponents argue that $\tau$ is the more natural circle constant and its use leads to conceptually simpler and more intuitive mathematical notation; in particular, $\tau$ is the angle measure of a whole [turn](wikipedia://en/855329) in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), whereas $\pi$ corresponds only to a half-turn, so proponents argue that angles in radians are more intuitive when expressed as fractions of $\tau$ compared to $\pi$.[^o100]

Critics have responded that the benefits of using $\tau$ over $\pi$ are trivial and that given the ubiquity and historical significance of $\pi$ a change is unlikely to occur.[^o104]

The proposal did not initially gain widespread acceptance in the mathematical community, but awareness of $\tau$ has become more widespread,[^o112] including having been added to several major programming languages and calculators.

## Fundamentals {#Fundamentals}

### Geometric definitions {#Geometric_definitions}

The number $\tau$ can be defined as the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of the [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916) to the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220):

$$\tau = \frac{\mbox{circumference}}{\mbox{radius}}.$$

(The ratio $\tfrac{\operatorname{circumference}}{\operatorname{radius}}$ is constant regardless of the circle's size, designating $\tau$ as the fixed ratio between the circumference and the radius of any circle.)

The ratio of a circle's circumference to its radius can be related to the number [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601):

$$\pi = \frac{\mbox{circumference}}{\mbox{diameter}} = \frac{\mbox{circumference}}{2 \cdot \mbox{radius}} = \frac{\tau}{2},$$

showing that $\tau$ equals $2\pi$. Accordingly, the number $\tau$ shares many of the properties of $\pi$, including being a [transcendental number](wikipedia://en/30325) and hence also [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689).

*Image: Tau-angles.svg.* Some special angles in radians, stated in terms of $\tau$

When [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) are used as the unit of [angular measure](wikipedia://en/1196), there are $\tau$ radians in one full [turn](wikipedia://en/855329) of a circle, and fractions of $\tau$ correspond (in radians) to angles that are the same fractions of a turn: for instance, $\tfrac{1}{8}\tau \operatorname{rad}$ is an eighth of a turn; $\tfrac{3}{4}\tau \operatorname{rad}$ is three-quarters of a turn.

### Other definitions {#Other_definitions}

Like $\pi$, $\tau$ can also be defined analytically, in terms of integrals, series or trigonometric functions.

$\tau$ can be defined as the smallest positive real number $x$ such that $\cos(x) = 1$, or as the [period](wikipedia://en/76848) of the [sine and cosine](wikipedia://en/5010838) functions. Equivalently, $\tau$ can be defined as the period of the complex [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678), which shares the same period as the sine and cosine functions as shown by [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613). (The sine, cosine and exponential functions themselves can be defined independently of geometry using [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448), allowing to define $\tau$ without any reference to circles or geometry.)

Other formulas equal to $\tau$ include [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) such as $\textstyle \int_{-2}^{2} \sqrt{4 - x^2} \, dx$ (which is half the area of a circle of radius 2), or sums of [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series), such as the [Gregory series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregory_series):

$$\tau = 8 \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{n}}{2n+1} = 8 - \frac{8}{3} + \frac{8}{5} - \frac{8}{7} + \frac{8}{9}...$$

More series definitions can be found at *[List of formulae involving $\pi$](wikipedia://en/4659082)*.

## History {#History}

The use of $\tau$ or of other dedicated symbols for $2\pi$ in lieu of $\pi$ has been advocated by several mathematicians and other proponents in recent decades. The number 6.28... has been historically studied and denoted by various symbols, but in the 18th century, $\pi$ became the universally-understood symbol for 3.14... instead.

### $\tau$ notation proposal {#notation_proposal}

The proposal to use the Greek letter $\tau$ as a circle constant representing $2\pi$ dates to [Michael Hartl](wikipedia://en/77109322)'s 2010 publication, *The Tau Manifesto*, although the symbol had been independently suggested earlier by Joseph Lindenburg (c. 1990), John Fisher (2004) and Peter Harremoës (2010).[^o10]

Hartl offered two reasons for the choice of notation. First, $\tau$ is the number of radians in one *turn*, and both $\tau$ and *turn* begin with a sound. Second, $\tau$ visually resembles $\pi$, whose association with the circle constant is unavoidable. Moreover, Hartl asserted that the use of a familiar Greek letter like $\tau$ was preferable to the choice of a completely new symbol (like Bob Palais' proposed symbol $\pi\!\;\!\!\!\pi$), since people would be immediately able to pronounce it when they encounter it.

### Earlier proposals {#Earlier_proposals}

There had been a number of earlier proposals for a new circle constant equal to $2\pi$, together with varying suggestions for its name and symbol.

In 2001, Dr. Bob Palais of the [University of Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Utah) asserted that $\pi$ is "wrong" as the fundamental circle constant and that $2\pi$ was the proper value.[^o98] He proposed using a "$\pi$ with three legs" symbol to denote the constant ($\pi\!\;\!\!\!\pi = 2\pi$), and referred to angles as fractions of a "turn" ($\tfrac 1 4 \pi\!\;\!\!\!\pi = \tfrac 1 4\,\mathrm{turn}$). Palais stated that the word "turn" served as both the name of the new constant and a reference to the ordinary language meaning of [turn](wikipedia://en/855329).[^o12]

In 2008, [Robert P. Crease](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_P._Crease) proposed defining a constant as the ratio of circumference to radius, an idea supported by [John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807), who is cited to have said that "$2\pi$ is obviously the correct constant".[^o13]

The same year, Thomas Colignatus proposed the uppercase Greek letter [theta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theta), $\Theta$, to represent $2\pi$ due to its visual resemblance of a circle.[^o109] For a similar reason another proposal suggested the Phoenician and Hebrew letter [teth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teth), 𐤈 or ט, (from which the letter theta was derived), due to its connection with wheels and circles in ancient cultures.[^o110][^o111]

### Historical use of other symbols to represent 6.28... {#Historical_use_of_other_symbols_to_represent_6.28...}

The number 6.28... has been studied historically long before contemporary proposals to name it $\tau$. In 1424, [Jamshid al-Kashi](wikipedia://en/10553199) computed the number 6.28... to 9 [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) ([base](wikipedia://en/701207) 60) digits.[^o15] The first 16 sexagesimal digits of $\tau$ are: 6;16,59,28,[^o16]

The meaning of the symbol $\pi$ was not originally defined as the ratio of circumference to diameter, and at times was used in representations of the constant 6.28... .

Early works in circle geometry used the letter $\pi$ to designate the [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) (i.e., [circumference](wikipedia://en/5916)) in different fractional representations of circle constants and in 1697 [David Gregory](wikipedia://en/703683) used $\tfrac{\pi}{\rho}$ (pi over rho) to denote the perimeter divided by the radius (6.28...).[^o96][^o97]

Subsequently $\pi$ came to be used as a single symbol to represent the ratios in whole. [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) initially used the single letter $\pi$ to denote the constant 6.28... in his 1727 *Essay Explaining the Properties of Air*.[^o17][^o18] Euler would later use the letter $\pi$ for 3.14... in his 1736 *[Mechanica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanica)*[^o19] and 1748 *[Introductio in analysin infinitorum](wikipedia://en/19777721),*[^o20] though defined as half the circumference of a [circle of radius 1](wikipedia://en/27072071) rather than the ratio of circumference to diameter. Elsewhere in *Mechanica*, Euler instead used the letter $\pi$ for one-fourth of the circumference of a unit circle, or 1.57... .[^o21][^o22] Usage of the letter $\pi$, sometimes for 3.14... and other times for 6.28..., became widespread, with the convention varying as late as 1761;[^o23] afterward, the use of $\pi$ for 3.14... exclusively became standard, as a result of the influence of Euler's later works.[^o24][^o108]

## Notation using *τ* {#Notation_using_τ}

Proponents argue that while use of $\tau$ in place of $2\pi$ does not change any of the underlying mathematics, it does lead to simpler and more intuitive notation in many areas. Michael Hartl's *Tau Manifesto* gives many examples of formulas that are asserted to be clearer where $\tau$ is used instead of $\pi$.[^o101][^o102][^o103]

### Units of angle {#Units_of_angle}

Hartl and Robert Palais[^o12] have argued that $\tau$ allows angles to be expressed more directly and in a way that makes clear the link between the radian measure and rotation around the unit circle.

Since an angle (in radians) of $\tau$ corresponds to a [turn](wikipedia://en/855329), an angle (in radians) given as a fraction of $\tau$ corresponds to the same fraction of a turn. (This is not the case if the angle is expressed as a fraction of $\pi$ instead, in which case it would correspond to the same fraction of a half-turn, not of a whole turn.) For instance, $\tfrac{3}{4}\tau \operatorname{rad}$ can be easily interpreted as $\tfrac{3}{4}$ of a turn, in contrast with the same angle written as $\tfrac{3}{2}\pi \operatorname{rad}$, where the meaning could be less intuitive. For this reason, Hartl has argued that the use of $\pi$ in mathematical education is pedagogically detrimental, and that using $\tau$ would improve clarity in mathematical teaching regarding [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261) and [radians](wikipedia://en/26003).

Critics have responded that a full rotation is not necessarily the correct or fundamental reference measure for angles and two other possibilities, the [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956) and straight angle, each have historical precedent. [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9417#System_of_measurement_and_arithmetic) used the right angle as the basic unit of angle, and David Butler has suggested that $\tfrac{1}{4}\tau = \tfrac{1}{2}\pi \approx 1.57$, which he denotes with the Greek letter $\eta$ ([eta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eta)), should be seen as the fundamental circle constant.[^o27]

### Trigonometric functions {#Trigonometric_functions}

*Image: Standard Trigonometric Functions.svg.* Plots of common trigonometric functions (sin, cos, tan, cot, sec, csc) with *x*-axis running from −τ through +τ

Hartl has argued that the periodic [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) are simplified when using $\tau$, as it corresponds to the [period](wikipedia://en/76848) of the [sine and cosine](wikipedia://en/5010838) functions: $\sin$ repeats with [period](wikipedia://en/76848) $T = \tau$, reaches a maximum at $\tfrac{1}{4}T = \tfrac{1}{4}\tau$ and a minimum at $\tfrac{3}{4}T = \tfrac{3}{4}\tau$. (By contrast, $\pi$ is only half the period of the $\sin$ and $\cos$ functions.)

More generally, given constants $\omega$ and $\phi$, a [sinusoidal](wikipedia://en/324749) function of the form $t \mapsto \cos(\omega t + \phi)$ or $t \mapsto \sin(\omega t + \phi)$ has the [period](wikipedia://en/76848) $T=\frac{\tau}{\omega}$, and the number of periods in each interval of length 1 is $f=\frac{1}{T}=\frac{\omega}{\tau}$. Here, $\omega$ can be interpreted as an [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) and $f$ as a [rotational frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_frequency).

As a result, in contexts related to sinusoidal waves or periodic rotation, $\tau=2\pi$ is the conversion factor between quantities corresponding to numbers of [turns](wikipedia://en/855329) or periods (i.e. [frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_frequency)) and quantities corresponding to numbers of [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) or [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) units (i.e. [angular frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency)). For instance:

- [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) $\omega$ is equal to $\tau$ times the [rotational frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_frequency) $f$ (or, equivalently, to $\tau$ divided by the [period duration](wikipedia://en/10779) $T$);
- angular [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber) $k$ is equal to $\tau$ times the ordinary [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber) $\tilde{\nu}$ (or, equivalently, to $\tau$ divided by the [spatial period](wikipedia://en/33125) $\lambda$).

The following table shows particular values of the [sin](wikipedia://en/5010838), [cos](wikipedia://en/5010838) and [tan](wikipedia://en/30367) functions, for arguments expressed in terms of $\tau$:

$$\begin{alignedat}{3} \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{16}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{2-\sqrt{2}}}{2} &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{16}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2}}}{2} &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{16}\right) &= \sqrt{2}-1 \\[0.6em] \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{12}\right) &= \frac{1}{2} &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{12}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{12}\right) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{3}} \\[0.6em] \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{10}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{10-2\sqrt{5}}}{4} &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{10}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{4} &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{10}\right) &= \sqrt{5-2\sqrt{5}} \\[0.6em] \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{8}\right) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{8}\right) &= \frac{1}{\sqrt{2}} &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{8}\right) &= 1 \\[0.6em] \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{6}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{6}\right) &= \frac{1}{2} &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{6}\right) &= \sqrt{3} \\[0.6em] \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{5}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{10+2\sqrt{5}}}{4} &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{5}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{4} &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{5}\right) &= \sqrt{5+2\sqrt{5}} \\[0.6em] \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{4}\right) &= 1 &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{4}\right) &= 0 &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{4}\right) &\text{ is undefined (or ∞)} \\[0.6em] \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{3}\right) &= \frac{\sqrt{3}}{2} &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{3}\right) &= \frac{-1}{2} &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{3}\right) &= -\sqrt{3} \\[0.6em] \sin\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right) &= 0 &\qquad \cos\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right) &= -1 &\qquad \tan\left(\frac{\tau}{2}\right) &= 0 \\[0.6em] \sin(\tau) &= 0 &\qquad \cos(\tau) &= 1 &\qquad \tan(\tau) &= 0 \end{alignedat}$$

### Area of a circle {#Area_of_a_circle}

Critics have argued that the formula for the [area of a circle](wikipedia://en/1896782) $A = \pi r^{2}$ is more complicated when restated as $A = \tfrac{1}{2}\tau r^{2}$. Hartl and others have responded that the $\tfrac{1}{2}$ factor is meaningful, arising from either [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) or geometric proofs for the area of a circle as [half the circumference times the radius](wikipedia://en/1896782#Triangle_proof). Moreover, Hartl has argued that $\tfrac{1}{2}\tau r^{2}$ can be seen as a special case of the more general formula $\tfrac{1}{2}\theta r^{2}$ for the area of a [circular sector](wikipedia://en/5578920#Sector_area) spanned by the angle $\theta$ when $\theta$ is taken to be $\tau$ (a whole turn).

::: {#o31 .statement cue=named-heading name="Euler's identity and roots of unity"}

### Euler's identity and roots of unity {#Euler's_identity_and_roots_of_unity}

[Euler's identity](wikipedia://en/46740), $e^{i\pi} + 1 = 0$, sometimes claimed to be "the most beautiful theorem in mathematics"[^o32], becomes $e^{i\tau/2} + 1 = 0$ in terms of $\tau$. Hartl has asserted that $e^{i\tau} = 1$ (which he also called "Euler's identity") is a more fundamental and meaningful equation.

John Conway noted[^o13] that Euler's identity is a specific case of the general formula of the $n$th [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity), $\sqrt[n]{1} = e^{i\tau k/n} (k = 1, 2, ..., n)$, which he maintained is preferable and more economical than Euler's identity.

:::

### Higher-dimensional spheres and balls {#Higher-dimensional_spheres_and_balls}

While $\tau$ corresponds to the circumference of a unit circle in a two-dimensional Euclidean plane, the situation is more complicated in higher dimensions, where the formulas for the [volume and surface area of a hypersphere in higher-dimensional Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/39782#Volume_and_surface_area) involve fractions and powers of $\pi$ (or $\tau$).

For a given integer $n$, the following formulas yield the generalized volume $V_n$ and surface area $S_{n-1}$ of a $n$-dimensional unit hypersphere (in $n$-dimensional Euclidean space):

$${\displaystyle S_{n-1} = \frac{2 \pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{n}{2}\bigr)} = \frac{(\frac{\pi}{2})^{\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \rfloor} \cdot 2^n}{(n-2)!!}; \quad V_n = \frac{\pi^{n/2}}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{n}{2} + 1\bigr)} = \frac{(\frac{\pi}{2})^{\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \rfloor} \cdot 2^n}{n!!} }$$

or equivalently in terms of $\tau$ instead of $\pi$:

$$S_{n-1} = \frac{2 (\frac{\tau}{2})^{n/2}}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{n}{2}\bigr)} = \frac{(\frac{\tau}{4})^{\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \rfloor} \cdot 2^n}{(n-2)!!}; \quad V_n = \frac{(\frac{\tau}{2})^{n/2}}{\Gamma\bigl(\frac{n}{2} + 1\bigr)} = \frac{(\frac{\tau}{4})^{\lfloor \frac{n}{2} \rfloor} \cdot 2^n}{n!!}$$

where $\Gamma$ is the [Euler gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) and $!!$ denotes the [double factorial](wikipedia://en/507208).

Moreover, there exist the following recurrence relations between successive values of $V_n$ and $S_n$:

$$V_n = \frac{S_{n-1}}{n}; \quad S_n = \tau V_{n-1}$$

For the first values of the dimension $n$, the values of the generalized volume $V_n$ and surface area $S_{n-1}$ of a unit hypersphere are as follows, here expressed in terms of $\tau$ instead of $\pi$:

| Dimension $n$ | Hyper-volume $V_{n}$ of a unit ball | Hyper-surface area $S_{n-1}$ of a unit ball |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $2$ | $2$ |
| 2 | $\frac{\tau}{2}$ | $\tau$ |
| 3 | $\frac{2\tau}{3}$ | $2\tau$ |
| 4 | $\frac{\tau^2}{8}$ | $\frac{\tau^2}{2}$ |

Only when the dimension $n$ is 2 does $\tau$ have a privileged status as the circumference of the unit circle (the unit circle being a 2-dimensional ball, and circumference corresponding to generalized 1-dimensional volume). In higher dimensions, the particular choice of the constant $\tau$ instead of $\pi$ becomes less advantageous. However, Hartl argues that circles and angles in the two-dimensional case are far more frequently encountered than higher-dimensional cases, and that it thus makes sense for the choice of the named constant $\tau$ to correspond to circumference in the two-dimensional case.

## In culture {#In_culture}

$\tau$ has made numerous appearances in culture. It is celebrated annually on June 28, known as Tau Day.[^o59] Supporters of $\tau$ are called tauists.[^o103] $\tau$ has been covered in videos by [Vi Hart](wikipedia://en/31441421),[^o60][^o61][^o62] *[Numberphile](wikipedia://en/61716514)*,[^o63][^o64][^o65] *[SciShow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SciShow)*,[^o66] [Steve Mould](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Mould),[^o67][^o68][^o69] [Khan Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khan_Academy),[^o70] and [3Blue1Brown](wikipedia://en/58357050),[^o22][^o71] and it has appeared in the comics *[xkcd](wikipedia://en/10825442)*,[^o72][^o73] *[Saturday Morning Breakfast Cereal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturday_Morning_Breakfast_Cereal)*,[^o74][^o75][^o76] and *[Sally Forth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sally_Forth_%28Greg_Howard_comic_strip%29)*.[^o77] The [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology) usually announces admissions on March 14 at 6:28 p.m., which is on [Pi Day](wikipedia://en/25013) at Tau Time.[^o78] Peter Harremoës has used $\tau$ in a mathematical research article which was granted the journal *Kybernetika**s Editor's Award for the year 2016.[^o105]***
