# Helly family

*Image: 3-Helly property family of edges.svg.* Each hyperedge of a [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535) is a set of vertices. No vertex is shared by all 4 edges. Some subfamilies with 3 edges created by removing an edge are non-intersecting (edges 1, 3 and 4), but others aren't (edges 1, 2 and 3 which all share vertex 3), so the 4 edge family is not minimal. However, it isn't possible to remove an edge from either of the non-intersecting 3 edge subfamilies to get a non-intersecting 2 edge subfamily. Thus, the edges form a Helly family of order 3.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Helly family"}

In [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), a **Helly family** of order $k$ is a family of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) in which every minimal *subfamily with an empty [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429)* has $k$ or fewer sets in it. Equivalently, every finite subfamily such that every $k$-fold intersection is [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) has non-empty total intersection.[^o2] The **$k$-Helly property** is the property of being a Helly family of order $k$.[^o3]

:::

The number $k$ is frequently omitted from these names in the case that $k = 2$. Thus, a set-family has the **Helly property** if, for every $n$ sets $s_1,\ldots,s_n$ in the family, if $\forall i,j\in[n]: s_i \cap s_j \neq\emptyset$, then $s_1 \cap \cdots \cap s_n \neq\emptyset$.

These concepts are named after [Eduard Helly](wikipedia://en/2202570) (1884–1943); [Helly's theorem](wikipedia://en/1409506) on [convex sets](wikipedia://en/6292), which gave rise to this notion, states that convex sets in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of dimension $n$ are a Helly family of order $n + 1$.[^o2]

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- In the family of all subsets of the set {*a*,*b*,*c*,*d*}, the subfamily {{*a*,*b*,*c*}, {*a*,*b*,*d*}, {*a*,*c*,*d*}, {*b*,*c*,*d*}} has an empty intersection, but removing any set from this subfamily causes it to have a nonempty intersection. Therefore, it is a minimal subfamily with an empty intersection. It has four sets in it, and is the largest possible minimal subfamily with an empty intersection, so the family of all subsets of the set {*a*,*b*,*c*,*d*} is a Helly family of order 4.
- Let *I* be a finite set of closed [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172) of the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) with an empty intersection. Let *A* be the interval whose left endpoint *a* is as large as possible, and let *B* be the interval whose right endpoint *b* is as small as possible. Then, if *a* were less than or equal to *b*, all numbers in the range \[*a*,*b*\] would belong to all intervals of *I*, violating the assumption that the intersection of *I* is empty, so it must be the case that *a* > *b*. Thus, the two-interval subfamily {*A*,*B*} has an empty intersection, and the family *I* cannot be minimal unless *I* = {*A*,*B*}. Therefore, all minimal families of intervals with empty intersections have two or fewer intervals in them, showing that the set of all intervals is a Helly family of order 2.[^o6]
- The family of infinite [arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/168389) of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) also has the 2-Helly property. That is, whenever a finite collection of progressions has the property that no two of them are disjoint, then there exists an integer that belongs to all of them; this is the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713).[^o3]

:::

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

More formally, a **Helly family of order *k*** is a [set system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_system) (*V*, *E*), with *E* a collection of [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of *V*, such that, for every finite *G* ⊆ *E* with

$$\bigcap_{X\in G} X=\varnothing,$$

we can find *H* ⊆ *G* such that

$$\bigcap_{X\in H} X=\varnothing$$

and

$\left|H\right|\le k.$[^o2]

In some cases, the same definition holds for every subcollection *G*, regardless of finiteness. However, this is a more restrictive condition. For instance, the [open intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) of the real line satisfy the Helly property for finite subcollections, but not for infinite subcollections: the intervals (0,1/*i*) (for *i* = 0, 1, 2, ...) have pairwise nonempty intersections, but have an empty overall intersection.

:::

## Helly dimension {#Helly_dimension}

If a family of sets is a Helly family of order *k*, that family is said to have **Helly number** *k*. The **Helly dimension** of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is one less than the Helly number of the family of metric balls in that space; Helly's theorem implies that the Helly dimension of a Euclidean space equals its dimension as a real [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370).[^o10]

The **Helly dimension** of a subset S of a Euclidean space, such as a polyhedron, is one less than the Helly number of the family of [translates](wikipedia://en/204682) of S.[^o11] For instance, the Helly dimension of any [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) is 1, even though such a shape may belong to a Euclidean space of much higher dimension.[^o12]

Helly dimension has also been applied to other mathematical objects. For instance (Domokos 2007) defines the Helly dimension of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) (an algebraic structure formed by an invertible and associative binary operation) to be one less than the Helly number of the family of [left cosets](wikipedia://en/98759) of the group.[^o14]

## The Helly property {#The_Helly_property}

If a family of nonempty sets has an empty intersection, its Helly number must be at least two, so the smallest *k* for which the *k*-Helly property is nontrivial is *k* = 2. The 2-Helly property is also known as the **Helly property**. A 2-Helly family is also known as a **Helly family**.[^o2][^o3]

A [convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_metric) [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) in which the closed [balls](wikipedia://en/160556) have the 2-Helly property (that is, a space with Helly dimension 1, in the stronger variant of Helly dimension for infinite subcollections) is called [injective](wikipedia://en/7840768) or hyperconvex.[^o16] The existence of the [tight span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tight_span) allows any metric space to be embedded isometrically into a space with Helly dimension 1.[^o17]

## The Helly property in hypergraphs {#The_Helly_property_in_hypergraphs}

A [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535) is equivalent to a set-family. In hypergraphs terms, a hypergraph *H* = (*V*, *E*) has the **Helly property** if for every *n* hyperedges $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ in *E*, if $\forall i,j\in[n]: e_i \cap e_j \neq\emptyset$, then $e_1 \cap \cdots \cap e_n \neq\emptyset$. For every hypergraph H, the following are equivalent:[^o19]

- *H* has the Helly property, and the intersection graph of *H* (the simple graph in which the vertices are *E* and two elements of *E* are linked iff they intersect) is a [perfect graph](wikipedia://en/670531).
- Every partial hypergraph of *H* (i.e., a hypergraph derived from *H* by deleting some hyperedges) has the [Konig property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konig_property), i.e., its maximum-[matching](wikipedia://en/63030703) size equals its minimum-[transversal](wikipedia://en/64318108) size.
- Every partial hypergraph of *H* has the property that its maximum degree equals its minimum [edge coloring](wikipedia://en/690647) number.
