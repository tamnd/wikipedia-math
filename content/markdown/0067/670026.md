# Syntactic monoid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Syntactic monoid"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), the **syntactic monoid** $M(L)$ of a [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939) $L$ is the minimal [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) that [recognizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recognizable_set) the language $L$. By the [Myhill–Nerode theorem](wikipedia://en/339992), the syntactic monoid is unique up to unique isomorphism.

:::

## Syntactic quotient {#Syntactic_quotient}

An [**alphabet**](wikipedia://en/4292269) is a finite [set](wikipedia://en/26691).

The **[free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166)** on a given alphabet is the monoid whose elements are all the [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) of zero or more elements from that set, with [string concatenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_concatenation) as the monoid operation and the [empty string](wikipedia://en/835827) as the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962).

Given a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of a free monoid $M$, one may define sets that consist of formal left or right [**inverses** of elements](wikipedia://en/45199#In_a_unital_magma) in $S$. These are called [quotients](wikipedia://en/208850), and one may define right or left quotients, depending on which side one is concatenating. Thus, the **right quotient** of $S$ by an element $m$ from $M$ is the set

$$S \ / \ m=\{u\in M \;\vert\; um\in S \}.$$

Similarly, the **left quotient** is

$$m \setminus S=\{u\in M \;\vert\; mu\in S \}.$$

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Disjunctive set"}

## Syntactic equivalence {#Syntactic_equivalence}

The syntactic quotient induces an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on $M$, called the **syntactic relation**, or **syntactic equivalence** (induced by $S$).

The **right syntactic equivalence** is the equivalence relation

$$s \sim_S t \ \Leftrightarrow\ S \,/ \,s \;=\; S \,/ \,t \ \Leftrightarrow\ (\forall x\in M\colon\ xs \in S \Leftrightarrow xt \in S).$$

Similarly, the **left syntactic equivalence** is

$$s \;{}_S{\sim}\; t \ \Leftrightarrow\ s \setminus S \;=\; t \setminus S \ \Leftrightarrow\ (\forall y\in M\colon\ sy \in S \Leftrightarrow ty \in S).$$

Observe that the *right* syntactic equivalence is a *left* [congruence](wikipedia://en/48167) with respect to [string concatenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/String_concatenation) and vice versa; i.e., $s \sim_S t \ \Rightarrow\ xs \sim_S xt\$ for all $x \in M$.

The **syntactic congruence** or **[Myhill](wikipedia://en/2611636) congruence**[^o5] is defined as[^o6]

$$s \equiv_S t \ \Leftrightarrow\ (\forall x, y\in M\colon\ xsy \in S \Leftrightarrow xty \in S).$$

The definition extends to a congruence defined by a subset $S$ of a general monoid $M$. A **disjunctive set** is a subset $S$ such that the syntactic congruence defined by $S$ is the equality relation.[^o7]

Let us call $[s]_S$ the equivalence class of $s$ for the syntactic congruence. The syntactic congruence is [compatible](wikipedia://en/310953) with concatenation in the monoid, in that one has

$$[s]_S[t]_S=[st]_S$$

for all $s,t\in M$. Thus, the syntactic quotient is a [monoid morphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monoid_morphism), and induces a [quotient monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_monoid)

$$M(S)= M \ / \ {\equiv_S}.$$

This monoid $M(S)$ is called the **syntactic monoid** of $S$. It can be shown that it is the smallest [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) that [recognizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recognizable_set) $S$; that is, $M(S)$ recognizes $S$, and for every monoid $N$ recognizing $S$, $M(S)$ is a quotient of a [submonoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submonoid) of $N$. The syntactic monoid of $S$ is also the [transition monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_monoid) of the [minimal automaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_automaton) of $S$.[^o5][^o6][^o8]

A **group language** is one for which the syntactic monoid is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447).[^o9]

## Examples {#Examples}

- Let $L$ be the language over $A = \{a, b\}$ of words of even length. The syntactic congruence has two classes, $L$ itself and $L_1$, the words of odd length. The syntactic monoid is the group of order 2 on $\{L, L_1\}$.[^o12]
- For the language $(ab+ba)^*$, the minimal automaton has 4 states and the syntactic monoid has 15 elements.[^o13]
- The [bicyclic monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicyclic_monoid) is the syntactic monoid of the [Dyck language](wikipedia://en/1852078) (the language of balanced sets of parentheses).
- The [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) on $A$ (where $\left|A\right| > 1$) is the syntactic monoid of the language $\{ww^R \mid w \in A^*\}$, where $w^R$ is the reversal of the word $w$. (For $\left|A\right| = 1$, one can use the language of square powers of the letter.)
- Every non-trivial finite monoid is homomorphic to the syntactic monoid of some non-trivial language,[^o14] but not every finite monoid is isomorphic to a syntactic monoid.[^o15]
- Every [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) is isomorphic to the syntactic monoid of some regular language.[^o14]
- The language over $\{a, b\}$ in which the number of occurrences of $a$ and $b$ are congruent modulo $2^n$ is a group language with syntactic monoid $\mathbb{Z} / 2^n\mathbb{Z}$.[^o9]
- [Trace monoids](wikipedia://en/10739141) are examples of syntactic monoids.
- [Marcel-Paul Schützenberger](wikipedia://en/2817337)[^o16] characterized [star-free languages](wikipedia://en/4922792) as those with finite [aperiodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aperiodic_monoid) syntactic monoids.[^o17]
