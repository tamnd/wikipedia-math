# Angular defect

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Angular defect"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **angular defect** or simply **defect** (also called **deficit** or **deficiency**) is the failure of some [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) to add up to the expected amount of 360° or 180°, when such angles in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) would. The opposite notion is the [*excess*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_excess).

:::

Classically, the defect arises in two contexts: in the Euclidean plane, angles about a point add up to 360°, while [interior angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_and_external_angle) in a triangle add up to 180°. However, on a [convex polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470), the angles of the faces meeting at a vertex add up to *less* than 360° (a defect), while the angles at some vertices of a [nonconvex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonconvex_polyhedron) may add up to *more* than 360° (an excess). In addition, the angles in a [hyperbolic triangle](wikipedia://en/1283865) add up to *less* than 180° (a defect), while those on a [spherical triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_triangle) add up to *more* than 180° (an excess).

In modern terms, the defect at a vertex is a discrete version of the [curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) of the polyhedral surface [concentrated at that point](wikipedia://en/37021). Negative defect indicates that the vertex resembles a [saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249) (negative curvature), whereas positive defect indicates that the vertex resembles a [local maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_maximum) or minimum (positive curvature). The [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229) gives the total curvature as $2\pi$ times the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) $\chi = 2$, so for a convex polyhedron the sum of the defects is $4\pi$, while a [toroidal polyhedron](wikipedia://en/24804975) has $\chi = 0$ and total defect zero.

## Defect of a vertex {#Defect_of_a_vertex}

For a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470), the defect at a vertex equals 2π minus the sum of all the angles at the vertex (all the faces at the vertex are included). If a polyhedron is convex, then the defect of each vertex is always positive. If the sum of the angles exceeds a full [turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28geometry%29), as occurs in some vertices of many non-convex polyhedra, then the defect is negative.

The concept of defect extends to higher dimensions as the amount by which the sum of the [dihedral angles](wikipedia://en/338046) of the [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29) at a [peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_%28mathematics%29) falls short of a full circle.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The defect of any of the vertices of a regular [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) (in which three regular [pentagons](wikipedia://en/20741014) meet at each vertex) is 36°, or π/5 radians, or 1/10 of a circle. Each of the angles measures 108°; three of these meet at each vertex, so the defect is 360° − (108° + 108° + 108°) = 36°.

The same procedure can be followed for the other [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905):

## Descartes' theorem on total angular defect {#Descartes'_theorem_on_total_angular_defect}

Descartes's theorem on the "total defect" of a polyhedron states that if the polyhedron is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to a sphere (i.e. topologically equivalent to a sphere, so that it may be deformed into a sphere by stretching without tearing), the "total defect", i.e. the sum of the defects of all of the vertices, is two full circles (or 720° or 4$\pi$ radians). The polyhedron need not be convex.[^o13]

A generalization says the number of circles in the total defect equals the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of the polyhedron. This is a special case of the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229), which relates the integral of the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) to the Euler characteristic. Here, the Gaussian curvature is concentrated at the vertices: on the faces and edges the curvature is zero (the surface is locally [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) to a Euclidean plane) and the integral of curvature at a vertex is equal to the defect there (by definition).

This can be used to calculate the number *V* of vertices of a polyhedron by totaling the angles of all the faces, and adding the total defect (which is $2\pi$ times the Euler characteristic). This total will have one complete circle for every vertex in the polyhedron.

A converse to Descartes' theorem is given by [Alexandrov's theorem on polyhedra](wikipedia://en/29103307), according to which a metric space that is locally Euclidean (hence zero curvature) except for a finite number of points of positive angular defect, adding to $4\pi$, can be realized uniquely as the surface of a convex polyhedron.

:::

## Positive defects on non-convex figures {#Positive_defects_on_non-convex_figures}

It is tempting to think that every non-convex polyhedron must have some vertices whose defect is negative, but this need not be the case if the Euler characteristic is positive (a topological sphere).

A counterexample is provided by a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) where one face is replaced by a [square pyramid](wikipedia://en/1128455): this [elongated square pyramid](wikipedia://en/1177939) is convex and the defects at each vertex are each positive. Now consider the same cube where the square pyramid goes into the cube: this is concave, but the defects remain the same, and so are all positive.

Two counterexamples that are self-intersecting polyhedra are the [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843) and the [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878), with twelve and twenty convex points respectively, all with positive defects.
