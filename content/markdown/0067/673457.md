# Vinculum (symbol)

A **vinculum** or constraint (la) is a horizontal line used in [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) for various purposes. It may be placed as an *[overline](wikipedia://en/4780165)* or *[underline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underline)* above or below a [mathematical expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_expression) to group the expression's elements. Historically, vincula were extensively used to group items together, especially in written mathematics, but in modern mathematics its use for this purpose has almost entirely been replaced by the use of [parentheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parentheses).[^o2] It was also used to [mark Roman numerals](wikipedia://en/25657#Vinculum) whose values are multiplied by 1,000.[^o3] Today, the common usage of a vinculum to indicate the repetend of a [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447)[^o4][^o5] is a significant exception and reflects the original usage.

## History {#History}

The vinculum, in its general use, was introduced by [Frans van Schooten](wikipedia://en/66858) in 1646 as he edited the works of [François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238) (who had himself not used this notation). However, earlier versions, such as using an underline as [Chuquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Chuquet) did in 1484, or in limited form as [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renee_Descartes) did in 1637, using it only in relation to the radical sign, were common.[^o7]

## Usage {#Usage}

### Modern {#Modern}

A vinculum can indicate a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) where *A* and *B* are the endpoints:

$$\overline{\rm AB}.$$

A vinculum can indicate the repetend of a [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447) value:

$\tfrac{1}{7}$ = 0.$\overline{142857}$ = 0.1428571428571428571...

A vinculum can indicate the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826):

$$\overline{2 + 3i} = 2 - 3i$$

Logarithm of a number less than 1 can be represented using vinculum:

$$\log 2 = 0.301 \Rightarrow \log 0.2 = \overline{1}.301 = -0.699$$

In [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844), a vinculum may be used to represent the operation of inversion (also known as the NOT function):

$$Y = \overline{AB},$$

meaning that Y is false only when both A and B are both true - or by extension, Y is true when either A or B is false.

Similarly, it is used to show the repeating terms in a [periodic continued fraction](wikipedia://en/10875031). [Quadratic irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/305331) are the only numbers that have these.

### Historical {#Historical}

Formerly its main use was as a notation to indicate a group (a bracketing device serving the same function as parentheses):

$$a-\overline{b+c},$$

meaning to add *b* and *c* first and then subtract the result from *a*, which would be written more commonly today as $a - (b + c)$. Parentheses, used for grouping, are only rarely found in the mathematical literature before the eighteenth century. The vinculum was used extensively, usually as an overline, but [Chuquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Chuquet) in 1484 used the underline version.[^o11]

### As a part of a radical {#As_a_part_of_a_radical}

The vinculum is used as part of the notation of a [radical](wikipedia://en/24971503) to indicate the [radicand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radicand) whose [root](wikipedia://en/235029) is being indicated. In the following, the quantity $ab+2$ is the whole radicand, and thus has a vinculum over it:

$$\sqrt[n]{ab+2}.$$

In 1637 [Descartes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renee_Descartes) was the first to unite the German radical sign √ with the vinculum to create the radical symbol in common use today.[^o13]

The symbol used to indicate a vinculum need not be a line segment (overline or underline); sometimes braces can be used (pointing either up or down).[^o14]
