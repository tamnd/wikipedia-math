# Differential (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Differential"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **differential** refers to several related notions[^o2] derived from the early days of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), put on a rigorous footing, such as [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) differences and the [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of functions.[^o3]

:::

The term is used in various branches of mathematics such as [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).

## Introduction {#Introduction}

The term **differential** is used nonrigorously in [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) to refer to an [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) ("infinitely small") change in some [varying quantity](wikipedia://en/3728109). For example, if *x* is a [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109), then a change in the value of *x* is often denoted Δ*x* (pronounced *[delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_%28Greek%29) x*). The differential *dx* represents an infinitely small change in the variable *x*. The idea of an infinitely small or infinitely slow change is, intuitively, extremely useful, and there are a number of ways to make the notion mathematically precise.

Using calculus, it is possible to relate the infinitely small changes of various variables to each other mathematically using [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921). If *y* is a function of *x*, then the differential *dy* of *y* is related to *dx* by the formula

$$dy = \frac{dy}{dx} \,dx,$$

where $\frac{dy}{dx} \,$denotes not 'dy divided by dx' as one would intuitively read, but 'the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of *y* with respect to *x* '. This formula summarizes the idea that the derivative of *y* with respect to *x* is the limit of the ratio of differences Δ*y*/Δ*x* as Δ*x* approaches zero.

### Basic notions {#Basic_notions}

- In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), the [differential](wikipedia://en/23997203) represents a change in the [linearization](wikipedia://en/1118789) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427).
  - The [total differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_differential) is its generalization for functions of multiple variables.
- In traditional approaches to calculus, differentials (e.g. *dx*, *dy*, *dt*, etc.) are interpreted as [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990). There are several methods of defining infinitesimals rigorously, but it is sufficient to say that an infinitesimal number is smaller in absolute value than any positive real number, just as an infinitely large number is larger than any real number.
- The [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative) is another name for the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) of [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) of a function from **R**<sup>*n*</sup> to **R**<sup>*m*</sup> (especially when this [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is viewed as a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102)).
- More generally, the [differential](wikipedia://en/1558510) or *[pushforward](wikipedia://en/1558510)* refers to the derivative of a map between [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) and the pushforward operations it defines. The differential is also used to define the dual concept of [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995).
- [Stochastic calculus](wikipedia://en/228001) provides a notion of [stochastic differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_differential) and an associated calculus for [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895).
- The [integrator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stieltjes_integral#Definition) in a [Stieltjes integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stieltjes_integral) is represented as the differential of a function. Formally, the differential appearing under the integral behaves exactly as a differential: thus, the [integration by substitution](wikipedia://en/193748) and [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) formulae for Stieltjes integral correspond, respectively, to the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) and [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) for the differential.

## History and usage {#History_and_usage}

[Infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) quantities played a significant role in the development of calculus. [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) used them, even though he did not believe that arguments involving infinitesimals were rigorous.[^o7] [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) referred to them as [fluxions](wikipedia://en/252953). However, it was [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) who coined the term *differentials* for infinitesimal quantities and introduced the notation for them which is still used today.

In [Leibniz's notation](wikipedia://en/410712), if *x* is a variable quantity, then *dx* denotes an infinitesimal change in the variable *x*. Thus, if *y* is a function of *x*, then the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of *y* with respect to *x* is often denoted *dy*/*dx*, which would otherwise be denoted (in the notation of Newton or [Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793)) *ẏ* or *y*. The use of differentials in this form attracted much criticism, for instance in the famous pamphlet [The Analyst](wikipedia://en/341394) by Bishop Berkeley. Nevertheless, the notation has remained popular because it suggests strongly the idea that the derivative of *y* at *x* is its [instantaneous rate of change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instantaneous_rate_of_change) (the [slope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope_%28mathematics%29) of the graph's [tangent line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line)), which may be obtained by taking the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) of the ratio Δ*y*/Δ*x* as Δ*x* becomes arbitrarily small. Differentials are also compatible with [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267), where a differential such as *dx* has the same dimensions as the variable *x*.

Calculus evolved into a distinct branch of mathematics during the 17th century CE, although there were antecedents going back to antiquity. The presentations of, e.g., Newton, Leibniz, were marked by non-rigorous definitions of terms like differential, [fluent](wikipedia://en/53398219) and "infinitely small". While many of the arguments in [Bishop Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Berkeley)'s 1734 [The Analyst](wikipedia://en/341394) are theological in nature, modern mathematicians acknowledge the validity of his argument against "[the Ghosts of departed Quantities](wikipedia://en/341394#Ghosts_of_departed_quantities)"; however, the modern approaches do not have the same technical issues. Despite the lack of rigor, immense progress was made in the 17th and 18th centuries. In the 19th century, Cauchy and others gradually developed the [epsilon-delta](wikipedia://en/285759#%28ε,_δ%29-definition_of_limit) approach to continuity, limits and derivatives, giving a solid conceptual foundation for calculus.

In the 20th century, several new concepts in, e.g., multivariable calculus, differential geometry, seemed to encapsulate the intent of the old terms, especially *differential*; both differential and infinitesimal are used with new, more rigorous, meanings.

Differentials are also used in the notation for [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) because an integral can be regarded as an infinite sum of infinitesimal quantities: the area under a graph is obtained by subdividing the graph into infinitely thin strips and summing their areas. In an expression such as

$$\int f(x) \,dx,$$

the integral sign (which is a modified [long s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_s)) denotes the infinite sum, *f*(*x*) denotes the "height" of a thin strip, and the differential *dx* denotes its infinitely thin width.

## Approaches {#Approaches}

Differential

There are several approaches for making the notion of differentials mathematically precise.

1. Differentials as [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102). This approach underlies the definition of the [derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative) and the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625).[^o9]
1. Differentials as [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) elements of [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346). This approach is popular in algebraic geometry.[^o10]
1. Differentials in smooth models of set theory. This approach is known as [synthetic differential geometry](wikipedia://en/3869427) or [smooth infinitesimal analysis](wikipedia://en/3869419) and is closely related to the algebraic geometric approach, except that ideas from [topos theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topos_theory) are used to *hide* the mechanisms by which nilpotent infinitesimals are introduced.[^o11]
1. Differentials as infinitesimals in [hyperreal number](wikipedia://en/51429) systems, which are extensions of the real numbers that contain invertible infinitesimals and infinitely large numbers. This is the approach of [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708) pioneered by [Abraham Robinson](wikipedia://en/46024).[^o12]

These approaches are very different from each other, but they have in common the idea of being *quantitative*, i.e., saying not just that a differential is infinitely small, but *how* small it is.

### Differentials as linear maps {#Differentials_as_linear_maps}

There is a simple way to make precise sense of differentials, first used on the Real line by regarding them as [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102). It can be used on $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{R}^n$, a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), or more generally, a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752). The case of the Real line is the easiest to explain. This type of differential is also known as a [covariant vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_vector) or [cotangent vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotangent_vector), depending on context.

#### Differentials as linear maps on R {#Differentials_as_linear_maps_on_R}

Suppose $f(x)$ is a real-valued function on $\mathbb{R}$. We can reinterpret the variable $x$ in $f(x)$ as being a function rather than a number, namely the [identity map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) on the real line, which takes a real number $p$ to itself: $x(p)=p$. Then $f(x)$ is the composite of $f$ with $x$, whose value at $p$ is $f(x(p))=f(p)$. The differential $\operatorname{d}f$ (which of course depends on $f$) is then a function whose value at $p$ (usually denoted $df_p$) is not a number, but a linear map from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$. Since a linear map from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ is given by a $1\times 1$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859), it is essentially the same thing as a number, but the change in the point of view allows us to think of $df_p$ as an infinitesimal and *compare* it with the *standard infinitesimal* $dx_p$, which is again just the identity map from $\mathbb{R}$ to $\mathbb{R}$ (a $1\times 1$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) with entry $1$). The identity map has the property that if $\varepsilon$ is very small, then $dx_p(\varepsilon)$ is very small, which enables us to regard it as infinitesimal. The differential $df_p$ has the same property, because it is just a multiple of $dx_p$, and this multiple is the derivative $f'(p)$ by definition. We therefore obtain that $df_p=f'(p)\,dx_p$, and hence $df=f'\,dx$. Thus we recover the idea that $f'$ is the ratio of the differentials $df$ and $dx$.

This would just be a trick were it not for the fact that:

1. it captures the idea of the derivative of $f$ at $p$ as the *best linear approximation* to $f$ at $p$;
1. it has many generalizations.

#### Differentials as linear maps on R<sup>n</sup> {#Differentials_as_linear_maps_on_Rn}

If $f$ is a function from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}$, then we say that $f$ is *differentiable*[^o16] at $p\in\mathbb{R}^n$ if there is a linear map $df_p$ from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}$ such that for any $\varepsilon>0$, there is a [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $N$ of $p$ such that for $x\in N$,

$$\left|f(x) - f(p) - df_p(x-p)\right| < \varepsilon \left|x-p\right| .$$

We can now use the same trick as in the one-dimensional case and think of the expression $f(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n)$ as the composite of $f$ with the standard coordinates $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$ on $\mathbb{R}^n$ (so that $x_j(p)$ is the $j$-th component of $p\in\mathbb{R}^n$). Then the differentials $\left(dx_1\right)_p, \left(dx_2\right)_p, \ldots, \left(dx_n\right)_p$ at a point $p$ form a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of linear maps from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}$ and therefore, if $f$ is differentiable at $p$, we can write $df_{p}$ as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of these basis elements:

$$df_p = \sum_{j=1}^n D_j f(p) \,(dx_j)_p.$$

The coefficients $D_j f(p)$ are (by definition) the [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) of $f$ at $p$ with respect to $x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n$. Hence, if $f$ is differentiable on all of $\mathbb{R}^n$, we can write, more concisely:

$$df = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_1} \,dx_1 + \frac{\partial f}{\partial x_2} \,dx_2 + \cdots +\frac{\partial f}{\partial x_n} \,dx_n.$$

In the one-dimensional case this becomes

$$df = \frac{df}{dx}dx$$

as before.

This idea generalizes straightforwardly to functions from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{R}^m$. Furthermore, it has the decisive advantage over other definitions of the derivative that it is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under changes of coordinates. This means that the same idea can be used to define the [differential](wikipedia://en/1558510) of [smooth maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map) between [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold).

Aside: Note that the existence of all the [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) of $f(x)$ at $x$ is a [necessary condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_condition) for the existence of a differential at $x$. However it is not a [sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficient_condition). For counterexamples, see [Gateaux derivative](wikipedia://en/2253139).

#### Differentials as linear maps on a vector space {#Differentials_as_linear_maps_on_a_vector_space}

The same procedure works on a vector space with enough additional structure to reasonably talk about continuity. The most concrete case is a Hilbert space, also known as a [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856), where the inner product and its associated [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) define a suitable concept of distance. The same procedure works for a Banach space, also known as a complete [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538). However, for a more general topological vector space, some of the details are more abstract because there is no concept of distance.

For the important case of a finite dimension, any inner product space is a Hilbert space, any normed vector space is a Banach space and any topological vector space is complete. As a result, we can define a coordinate system from an arbitrary basis and use the same technique as for $\mathbb{R}^n$.

### Differentials as germs of functions {#Differentials_as_germs_of_functions}

This approach works on any [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219). If

1. $U$ and $V$ are open sets containing $p$
1. $f\colon U\to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous
1. $g\colon V\to \mathbb{R}$ is continuous

then $f$ is equivalent to $g$ at $p$, denoted $f \sim_p g$, if and only if there is an open $W \subseteq U \cap V$ containing $p$ such that $f(x) = g(x)$ for every $x$ in $W$. The germ of $f$ at $p$, denoted $[f]_p$, is the set of all real continuous functions equivalent to $f$ at $p$; if $f$ is smooth at $p$ then $[f]_p$ is a smooth germ. If

1. $U_1$, $U_2$ $V_1$ and $V_2$ are open sets containing $p$
1. $f_1\colon U_1\to \mathbb{R}$, $f_2\colon U_2\to \mathbb{R}$, $g_1\colon V_1\to \mathbb{R}$ and $g_2\colon V_2\to \mathbb{R}$ are smooth functions
1. $$f_1 \sim_p g_1$$
1. $$f_2 \sim_p g_2$$
1. $r$ is a real number

then

1. $$r*f_1 \sim_p r*g_1$$
1. $$f_1+f_2\colon U_1 \cap U_2\to \mathbb{R} \sim_p g_1+g_2\colon V_1 \cap V_2\to \mathbb{R}$$
1. $$f_1*f_2\colon U_1 \cap U_2\to \mathbb{R} \sim_p g_1*g_2\colon V_1 \cap V_2\to \mathbb{R}$$

This shows that the germs at p form an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788).

Define $\mathcal{I}_p$ to be the set of all smooth germs vanishing at $p$ and $\mathcal{I}_p^2$ to be the [product](wikipedia://en/25977#Ideal_operations) of [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) $\mathcal{I}_p \mathcal{I}_p$. Then a differential at $p$ (cotangent vector at $p$) is an element of $\mathcal{I}_p/\mathcal{I}_p^2$. The differential of a smooth function $f$ at $p$, denoted $\mathrm d f_p$, is $[f-f(p)]_p/\mathcal{I}_p^2$.

A similar approach is to define differential equivalence of first order in terms of derivatives in an arbitrary coordinate patch. Then the differential of $f$ at $p$ is the set of all functions differentially equivalent to $f-f(p)$ at $p$.

### Algebraic geometry {#Algebraic_geometry}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), differentials and other infinitesimal notions are handled in a very explicit way by accepting that the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) or [structure sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_sheaf) of a space may contain [nilpotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_element). The simplest example is the ring of [dual numbers](wikipedia://en/42169) **R**\[*ε*\], where *ε*<sup>2</sup> = 0.

This can be motivated by the algebro-geometric point of view on the derivative of a function *f* from **R** to **R** at a point *p*. For this, note first that *f* − *f*(*p*) belongs to the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) *I*<sub>*p*</sub> of functions on **R** which vanish at *p*. If the derivative *f* vanishes at *p*, then *f* − *f*(*p*) belongs to the square *I*<sub>*p*</sub><sup>2</sup> of this ideal. Hence the derivative of *f* at *p* may be captured by the equivalence class \[*f* − *f*(*p*)\] in the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) *I*<sub>*p*</sub>/*I*<sub>*p*</sub><sup>2</sup>, and the [1-jet](wikipedia://en/1521924) of *f* (which encodes its value and its first derivative) is the equivalence class of *f* in the space of all functions modulo *I*<sub>*p*</sub><sup>2</sup>. Algebraic geometers regard this equivalence class as the *restriction* of *f* to a *thickened* version of the point *p* whose coordinate ring is not **R** (which is the quotient space of functions on **R** modulo *I*<sub>*p*</sub>) but **R**\[*ε*\] which is the quotient space of functions on **R** modulo *I*<sub>*p*</sub><sup>2</sup>. Such a thickened point is a simple example of a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754).[^o10]

#### Algebraic geometry notions {#Algebraic_geometry_notions}

Differentials are also important in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), and there are several important notions.

- [Abelian differentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_differential) usually mean differential one-forms on an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) or [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181).
- [Quadratic differentials](wikipedia://en/2610010) (which behave like "squares" of abelian differentials) are also important in the theory of Riemann surfaces.
- [Kähler differentials](wikipedia://en/603916) provide a general notion of differential in algebraic geometry.

### Synthetic differential geometry {#Synthetic_differential_geometry}

A fifth approach to infinitesimals is the method of [synthetic differential geometry](wikipedia://en/3869427)[^o22] or [smooth infinitesimal analysis](wikipedia://en/3869419).[^o23] This is closely related to the algebraic-geometric approach, except that the infinitesimals are more implicit and intuitive. The main idea of this approach is to replace the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) with another [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of *smoothly varying sets* which is a [topos](wikipedia://en/21391464). In this category, one can define the real numbers, smooth functions, and so on, but the real numbers *automatically* contain nilpotent infinitesimals, so these do not need to be introduced by hand as in the algebraic geometric approach. However the [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) in this new category is not identical to the familiar logic of the category of sets: in particular, the [law of the excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_excluded_middle) does not hold. This means that set-theoretic mathematical arguments only extend to smooth infinitesimal analysis if they are *[constructive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics)* (e.g., do not use [proof by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction)). [Constructivists](wikipedia://en/21059) regard this disadvantage as a positive thing, since it forces one to find constructive arguments wherever they are available.

### Nonstandard analysis {#Nonstandard_analysis}

The final approach to infinitesimals again involves extending the real numbers, but in a less drastic way. In the [nonstandard analysis](wikipedia://en/21708) approach there are no nilpotent infinitesimals, only invertible ones, which may be viewed as the [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of infinitely large numbers.[^o12] Such extensions of the real numbers may be constructed explicitly using equivalence classes of sequences of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), so that, for example, the sequence (1, 1/2, 1/3, ..., 1/*n*, ...) represents an infinitesimal. The [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) of this new set of [hyperreal numbers](wikipedia://en/51429) is the same as the logic for the usual real numbers, but the [completeness axiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completeness_axiom) (which involves [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428)) does not hold. Nevertheless, this suffices to develop an elementary and quite intuitive approach to calculus using infinitesimals, see [transfer principle](wikipedia://en/637138).

## Differential geometry {#Differential_geometry}

The notion of a differential motivates several concepts in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) (and [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562)).

- The [differential (Pushforward)](wikipedia://en/1558510) of a map between manifolds.
- [Differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519) provide a framework which accommodates multiplication and differentiation of differentials.
- The [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) is a notion of differentiation of differential forms which generalizes the [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative) of a function (which is a [differential 1-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_1-form)).
- [Pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) is, in particular, a geometric name for the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) for composing a map between manifolds with a differential form on the target manifold.
- [Covariant derivatives or differentials](wikipedia://en/431848) provide a general notion for differentiating of [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) and [tensor fields](wikipedia://en/256738) on a manifold, or, more generally, sections of a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524): see [Connection (vector bundle)](wikipedia://en/391816). This ultimately leads to the general concept of a [connection](wikipedia://en/308815).

## Other meanings {#Other_meanings}

The term *differential* has also been adopted in homological algebra and algebraic topology, because of the role the exterior derivative plays in de Rham cohomology: in a [cochain complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochain_complex) $(C_\bullet, d_\bullet),$ the maps (or *coboundary operators*) *d<sub>i</sub>* are often called differentials. Dually, the boundary operators in a chain complex are sometimes called *codifferentials*.

The properties of the differential also motivate the algebraic notions of a *[derivation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_%28abstract_algebra%29)* and a *[differential algebra](wikipedia://en/1816587)*.
