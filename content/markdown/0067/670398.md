# Ancillary statistic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ancillary statistic"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **ancillarity** is a property of a [statistic](wikipedia://en/26703) computed on a [sample dataset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28statistics%29) in relation to a [parametric model](wikipedia://en/2883137) of the dataset. An ancillary statistic has the same distribution regardless of the value of the parameters and thus provides no information about them.[^o2][^o3][^o4] It is opposed to the concept of a [complete statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_statistic) which contains no ancillary information. It is closely related to the concept of a [sufficient statistic](wikipedia://en/140841) which contains all of the information that the dataset provides about the parameters.

:::

A ancillary statistic is a specific case of a [pivotal quantity](wikipedia://en/6081205) that is computed only from the data and not from the parameters. They can be used to construct [prediction intervals](wikipedia://en/536062). They are also used in connection with [Basu's theorem](wikipedia://en/7807871) to prove independence between statistics.[^o5]

This concept was first introduced by [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) in the 1920s,[^o6] but its formal definition was only provided in 1964 by [Debabrata Basu](wikipedia://en/1978708).[^o7][^o8]

::: {#o10 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Suppose *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> are [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_identically-distributed_random_variables), and are [normally distributed](wikipedia://en/21462) with unknown [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) *μ* and known [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) 1. Let

$$\overline{X}_n = \frac{X_1+\,\cdots\,+X_n}{n}$$

be the [sample mean](wikipedia://en/612).

The following statistical measures of dispersion of the sample

- [Range](wikipedia://en/27588): max(*X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub>) − min(*X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X<sub>n</sub>*)
- [Interquartile range](wikipedia://en/14812): *Q*<sub>3</sub> − *Q*<sub>1</sub>
- [Sample variance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_variance):

$$\hat{\sigma}^2:=\,\frac{\sum \left(X_i-\overline{X}\right)^2}{n}$$

are all *ancillary statistics*, because their sampling distributions do not change as *μ* changes. Computationally, this is because in the formulas, the *μ* terms cancel – adding a constant number to a distribution (and all samples) changes its sample maximum and minimum by the same amount, so it does not change their difference, and likewise for others: these measures of dispersion do not depend on location.

Conversely, given i.i.d. normal variables with known mean 1 and unknown variance *σ*<sup>2</sup>, the sample mean $\overline{X}$ is *not* an ancillary statistic of the variance, as the sampling distribution of the sample mean is *N*(1, *σ*<sup>2</sup>/*n*), which does depend on *σ* <sup>2</sup> – this measure of location (specifically, its [standard error](wikipedia://en/552520)) depends on dispersion.[^o11]

### In location-scale families {#In_location-scale_families}

In a [location family of distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location_family), $(X_1 - X_n, X_2 - X_n, \dots, X_{n-1} - X_n)$ is an ancillary statistic.

In a [scale family of distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_family), $\left( \frac{X_1}{X_n}, \frac{X_2}{X_n}, \dots, \frac{X_{n-1}}{X_n} \right)$ is an ancillary statistic.

In a [location-scale family of distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location-scale_family), $( \frac{X_1 - X_n}{S}, \frac{X_2 - X_n}{S}, \dots, \frac{X_{n - 1} - X_n}{S} )$, where $S^2$ is the sample variance, is an ancillary statistic.[^o4][^o13]

:::

## In recovery of information {#In_recovery_of_information}

It turns out that, if $T_1$ is a non-sufficient statistic and $T_2$ is ancillary, one can sometimes recover all the information about the unknown parameter contained in the entire data by reporting $T_1$ while conditioning on the observed value of $T_2$. This is known as *conditional inference*.[^o4]

For example, suppose that $X_1, X_2$ follow the $N(\theta, 1)$ distribution where $\theta$ is unknown. Note that, even though $X_1$ is not sufficient for $\theta$ (since its Fisher information is 1, whereas the Fisher information of the complete statistic $\overline{X}$ is 2), by additionally reporting the ancillary statistic $X_1 - X_2$, one obtains a joint distribution with Fisher information 2.[^o4]

## Ancillary complement {#Ancillary_complement}

Given a statistic *T* that is not [sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficiency_%28statistics%29), an **ancillary complement** is a statistic *U* that is ancillary and such that (*T*, *U*) is sufficient.[^o3] Intuitively, an ancillary complement "adds the missing information" (without duplicating any).

The statistic is particularly useful if one takes *T* to be a [maximum likelihood estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_likelihood_estimator), which in general will not be sufficient; then one can ask for an ancillary complement. In this case, Fisher argues that one must condition on an ancillary complement to determine information content: one should consider the [Fisher information](wikipedia://en/598971) content of *T* to not be the [marginal](wikipedia://en/506077) of *T*, but the conditional distribution of *T*, given *U*: how much information does *T* *add*? This is not possible in general, as no ancillary complement need exist, and if one exists, it need not be unique, nor does a maximum ancillary complement exist.

::: {#o17 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

In [baseball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baseball), suppose a scout observes a batter in *N* at-bats. Suppose (unrealistically) that the number *N* is chosen by some random process that is [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) of the batter's ability – say a coin is tossed after each at-bat and the result determines whether the scout will stay to watch the batter's next at-bat. The eventual data are the number *N* of at-bats and the number *X* of hits: the data (*X*, *N*) are a sufficient statistic. The observed [batting average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batting_average_%28baseball%29) *X*/*N* fails to convey all of the information available in the data because it fails to report the number *N* of at-bats (e.g., a batting average of 0.400, which is [very high](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Major_League_Baseball_batting_champions), based on only five at-bats does not inspire anywhere near as much confidence in the player's ability than a 0.400 average based on 100 at-bats). The number *N* of at-bats is an ancillary statistic because

- It is a part of the observable data (it is a *statistic*), and
- Its probability distribution does not depend on the batter's ability, since it was chosen by a random process independent of the batter's ability.

This ancillary statistic is an **ancillary complement** to the observed batting average *X*/*N*, i.e., the batting average *X*/*N* is not a [sufficient statistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficiency_%28statistics%29), in that it conveys less than all of the relevant information in the data, but conjoined with *N*, it becomes sufficient.

:::
