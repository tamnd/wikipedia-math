# First uncountable ordinal

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="First uncountable ordinal"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **first uncountable ordinal**, traditionally denoted by $\omega_1$ or sometimes by $\Omega$, is the smallest [ordinal number](wikipedia://en/26547932) that is the [order type](wikipedia://en/4499021) of an [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) [well-ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordered_set). It is the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) (least upper bound) of all countable ordinals. In the [von Neumann representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_ordinal), the elements of $\omega_1$ are the countable ordinals (including finite ordinals),[^o2] of which there are uncountably many.

:::

The [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of the set $\omega_1$ is the first uncountable [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173), $\aleph_1$ ([aleph-one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleph-one)). The ordinal $\omega_1$ is thus the [initial ordinal](wikipedia://en/26547932#Initial_ordinal_of_a_cardinal) of $\aleph_1$. Like all other initial ordinals of infinite cardinals, $\omega_1$ is a [limit ordinal](wikipedia://en/373137), i.e. there is no ordinal $\alpha$ such that $\omega_1 = \alpha+1$. Formally, cardinal numbers are usually represented as their initial ordinals, in which case $\omega_1$ and $\aleph_1$ are considered equal as sets. More generally, for any ordinal $\alpha$, $\omega_\alpha$ denotes the initial ordinal of the cardinal $\aleph_\alpha$.

The [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705) (CH) states that $\beth_1 = \aleph_1$ (where $\beth_1 = 2 ^ {\aleph_0} = \vert \mathbb{R} \vert$ is the second [beth number](wikipedia://en/393359)), which implies that $\vert \omega_1 \vert = \vert \mathbb{R} \vert$, i.e., the countable ordinals are [equinumerous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinumerous) to the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). If CH does not hold, but the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) (AC) does, then $\vert \omega_1 \vert$, as the smallest uncountable cardinal, is strictly less than $\vert \mathbb{R} \vert$. If AC also does not hold then $\vert \omega_1 \vert$ may be [incomparable](wikipedia://en/3834818) with $\vert \mathbb{R} \vert$, but never larger than $\vert \mathbb{R} \vert$.[^o3]

The existence of $\omega_1$ does not depend on AC, as it can be constructed explicitly as the [Hartogs number](wikipedia://en/2977752) of $\omega_0 = \mathbb{N}$. More concretely, the set of all well-orderings on $\mathbb{N}$ can be constructed as a subset of all binary relations on $\mathbb{N}$, and thus applying the [axiom of replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_replacement) to replace every well-ordering with its order type will give $\omega_1$.

## Topological properties {#Topological_properties}

Any ordinal number can be turned into a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) by using the [order topology](wikipedia://en/213650). When viewed as a topological space, $\omega_1$ is often written as $[0,\omega_1)$, to emphasize that it is the space consisting of all ordinals smaller than $\omega_1$.

If the [axiom of countable choice](wikipedia://en/421086) holds, every [increasing ω-sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of elements of $[0,\omega_1]$ converges to a [limit](wikipedia://en/285773) in $[0,\omega_1]$. The reason is that the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) (i.e., supremum) of every countable set of countable ordinals is another countable ordinal.

The topological space $[0,\omega_1)$ is [sequentially compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequentially_compact), but not [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). As a consequence, it is not [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738). It is, however, [countably compact](wikipedia://en/8293816) and thus not [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480) (a countably compact space is compact if and only if it is Lindelöf). In terms of [axioms of countability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axioms_of_countability), $[0,\omega_1)$ is [first-countable](wikipedia://en/904940), but neither [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) nor [second-countable](wikipedia://en/901459).

The space $[0,\omega_1]=\omega_1 + 1$ is compact and not first-countable. $\omega_1$ is used to define the [long line](wikipedia://en/338403) and the [Tychonoff plank](wikipedia://en/12589048)—two important counterexamples in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954).
