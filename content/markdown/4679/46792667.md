# Lusternik–Schnirelmann theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lusternik–Schnirelmann theorem"}

*Image: Uniform tiling 332-t02.svg.* A two-dimensional sphere **S**<sup>2</sup> covered by three closed sets, the closures of the red, yellow, and blue areas, in the pattern of a [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280). The red and yellow sets do not contain any antipodal pairs (each red or yellow triangle is opposite a triangle of the other color), but the center of each blue quadrilateral is antipodal to another blue quadrilateral.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Lusternik–Schnirelmann theorem**, aka **Lusternik–Schnirelmann–Borsuk theorem** or **LSB theorem** gives a [sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficient_condition) for the inclusion of [antipodal points](wikipedia://en/367577) in a [cover](wikipedia://en/317552) of a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859).

:::

It is named after [Lazar Lyusternik](wikipedia://en/4201372) and [Lev Schnirelmann](wikipedia://en/747223), who published it in 1930.[^o2][^o3][^o4]

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The sphere or [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) $S^n$ is an $n$-dimensional surface, conventionally embedded as the set of [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) in the $(n+1)$-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$. In this embedding, the pairs of unit vectors $(x,-x)$ are antipodal (polar opposites). A cover is a family of subsets of the sphere whose union is the entire sphere. Intuitively, a subset of the sphere is a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) if it includes all of its boundary points, or more formally if it contains all [limits of sequences](wikipedia://en/26551602) of points in the subset. The Lusternik–Schnirelmann theorem can then be stated as:

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Lusternik–Schnirelmann theorem"}

**Lusternik–Schnirelmann theorem.**

If the sphere $S^n$ is covered by $n+1$ closed sets, then at least one of these sets contains a pair of antipodal points.

:::

The related [Lusternik–Schnirelmann category](wikipedia://en/764433) of a topological space is the minimum number of subsets in a cover for which each subset is topologically simple (in a technical sense). It is at most two for a sphere, which can be covered by two [hemispheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hemisphere_%28geometry%29). However, the sphere can be used to construct a more complicated [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbb{RP}^n$, the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) obtained by treating each pair of antipodal points as a single point. The Lusternik–Schnirelmann theorem implies that the Lusternik–Schnirelmann category of $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is at least $n+1$, and it turns out to equal $n+1$.

:::

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The theorem can be proved by leveraging the relationship between $S^n$ and $\mathbb{RP}^n$, and the Borsuk-Ulam theorem in higher dimensions.[^o10]

Real Projective Space $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is defined as the quotient space of $S^n$ by the antipodal map, where each point $x \in S^n$ is identified with its antipodal point $-x \in S^n$. In other words, $\mathbb{RP}^n = S^n / \sim$, where $x \sim -x$.

A pair of antipodal points in $S^n$ corresponds to a single point in $\mathbb{RP}^n$. The concept of "containing a pair of antipodal points" in a subset of $S^n$ is equivalent to that subset having a non-empty intersection with the pre-image of some point in $\mathbb{RP}^n$ under the projection map $\pi: S^n \to \mathbb{RP}^n$.

The [Borsuk-Ulam theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borsuk-Ulam_theorem) states that for any continuous map $f: S^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$, there exists at least one pair of antipodal points $\{x, -x\}$ in $S^n$ such that $f(x) = f(-x)$.

Consider $S^n$ expressed as the union of $n+1$ closed sets $A_1, A_2, \ldots, A_{n+1}$.

Define distance functions $d_i(x) = \inf_{y \in A_i} |x-y|$ for $i=1, \ldots, n$. These are continuous functions.

Construct a map $F: S^n \to \mathbb{R}^n$ given by $F(x) = (d_1(x), d_2(x), \ldots, d_n(x))$.

By the Borsuk-Ulam theorem, there exists a pair of antipodal points $\{x_0, -x_0\}$ such that $F(x_0) = F(-x_0)$, meaning $d_i(x_0) = d_i(-x_0)$ for all $i=1, \ldots, n$.

If any $d_i(x_0) = 0$, then $x_0 \in A_i$. Since $d_i(x_0) = d_i(-x_0) = 0$, then $-x_0 \in A_i$ as well (because $A_i$ is closed). Thus, $A_i$ contains a pair of antipodal points.

If $d_i(x_0) > 0$ for all $i=1, \ldots, n$, then $x_0 \notin A_i$ for $i=1, \ldots, n$. Since $S^n = \bigcup_{j=1}^{n+1} A_j$, it must be that $x_0 \in A_{n+1}$. Similarly, since $d_i(-x_0) > 0$ for all $i=1, \ldots, n$, then $-x_0 \notin A_i$ for $i=1, \ldots, n$, implying $-x_0 \in A_{n+1}$. Therefore, $A_{n+1}$ contains a pair of antipodal points.

:::
