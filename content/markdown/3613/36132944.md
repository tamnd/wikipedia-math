# Motion (geometry)

*Image: Glide reflection.svg.* A [glide reflection](wikipedia://en/412984) is a type of Euclidean motion.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Motion"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **motion** is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018). For instance, a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) equipped with the [Euclidean distance](wikipedia://en/53932) [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) in which a mapping associating [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) figures is a motion.[^o2]

:::

Motions can be divided into [direct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid_motion) (also known as proper or rigid) and indirect (or improper) motions. *Direct motions* include [translations](wikipedia://en/204682) and [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789), which preserve the [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29) of a [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160) [shape](wikipedia://en/169191). *Indirect motions* include [reflections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_%28geometry%29), [glide reflections](wikipedia://en/412984), and [Improper rotations](wikipedia://en/244324), that invert the orientation of a chiral shape. Some geometers define motion in such a way that only direct motions are motions.

More generally, the term *motion* is a synonym for [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) isometry in metric geometry,[^o3] including [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020) and [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291). In the latter case, [hyperbolic motions](wikipedia://en/1229416) provide an approach to the subject for beginners.

## In differential geometry {#In_differential_geometry}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) is called a motion if it induces an isometry between the tangent space at a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) point and the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at the image of that point.[^o5][^o6]

## Group of motions {#Group_of_motions}

Given a geometry, the set of motions forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under composition of mappings. This **group of motions** is noted for its properties. For example, the [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164) is noted for the [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of [translations](wikipedia://en/204682). In the plane, a direct Euclidean motion is either a translation or a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009), while in [space](wikipedia://en/27667) every direct Euclidean motion may be expressed as a [screw displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screw_displacement) according to [Chasles' theorem](wikipedia://en/42392462). When the underlying space is a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652), the group of motions is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945). Furthermore, the manifold has [constant curvature](wikipedia://en/3638803) if and only if, for every pair of points and every isometry, there is a motion taking one point to the other for which the motion induces the isometry.[^o8]

The idea of a group of motions for [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) has been advanced as Lorentzian motions. For example, fundamental ideas were laid out for a plane characterized by the [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) $\ x^2 - y^2 \$ in [American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly).[^o9] The motions of [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) were described by [Sergei Novikov](wikipedia://en/628515) in 2006:[^o10]

The physical principle of constant velocity of light is expressed by the requirement that the change from one [inertial frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertial_frame) to another is determined by a motion of Minkowski space, i.e. by a transformation

$$\phi : R^{1,3} \mapsto R^{1,3}$$

preserving space-time intervals. This means that

$$\langle \phi(x) - \phi(y),\ \phi(x) - \phi(y) \rangle \ =\ \langle x - y,\ x - y \rangle$$

for each pair of points *x* and *y* in R<sup>1,3</sup>.

## History {#History}

An early appreciation of the role of motion in geometry was given by [Alhazen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhazen) (965 to 1039). His work "Space and its Nature"[^o12] uses comparisons of the dimensions of a mobile body to quantify the vacuum of imaginary space. He was criticised by [Omar Khayyam](wikipedia://en/92550) who noted that [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) had condemned the use of motion in geometry.[^o13]

In the 19th century [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) became a proponent of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) as a means to classify geometries according to their "groups of motions". He proposed using [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186) in his [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382), a suggestion that was widely adopted. He noted that every Euclidean congruence is an [affine mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_mapping), and each of these is a [projective transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation); therefore the group of projectivities contains the group of affine maps, which in turn contains the group of Euclidean congruences. The term *motion*, shorter than *transformation*, puts more emphasis on the adjectives: projective, affine, Euclidean. The context was thus expanded, so much that "In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the allowed movements are continuous invertible deformations that might be called elastic motions."[^o14]

The science of [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914) is dedicated to rendering [physical motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_%28physics%29) into expression as mathematical transformation. Frequently the transformation can be written using vector algebra and linear mapping. A simple example is a [turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28geometry%29#Kinematics_of_turns) written as a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) multiplication: $z \mapsto \omega z \$ where $\ \omega = \cos \theta + i \sin \theta, \quad i^2 = -1$. Rotation in [space](wikipedia://en/27667) is achieved by [use of quaternions](wikipedia://en/186057), and [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404) of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) by use of [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070). Early in the 20th century, [hypercomplex number](wikipedia://en/51438) systems were examined. Later their [automorphism groups](wikipedia://en/56661772) led to exceptional groups such as [G2](wikipedia://en/292864).

In the 1890s logicians were reducing the [primitive notions](wikipedia://en/23706) of [synthetic geometry](wikipedia://en/267484) to an absolute minimum. [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) and [Mario Pieri](wikipedia://en/2452874) used the expression *motion* for the congruence of point pairs. [Alessandro Padoa](wikipedia://en/2452868) celebrated the reduction of primitive notions to merely *point* and *motion* in his report to the 1900 [International Congress of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Congress_of_Philosophy). It was at this congress that [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) was exposed to continental logic through Peano. In his book *[Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principles_of_Mathematics)* (1903), Russell considered a motion to be a Euclidean isometry that preserves [orientation](wikipedia://en/1391942).[^o15]

In 1914 [D. M. Y. Sommerville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._M._Y._Sommerville) used the idea of a geometric motion to establish the idea of distance in hyperbolic geometry when he wrote *Elements of Non-Euclidean Geometry*.[^o16] He explains:

By a motion or displacement in the general sense is not meant a change of position of a single point or any bounded figure, but a displacement of the whole space, or, if we are dealing with only two dimensions, of the whole plane. A motion is a transformation which changes each point *P* into another point *P* ′ in such a way that distances and angles are unchanged.

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="Axioms of motion"}

## Axioms of motion {#Axioms_of_motion}

[László Rédei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/László_Rédei) gives as [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) of motion:[^o19]

Any motion is a one-to-one mapping of space R onto itself such that every three points on a line will be transformed into (three) points on a line. The identical mapping of space R is a motion. The product of two motions is a motion. The inverse mapping of a motion is a motion. If we have two planes A, A' two lines g, g' and two points P, P' such that P is on g, g is on A, P' is on g' and g' is on A' then there exist a motion mapping A to A', g to g' and P to P' There is a plane A, a line g, and a point P such that P is on g and g is on A then there exist four motions mapping A, g and P onto themselves, respectively, and not more than two of these motions may have every point of g as a fixed point, while there is one of them (i.e. the identity) for which every point of A is fixed. There exists three points A, B, P on line g such that P is between A and B and for every point C (unequal P) between A and B there is a point D between C and P for which no motion with P as fixed point can be found that will map C onto a point lying between D and P.

Axioms 2 to 4 imply that motions form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447).

Axiom 5 means that the group of motions provides [group actions](wikipedia://en/12781) on R that are [transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_%28group_action%29) so that there is a motion that maps every line to every line

:::
