# Frobenius theorem (real division algebras)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frobenius theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), the **Frobenius theorem**, [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by [Ferdinand Georg Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048) in 1877, characterizes the [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [associative](wikipedia://en/2112) [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625) over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). According to the [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977), every such algebra is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to one of the following:

:::

- $\mathbf{R}$ (the real numbers),
- $\mathbf{C}$ (the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)), or
- $\mathbf{H}$ (the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440)).

These algebras have real dimension $1, 2$, and $4$, respectively. Of these three algebras, $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{C}$ are [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29), but $\mathbf{H}$ is not.

::: {#o3 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The main ingredients for the following proof are the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547) and the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414).

### Introducing some notation {#Introducing_some_notation}

- Let $D$ be the division algebra in question.
- Let $n$ be the dimension of $D$.
- We identify the real multiples of $1$ with $\mathbf{R}$.
- When we write $a \le 0$ for an element $a$ of $D$, we imply that $a$ is contained in $\mathbf{R}$.
- We can consider $D$ as a finite-dimensional $\mathbf{R}$-[vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). Any element $d$ of $D$ defines an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) of $D$ by left-multiplication, we identify $d$ with that endomorphism. Therefore, we can speak about the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of $d$, and its [characteristic](wikipedia://en/218268)- and [minimal polynomials](wikipedia://en/9667107).
- For any $z$ in $\mathbf{C}$ define the following real [quadratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial) [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000):

$$Q(z; x) = x^2 - 2\operatorname{Re}(z)x + |z|^2 = (x-z)(x-\overline{z}) \in \mathbf{R}[x].$$

Note that if $z \in \mathbf{C}\,\setminus\,\mathbf{R}$ then $Q(z; x)$ is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) over $\mathbf{R}$.

### The claim {#The_claim}

The key to the argument is the following

**Claim.** The set $V$ of all elements $a$ of $D$ such that $a^{2} \le 0$ is a vector subspace of $D$ of dimension $n - 1$. Moreover $D = \mathbf{R} \oplus V$ as $\mathbf{R}$-vector spaces, which implies that $V$ generates $D$ as an algebra.

::: {#o6 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof of Claim"}

**Proof of Claim:** Pick $a$ in $D$ with characteristic polynomial $p(x)$. By the fundamental theorem of algebra, we can write

$$p(x) = (x-t_1)\cdots(x-t_r) (x-z_1)(x - \overline{z_1}) \cdots (x-z_s)(x - \overline{z_s}), \qquad t_i \in \mathbf{R}, \quad z_j \in \mathbf{C} \setminus \mathbf{R}.$$

We can rewrite $p(x)$ in terms of the polynomials $Q(z; x)$:

$$p(x) = (x-t_1)\cdots(x-t_r) Q(z_1; x) \cdots Q(z_s; x).$$

Since $z_{j} \in \mathbf{C}\,\setminus\,\mathbf{R}$, the polynomials $Q(z_{j}; x)$ are all irreducible over $\mathbf{R}$. By the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, $p(a) = 0$ and because $D$ is a division algebra, it follows that either $a - t_{i} = 0$ for some $i$ or that $Q(z_{j}; a) = 0$ for some $j$. The first case implies that $a$ is real. In the second case, it follows that $Q(z_{j}; x)$ is the minimal polynomial of $a$. Because $p(x)$ has the same complex [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) as the minimal polynomial and because it is real it follows that

$$p(x) = Q(z_j; x)^k = \left(x^2 - 2\operatorname{Re}(z_j) x + |z_j|^2 \right)^k$$

for some $k$. Since $p(x)$ is the characteristic polynomial of $a$ the coefficient of $x^{\,2k -\,1}$ in $p(x)$ is $\operatorname{tr}(a)$ up to a sign. Therefore, we read from the above equation we have: $\operatorname{tr}(a) = 0$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\operatorname{Re}(z_{j}) = 0$, in other words $\operatorname{tr}(a) = 0$ if and only if $a^{2} = -|z_{j}|^{2} < 0$.

So $V$ is the subset of all $a$ with $\operatorname{tr}(a) = 0$. In particular, it is a vector subspace. The [rank–nullity theorem](wikipedia://en/330310) then implies that $V$ has dimension $n - 1$ since it is the [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915) of $\operatorname{tr} : D \to \mathbf{R}$. Since $\mathbf{R}$ and $V$ are disjoint (i.e. they satisfy $\mathbf R \cap V = \{0\}$), and their dimensions sum to $n$, we have that $D = \mathbf{R} \oplus V$.

:::

### The finish {#The_finish}

For $a, b$ in $V$ define $B(a, b) = (-ab - ba)/2$. Because of the identity $(a + b)^{2} - a^{2} - b^{2} = ab + ba$, it follows that $B(a, b)$ is real. Furthermore, since $a^{2} \le 0$, we have: $B(a, a) > 0$ for $a \ne 0$. Thus $B$ is a [positive-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_bilinear_form) [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375), in other words, an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) on $V$.

Let $W$ be a subspace of $V$ that generates $D$ as an algebra and which is minimal with respect to this property. Let $e_{1}, ..., e_{k}$ be an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) of $W$ with respect to $B$. Then orthonormality implies that:

$$e_i^2 =-1, \quad e_i e_j = - e_j e_i.$$

The form of $D$ then depends on $k$:

If $k = 0$, then $D$ is isomorphic to $\mathbf{R}$.

If $k = 1$, then $D$ is generated by $1$ and $e_{1}$ subject to the relation $e^{2}_{1} = -1$. Hence it is isomorphic to $\mathbf{C}$.

If $k = 2$, it has been shown above that $D$ is generated by $1, e_{1}, e_{2}$ subject to the relations

$$e_1^2 = e_2^2 =-1, \quad e_1 e_2 = - e_2 e_1, \quad (e_1 e_2)(e_1 e_2) =-1.$$

These are precisely the relations for $\mathbf{H}$.

If $k > 2$, then $D$ cannot be a division algebra. Assume that $k > 2$. Define $u = e_{1}e_{2}e_{k}$ and consider $u^{2}=(e_{1}e_{2}e_{k})*(e_{1}e_{2}e_{k})$. By rearranging the elements of this expression and applying the orthonormality relations among the basis elements we find that $u^{2} = 1$. If $D$ were a division algebra, $0 = u^{2} - 1 = (u - 1)(u + 1)$ implies $u = \pm1$, which in turn means: $e_{k} = \mp e_{1}e_{2}$ and so $e_{1}, ..., e_{k-1}$ generate $D$. This contradicts the minimality of $W$.

:::

## Remarks and related results {#Remarks_and_related_results}

- The fact that $D$ is generated by $e_{1}, ..., e_{k}$ subject to the above relations means that $D$ is the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. The last step shows that the only real Clifford algebras which are division algebras are $C\ell^{0}, C\ell^{1}$ and $C\ell^{2}$.
- As a consequence, the only commutative division algebras are $\mathbf{R}$ and $\mathbf{C}$. Also note that $\mathbf{H}$ is not a $\mathbf{C}$-algebra. If it were, then the [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) of $\mathbf{H}$ has to contain $\mathbf{C}$, but the center of $\mathbf{H}$ is $\mathbf{R}$.
- This theorem is closely related to [Hurwitz's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurwitz's_theorem_%28normed_division_algebras%29), which states that the only real [normed division algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_division_algebra) are $\mathbf{R}, \mathbf{C}, \mathbf{H}$, and the (non-associative) algebra $\mathbf{O}$.
- **Pontryagin variant.** If $D$ is a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632) division [ring](wikipedia://en/60097), then $D = \mathbf{R}, \mathbf{C}$, or $\mathbf{H}$.
