# Harold Davenport

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Harold Davenport"}

**Harold Davenport** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society)[^o3] (30 October 1907 – 9 June 1969) was an English mathematician, known for his extensive work in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Born on 30 October 1907 in [Huncoat, Lancashire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huncoat), Davenport was educated at [Accrington Grammar School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accrington_Grammar_School), the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester) (graduating in 1927), and [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge). He became a research student of [John Edensor Littlewood](wikipedia://en/158371),[^o2] working on the question of the distribution of [quadratic residues](wikipedia://en/200091).

## First steps in research {#First_steps_in_research}

The attack on the distribution question leads quickly to problems that are now seen to be special cases of those on [local zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta-function), for the particular case of some special [hyperelliptic curves](wikipedia://en/499361) such as $Y^2 = X(X-1)(X-2)\ldots (X-k)$.

Bounds for the zeroes of the local zeta-function immediately imply bounds for sums $\sum \chi(X(X-1)(X-2)\ldots (X-k))$, where χ is the [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567) *[modulo](wikipedia://en/20087)* a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*, and the sum is taken over a [complete set of residues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_set_of_residues) mod *p*.

In the light of this connection it was appropriate that, with a Trinity research fellowship, Davenport in 1932–1933 spent time in [Marburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marburg) and [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen) working with [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118), an expert on the algebraic theory. This produced the work on the [Hasse–Davenport relations](wikipedia://en/14619729) for [Gauss sums](wikipedia://en/6378204), and contact with [Hans Heilbronn](wikipedia://en/4388848), with whom Davenport would later collaborate. In fact, as Davenport later admitted, his inherent prejudices against algebraic methods ("what can you *do* with algebra?") probably limited the amount he learned, in particular in the "new" [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and [Artin](wikipedia://en/245351)/[Noether](wikipedia://en/149896) approach to [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384).

He proved in 1946 that [8436](wikipedia://en/565218) is the largest [tetrahedral number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_number) of the form $2^a+3^b+1$ for some nonnegative integers $a$ and $b$ and also in 1947 that [5040](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5040_%28number%29) is the largest [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) of the form $n(n+4)(n+6)$ for some integer $n$ by using [Brun sieve](wikipedia://en/18731525) and other advanced methods.

## Later career {#Later_career}

He took an appointment at the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/School_of_Mathematics,_University_of_Manchester) in 1937, just at the time when [Louis Mordell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Mordell) had recruited émigrés from continental Europe to build an outstanding department. He moved into the areas of [diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558) and [geometry of numbers](wikipedia://en/250153). These were fashionable, and complemented the technical expertise he had in the [Hardy–Littlewood circle method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Littlewood_circle_method); he was later, though, to let drop the comment that he wished he'd spent more time on the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125).

He was President of the [London Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/591097) from 1957 to 1959.[^o8] After professorial positions at the [University of Wales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wales) and [University College London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_London), he was appointed to the [Rouse Ball Chair of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rouse_Ball_Chair_of_Mathematics) in Cambridge in 1958. There he remained until his death, of lung cancer.

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Davenport married Anne Lofthouse, whom he met at the [University College of North Wales at Bangor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_of_North_Wales,_Bangor) in 1944; they had two children, Richard and [James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Davenport_%28professor%29), the latter going on to become Hebron and Medlock Professor of Information Technology at the [University of Bath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bath).

## Influence {#Influence}

section From about 1950, Davenport was the obvious leader of a "school", somewhat unusually in the context of British mathematics. The successor to the school of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) of [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) and [J. E. Littlewood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._E._Littlewood), it was also more narrowly devoted to number theory, and indeed to its analytic side, as had flourished in the 1930s. This implied problem-solving, and hard-analysis methods. The outstanding works of [Klaus Roth](wikipedia://en/787827) and [Alan Baker](wikipedia://en/18950800) exemplify what this can do, in diophantine approximation. Two reported sayings, "the problems are there", and "I don't care how you get hold of the gadget, I just want to know how big or small it is", sum up the attitude, and could be transplanted today into any discussion of [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170). This concrete emphasis on problems stood in sharp contrast with the abstraction of [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394), who were then active just across the [English Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Channel).
