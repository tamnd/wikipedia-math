# John Selfridge

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="John Selfridge"}

**John Lewis Selfridge** (February 17, 1927 – October 31, 2010[^o3]) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who contributed to the fields of [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513), [computational number theory](wikipedia://en/511466), and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170).

:::

## Education {#Education}

Selfridge received his Ph.D. in 1958 from the [University of California, Los Angeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Los_Angeles) under the supervision of [Theodore Motzkin](wikipedia://en/13554055).

## Career {#Career}

Selfridge served on the faculties of the [University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana-Champaign) and [Northern Illinois University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Illinois_University) (NIU) from 1971 to 1991 (retirement), chairing the NIU Department of Mathematical Sciences 1972–1976 and 1986–1990. He was executive editor of *[Mathematical Reviews](wikipedia://en/1485646)* from 1978 to 1986, overseeing the computerization of its operations.[^o7] He was a founder of the [Number Theory Foundation](wikipedia://en/13053993),[^o8] which has named its [Selfridge prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfridge_prize) in his honour.

## Research {#Research}

In 1962, he proved that 78,557 is a [Sierpinski number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_number); he showed that, when *k* = 78,557, all numbers of the form *k*2<sup>*n*</sup> + 1 have a [factor](wikipedia://en/82341) in the [covering set](wikipedia://en/2034219) {3, 5, 7, 13, 19, 37, 73}. Five years later, he and [Sierpiński](wikipedia://en/34122) conjectured that 78,557 is the smallest Sierpinski number, and thus the answer to the Sierpinski problem. A distributed computing project, [Seventeen or Bust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seventeen_or_Bust), is devoted to finding a computational proof of this statement.

In 1964, Selfridge and Alexander Hurwitz proved that the 14th [Fermat number](wikipedia://en/91127) $2^{2^{14}} + 1$ was composite. [^o10] However, their proof did not provide a factor. It was not until 2010 that the first factor of the 14th Fermat number was found. [^o11] [^o12]

In 1975 [John Brillhart](wikipedia://en/11176492), [Derrick Henry Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_Henry_Lehmer), and Selfridge developed a method of proving the primality of p given only partial factorizations of *p* − 1 and *p* + 1. [^o13] Together with [Samuel Wagstaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Wagstaff) they also all participated in the [Cunningham project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cunningham_project).

Together with [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284), Selfridge solved a 150-year-old problem, proving that the product of consecutive numbers is never a power.[^o14] It took them many years to find the proof, and John made extensive use of computers, but the final version of the proof requires only a modest amount of computation, namely evaluating an easily computed function f(n) for 30,000 consecutive values of *n*. Selfridge suffered from [writer's block](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writer's_block) and thanked "R. B. Eggleton for reorganizing and writing the paper in its final form".[^o14]

Selfridge also developed the [Selfridge–Conway discrete procedure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfridge–Conway_discrete_procedure) for creating an [envy-free cake-cutting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Envy-free_cake-cutting) among three people. Selfridge developed this in 1960, and [John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) independently discovered it in 1993. Neither of them ever published the result, but [Richard Guy](wikipedia://en/816308) told many people Selfridge's solution in the 1960s, and it was eventually attributed to the two of them in a number of books and articles.[^o15]

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Conjecture about Fermat numbers"}

### Conjecture about Fermat numbers {#Conjecture_about_Fermat_numbers}

Selfridge made the following conjecture about the [Fermat numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_numbers) *F<sub>n</sub>* = 2<sup>2<sup>*n*</sup></sup> + 1 . Let *g*(*n*) be the number of distinct prime factors of *F<sub>n</sub>* . As to 2024, *g*(*n*) is known only up to *n* = 11, and it is monotonic. Selfridge conjectured that contrary to appearances, *g*(*n*) is *not* monotonic. In support of his conjecture he showed a sufficient (but not necessary) condition for its truth is the existence of another Fermat *prime* beyond the five known (3, 5, 17, 257, 65537).[^o18]

:::

::: {#o20 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Selfridge primality test"}

### Conjectured primality test {#Conjectured_primality_test}

This conjecture is also called the PSW conjecture, after Selfridge, [Carl Pomerance](wikipedia://en/2134159), and [Samuel Wagstaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Wagstaff).

Let *p* be an odd number, with *p* ≡ ± 2 (mod 5). Selfridge conjectured that if

- 2<sup>*p*−1</sup> ≡ 1 (mod *p*) and at the same time
- *f*<sub>*p*+1</sub> ≡ 0 (mod *p*),

where *f*<sub>*k*</sub> is the *k*th [Fibonacci number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number), then *p* is a prime number, and he offered \$500 for a counterexample. He also offered \$20 for a proof that the conjecture was true. The [Number Theory Foundation](wikipedia://en/13053993) will now cover this prize. A counterexample will actually earn its discoverer \$620, because [Samuel Wagstaff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Wagstaff) offers \$100 for a counterexample or a proof, and [Carl Pomerance](wikipedia://en/2134159) offers \$20 for a counterexample and \$500 for a proof. Selfridge requires that a factorization be supplied, but Pomerance does not. The related test that *f*<sub>*p*−1</sub> ≡ 0 (mod *p*) for *p* ≡ ±1 (mod 5) is unsound:[^o21] the smallest counterexample for *p* ≡ 1 (mod 5) is 6601 = 7 × 23 × 41 and the smallest for *p* ≡ −1 (mod 5) is 30889 = 17 × 23 × 79. Pomerance has given a heuristic argument suggesting that Selfridge's conjecture is likely to be false (i.e. that a counterexample is likely to exist).

:::
