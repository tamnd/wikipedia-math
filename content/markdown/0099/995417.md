# Geodetic datum

*Image: Chicago City Datum.jpg.* City of Chicago Datum Benchmark

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geodetic datum"}

A **geodetic datum** or **geodetic system** (also: **geodetic reference datum**, **geodetic reference system**, or **geodetic reference frame**, or **terrestrial reference frame**) is a global [datum reference](wikipedia://en/1013923) or [reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_frame) for unambiguously representing the position of [locations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Location) on [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228) by means of either [geodetic coordinates](wikipedia://en/27457201) (and related [vertical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_coordinate)) or [geocentric coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_coordinates).[^o2] Datums[^o3] are crucial to any technology or technique based on spatial location, including [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608), [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation), [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying), [geographic information systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_information_system), [remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing), and [cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography). A **horizontal datum** is used to measure a [horizontal position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_position), across the [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228)'s surface, in [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) and [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) or another related coordinate system. A *[vertical datum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_datum)* is used to measure the elevation or depth relative to a standard origin, such as mean [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level) (MSL). A **three-dimensional datum** enables the expression of both horizontal and vertical position components in a unified form.[^o4] The concept can be generalized for other celestial bodies as in *[planetary datums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_datum)*.

:::

Since the rise of the [global positioning system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_positioning_system) (GPS), the [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381) and datum [WGS 84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Geodetic_System) it uses has supplanted most others in many applications. The WGS 84 is intended for global use, unlike most earlier datums. Before GPS, there was no precise way to measure the position of a location that was far from reference points used in the realization of local datums, such as from the [Prime Meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Meridian) at the [Greenwich Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Observatory,_Greenwich) for longitude, from the [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) for latitude, or from the nearest coast for sea level. Astronomical and chronological methods have limited precision and accuracy, especially over long distances. Even GPS requires a predefined framework on which to base its measurements, so WGS 84 essentially functions as a datum, even though it is different in some particulars from a traditional standard horizontal or vertical datum.

A standard datum specification (whether horizontal, vertical, or 3D) consists of several parts: a model for Earth's shape and dimensions, such as a *[reference ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid)* or a *[geoid](wikipedia://en/176244)*; an *origin* at which the ellipsoid/geoid is tied to a known (often monumented) location on or inside Earth (not necessarily at 0 latitude 0 longitude); and multiple [control points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodetic_control_network) or reference points that have been precisely measured from the origin and physically monumented. Then the coordinates of other places are measured from the nearest control point through [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying). Because the ellipsoid or geoid differs between datums, along with their origins and orientation in space, the relationship between coordinates referred to one datum and coordinates referred to another datum is undefined and can only be approximated. Using local datums, the disparity on the ground between a point having the same horizontal coordinates in two different datums could reach kilometers if the point is far from the origin of one or both datums. This phenomenon is called *[datum shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datum_shift)* or, more generally, *datum transformation*, as it may involve rotation and scaling, in addition to displacement.

Because Earth is an imperfect ellipsoid, local datums can give a more accurate representation of some specific area of coverage than WGS 84 can. [OSGB36](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordnance_Survey_National_Grid), for example, is a better approximation to the [geoid](wikipedia://en/176244) covering the British Isles than the global WGS 84 ellipsoid.[^o5] However, as the benefits of a global system often outweigh the greater accuracy, the global WGS 84 datum has become widely adopted.[^o6]

## History {#History}

History of navigation

*Image: Index chart of the Great Trigonometrical Survey of India (vectorized).svg.* The Great Trigonometrical Survey of India, one of the first surveys comprehensive enough to establish a geodetic datum.

The spherical nature of Earth was known by the ancient Greeks, who also developed the concepts of latitude and longitude, and the first astronomical methods for measuring them. These methods, preserved and further developed by [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) and Indian astronomers, were sufficient for the global explorations of the 15th and 16th Centuries.

However, the scientific advances of the [Age of Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Enlightenment) brought a recognition of errors in these measurements, and a demand for greater precision. This led to technological innovations such as the 1735 [Marine chronometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_chronometer) by [John Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harrison), but also to a reconsideration of the underlying assumptions about the shape of Earth itself. [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) postulated that the [conservation of momentum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conservation_of_momentum) should make Earth [oblate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_bulge) (wider at the equator than the corresponding sphere), while the early surveys of [Jacques Cassini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Cassini) (1720) led him to believe Earth was [prolate](wikipedia://en/82365) (narrower at the equator). The subsequent French geodesic missions (1735-1739) to [Lapland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Geodesic_Mission_to_Lapland) and [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Geodesic_Mission) corroborated Newton, but also discovered variations in gravity that would eventually lead to the [geoid](wikipedia://en/176244) model.

A contemporary development was the use of the [trigonometric survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_survey) to accurately measure distance and location over great distances. Starting with the surveys of [Jacques Cassini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Cassini) (1718) and the [Anglo-French Survey (1784–1790)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-French_Survey_%281784–1790%29), by the end of the 18th century, survey control networks covered [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography_of_France#Cassini_maps) and [the United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_Triangulation_of_Great_Britain). More ambitious undertakings such as the [Struve Geodetic Arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Struve_Geodetic_Arc) across Eastern Europe (1816-1855) and the [Great Trigonometrical Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Trigonometrical_Survey) of India (1802-1871) took much longer, but resulted in more accurate estimations of the shape of the [Earth ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/16112368). The first triangulation across the United States was not completed until 1899.

The U.S. survey resulted in the [North American Datum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Datum) (horizontal) of 1927 (NAD 27) and the Vertical Datum of 1929 (NAVD29), the first standard datums available for public use. This was followed by the release of national and regional datums over the next several decades. Improving measurements, including the use of early [satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite), enabled more accurate datums in the later 20th century, such as [NAD 83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Datum#North_American_Datum_of_1983) in North America, [ETRS89](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETRS89) in Europe, and [GDA94](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GDA94) in Australia. At this time global datums were also first developed for use in [satellite navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation) systems, especially the [World Geodetic System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Geodetic_System) (WGS 84) used in the U.S. [global positioning system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_positioning_system) (GPS), and the [International Terrestrial Reference System and Frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Terrestrial_Reference_System_and_Frame) (ITRF) used in the European [Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_%28satellite_navigation%29) system.

## Dimensions {#Dimensions}

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Horizontal datum"}

### Horizontal datum {#Horizontal_datum}

A horizontal datum is a model used to precisely measure positions on Earth; it is thus a crucial component of any [spatial reference system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_reference_system) or [map projection](wikipedia://en/51784). A horizontal datum binds a specified [reference ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid), a mathematical model of the shape of the earth, to the physical earth. Thus, the [geographic coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate_system) on that ellipsoid can be used to measure the latitude and longitude of real-world locations. Regional horizontal datums, such as [NAD 27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Datum#North_American_Datum_of_1927) and [NAD 83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAD_83), usually create this binding with a series of physically monumented [geodetic control points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodetic_control_network) of known location. Global datums, such as [WGS 84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WGS_84) and [ITRF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ITRF), are typically bound to the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) of the Earth (making them useful for tracking satellite orbits and thus for use in [satellite navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_navigation) systems.

A specific point can have substantially different coordinates, depending on the datum used to make the measurement. For example, coordinates in NAD 83 can differ from NAD 27 by up to several hundred feet. There are hundreds of local horizontal datums around the world, usually referenced to some convenient local reference point. Contemporary datums, based on increasingly accurate measurements of the shape of Earth, are intended to cover larger areas. The [WGS 84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WGS_84) datum, which is almost identical to the [NAD 83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAD_83) datum used in North America and the [ETRS89](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETRS89) datum used in Europe, is a common standard datum.

:::

### Vertical datum {#Vertical_datum}

A **vertical datum** is a reference surface for [vertical positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_position), such as the [elevations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation) of Earth features including [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain), [bathymetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathymetry), [water level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_level), and human-made structures.

An approximate definition of [sea level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level) is the datum [WGS 84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Geodetic_System), an [ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid), whereas a more accurate definition is Earth Gravitational Model 2008 (EGM2008), using at least 2,159 [spherical harmonics](wikipedia://en/203056). Other datums are defined for other areas or at other times; [ED50](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ED50) was defined in 1950 over Europe and differs from WGS 84 by a few hundred meters depending on where in Europe you look. [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars) has no [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceans) and so no sea level, but at least two [martian datums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_of_Mars#Zero_elevation) have been used to locate places there.

## Geodetic coordinates {#Geodetic_coordinates}

*Image: Geocentric vs geodetic latitude.svg.* The same position on a [spheroid](wikipedia://en/82365) has a different angle for latitude depending on whether the angle is measured from the normal line segment *CP* of the [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381) (angle *α*) or the line segment *OP* from the center (angle *β*). The "flatness" of the spheroid (orange) in the image is greater than that of Earth; as a result, the corresponding difference between the "geodetic" and "geocentric" latitudes is also exaggerated.

In *geodetic coordinates*, Earth's surface is approximated by an [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/16112368), and locations near the surface are described in terms of *[geodetic latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodetic_latitude)* ($\phi$), *[longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude)* ($\lambda$), and *[ellipsoidal height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipsoidal_height)* ($h$).[^o13]

## Earth reference ellipsoid {#Earth_reference_ellipsoid}

### Defining and derived parameters {#Defining_and_derived_parameters}

The ellipsoid is completely parameterised by the semi-major axis $a$ and the flattening $f$.

::: {#o16 .table cue=table}

| Parameter | Symbol |
|---|---|
| Semi-major axis | $a$ |
| Reciprocal of flattening | $\frac{1}{f}$ |

:::

From $a$ and $f$ it is possible to derive the semi-minor axis $b$, first eccentricity $e$ and second eccentricity $e'$ of the ellipsoid

::: {#o17 .table cue=table}

| Parameter | Value |
|---|---|
| Semi-minor axis | $b = a(1 - f)$ |
| First eccentricity squared | $e^2 = 1 - \frac{b^2}{a^2} = f(2 - f)$ |
| Second eccentricity squared | ${e'}^2 = \frac{a^2}{b^2} - 1 = \frac{f(2 - f)}{(1 - f)^2}$ |

:::

## Datum transformation {#Datum_transformation}

The difference in co-ordinates between datums is commonly referred to as *datum shift*. The datum shift between two particular datums can vary from one place to another within one country or region, and can be anything from zero to hundreds of meters (or several kilometers for some remote islands). The [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole), [South Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pole) and [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) will be in different positions on different datums, so [True North](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_North) will be slightly different. Different datums use different interpolations for the precise shape and size of Earth ([reference ellipsoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid)). For example, in Sydney there is a 200 metres (700 feet) difference between GPS coordinates configured in GDA (based on global standard WGS 84) and AGD (used for most local maps), which is an unacceptably large error for some applications, such as [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying) or site location for [scuba diving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scuba_diving).[^o27]

Datum conversion is the process of converting the coordinates of a point from one datum system to another. Because the survey networks upon which datums were traditionally based are irregular, and the error in early surveys is not evenly distributed, datum conversion cannot be performed using a simple parametric function. For example, converting from [NAD 27](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Datum#North_American_Datum_of_1927) to [NAD 83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAD_83) is performed using NADCON (later improved as HARN), a raster grid covering North America, with the value of each cell being the average adjustment distance for that area in latitude and longitude. Datum conversion may frequently be accompanied by a change of [map projection](wikipedia://en/51784).

## Discussion and examples {#Discussion_and_examples}

A geodetic reference datum is a known and constant surface which is used to describe the location of unknown points on Earth. Since reference datums can have different radii and different center points, a specific point on Earth can have substantially different coordinates depending on the datum used to make the measurement. There are hundreds of locally developed reference datums around the world, usually referenced to some convenient local reference point. Contemporary datums, based on increasingly accurate measurements of the shape of Earth, are intended to cover larger areas. The most common reference Datums in use in [North America](wikipedia://en/21139) are NAD 27, NAD 83, and [WGS 84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WGS_84).

The [North American Datum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_American_Datum) of 1927 (NAD 27) is "the horizontal control datum for the United States that was defined by a location and azimuth on the Clarke spheroid of 1866, with origin at (the survey station) [Meades Ranch (Kansas)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meades_Ranch_Triangulation_Station)." ... The geoidal height at Meades Ranch was assumed to be zero, as sufficient gravity data was not available, and this was needed to relate surface measurements to the datum. "Geodetic positions on the North American Datum of 1927 were derived from the (coordinates of and an azimuth at Meades Ranch) through a readjustment of the triangulation of the entire network in which Laplace azimuths were introduced, and the Bowie method was used."[^o29] NAD 27 is a local referencing system covering North America.

The North American Datum of 1983 (NAD 83) is "The horizontal control datum for the United States, Canada, Mexico, and Central America, based on a geocentric origin and the Geodetic Reference System 1980 ([GRS{{nbsp}}80](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GRS{{nbsp}}80)). "This datum, designated as NAD 83…is based on the adjustment of 250,000 points including 600 satellite Doppler stations which constrain the system to a geocentric origin." NAD 83 may be considered a local referencing system.

WGS 84 is the [World Geodetic System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Geodetic_System) of 1984. It is the reference frame used by the [U.S. Department of Defense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Defense) (DoD) and is defined by the [National Geospatial-Intelligence Agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Geospatial-Intelligence_Agency) (NGA) (formerly the Defense Mapping Agency, then the National Imagery and Mapping Agency). WGS 84 is used by the DoD for all its mapping, charting, surveying, and navigation needs, including its [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS) "broadcast" and "precise" orbits. WGS 84 was defined in January 1987 using Doppler satellite surveying techniques. It was used as the reference frame for broadcast GPS Ephemerides (orbits) beginning January 23, 1987. At 0000 GMT January 2, 1994, WGS 84 was upgraded in accuracy using GPS measurements. The formal name then became WGS 84 (G730), since the upgrade date coincided with the start of GPS Week 730. It became the reference frame for broadcast orbits on June 28, 1994. At 0000 GMT September 30, 1996 (the start of GPS Week 873), WGS 84 was redefined again and was more closely aligned with [International Earth Rotation Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Earth_Rotation_Service) (IERS) frame [ITRF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Terrestrial_Reference_System) 94. It was then formally called WGS 84 (G873). WGS 84 (G873) was adopted as the reference frame for broadcast orbits on January 29, 1997.[^o30] Another update brought it to WGS 84 (G1674).

The WGS 84 datum, within two meters of the NAD 83 datum used in North America, is the only world referencing system in place today. WGS 84 is the default standard datum for coordinates stored in recreational and commercial GPS units.

Users of GPS are cautioned that they must always check the datum of the maps they are using. To correctly enter, display, and to store map related map coordinates, the datum of the map must be entered into the GPS map datum field.

## Plate movement {#Plate_movement}

The Earth's [tectonic plates](wikipedia://en/24944) move relative to one another in different directions at speeds on the order of 50 to 100 per year.[^o48] Therefore, locations on different plates are in motion relative to one another. For example, the longitudinal difference between a point on the equator in Uganda, on the [African Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African_Plate), and a point on the equator in Ecuador, on the [South American Plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_American_Plate), increases by about 0.0014 [arcseconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_of_arc#Symbols_and_abbreviations) per year. These tectonic movements likewise affect latitude.

If a global reference frame (such as [WGS 84](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WGS_84)) is used, the coordinates of a place on the surface generally will change from year to year. Most mapping, such as within a single country, does not span plates. To minimize coordinate changes for that case, a different reference frame can be used, one whose coordinates are fixed to that particular plate. Examples of these reference frames are "[NAD 83](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAD_83)" for North America and "[ETRS89](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETRS89)" for Europe.
