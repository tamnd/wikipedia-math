# Skew apeirohedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Skew apeirohedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **skew apeirohedron** is an infinite [skew polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_polyhedron) consisting of nonplanar [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) or nonplanar [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719), allowing the figure to extend indefinitely without folding round to form a [closed surface](wikipedia://en/27865#Closed_surfaces).

:::

Skew apeirohedra have also been called **polyhedral sponges**.

Many are directly related to a [convex uniform honeycomb](wikipedia://en/2961), being the [polygonal](wikipedia://en/23621) surface of a [honeycomb](wikipedia://en/6008654) with some of the [cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_%28geometry%29) removed. Characteristically, an infinite skew polyhedron divides 3-dimensional space into two halves. If one half is thought of as *solid* the figure is sometimes called a **partial honeycomb**.

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Coxeter-Petrie polyhedron"}

## Regular skew apeirohedra {#Regular_skew_apeirohedra}

According to [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter), in 1926 [John Flinders Petrie](wikipedia://en/23915649) generalized the concept of [regular skew polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon) (nonplanar polygons) to *regular skew polyhedra* (apeirohedra).[^o4]

Coxeter and Petrie found three of these that filled 3-space:

::: {#o5 .table cue=table}

| Regular skew apeirohedra |  |  |
|---|---|---|
| *Image: Mucube external.png.* {4,6\|4} **mucube** | *Image: Muoctahedron external.png.* {6,4\|4} **muoctahedron** | *Image: Mutetrahedron external.png.* {6,6\|3} **mutetrahedron** |

:::

There also exist [chiral](wikipedia://en/20861435) skew apeirohedra of types {4,6}, {6,4}, and {6,6}. These skew apeirohedra are [vertex-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex-transitive), [edge-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge-transitive), and [face-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-transitive), but not [mirror symmetric](wikipedia://en/1390873) (Schulte 2004).

Beyond Euclidean 3-space, in 1967 C. W. L. Garner published a set of 31 regular skew polyhedra in hyperbolic 3-space.[^o7]

:::

## Gott's regular pseudopolyhedrons {#Gott's_regular_pseudopolyhedrons}

[J. Richard Gott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Richard_Gott) in 1967 published a larger set of seven infinite skew polyhedra which he called *regular pseudopolyhedrons*, including the three from Coxeter as {4,6}, {6,4}, and {6,6} and four new ones: {5,5}, {4,5}, {3,8}, {3,10}.[^o9][^o10]

Gott relaxed the definition of regularity to allow his new figures. Where Coxeter and Petrie had required that the vertices be symmetrical, Gott required only that they be congruent. Thus, Gott's new examples are not regular by Coxeter and Petrie's definition.

Gott called the full set of *regular polyhedra*, *regular tilings*, and *regular pseudopolyhedra* as **regular generalized polyhedra**, representable by a {p,q} [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394), with by p-gonal faces, *q* around each vertex. However neither the term "pseudopolyhedron" nor Gott's definition of regularity have achieved wide usage.

Crystallographer [A.F. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.F._Wells) in 1960's also published a list of skew apeirohedra. [Melinda Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melinda_Green) published [many more](http://superliminal.com/geometry/infinite/infinite.htm) in 1998.

### Prismatic forms {#Prismatic_forms}

::: {#o13 .table cue=table}

| *Image: Five-square skew polyhedron.png.* Prismatic form: {4,5} |
|---|

:::

There are two *prismatic* forms:

1. {4,5}: 5 squares on a vertex (Two parallel [square tilings](wikipedia://en/2863809) connected by [cubic](wikipedia://en/6285) holes.)
1. {3,8}: 8 triangles on a vertex (Two parallel [triangle tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_tiling) connected by [octahedral](wikipedia://en/80177635) holes.)

### Other forms {#Other_forms}

{3,10} is also formed from parallel planes of *triangular tilings*, with alternating octahedral holes going both ways.

{5,5} is composed of 3 coplanar [pentagons](wikipedia://en/20741014) around a vertex and two perpendicular pentagons filling the gap.

Gott also acknowledged that there are other periodic forms of the regular planar tessellations. Both the *square tiling* {4,4} and *triangular tiling* {3,6} can be curved into approximating infinite cylinders in 3-space.

::: {#o16 .statement cue=heading name="Theorems"}

### Theorems {#Theorems}

He wrote some theorems:

1. For every regular polyhedron {p,q}: (p−2)\*(q−2)\<4. For Every regular tessellation: (p−2)\*(q−2)=4. For every regular pseudopolyhedron: (p−2)\*(q−2)>4.
1. The number of faces surrounding a given face is p\*(q−2) in any regular generalized polyhedron.
1. Every regular pseudopolyhedron approximates a negatively curved surface.
1. The seven regular pseudopolyhedra are repeating structures.

:::
