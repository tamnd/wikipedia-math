# Split graph

*Image: Split graph.svg.* A split graph, partitioned into a clique and an independent set.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Split graph"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of mathematics, a **split graph** is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) in which the [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) can be [partitioned](wikipedia://en/11973947) into a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) and an [independent set](wikipedia://en/524501). Split graphs were first studied by (txt), and independently introduced by (txt), where they called these graphs "polar graphs" (полярные графы).[^o4]

:::

A split graph may have more than one partition into a clique and an independent set; for instance, the [path](wikipedia://en/4587190) $a-b-c$ is a split graph, the vertices of which can be partitioned in three different ways:

1. the clique $\{a, b\}$ and the independent set $\{c\}$
1. the clique $\{b, c\}$ and the independent set $\{a\}$
1. the clique $\{b\}$ and the independent set $\{a, c\}$

Split graphs can be characterized in terms of their [forbidden induced subgraphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbidden_induced_subgraph): a graph is split if and only if no [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) is a [cycle](wikipedia://en/878962) on four or five vertices, or a pair of disjoint edges (the complement of a 4-cycle).[^o5]

## Relation to other graph families {#Relation_to_other_graph_families}

From the definition, split graphs are clearly closed under [complementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28graph_theory%29).[^o7] Another characterization of split graphs involves complementation: they are [chordal graphs](wikipedia://en/744165) the [complements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complement_%28graph_theory%29) of which are also chordal.[^o8] Just as chordal graphs are the [intersection graphs](wikipedia://en/7726759) of subtrees of trees, split graphs are the intersection graphs of distinct substars of [star graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_graph).[^o9] [Almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) chordal graphs are split graphs; that is, in the limit as *n* goes to infinity, the fraction of *n*-vertex chordal graphs that are split approaches one.[^o10]

Because chordal graphs are [perfect](wikipedia://en/670531), so are the split graphs. The **double split graphs**, a family of graphs derived from split graphs by doubling every vertex (so the clique comes to induce an antimatching and the independent set comes to induce a matching), figure prominently as one of five basic classes of perfect graphs from which all others can be formed in the proof by (Chudnovsky Robertson Seymour Thomas 2006) of the [Strong Perfect Graph Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_Perfect_Graph_Theorem).

If a graph is both a split graph and an [interval graph](wikipedia://en/363423), then its complement is both a split graph and a [comparability graph](wikipedia://en/6978672), and vice versa. The split comparability graphs, and therefore also the split interval graphs, can be characterized in terms of a set of three forbidden induced subgraphs.[^o12] The split [cographs](wikipedia://en/1175666) are exactly the [threshold graphs](wikipedia://en/11503485). The split [permutation graphs](wikipedia://en/17625375) are exactly the interval graphs that have interval graph complements;[^o13] these are the permutation graphs of [skew-merged permutations](wikipedia://en/57781155).[^n1] Split graphs have [cochromatic number](wikipedia://en/702837) 2.

## Algorithmic problems {#Algorithmic_problems}

Let $G$ be a split graph, partitioned into a clique $C$ and an independent set $i$. Then every [maximal clique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_clique) in a split graph is either $C$ itself, or the [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28graph_theory%29) of a vertex in $i$. Thus, it is easy to identify the maximum clique, and complementarily the [maximum independent set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_independent_set) in a split graph. In any split graph, one of the following three possibilities must be true:[^o16]

1. There exists a vertex $x$ in $i$ such that $C \cup \{x\}$ is complete. In this case, $C \cup \{x\}$ is a maximum clique and $i$ is a maximum independent set.
1. There exists a vertex $x$ in $C$ such that $i \cup \{x\}$ is independent. In this case, $i \cup \{x\}$ is a maximum independent set and $C$ is a maximum clique.
1. $C$ is a maximal clique and $i$ is a maximal independent set. In this case, $G$ has a unique partition $(C, i)$ into a clique and an independent set, $C$ is the maximum clique, and $i$ is the maximum independent set.

Some other optimization problems that are [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) on more general graph families, including [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/426743), are similarly straightforward on split graphs. Finding a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) remains [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) even for split graphs which are [strongly chordal graphs](wikipedia://en/24809733).[^o17] It is also well known that the Minimum Dominating Set problem remains [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) for split graphs.[^o18]

## Degree sequences {#Degree_sequences}

One remarkable property of split graphs is that they can be recognized solely from their [degree sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_sequence). Let the degree sequence of a graph $G$ be $d_{1} \ge d_{2} \ge \ldots \ge d_{n}$, and let $m$ be the largest value of $i$ such that $d_{i} \ge i - 1$. Then $G$ is a split graph if and only if

$$\sum_{i=1}^m d_i = m(m-1) + \sum_{i=m+1}^n d_i.$$

If this is the case, then the $m$ vertices with the largest degrees form a maximum clique in $G$, and the remaining vertices constitute an independent set.[^o20]

The [splittance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Splittance) of an arbitrary graph measures the extent to which this inequality fails to be true. If a graph is not a split graph, then the smallest sequence of edge insertions and removals that make it into a split graph can be obtained by adding all missing edges between the $m$ vertices with the largest degrees, and removing all edges between pairs of the remaining vertices; the splittance counts the number of operations in this sequence.[^n2]

## Counting split graphs {#Counting_split_graphs}

(Royle 2000) showed that ([unlabeled](wikipedia://en/1609861)) *n*-vertex split graphs are in [one-to-one correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_correspondence) with certain [Sperner families](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sperner_families). Using this fact, he determined a formula for the number of [nonisomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) split graphs on *n* vertices. For small values of *n*, starting from *n* = 1, these numbers are

1, 2, 4, 9, 21, 56, 164, 557, 2223, 10766, 64956, 501696, ... .

This [enumerative](wikipedia://en/9982439) result was also proved earlier by (Tyshkevich Chernyak 1990).
