# 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯

*Image: Sum1111Asymptote.svg.* Asymptotic behavior of the smoothing. The *y*-intercept of the line is −$\tfrac{1}{2}$.[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯**, also written $\textstyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} n^0$, $\textstyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1^n$, or simply $\textstyle \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1$, is a [divergent series](wikipedia://en/876428). Nevertheless, it is sometimes imputed to have a value of $-\tfrac{1}{2}$, especially in physics. This value can be justified by certain mathematical methods for obtaining values from divergent series, including [zeta function regularization](wikipedia://en/3014017).

:::

## As a divergent series {#As_a_divergent_series}

1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯ is a divergent series, meaning that its sequence of [partial sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum) does not converge to a [limit](wikipedia://en/285773) in the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).

The sequence 1<sup>$n$</sup> can be thought of as a [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) with the [common ratio](wikipedia://en/12630#Common_ratio_r) 1. For some other divergent geometric series, including [Grandi's series](wikipedia://en/7830441) with ratio −1, and the series 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ⋯ with ratio 2, one can use the general solution for the sum of a geometric series with base 1 and ratio $r$, obtaining $\tfrac{1}{1-r}$, but this summation method fails for 1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯, producing a [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663).

Together with Grandi's series, this is one of two geometric series with [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) ratio that diverges both for the real numbers and for all systems of [$p$-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423).

In the context of the [extended real number line](wikipedia://en/51698)

$$\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} 1 = +\infty \, ,$$

since its sequence of partial sums increases [monotonically](wikipedia://en/48260) without bound.

## Zeta function regularization {#Zeta_function_regularization}

Where the sum of $n^{0}$ occurs in [physical](wikipedia://en/19594028) applications, it may sometimes be interpreted by [zeta function regularization](wikipedia://en/3014017), as the value at $s = 0$ of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809):

$$\zeta(s)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n^s}=\frac{1}{1-2^{1-s}}\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{(-1)^{n+1}}{n^s}\,.$$

The two formulas given above are not valid at zero however, but the [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) is

$$\zeta(s) = 2^s\pi^{s-1}\ \sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\ \Gamma(1-s)\ \zeta(1-s)$$

Using this one gets (given that $\Gamma(1) = 1$),

$$\zeta(0) = \frac{1}{\pi} \lim_{s \rightarrow 0} \ \sin\left(\frac{\pi s}{2}\right)\ \zeta(1-s) = \frac{1}{\pi} \lim_{s \rightarrow 0} \ \left( \frac{\pi s}{2} - \frac{\pi^3 s^3}{48} + ... \right)\ \left( -\frac{1}{s} + ... \right) = -\frac{1}{2}$$

where the power series expansion for $\zeta(s)$ about $s = 1$ follows because $\zeta(s)$ has a simple pole of [residue](wikipedia://en/73102) one there. In this sense, $1 + 1 + 1 + 1 + \cdots = \zeta(0) = -\tfrac{1}{2}$.

[Emilio Elizalde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emilio_Elizalde) presents a comment from others about the series, suggesting the centrality of the zeta function regularization of this series in physics: In a short period of less than a year, two distinguished physicists, [A. Slavnov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Slavnov) and [F. Yndurain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_José_Ynduráin), gave seminars in Barcelona, about different subjects. It was remarkable that, in both presentations, at some point the speaker addressed the audience with these words: **As everybody knows*, 1 + 1 + 1 + ⋯ = −$\tfrac{1}{2}$.' Implying maybe:*If you do not know this, it is no use to continue listening.*[^o5]***
