# Undefined (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Undefined"}

In mathematics, the term **undefined** refers to a [value](wikipedia://en/4095147), [function](wikipedia://en/185427), or other [expression](wikipedia://en/609125) that cannot be assigned a meaning within a specific [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102).[^o2]

:::

Attempting to assign or use an undefined value within a particular formal system, may produce [contradictory](wikipedia://en/75807) or meaningless results within that system. In practice, mathematicians may use the term *undefined* to warn that a particular calculation or property can produce mathematically [inconsistent](wikipedia://en/75802) results, and therefore, it should be avoided.[^o3] Caution must be taken to avoid the use of such undefined values in a deduction or proof.

Whether a particular function or value is undefined, depends on the rules of the formal system in which it is used. For example, the [imaginary number](wikipedia://en/14554) $\sqrt{-1}$ is undefined within the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). So it is meaningless to reason about the value, solely within the [discourse](wikipedia://en/229180) of real numbers. However, defining the imaginary number $i$ to be equal to $\sqrt{-1}$, allows there to be a [consistent](wikipedia://en/75802) set of mathematics referred to as the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) plane. Therefore, within the [discourse](wikipedia://en/229180) of complex numbers, $\sqrt{-1}$ is in fact defined.

Many new fields of mathematics have been created, by taking previously undefined functions and values, and assigning them new meanings.[^o4] Most mathematicians generally consider these innovations significant, to the extent that they are both internally [consistent](wikipedia://en/75802) and practically useful. For example, [Ramanujan summation](wikipedia://en/9052595) may seem unintuitive, as it works upon [divergent series](wikipedia://en/876428) that assign finite values to apparently infinite sums such as [1 + 2 + 3 + 4 + ⋯](wikipedia://en/9723822). However, Ramanujan summation is useful for modelling a number of real-world phenomena, including the [Casimir effect](wikipedia://en/7555) and [bosonic string theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosonic_string_theory).

A [function](wikipedia://en/185427) may be said to be undefined, outside of its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263). As one example, $f(x)=\frac{1}{x}$ is undefined when $x=0$. As [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663) is undefined in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), $x=0$ is not part of the domain of $f(x)$.

## Other shades of meaning {#Other_shades_of_meaning}

In some mathematical contexts, undefined can refer to a [primitive notion](wikipedia://en/23706) which is not defined in terms of simpler concepts.[^o6] For example, in [Elements](wikipedia://en/244107), [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) defines a point merely as "that of which there is no part", and a line merely as "length without breadth".[^o7] Although these terms are not further defined, Euclid uses them to construct more complex geometric concepts.[^o8]

Contrast also the term *[undefined behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undefined_behavior)* in computer science, in which the term indicates that a function may produce or return *any* result, which may or may not be correct.

## Common examples of undefined expressions {#Common_examples_of_undefined_expressions}

Many fields of mathematics refer to various kinds of expressions as undefined. Therefore, the following examples of undefined expressions are not exhaustive.

### Division by zero {#Division_by_zero}

In [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), and therefore [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663) is undefined.[^o11] Use of a division by zero in an arithmetical calculation or proof, can produce absurd or meaningless results.

Assuming that division by zero exists, can produce [inconsistent](wikipedia://en/75802) logical results, such as the following fallacious "proof" that one is equal to two[^o12]:

*Incorrect "proof" that $1=2$*

| $x$ | $= y$ | Define $x$ as equal to $y$ |
|---|---|---|
| $x^2$ | $= xy$ | Multiply both sides of equation by $x$ |
| $x^2 - y^2$ | $= xy - y^2$ | Subtract $y^2$ from both sides |
| $(x+y)(x-y)$ | $= y(x-y)$ | Factor both sides of equation |
| $x+y$ | $=y$ | Divide both sides of equation by $x-y$ |
| $2y$ | $=y$ | Replace $x$ with $y$, because we know that $x=y$ |
| $2$ | $=1$ | Divide both sides by $y$ |

The above "proof" is not meaningful. Since we know that $x=y$, if we divide both sides of the equation by $x-y$, we divide both sides of the equation by zero. This operation is undefined in arithmetic, and therefore deductions based on division by zero can be contradictory.

If we assume that a non-zero answer $n$ exists, when some number $k \mid k \neq 0$ is divided by zero, then that would imply that $k = n \times 0$. But there is no number, which when multiplied by zero, produces a number that is not zero. Therefore, our assumption is incorrect.[^o11]

### Zero to the power of zero {#Zero_to_the_power_of_zero}

Depending on the particular context, mathematicians may refer to [zero to the power of zero](wikipedia://en/32130416) as undefined,[^o14] indefinite,[^o15] or equal to 1.[^o16] Controversy exists as to which definitions are mathematically rigorous, and under what conditions.[^o17][^o18]

### The square root of a negative number {#The_square_root_of_a_negative_number}

When restricted to the field of real numbers, the square root of a negative number is undefined, as no real number exists which, when squared, equals a negative number. Mathematicians, including [Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145), [John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290), [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), and [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125), explored formal definitions for the square roots of negative numbers, giving rise to the field of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759).[^o20]

### In trigonometry {#In_trigonometry}

In trigonometry, for all $n \in \mathbb{Z}$, the functions $\tan \theta$ and $\sec \theta$ are undefined for $\theta = \pi \left(n - \frac{1}{2}\right)$, while the functions $\cot \theta$ and $\csc \theta$ are undefined for all $\theta = \pi n$. This is a consequence of the [identities](wikipedia://en/25804104) of these functions, which would imply a [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663) at those points.[^o22]

Also, $\arcsin k$ and $\arccos k$ are both undefined when $k > 1$ or $k<-1$, because the range of the $\sin$ and $\cos$ functions is between $-1$ and $1$ inclusive.

### In complex analysis {#In_complex_analysis}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a point $z$ on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) where a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) is undefined, is called a [singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity). Some different types of singularities include:

- [Removable singularities](wikipedia://en/81644) - in which the function can be extended holomorphically to $z$
- [Poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) - in which the function can be extended [meromorphically](wikipedia://en/80857) to $z$
- [Essential singularities](wikipedia://en/80862) - in which no meromorphic extension to $z$ can exist

## Related terms {#Related_terms}

### Indeterminate {#Indeterminate}

The term *undefined* should be contrasted with the term *[indeterminate](wikipedia://en/366723)*. In the first case, undefined generally indicates that a value or property can have *no* meaningful definition. In the second case, indeterminate generally indicates that a value or property can have *many* meaningful definitions. Additionally, it seems to be generally accepted that undefined values *may not* be safely used within a particular formal system, whereas indeterminate values *might* be, depending on the relevant rules of the particular formal system.[^o26]
