# Lamé's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lamé's theorem"}

**Lamé's Theorem** is the result of [Gabriel Lamé](wikipedia://en/52145)'s analysis of the complexity of the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377). Using [Fibonacci numbers](wikipedia://en/10918), he proved in 1844[^o2][^o3] that when looking for the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) (GCD) of two [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *a* and *b*, the algorithm finishes in at most 5*k* steps, where *k* is the number of digits (decimal) of *b*.[^o4][^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The number of division steps in the Euclidean algorithm with entries $u\,\!$ and $v\,\!$ is less than $5$ times the number of decimal digits of $\min(u,v)\,\!$.

:::

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Let $u>v$ be two positive integers. Applying to them the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) provides two sequences $(q_1,\ldots, q_{n})$ and $(v_2,\ldots, v_{n})$ of positive integers such that, setting $v_0=u,$ $v_1=v$ and $v_{n+1}=0,$ one has

$$v_{i-1}=q_iv_i +v_{i+1}$$

for $i=1,\ldots, n,$ and

$$u>v>v_2>\cdots >v_{n}>0.$$

The number $n$ is called the *number of steps* of the Euclidean algorithm, since it is the number of [Euclidean divisions](wikipedia://en/679987) that are performed.

The [Fibonacci numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_number) are defined by $F_0=0,$ $F_1=1,$ and

$$F_{n+1}=F_n+F_{n-1}$$

for $n>0.$

The above relations show that $v_{n}\ge 1=F_2,$ and $v_{n-1}\ge 2=F_3.$ By [induction](wikipedia://en/18881),

$$\begin{aligned} v_{n-i-1}&=q_{n-i}v_{n-i}+v_{n-i+1}\\ &\ge v_{n-i}+v_{n-i+1}\\ &\ge F_{i+2}+F_{i+1} =F_{i+3}. \end{aligned}$$

So, if the Euclidean algorithm requires $n$ steps, one has $v\ge F_{n+1}.$

One has $F_{k}\ge \varphi^{k-2}$ for every integer $k>2$, where $\varphi=\frac {1+\sqrt 5}2$ is the [Golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386). This can be proved by induction, starting with $F_2 =\varphi^0=1,$ $F_3=2> \varphi,$ and continuing by using that $\varphi^2=\varphi +1:$

$$\begin{aligned} F_{k+1}&=F_k+F_{k-1}\\ &\ge \varphi^{k-2}+\varphi^{k-3}\\ &=\varphi^{k-3}(1+\varphi)\\ &=\varphi^{k-1}. \end{aligned}$$

So, if $n$ is the number of steps of the Euclidean algorithm, one has

$$v\ge \varphi^{n-1},$$

and thus

$$n-1 \le \frac {\log_{10} v}{\log_{10} \varphi}< 5\log_{10} v,$$

using $\frac 1{\log_{10} \varphi}<5.$

If $k$ is the number of [decimal digits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_digit) of $v$, one has $v<10^k$ and $\log_{10} v<k.$ So,

$$n-1<5k,$$

and, as both members of the inequality are integers,

$$n\le 5k,$$

which is exactly what Lamé's theorem asserts.

As a side result of this proof, one gets that the pairs of integers $(u,v)$ that give the maximum number of steps of the Euclidean algorithm (for a given size of $v$) are the pairs of consecutive Fibonacci numbers.

:::
