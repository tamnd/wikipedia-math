# Trace field of a representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trace field of a representation"}

In mathematics, the **trace field** of a [linear group](wikipedia://en/3983656) is the field generated by the [traces](wikipedia://en/43270) of its elements. It is mostly studied for [Kleinian](wikipedia://en/1362652) and [Fuchsian groups](wikipedia://en/471365), though related objects are used in the theory of [lattices](wikipedia://en/15782871) in Lie groups, often under the name *field of definition*.

:::

## Fuchsian and Kleinian groups {#Fuchsian_and_Kleinian_groups}

### Trace field and invariant trace fields for Fuchsian groups {#Trace_field_and_invariant_trace_fields_for_Fuchsian_groups}

Fuchsian groups are discrete subgroups of $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$. The trace of an element in $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb R)$ is well-defined up to sign (by taking the trace of an arbitrary preimage in $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)$) and the *trace field* of $\Gamma$ is the field generated over $\mathbb Q$ by the traces of all elements of $\Gamma$ (see for example in (Maclachlan Reid 2003)).

The *invariant trace field* is equal to the trace field of the subgroup $\Gamma^{(2)}$ generated by all squares of elements of $\Gamma$ (a finite-index subgroup of $\Gamma$).[^n1]

The invariant trace field of Fuchsian groups is stable under taking [commensurable](wikipedia://en/29343178) groups. This is not the case for the trace field;[^n2] in particular the trace field is in general different from the invariant trace field.

### Quaternion algebras for Fuchsian groups {#Quaternion_algebras_for_Fuchsian_groups}

Let $\Gamma$ be a Fuchsian group and $k$ its trace field. Let $A$ be the $k$-subalgebra of the matrix algebra $M_2(\mathbb R)$ generated by the preimages of elements of $\Gamma$. The algebra $A$ is then as simple as possible, more precisely:[^n3]

*If $\Gamma$ is of the [first or second type](wikipedia://en/471365#Limit_set) then $A$ is a [quaternion algebra](wikipedia://en/2776890) over $k$.*

The algebra $A$ is called the quaternion algebra of $\Gamma$. The quaternion algebra of $\Gamma^{(2)}$ is called the *invariant quaternion algebra* of $\Gamma$, denoted by $A\Gamma$. As for trace fields, the former is not the same for all groups in the same commensurability class but the latter is.

If $\Gamma$ is an [arithmetic Fuchsian group](wikipedia://en/50882022) then $k\Gamma$ and $A\Gamma$ together are a number field and quaternion algebra from which a group commensurable to $\Gamma$ may be derived.[^n4]

### Kleinian groups {#Kleinian_groups}

The theory for [Kleinian groups](wikipedia://en/1362652) (discrete subgroups of $\mathrm{PSL}_2(\mathbb C)$) is mostly similar as that for Fuchsian groups.[^n5] One big difference is that the trace field of a group of finite covolume is always a number field.[^n6]

## Trace fields and fields of definition for subgroups of Lie groups {#Trace_fields_and_fields_of_definition_for_subgroups_of_Lie_groups}

::: {#o15 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

When considering subgroups of general [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) (which are not necessarily defined as a [matrix groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_group)) one has to use a linear representation of the group to take traces of elements. The most natural one is the [adjoint representation](wikipedia://en/302188). It turns out that for applications it is better, even for groups which have a natural lower-dimensional linear representation (such as the [special linear groups](wikipedia://en/144823) $\mathrm{SL}_n(\mathbb R)$), to always define the trace field using the adjoint representation. Thus we have the following definition, originally due to [Ernest Vinberg](wikipedia://en/19177826),[^n7] who used the terminology "field of definition".[^n8]

*Let $G$ be a Lie group and $\Gamma \subset G$ a subgroup. Let $\rho$ be the adjoint representation of $G$. The trace field of $\Gamma$ is the field:*

$$k\Gamma = \mathbb Q(\{ \operatorname{trace}(\rho(\gamma)) : \gamma \in \Gamma \}).$$

If two [Zariski-dense](wikipedia://en/241744) subgroups of $G$ are commensurable then they have the same trace field in this sense.

:::

### The trace field for lattices {#The_trace_field_for_lattices}

Let $G$ be a [semisimple Lie group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_Lie_group) and $\Gamma \subset G$ a [lattice](wikipedia://en/15782871). Suppose further that either $\Gamma$ is irreducible and $G$ is not locally isomorphic to $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)$, or that $\Gamma$ has no factor [locally isomorphic](wikipedia://en/17945#The_Lie_algebra_associated_with_a_Lie_group) to $\mathrm{SL}_2(\mathbb R)$. Then [local rigidity](wikipedia://en/50722101) implies the following result.

*The field $k\Gamma$ is a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field).*

Furthermore, there exists an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) $\mathbf G$ over $k\Gamma$ such that the group of [real points](wikipedia://en/2960200#Rational_points_of_schemes) $\mathbf G(\mathbb R)$ is isomorphic to $\rho(G)$ and $\rho(\Gamma)$ is contained in a conjugate of $\mathbf G(k\Gamma)$.[^n7][^n9] Thus $k\Gamma$ is a "field of definition" for $\Gamma$ in the sense that it is a [field of definition](wikipedia://en/9213667) of its [Zariski closure](wikipedia://en/241744) in the adjoint representation.

In the case where $\Gamma$ is [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/630741) then it is commensurable to the arithmetic group defined by $\mathbf G$.

For Fuchsian groups the field $k\Gamma$ defined above is equal to its invariant trace field. For Kleinian groups they are the same if we use the adjoint representation over the complex numbers.[^n10]
