# Glossary of calculus

This **glossary of calculus** is a list of definitions about **[calculus](wikipedia://en/5176)**, its sub-disciplines, and related fields.

## A {#A}

::: {#o3 .entry cue=term name="Abel's test"}

**[Abel's test](wikipedia://en/7088921)**

A method of testing for the [convergence](wikipedia://en/1743431) of an [infinite series](wikipedia://en/15287).

:::

::: {#o4 .entry cue=term name="absolute convergence"}

**[absolute convergence](wikipedia://en/61532)**

An [infinite series](wikipedia://en/15287) of numbers is said to **converge absolutely** (or to be **absolutely convergent**) if the sum of the [absolute values](wikipedia://en/991) of the summands is finite. More precisely, a real or complex series $\textstyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n$ is said to **converge absolutely** if $\textstyle\sum_{n=0}^\infty \left|a_n\right| = L$ for some real number $\textstyle L$. Similarly, an [improper integral](wikipedia://en/346030) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427), $\textstyle\int_0^\infty f(x)\,dx$, is said to converge absolutely if the integral of the absolute value of the integrand is finite—that is, if $\textstyle\int_0^\infty \left|f(x)\right|dx = L.$

:::

::: {#o5 .entry cue=term name="absolute maximum"}

**[absolute maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima)**

The highest value a function attains.

:::

::: {#o6 .entry cue=term name="absolute minimum"}

**[absolute minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima)**

The lowest value a function attains.

:::

::: {#o7 .entry cue=term name="absolute value"}

**[absolute value](wikipedia://en/991)**

The **absolute value** or **modulus** $|x|$ of a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x$ is the [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) value of $x$ without regard to its [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270). Namely, $|x| = x$ for a [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) $x$, $|x| = -x$ for a [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) $x$ (in which case $-x$ is positive), and $|0| = 0$. For example, the absolute value of 3 is 3, and the absolute value of −3 is also 3. The absolute value of a number may be thought of as its [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) from zero.

:::

::: {#o8 .entry cue=term name="alternating series"}

**[alternating series](wikipedia://en/494308)**

An [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) whose terms alternate between positive and negative.

:::

::: {#o9 .entry cue=term name="alternating series test"}

**[alternating series test](wikipedia://en/1598759)**

Is the method used to prove that an [alternating series](wikipedia://en/494308) with terms that decrease in absolute value is a [convergent series](wikipedia://en/1743431). The test was used by [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) and is sometimes known as **Leibniz's test**, **Leibniz's rule**, or the **Leibniz criterion**.

:::

::: {#o10 .entry cue=term name="annulus"}

**[annulus](wikipedia://en/356158)**

A ring-shaped object, a region bounded by two [concentric circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric_circles).

:::

::: {#o11 .entry cue=term name="antiderivative"}

**[antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823)**

An **antiderivative**, **primitive function**, **primitive integral** or **indefinite integral** of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ is a differentiable function $F$ whose [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is equal to the original function $f$. This can be stated symbolically as $F' = f$.[^o12][^o13] The process of solving for antiderivatives is called **antidifferentiation** (or **indefinite integration**) and its opposite operation is called differentiation, which is the process of finding a derivative.

::: {#o16 .entry cue=term name="asymptote"}

**[asymptote](wikipedia://en/3107)**

In [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), an **asymptote** of a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) is a line such that the distance between the curve and the line approaches zero as one or both of the *x* or *y* coordinates [tends to infinity](wikipedia://en/285759#Limits_at_infinity). Some sources include the requirement that the curve may not cross the line infinitely often, but this is unusual for modern authors.[^o17] In [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) and related contexts, an asymptote of a curve is a line which is [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to the curve at a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139).[^o18][^o19]

:::

::: {#o20 .entry cue=term name="automatic differentiation"}

**[automatic differentiation](wikipedia://en/734787)**

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805), **automatic differentiation** (**AD**), also called **algorithmic differentiation** or **computational differentiation**,[^o21][^o22] is a set of techniques to numerically evaluate the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of a function specified by a computer program. AD exploits the fact that every computer program, no matter how complicated, executes a sequence of elementary arithmetic operations (addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, etc.) and elementary functions (exp, log, sin, cos, etc.). By applying the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) repeatedly to these operations, derivatives of arbitrary order can be computed automatically, accurately to working precision, and using at most a small constant factor more arithmetic operations than the original program.

## B {#B}

::: {#o25 .entry cue=term name="binomial coefficient"}

**[binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668)**

Any of the positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) that occurs as a [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) in the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) is a **binomial coefficient**. Commonly, a binomial coefficient is indexed by a pair of integers $n \ge k \ge 0$ and is written $\tbinom{n}{k}.$ It is the [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) of the $x^{k}$ term in the [polynomial expansion](wikipedia://en/4173350) of the [binomial](wikipedia://en/542399) [power](wikipedia://en/99491) $(1 + x)^{n}$, and it is given by the formula

$$\binom{n}{k} = \frac{n!}{k! (n-k)!}.$$

:::

::: {#o26 .entry cue=term name="binomial theorem (or binomial expansion)"}

**[binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677)**(or**[binomial expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_expansion))**

Describes the algebraic expansion of [powers](wikipedia://en/99491) of a [binomial](wikipedia://en/542399).

:::

::: {#o27 .entry cue=term name="bounded function"}

**[bounded function](wikipedia://en/311509)**

A [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f* defined on some [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *X* with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) values is called **bounded**, if the set of its values is [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258). In other words, [there exists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_exists) a real number *M* such that

$$|f(x)|\le M$$

[for all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_all) *x* in *X*. A function that is *not* bounded is said to be **unbounded**.

Sometimes, if *f*(*x*) ≤ *A* for all *x* in *X*, then the function is said to be **bounded above** by *A*. On the other hand, if *f*(*x*) ≥ *B* for all *x* in *X*, then the function is said to be **bounded below** by *B*.

## C {#C}

::: {#o30 .entry cue=term name="calculus"}

**[calculus](wikipedia://en/5176)**

(From [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *calculus*, literally 'small pebble', used for counting and calculations, as on an [abacus](wikipedia://en/655))[^o31] is the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) study of continuous change, in the same way that [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) is the study of shape and [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) is the study of generalizations of [arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operations).

:::

::: {#o32 .entry cue=term name="Cavalieri's principle"}

**[Cavalieri's principle](wikipedia://en/21697672)**

**Cavalieri's principle**, a modern implementation of the **method of indivisibles**, named after [Bonaventura Cavalieri](wikipedia://en/412561), is as follows:[^o33]

**2-dimensional case**: Suppose two regions in a plane are included between two parallel lines in that plane. If every line parallel to these two lines intersects both regions in line segments of equal length, then the two regions have equal areas.

**3-dimensional case**: Suppose two regions in three-space (solids) are included between two parallel planes. If every plane parallel to these two planes intersects both regions in [cross-sections](wikipedia://en/1337370) of equal area, then the two regions have equal volumes.

:::

::: {#o34 .entry cue=term name="chain rule"}

**[chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113)**

The **chain rule** is a [formula](wikipedia://en/164040) for computing the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of two or more [functions](wikipedia://en/185427). That is, if *f* and *g* are functions, then the chain rule expresses the derivative of their composition $f \circ g$ (the function which maps *x* to *f*(*g*(*x*)) ) in terms of the derivatives of *f* and *g* and the [product of functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointwise_product) as follows:

$$(f\circ g)'=(f'\circ g)\cdot g'.$$

This may equivalently be expressed in terms of the variable. Let $F = f \circ g$, or equivalently, $F(x) = f(g(x))$ for all *x*. Then one can also write

$$F'(x) = f'(g(x)) g'(x).$$

The chain rule may be written in [Leibniz's notation](wikipedia://en/410712) in the following way. If a variable *z* depends on the variable *y*, which itself depends on the variable *x*, so that *y* and *z* are therefore [dependent variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dependent_variable), then *z*, via the intermediate variable of *y*, depends on *x* as well. The chain rule then states,

$$\frac{dz}{dx} = \frac{dz}{dy} \cdot \frac{dy}{dx}.$$

The two versions of the chain rule are related; if $z=f(y)$ and $y=g(x)$, then

$$\frac{dz}{dx}=\frac{dz}{dy}\cdot\frac{dy}{dx} = f'(y)g'(x) = f'(g(x))g'(x).$$

In [integration](wikipedia://en/15532), the counterpart to the chain rule is the [substitution rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitution_rule).

::: {#o35 .entry cue=term name="change of variables"}

**[change of variables](wikipedia://en/2146848)**

Is a basic technique used to simplify problems in which the original [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) are replaced with [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) of other variables. The intent is that when expressed in new variables, the problem may become simpler, or equivalent to a better understood problem.

:::

::: {#o36 .entry cue=term name="cofunction"}

**[cofunction](wikipedia://en/3986044)**

A [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f* is **cofunction** of a function *g* if *f*(*A*) = *g*(*B*) whenever *A* and *B* are [complementary angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complementary_angles).[^o37] This definition typically applies to [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367).[^o38][^o39] The prefix "co-" can be found already in [Edmund Gunter](wikipedia://en/1399676)'s *Canon triangulorum* (1620).[^o40][^o41]

:::

::: {#o42 .entry cue=term name="concave function"}

**[concave function](wikipedia://en/263321)**

Is the [negative](wikipedia://en/228312) of a [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568). A concave function is also [synonymously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synonym) called **concave downwards**, **concave down**, **convex upwards**, **convex cap** or **upper convex**.

:::

::: {#o43 .entry cue=term name="constant of integration"}

**[constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939)**

The [indefinite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integral) of a given function (i.e., the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823) of the function) on a [connected domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_set) is only defined [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an additive constant, the **constant of integration**.[^o44][^o45] This constant expresses an ambiguity inherent in the construction of antiderivatives. If a function $f(x)$ is defined on an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) and $F(x)$ is an antiderivative of $f(x)$, then the set of *all* antiderivatives of $f(x)$ is given by the functions $F(x) + C$, where *C* is an arbitrary constant (meaning that *any* value for *C* makes $F(x) + C$ a valid antiderivative). The constant of integration is sometimes omitted in [lists of integrals](wikipedia://en/234855) for simplicity.

:::

::: {#o46 .entry cue=term name="continuous function"}

**[continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122)**

Is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) for which sufficiently small changes in the input result in arbitrarily small changes in the output. Otherwise, a function is said to be a *discontinuous* function. A continuous function with a continuous [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) is called a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660).

:::

::: {#o47 .entry cue=term name="continuously differentiable"}

**[continuously differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206#Differentiability_classes)**

A function *f* is said to be *continuously differentiable* if the derivative *f*(*x*) exists and is itself a continuous function.

::: {#o48 .entry cue=term name="contour integration"}

**[contour integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methods_of_contour_integration)**

In the mathematical field of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), **contour integration** is a method of evaluating certain [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) along paths in the complex plane.[^o49][^o50][^o51]

:::

::: {#o52 .entry cue=term name="convergence tests"}

**[convergence tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_test)**

Are methods of testing for the [convergence](wikipedia://en/1743431), [conditional convergence](wikipedia://en/1198956), [absolute convergence](wikipedia://en/61532), [interval of convergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interval_of_convergence) or divergence of an [infinite series](wikipedia://en/15287) $\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n$.

:::

::: {#o53 .entry cue=term name="convergent series"}

**[convergent series](wikipedia://en/1743431)**

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [series](wikipedia://en/15287) is the [sum](wikipedia://en/246160) of the terms of an [infinite sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence) of numbers.

Given an infinite sequence $\left ( a_1,\ a_2,\ a_3,\dots \right )$, the *n*th [partial sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum) $S_n$ is the sum of the first *n* terms of the sequence, that is,

$$S_n = \sum_{k=1}^n a_k.$$

A series is **convergent** if the sequence of its partial sums $\left \{ S_1,\ S_2,\ S_3,\dots \right \}$ tends to a [limit](wikipedia://en/285773); that means that the partial sums become closer and closer to a given number when the number of their terms increases. More precisely, a series converges, if there exists a number $\ell$ such that for any arbitrarily small positive number $\varepsilon$, there is a (sufficiently large) [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $N$ such that for all $n \ge \ N$,

$$\left | S_n - \ell \right \vert \le \ \varepsilon.$$

If the series is convergent, the number $\ell$ (necessarily unique) is called the **sum of the series**.

Any series that is not convergent is said to be [divergent](wikipedia://en/876428).

:::

::: {#o54 .entry cue=term name="convex function"}

**[convex function](wikipedia://en/245568)**

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [real-valued function](wikipedia://en/22284647) defined on an [*n*-dimensional interval](wikipedia://en/49172#Multi-dimensional_intervals) is called **convex** (or **convex downward** or **concave upward**) if the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) between any two points on the [graph of the function](wikipedia://en/87352) lies above or on the graph, in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) (or more generally a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370)) of at least two dimensions. Equivalently, a function is convex if its [epigraph](wikipedia://en/5744061) (the set of points on or above the graph of the function) is a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292). For a twice differentiable function of a single variable, if the second derivative is always greater than or equal to zero for its entire domain then the function is convex.[^o55] Well-known examples of convex functions include the [quadratic function](wikipedia://en/187240) $x^2$ and the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) $e^x$.

:::

::: {#o56 .entry cue=term name="Cramer's rule"}

**[Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008)**

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), **Cramer's rule** is an explicit formula for the solution of a [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087) with as many equations as unknowns, valid whenever the system has a unique solution. It expresses the solution in terms of the [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468) of the (square) coefficient [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) and of matrices obtained from it by replacing one column by the column vector of right-hand-sides of the equations. It is named after [Gabriel Cramer](wikipedia://en/499331) (1704–1752), who published the rule for an arbitrary number of unknowns in 1750,[^o57][^o58] although [Colin Maclaurin](wikipedia://en/7824) also published special cases of the rule in 1748[^o59] (and possibly knew of it as early as 1729).[^o60][^o61][^o62]

:::

::: {#o63 .entry cue=term name="critical point"}

**[critical point](wikipedia://en/1951424)**

A **critical point** or **stationary point** of a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) of a [real](wikipedia://en/1268958) or [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable) is any value in its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) where its [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is 0.[^o64][^o65]

:::

::: {#o66 .entry cue=term name="curve"}

**[curve](wikipedia://en/89246)**

A **curve** (also called a **curved line** in older texts) is, generally speaking, an object similar to a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) but that need not be [straight](wikipedia://en/91591).

:::

::: {#o67 .entry cue=term name="curve sketching"}

**[curve sketching](wikipedia://en/263323)**

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **curve sketching** (or **curve tracing**) includes techniques that can be used to produce a rough idea of overall shape of a [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) given its equation without computing the large numbers of points required for a detailed plot. It is an application of the theory of curves to find their main features. Here input is an equation. In [digital geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_geometry) it is a method of drawing a curve pixel by pixel. Here input is an array (digital image).

Is the highest degree of its [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) (individual terms) with non-zero coefficients. ::: {#o72 .entry cue=term name="derivative"}

**[derivative](wikipedia://en/7921)**

The **derivative** of a [function of a real variable](wikipedia://en/1268958) measures the sensitivity to change of the function value (output value) with respect to a change in its argument (input value). Derivatives are a fundamental tool of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176). For example, the derivative of the position of a moving object with respect to [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) is the object's [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903): this measures how quickly the position of the object changes when time advances.

:::

::: {#o73 .entry cue=term name="derivative test"}

**[derivative test](wikipedia://en/742352)**

A **derivative test** uses the [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of a function to locate the [critical points](wikipedia://en/885651) of a function and determine whether each point is a [local maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_maximum), a [local minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_minimum), or a [saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249). Derivative tests can also give information about the [concavity](wikipedia://en/263321) of a function.

:::

::: {#o74 .entry cue=term name="differentiable function"}

**[differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206)**

A **differentiable function** of one [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) variable is a function whose [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) exists at each point in its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263). As a result, the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of a differentiable function must have a (non-[vertical](wikipedia://en/5120956)) [tangent line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) at each point in its domain, be relatively smooth, and cannot contain any breaks, bends, or [cusps](wikipedia://en/6851758).

:::

::: {#o75 .entry cue=term name="differential (infinitesimal)"}

**[differential (infinitesimal)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28infinitesimal%29)**

The term **differential** is used in [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) to refer to an [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) (infinitely small) change in some [varying quantity](wikipedia://en/3728109). For example, if *x* is a [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109), then a change in the value of *x* is often denoted Δ*x* (pronounced *[delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_%28Greek%29) x*). The differential *dx* represents an infinitely small change in the variable *x*. The idea of an infinitely small or infinitely slow change is extremely useful intuitively, and there are a number of ways to make the notion mathematically precise.

Using calculus, it is possible to relate the infinitely small changes of various variables to each other mathematically using [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921). If *y* is a function of *x*, then the differential *dy* of *y* is related to *dx* by the formula

$$dy = \frac{dy}{dx} \,dx,$$

where *dy*/*dx* denotes the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of *y* with respect to *x*. This formula summarizes the intuitive idea that the derivative of *y* with respect to *x* is the limit of the ratio of differences Δ*y*/Δ*x* as Δ*x* becomes infinitesimal.

**[differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416)**

Is a subfield of calculus[^o77] concerned with the study of the rates at which quantities change. It is one of the two traditional divisions of calculus, the other being [integral calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_calculus), the study of the area beneath a curve.[^o78]

In applications, the functions usually represent physical quantities, the derivatives represent their rates of change, and the equation defines a relationship between the two.

::: {#o81 .entry cue=term name="differential of a function"}

**[differential of a function](wikipedia://en/23997203)**

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), the **differential** represents the [principal part](wikipedia://en/1225337#Calculus) of the change in a function *y* = *f*(*x*) with respect to changes in the independent variable. The differential *dy* is defined by

$$dy = f'(x)\,dx,$$

where $f'(x)$ is the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of *f* with respect to *x*, and *dx* is an additional real [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) (so that *dy* is a function of *x* and *dx*). The notation is such that the equation

$$dy = \frac{dy}{dx}\, dx$$

holds, where the derivative is represented in the [Leibniz notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_notation) *dy*/*dx*, and this is consistent with regarding the derivative as the quotient of the differentials. One also writes

$$df(x) = f'(x)\,dx.$$

The precise meaning of the variables *dy* and *dx* depends on the context of the application and the required level of mathematical rigor. The domain of these variables may take on a particular geometrical significance if the differential is regarded as a particular [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519), or analytical significance if the differential is regarded as a [linear approximation](wikipedia://en/1107596) to the increment of a function. Traditionally, the variables *dx* and *dy* are considered to be very small ([infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990)), and this interpretation is made rigorous in [non-standard analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_analysis).

::: {#o84 .entry cue=term name="Dirichlet's test"}

**[Dirichlet's test](wikipedia://en/3141677)**

Is a method of testing for the [convergence](wikipedia://en/1743431) of a [series](wikipedia://en/15287). It is named after its author [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002), and was published posthumously in the *[Journal de Mathématiques Pures et Appliquées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_de_Mathématiques_Pures_et_Appliquées)* in 1862.[^o85] The test states that if $\{a_n\}$ is a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) and $\{b_n\}$ a sequence of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) satisfying

$$a_{n+1} \le a_n$$

$$\lim_{n \rightarrow \infty}a_n = 0$$

$\left|\sum^{N}_{n=1}b_n\right|\leq M$ for every positive integer *N*

where *M* is some constant, then the series

$$\sum^{\infty}_{n=1}a_n b_n$$

converges.

**[essential discontinuity](wikipedia://en/2661487#Essential_discontinuity)**

For an essential discontinuity, only one of the two one-sided limits needs not exist or be infinite. Consider the function

In this case, $\scriptstyle L^{-}$ doesn't exist and $\scriptstyle L^{+}$ is infinite – thus satisfying twice the conditions of essential discontinuity. So *x*<sub>0</sub> is an *essential discontinuity*, *infinite discontinuity*, or *discontinuity of the second kind*. ::: {#o102 .entry cue=term name="extreme value theorem"}

**[extreme value theorem](wikipedia://en/276881)**

States that if a real-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f* is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) on the [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_interval#Classification_of_intervals) interval \[*a*,*b*\], then *f* must attain a [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum) and a [minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum), each at least once. That is, there exist numbers *c* and *d* in \[*a*,*b*\] such that:

$$f(c) \ge f(x) \ge f(d)\quad\text{for all }x\in [a,b].$$

A related theorem is **the boundedness theorem** which states that a continuous function *f* in the closed interval \[*a*,*b*\] is [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) on that interval. That is, there exist real numbers *m* and *M* such that:

$$m < f(x) < M\quad\text{for all }x \in [a,b].$$

The extreme value theorem enriches the boundedness theorem by saying that not only is the function bounded, but it also attains its least upper bound as its maximum and its greatest lower bound as its minimum.

::: {#o108 .entry cue=term name="Faà di Bruno's formula"}

**[Faà di Bruno's formula](wikipedia://en/425943)**

Is an identity in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) generalizing the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) to higher derivatives, named after (txt), though he was not the first to state or prove the formula. In 1800, more than 50 years before Faà di Bruno, the French mathematician [Louis François Antoine Arbogast](wikipedia://en/91156) stated the formula in a calculus textbook,[^o110] considered the first published reference on the subject.[^o111]

Perhaps the most well-known form of Faà di Bruno's formula says that

$${d^n \over dx^n} f(g(x))=\sum \frac{n!}{m_1!\,1!^{m_1}\,m_2!\,2!^{m_2}\,\cdots\,m_n!\,n!^{m_n}}\cdot f^{(m_1+\cdots+m_n)}(g(x))\cdot \prod_{j=1}^n\left(g^{(j)}(x)\right)^{m_j},$$

where the sum is over all *n*-[tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) of nonnegative integers (*m*<sub>1</sub>, …, *m*<sub>*n*</sub>) satisfying the constraint

$$1\cdot m_1+2\cdot m_2+3\cdot m_3+\cdots+n\cdot m_n=n.$$

Sometimes, to give it a memorable pattern, it is written in a way in which the coefficients that have the combinatorial interpretation discussed below are less explicit:

$${d^n \over dx^n} f(g(x)) =\sum \frac{n!}{m_1!\,m_2!\,\cdots\,m_n!}\cdot f^{(m_1+\cdots+m_n)}(g(x))\cdot \prod_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{g^{(j)}(x)}{j!}\right)^{m_j}.$$

Combining the terms with the same value of *m*<sub>1</sub> + *m*<sub>2</sub> + ... + *m*<sub>*n*</sub> = *k* and noticing that *m*<sub>*j*</sub> has to be zero for *j* > *n* − *k* + 1 leads to a somewhat simpler formula expressed in terms of [Bell polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_polynomial) *B*<sub>*n*,*k*</sub>(*x*<sub>1</sub>,...,*x*<sub>*n*−*k*+1</sub>):

$${d^n \over dx^n} f(g(x)) = \sum_{k=1}^n f^{(k)}(g(x))\cdot B_{n,k}\left(g'(x),g''(x),\dots,g^{(n-k+1)}(x)\right).$$

::: {#o121 .entry cue=term name="fundamental theorem of calculus"}

**[fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696)**

The **fundamental theorem of calculus** is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) that links the concept of [differentiating](wikipedia://en/7921) a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) with the concept of [integrating](wikipedia://en/15532) a function. The first part of the theorem, sometimes called the **first fundamental theorem of calculus**, states that one of the [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823) (also called *indefinite integral*), say *F*, of some function *f* may be obtained as the integral of *f* with a variable bound of integration. This implies the existence of [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823) for [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122).[^o122] Conversely, the second part of the theorem, sometimes called the **second fundamental theorem of calculus**, states that the integral of a function *f* over some interval can be computed by using any one, say *F*, of its infinitely many [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823). This part of the theorem has key practical applications, because explicitly finding the antiderivative of a function by [symbolic integration](wikipedia://en/1652672) avoids [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089) to compute integrals. This provides generally a better numerical accuracy.

**[general Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/1116115)**

The **general Leibniz rule**,[^o125] named after [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281), generalizes the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) (which is also known as "Leibniz's rule"). It states that if $f$ and $g$ are $n$-times [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206), then the product $fg$ is also $n$-times differentiable and its $n$th derivative is given by

where ${n \choose k}={n!\over k! (n-k)!}$ is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668) and $f^{(0)}\equiv f.$

This can be proved by using the product rule and [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881).

**[harmonic progression](wikipedia://en/24307466)**

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **harmonic progression** (or **harmonic sequence**) is a progression formed by taking the reciprocals of an [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389). It is a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of the form

$$\frac{1}{a} ,\ \frac{1}{a+d}\ , \frac{1}{a+2d}\ , \frac{1}{a+3d}\ , \cdots, \frac{1}{a+kd},$$

where −a/*d* is not a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) and *k* **is** a natural number.

Equivalently, a sequence is a harmonic progression when each term is the [harmonic mean](wikipedia://en/14463) of the neighboring terms.

It is not possible for a harmonic progression (other than the trivial case where *a* = 1 and *k* = 0) to sum to an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563). The reason is that, necessarily, at least one denominator of the progression will be divisible by a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) that does not divide any other denominator.[^o139]

::: {#o140 .entry cue=term name="higher derivative"}

**[higher derivative](wikipedia://en/7921#Higher_derivatives)**

Let $f$ be a differentiable function, and let $f'$ be its derivative. The derivative of $f'$ (if it has one) is written $f''$ and is called the *[second derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219) of $f$*. Similarly, the derivative of the second derivative, if it exists, is written $f'''$ and is called the *[third derivative](wikipedia://en/9092737) of $f$*. Continuing this process, one can define, if it exists, the $n$th derivative as the derivative of the $(n-1)$th derivative. These repeated derivatives are called *higher-order derivatives*. The $n$th derivative is also called the **derivative of order $n$**.

::: {#o141 .entry cue=term name="homogeneous linear differential equation"}

**[homogeneous linear differential equation](wikipedia://en/6954092)**

A [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) can be **homogeneous** in either of two respects.

A [first order differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_order_differential_equation) is said to be homogeneous if it may be written

$$f(x,y)dy = g(x,y)dx,$$

where $f$ and $g$ are [homogeneous functions](wikipedia://en/622844) of the same degree of $x$ and $y$. In this case, the change of variable $y = ux$ leads to an equation of the form

$$\frac{dx}x = h(u)du,$$

which is easy to solve by [integration](wikipedia://en/15532) of the two members.

Otherwise, a differential equation is homogeneous if it is a homogeneous function of the unknown function and its derivatives. In the case of [linear differential equations](wikipedia://en/379868), this means that there are no constant terms. The solutions of any linear [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753) of any order may be deduced by integration from the solution of the homogeneous equation obtained by removing the constant term.

**[improper fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Improper_fraction)**

Common fractions can be classified as either proper or improper. When the numerator and the denominator are both positive, the fraction is called proper if the numerator is less than the denominator, and improper otherwise.[^o152] In general, a common fraction is said to be a proper fraction if the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the fraction is strictly less than one—that is, if the fraction is greater than −1 and less than 1.[^o154][^o155] It is said to be an improper fraction, or sometimes top-heavy fraction,[^o156] if the absolute value of the fraction is greater than or equal to 1. Examples of proper fractions are 2/3, –3/4, and 4/9; examples of improper fractions are 9/4, –4/3, and 3/3.

::: {#o157 .entry cue=term name="improper integral"}

**[improper integral](wikipedia://en/346030)**

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), an improper integral is the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) of a [definite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_integral) as an endpoint of the interval(s) of integration approaches either a specified [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438), $\infty$, $-\infty$, or in some instances as both endpoints approach limits. Such an integral is often written symbolically just like a standard definite integral, in some cases with *infinity* as a limit of integration.

Specifically, an improper integral is a limit of the form:

$$\lim_{b\to\infty} \int_a^bf(x)\, dx, \qquad \lim_{a\to -\infty} \int_a^bf(x)\, dx,$$

or

$$\lim_{c\to b^-} \int_a^cf(x)\, dx,\quad \lim_{c\to a^+} \int_c^bf(x)\, dx,$$

in which one takes a limit in one or the other (or sometimes both) endpoints (Apostol 1967, §10.23).

::: {#o161 .entry cue=term name="instantaneous velocity"}

**[instantaneous velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903#Instantaneous_velocity)**

If we consider $\boldsymbol{v}$ as velocity and $\boldsymbol{x}$ as the displacement (change in position) vector, then we can express the (instantaneous) velocity of a particle or object, at any particular time $t$, as the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the position with respect to time:

$$\boldsymbol{v} = \lim_{{\Delta t}\to 0} \frac{\Delta \boldsymbol{x}}{\Delta t} = \frac{d\boldsymbol{x}}{d\mathit{t}} .$$

From this derivative equation, in the one-dimensional case it can be seen that the area under a velocity vs. time ($\boldsymbol{v}$ vs. $t$ graph) is the displacement, $\boldsymbol{x}$. In calculus terms, the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the velocity function $\boldsymbol{v}(t)$ is the displacement function $\boldsymbol{x}(t)$. In the figure, this corresponds to the yellow area under the curve labeled $\boldsymbol{s}$ ($\boldsymbol{s}$ being an alternative notation for displacement).

$$\boldsymbol{x} = \int \boldsymbol{v} \ d\mathit{t} .$$

Since the derivative of the position with respect to time gives the change in position (in [metres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre)) divided by the change in time (in [seconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second)), velocity is measured in [metres per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre_per_second) (m/s). Although the concept of an instantaneous velocity might at first seem counter-intuitive, it may be thought of as the velocity that the object would continue to travel at if it stopped accelerating at that moment. .

::: {#o165 .entry cue=term name="integration by parts"}

**[integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252)**

In calculus, and more generally in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), **integration by parts** or **partial integration** is a process that finds the [integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_%28mathematics%29) of a [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of functions in terms of the integral of their derivative and antiderivative. It is frequently used to transform the antiderivative of a product of functions into an antiderivative for which a solution can be more easily found. The rule can be readily derived by integrating the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) of [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921).

If $u = u(x)$ and $du = u(x) dx$, while $v = v(x)$ and $dv = v(x) dx$, then integration by parts states that:

$$\begin{aligned} \int_a^b u(x) v'(x) \, dx &= \Big[u(x) v(x)\Big]_a^b - \int_a^b u'(x) v(x) \, dx\\ &= u(b) v(b) - u(a) v(a) - \int_a^b u'(x) v(x) \, dx \end{aligned}$$

or more compactly:

$$\int u \, dv = uv - \int v \, du.$$

Mathematician [Brook Taylor](wikipedia://en/210429) discovered integration by parts, first publishing the idea in [1715](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1715_in_science).[^o166][^o167] More general formulations of integration by parts exist for the [Riemann–Stieltjes](wikipedia://en/330491#Properties_and_relation_to_the_Riemann_integral) and [Lebesgue–Stieltjes integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue–Stieltjes_integral#Integration_by_parts). The discrete analogue for sequences is called [summation by parts](wikipedia://en/246188). .

If a continuous function has values of opposite sign inside an interval, then it has a root in that interval (**Bolzano's theorem**).

The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a continuous function over an interval is itself an interval. .

::: {#o182 .entry cue=term name="jump discontinuity"}

**[jump discontinuity](wikipedia://en/2661487#Jump_discontinuity)**

Consider the function

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} x^2 & \mbox{ for } x < 1 \\ 0 & \mbox{ for } x = 1 \\ 2 - (x-1)^2 & \mbox{ for } x > 1 \end{cases}$$

Then, the point $x_{0}$ = 1 is a *jump discontinuity*.

In this case, a single limit does not exist because the one-sided limits, $L^{-}$ and $L^{+}$, exist and are finite, but are not equal: since, $L^{-}$ ≠ $L^{+}$, the limit $L$ does not exist. Then, $x_{0}$ is called a *jump discontinuity*, *step discontinuity*, or *discontinuity of the first kind*. For this type of discontinuity, the function $f$ may have any value at $x_{0}$.

::: {#o185 .entry cue=term name="L'Hôpital's rule"}

**[L'Hôpital's rule](wikipedia://en/18531)**

**L'Hôpital's rule** or **L'Hospital's rule** uses [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) to help evaluate [limits](wikipedia://en/285759) involving [indeterminate forms](wikipedia://en/366723). Application (or repeated application) of the rule often converts an indeterminate form to an expression that can be evaluated by substitution, allowing easier evaluation of the limit. The rule is named after the 17th-century [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Guillaume de l'Hôpital](wikipedia://en/1728613). Although the contribution of the rule is often attributed to L'Hôpital, the theorem was first introduced to L'Hôpital in 1694 by the Swiss mathematician [Johann Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/268886).

L'Hôpital's rule states that for functions $f$ and $g$ which are [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) on an open [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $I$ except possibly at a point $c$ contained in $I$, if $\lim_{x\to c}f(x)=\lim_{x\to c}g(x)=0 \text{ or } \pm\infty,$ $g'(x)\ne 0$ for all $x$ in $I$ with $x \ne c$, and $\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}$ exists, then

$$\lim_{x\to c}\frac{f(x)}{g(x)} = \lim_{x\to c}\frac{f'(x)}{g'(x)}.$$

The differentiation of the numerator and denominator often simplifies the quotient or converts it to a limit that can be evaluated directly.

## Q {#Q}

::: {#o233 .entry cue=term name="quadratic function"}

**[quadratic function](wikipedia://en/187240)**

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), a **quadratic function**, a **quadratic polynomial**, a **polynomial of degree 2**, or simply a **quadratic**, is a [polynomial function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function) with one or more variables in which the highest-degree term is of the second degree. For example, a quadratic function in three variables *x*, *y,* and *z* contains exclusively terms *x*<sup>2</sup>, *y*<sup>2</sup>, *z*<sup>2</sup>, *xy*, *xz*, *yz*, *x*, *y*, *z*, and a constant:

$$f(x,y,z)=ax^2+by^2+cz^2+dxy+exz+fyz+gx+hy+iz +j,$$

with at least one of the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) *a, b, c, d, e,* or *f* of the second-degree terms being non-zero.

A *univariate* (single-variable) quadratic function has the form[^o234]

$$f(x)=ax^2+bx+c,\quad a \ne 0$$

in the single variable *x*. The [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of a univariate quadratic function is a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) whose axis of symmetry is parallel to the $y$-axis, as shown at right.

If the quadratic function is set equal to zero, then the result is a [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175). The solutions to the univariate equation are called the [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of the univariate function.

The bivariate case in terms of variables *x* and *y* has the form

$$f(x,y) = a x^2 + by^2 + cx y+ d x+ ey + f \,\!$$

with at least one of *a, b, c* not equal to zero, and an equation setting this function equal to zero gives rise to a [conic section](wikipedia://en/19008673) (a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) or other [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277), a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231), or a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052)).

In general there can be an arbitrarily large number of variables, in which case the resulting [surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_%28geometry%29) is called a [quadric](wikipedia://en/145570), but the highest degree term must be of degree 2, such as *x*<sup>2</sup>, *xy*, *yz*, etc.
