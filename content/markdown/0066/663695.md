# Engineered language

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Engineered language"}

**Engineered languages** (often abbreviated to **engelangs**, or, less commonly, **engilangs**) are [constructed languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructed_languages) devised to test or prove some hypotheses about how languages work or might work. There are at least three subcategories, [philosophical languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_languages) (or ideal languages), logical languages (sometimes abbreviated as *loglangs*), and [experimental languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_language). Raymond Brown describes engineered languages as "languages that are designed to specified *objective* criteria, and modeled to meet those criteria".[^o2]

:::

Some engineered languages have been considered candidate global [auxiliary languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_auxiliary_language), and some languages intended as international auxiliary languages have certain "engineered" aspects (in which they are more regular and systematic than their natural language sources).

## Logical languages {#Logical_languages}

Logical languages are meant to allow (or enforce) syntactically unambiguous statements. They are typically based on [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic) but can also be based on any system of formal [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065). The two best-known logical languages are the predicate languages [Loglan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loglan) and its successor [Lojban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lojban). They both aim to eliminate [syntactical ambiguity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactical_ambiguity) and reduce [semantic ambiguity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_ambiguity) to a minimum. In particular, the [grammar of Lojban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lojban_grammar) is carefully engineered to express such predicate logic in an unambiguous manner. [Toaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toaq) and [Eberban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eberban) are also logical languages.

## Philosophical languages {#Philosophical_languages}

Philosophical languages are designed to reflect some aspect of philosophy, particularly with respect to the nature or potential of any given language. [John Wilkins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilkins)' [Real Character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/An_Essay_towards_a_Real_Character_and_a_Philosophical_Language) and Edward Powell Foster's [Ro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ro_%28language%29) constructed their words using a taxonomic tree. Vocabularies of [oligosynthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligosynthetic) languages, for example [aUI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AUI_%28constructed_language%29)[^o5] and Ygyde,[^o6][^o7] are made of [compound words](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_word), which are coined from a small (theoretically minimal) set of [morphemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morpheme). Sonja Lang's [Toki Pona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toki_Pona) is based on [minimalistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimalism) [simplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voluntary_simplicity).

## Experimental languages {#Experimental_languages}

An experimental language is a [constructed language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructed_language) designed for the purpose of exploring some element in the theory of [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics). Most such languages are concerned with the relation between [language and thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language_and_thought); however, languages have been constructed to explore other aspects of language as well.

In [science fiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_fiction), much work has been done on the assumption popularly known as the [Sapir–Whorf hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapir–Whorf_hypothesis). [Suzette Haden Elgin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suzette_Haden_Elgin)'s [Láadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Láadan) is designed to lexicalize and grammaticalize the concepts and distinctions important to women, based on [muted group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muted_group_theory).

[Story of Your Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Story_of_Your_Life) is a short story by [Ted Chiang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ted_Chiang) that heavily features a written-only non-linear language (text in it has no discernible beginning or end) in the form of its [Heptapod B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heptapod_languages#Heptapod_language_in_"Story_of_Your_Life"), though the language is only described vaguely and never actually shown. This story was adapted into the movie [Arrival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arrival_%28film%29), though its version of [Heptapod B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heptapod_languages#Heptapod_language_in_Arrival) is only non-linear to a certain extent.
