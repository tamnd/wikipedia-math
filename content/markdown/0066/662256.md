# Von Neumann conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Von Neumann conjecture"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **von Neumann conjecture** stated that a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* is non-[amenable](wikipedia://en/657430) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *G* contains a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) that is a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) on two [generators](wikipedia://en/99945). The [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) was disproved in 1980.

:::

In 1929, during his work on the [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220), [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) defined the concept of [amenable groups](wikipedia://en/657430) and showed that no amenable group contains a free subgroup of [rank](wikipedia://en/59735) 2. The suggestion that the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) might hold, that is, that every non-amenable group contains a free subgroup on two generators, was made by a number of different authors in the 1950s and 1960s. Although von Neumann's name is popularly attached to the conjecture, its first written appearance seems to be due to [Mahlon Marsh Day](wikipedia://en/71432032) in 1957.

The [Tits alternative](wikipedia://en/12704641) is a fundamental [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) which, in particular, establishes the conjecture within the class of [linear groups](wikipedia://en/3983656).

The historically first potential [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) is [Thompson group *F*](wikipedia://en/612245). While its amenability is a wide-[open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335), the general conjecture was shown to be false in 1980 by [Alexander Ol'shanskii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Ol'shanskii); he demonstrated that [Tarski monster groups](wikipedia://en/727196), constructed by him, which are easily seen not to have free subgroups of rank 2, are not amenable. Two years later, [Sergei Adian](wikipedia://en/5028401) showed that certain [Burnside groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnside_group) are also counterexamples. None of these counterexamples are [finitely presented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group), and for some years it was considered possible that the conjecture held for finitely presented groups. However, in 2003, Alexander Ol'shanskii and [Mark Sapir](wikipedia://en/58965999) exhibited a collection of finitely presented groups which do not satisfy the conjecture.

In 2013, [Nicolas Monod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Monod) found an easy counterexample to the conjecture. Given by piecewise projective [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660) of the line, the group is remarkably simple to understand. Even though it is not amenable, it shares many known properties of amenable groups in a straightforward way. In 2013, Yash Lodha and [Justin Tatch Moore](wikipedia://en/56288965) isolated a finitely presented non-amenable subgroup of Monod's group. This provides the first [torsion-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion-free_group) finitely presented counterexample, and admits a [presentation](wikipedia://en/99494) with 3 generators and 9 relations. Lodha later showed that this group satisfies the [property $F_{\infty}$](wikipedia://en/60316348), which is a stronger finiteness property.
