# Cubic crystal system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cubic crystal system"}

*Image: Pyrite\_Cubes.JPG.* A rock containing three crystals of [pyrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrite) (FeS<sub>2</sub>). The crystal structure of pyrite is primitive cubic, and this is reflected in the cubic symmetry of its natural [crystal facets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facet).

*Image: Kubisches\_Kristallsystem.jpg.* A network model of a primitive cubic system

*Image: FCC\_primative-cubic\_cells.svg.* The primitive and face-centered cubic (also known as cubic close-packed) unit cells

In [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography), the **cubic** (or **isometric**) **crystal system** is a [crystal system](wikipedia://en/456410) where the [unit cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure#Unit_cell) is in the shape of a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285). This is one of the most common and simplest shapes found in [crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal) and [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral).

:::

There are three main varieties of these crystals:

- **Primitive cubic** (abbreviated *cP* and alternatively called **simple cubic**)
- **Body-centered cubic** (abbreviated *cI* or **bcc**)
- **Face-centered cubic** (abbreviated *cF* or **fcc**)

Note: the term **fcc** is often used synonymously for the [*cubic close-packed*](wikipedia://en/901260) or **ccp** structure occurring in metals. However, **fcc** stands for a face-centered cubic Bravais lattice, which is not necessarily close-packed when a motif is set onto the lattice points. E.g. the diamond and the zincblende lattices are **fcc** but not close-packed. Each is subdivided into other variants listed below. Although the *unit cells* in these crystals are conventionally taken to be cubes, the [primitive unit cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_cell) often are not.

## Bravais lattices {#Bravais_lattices}

The primitive cubic lattice (cP) consists of one [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) point on each corner of the cube; this means each simple cubic unit cell has in total one [lattice point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_point). Each atom at a lattice point is then shared equally between eight adjacent cubes, and the unit cell therefore contains in total one atom ($\tfrac{1}{8}$ × 8) The associated tesselation is trivially the [cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3733318).[^o6]

The body-centered cubic lattice (cI) has one lattice point in the center of the unit cell in addition to the eight corner points. It has a net total of two lattice points per unit cell ($\tfrac{1}{8}$ × 8 + 1). The corresponding shape is the [rhombic dodecahedral honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombic_dodecahedral_honeycomb) or [hexakis cubic honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexakis_cubic_honeycomb).[^o6]

*Image: BCC primitive.jpg.* primitive cell of BCC lattice

The face-centered cubic lattice (cF) has lattice points on the faces of the cube that each give exactly one half contribution, in addition to the corner lattice points, giving a total of four lattice points per unit cell ($\tfrac{1}{8}$ × 8 from the corners plus $\tfrac{1}{2}$ × 6 from the faces). The corresponding tesselation, when lattice points are taken as vertices, is the [tetrahedral-octahedral honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3735260).

*Image: FCC translation.png.* primitive translations of FCC lattice

The face-centered cubic lattice is closely related to the [hexagonal close packed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_crystal_system) (hcp) system, where the two systems differ only in the relative placements of their hexagonal layers. The [\[111](wikipedia://en/1089079)\] plane of a face-centered cubic lattice is a hexagonal grid.

Attempting to create a base-centered cubic lattice (i.e., putting an extra lattice point in the center of each horizontal face) results in a simple [tetragonal](wikipedia://en/198204) [Bravais lattice](wikipedia://en/661808).

[Coordination number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_number) (CN) is the number of nearest neighbors of a central atom in the structure.[^o6] Each sphere in a cP lattice has coordination number 6, in a cI lattice 8, and in a cF lattice 12.

[Atomic packing factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_packing_factor) (APF) is the fraction of volume that is occupied by atoms. The cP lattice has an APF of about 0.524, the cI lattice an APF of about 0.680, and the cF lattice an APF of about 0.740.

## Single element structures {#Single_element_structures}

*Image: visualisation\_diamond\_cubic.svg.* Visualisation of a diamond cubic unit cell: 1. Components of a unit cell, 2. One unit cell, 3. A lattice of 3 x 3 x 3 unit cells

As a rule, since atoms in a solid attract each other, the more tightly packed arrangements of atoms tend to be more common. (Loosely packed arrangements do occur, though, for example if the [orbital hybridization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_hybridisation) demands certain [bond angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_geometry).) Accordingly, the primitive cubic structure, with especially low atomic packing factor, is rare in nature, but is found in [polonium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polonium).[^o14] The *bcc* and *fcc*, with their higher densities, are both quite common in nature. Examples of *bcc* include [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), [chromium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromium), [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten), and [niobium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niobium). Examples of *fcc* include [aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium), [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold) and [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver).

Another important cubic crystal structure is the [diamond cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cubic) structure, which can appear in [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon), [silicon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon), [germanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanium), and [tin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tin). Unlike fcc and bcc, this structure is not a lattice, since it contains multiple atoms in its [primitive cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_cell). Other cubic elemental structures include the [A15 structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A15_phases) found in [tungsten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tungsten), and the extremely complicated structure of [manganese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manganese).

## Multi-element structures {#Multi-element_structures}

Compounds that consist of more than one element (e.g. [binary compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_compound)) often have crystal structures based on the cubic crystal system. Some of the more common ones are listed here. These structures can be viewed as two or more interpenetrating sublattices where each sublattice occupies the [interstitial sites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstitial_site) of the others.

### Caesium chloride structure {#Caesium_chloride_structure}

Caesium chloride crystal structure

*Image: CsCl crystal.svg.* A [caesium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesium_chloride) unit cell. The two colors of spheres represent the two types of atoms.

One structure is the "interpenetrating primitive cubic" structure, also called a "caesium chloride" or B2 structure. This structure is often confused for a body-centered cubic structure because the arrangement of atoms is the same. However, the caesium chloride structure has a basis composed of two different atomic species. In a body-centered cubic structure, there would be translational symmetry along the \[111\] direction. In the caesium chloride structure, translation along the \[111\] direction results in a change of species. The structure can also be thought of as two separate simple cubic structures, one of each species, that are superimposed within each other. The corner of the chloride cube is the center of the caesium cube, and vice versa.[^o17]

*Image: Cesium\_Chloride.jpg.* This graphic shows the interlocking simple cubic lattices of cesium and chlorine. You can see them separately and as they are interlocked in what looks like a body-centered cubic arrangement

It works the same way for the NaCl structure described in the next section. If you take out the Cl atoms, the leftover Na atoms still form an FCC structure, not a simple cubic structure.

In the unit cell of CsCl, each ion is at the center of a cube of ions of the opposite kind, so the [coordination number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_number) is eight. The central [cation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cation) is coordinated to 8 anions on the corners of a cube as shown, and similarly, the central anion is coordinated to 8 cations on the corners of a cube. Alternately, one could view this lattice as a simple cubic structure with a secondary atom in its [cubic void](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstitial_site).

In addition to caesium chloride itself, the structure also appears in certain other [alkali halides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metal_halide) when prepared at low temperatures or high pressures.[^o18] Generally, this structure is more likely to be formed from two elements whose ions are of roughly the same size (for example, ionic radius of Cs<sup>\+</sup> = 167 pm, and Cl<sup>−</sup> = 181 pm).

The [space group](wikipedia://en/463721) of the [caesium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesium_chloride) (CsCl) structure is called Pm$\overline{3}$m (in [Hermann–Mauguin notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann–Mauguin_notation)), or "221" (in the International Tables for Crystallography). The [Strukturbericht designation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strukturbericht_designation) is "B2".[^o19]

There are nearly a hundred [rare earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rare-earth_element) [intermetallic compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intermetallic_compounds) that crystallize in the CsCl structure, including many [binary compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_phase) of rare earths with [magnesium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnesium),[^o20] and with elements in groups [11](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_11_element), [12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_12_element),[^o21][^o22] and [13](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_13_element). Other compounds showing caesium chloride like structure are [CsBr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesium_bromide), [CsI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesium_iodide), high-temperature [RbCl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubidium_chloride), AlCo, AgZn, BeCu, MgCe, RuAl and SrTl.

### Rock-salt structure {#Rock-salt_structure}

Rock salt crystal structure

*Image: NaCl octahedra in crystal.svg.* The rock-salt crystal structure. Each atom has six nearest neighbours, with [octahedral geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_geometry).

The [space group](wikipedia://en/463721) of the rock-salt or [halite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halite) (sodium chloride) structure is denoted as Fm$\overline{3}$m (in [Hermann–Mauguin notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann–Mauguin_notation)), or "225" (in the International Tables for Crystallography). The [Strukturbericht designation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strukturbericht_designation) is "B1".[^o25]

In the rock-salt structure, each of the two atom types forms a separate face-centered cubic lattice, with the two lattices interpenetrating so as to form a 3D checkerboard pattern. The rock-salt structure has [octahedral](wikipedia://en/80177635) [coordination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_number): Each atom's nearest neighbors consist of six atoms of the opposite type, positioned like the six vertices of a [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635#Regular_octahedron). In sodium chloride there is a 1:1 ratio of sodium to chlorine atoms. The structure can also be described as an FCC lattice of sodium with chlorine occupying each [octahedral void](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstitial_site) or vice versa.[^o17]

Examples of compounds with this structure include sodium chloride itself, along with almost all other alkali halides, and "many divalent metal oxides, sulfides, selenides, and tellurides".[^o18] According to the [radius ratio rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cation-anion_radius_ratio), this structure is more likely to be formed if the cation is somewhat smaller than the anion (a cation/anion radius ratio of 0.414 to 0.732).

The interatomic distance (distance between cation and anion, or half the unit cell length *a*) in some rock-salt-structure crystals are: 2.3 Å (2.3 × 10<sup>−10</sup> m) for NaF,[^o26] 2.8 Å for NaCl,[^o27] and 3.2 Å for SnTe.[^o28] Most of the [alkali metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metal) [hydrides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydride) and [halides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halide) have the rock-salt structure, though a few have the [caesium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caesium_chloride) structure instead.

### Fluorite structure {#Fluorite_structure}

Fluorite crystal structure Much like the rock-salt structure, the [fluorite structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorite_structure) (AB<sub>2</sub>) is also an Fm$\overline{3}$m structure but has 1:2 ratio of ions. The anti-fluorite structure is nearly identical, except the positions of the anions and cations are switched in the structure. They are designated [Wyckoff positions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyckoff_positions) 4a and 8c whereas the rock-salt structure positions are 4a and 4b.[^o67][^o68]

### Zincblende structure {#Zincblende_structure}

Zincblende crystal structure

*Image: Sphalerite-unit-cell-depth-fade-3D-balls.png.* A zincblende unit cell

The [space group](wikipedia://en/463721) of the Zincblende structure is called F$\overline{4}$3m (in [Hermann–Mauguin notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann–Mauguin_notation)), or 216.[^o71][^o72] The Strukturbericht designation is "B3".[^o73]

The Zincblende structure (also written "zinc blende") is named after the mineral zincblende ([sphalerite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphalerite)), one form of [zinc sulfide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zinc_sulfide) (β-ZnS). As in the rock-salt structure, the two atom types form two interpenetrating face-centered cubic lattices. However, it differs from rock-salt structure in how the two lattices are positioned relative to one another. The zincblende structure has [tetrahedral](wikipedia://en/30606) [coordination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_number): Each atom's nearest neighbors consist of four atoms of the opposite type, positioned like the four vertices of a [regular tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606#Regular_tetrahedron). In zinc sulfide the ratio of zinc to sulfur is 1:1.[^o17] Altogether, the arrangement of atoms in zincblende structure is the same as [diamond cubic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diamond_cubic) structure, but with alternating types of atoms at the different lattice sites. The structure can also be described as an FCC lattice of zinc with sulfur atoms occupying half of the [tetrahedral voids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstitial_site) or vice versa.[^o17]

Examples of compounds with this structure include zincblende itself, [lead(II) nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead%28II%29_nitrate), many compound semiconductors (such as [gallium arsenide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallium_arsenide) and [cadmium telluride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cadmium_telluride)), and a wide array of other binary compounds. The [boron group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boron_group) [pnictogenides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pnictogenide) usually have a zincblende structure, though the [nitrides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitride) are more common in the [wurtzite structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wurtzite_structure), and their zincblende forms are less well known [polymorphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymorphism_%28materials_science%29).[^o74][^o75]

### Heusler structure {#Heusler_structure}

The Heusler structure, based on the structure of Cu<sub>2</sub>MnAl, is a common structure for [ternary compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternary_compound) involving [transition metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_metals). It has the space group Fm$\overline{3}$m (No. 225), and the [Strukturbericht designation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strukturbericht_designation) is L2<sub>1</sub>. Together with the closely related half-Heusler and inverse-Huesler compounds, there are hundreds of examples.

### Iron monosilicide structure {#Iron_monosilicide_structure}

Iron monosilicide structure type

*Image: MnSi lattice.png.* Diagram of the [iron monosilicide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_monosilicide) structure.

The space group of the iron monosilicide structure is P2<sub>1</sub>3 (No. 198), and the [Strukturbericht designation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strukturbericht_designation) is B20. This is a [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160) structure, and is sometimes associated with [helimagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helimagnetic) properties. There are four atoms of each element for a total of eight atoms in the unit cell.

Examples occur among the transition metal silicides and germanides, as well as a few other compounds such as [gallium palladide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gallium_palladide).

## Weaire–Phelan structure {#Weaire–Phelan_structure}

*Image: 12-14-hedral\_honeycomb.png.* Weaire–Phelan structure

A [Weaire–Phelan structure](wikipedia://en/2795211) has Pm$\overline{3}$n (223) symmetry.

It has three orientations of stacked [tetradecahedrons](wikipedia://en/12786864) with [pyritohedral](wikipedia://en/8407#Pyritohedron) cells in the gaps. It is found as a [crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure) in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) where it is usually known as a "type I [clathrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clathrate_compound) structure". [Gas hydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clathrate_hydrate) formed by methane, propane, and carbon dioxide at low temperatures have a structure in which water molecules lie at the nodes of the Weaire–Phelan structure and are [hydrogen bonded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bond) together, and the larger gas molecules are trapped in the polyhedral cages.
