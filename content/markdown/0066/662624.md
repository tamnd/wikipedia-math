# Noncommutative topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Noncommutative topology"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **noncommutative topology** is a term used for the relationship between [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological) and [C\*-algebraic](wikipedia://en/7184) concepts. The term has its origins in the [Gelfand–Naimark theorem](wikipedia://en/315414), which implies the [duality](wikipedia://en/379051) of the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637) and the category of [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_operation) C\*-algebras. Noncommutative topology is related to analytic [noncommutative geometry](wikipedia://en/295917).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

The premise behind noncommutative topology is that a noncommutative C\*-algebra can be treated like the algebra of complex-valued [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) on a 'noncommutative space' which does not exist classically. Several topological properties can be formulated as properties of the C\*-algebras without making reference to commutativity or the underlying space, and so have an immediate generalization. Among these are:

- [compactness](wikipedia://en/6042) ([unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra))
- [σ-compactness](wikipedia://en/346681) ([σ-unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Σ-unital_algebra))
- [dimension](wikipedia://en/398886) ([real](wikipedia://en/48465743) or [stable rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_stable_rank))
- [connectedness](wikipedia://en/6233) ([projectionless](wikipedia://en/1087483))
- [extremally disconnected spaces](wikipedia://en/5783949) ([AW\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/39435930))

Individual elements of a commutative C\*-algebra correspond with continuous functions. And so certain types of functions can correspond to certain properties of a C\*-algebra. For example, [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint) elements of a commutative C\*-algebra correspond to real-valued continuous functions. Also, [projections](wikipedia://en/519182#Orthogonal_projections) (i.e. self-adjoint [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent)) correspond to [indicator functions](wikipedia://en/240790) of [clopen sets](wikipedia://en/233398).

Categorical constructions lead to some examples. For example, the [coproduct](wikipedia://en/310889) of spaces is the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/1564380) and thus corresponds to the [direct sum of algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_algebras), which is the [product](wikipedia://en/237868) of C\*-algebras. Similarly, [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) corresponds to the coproduct of C\*-algebras, the [tensor product of algebras](wikipedia://en/372395). In a more specialized setting, compactifications of topologies correspond to unitizations of algebras. So the [one-point compactification](wikipedia://en/55611) corresponds to the minimal unitization of C\*-algebras, the [Stone–Čech compactification](wikipedia://en/52897) corresponds to the [multiplier algebra](wikipedia://en/13542720), and [corona sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corona_set) correspond with [corona algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corona_algebra).

There are certain examples of properties where multiple generalizations are possible and it is not clear which is preferable. For example, [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437) can correspond either to [states](wikipedia://en/454959) or tracial states. Since all states are vacuously tracial states in the commutative case, it is not clear whether the tracial condition is necessary to be a useful generalization.

## K-theory {#K-theory}

One of the major examples of this idea is the generalization of [topological K-theory](wikipedia://en/2912468) to noncommutative C\*-algebras in the form of [operator K-theory](wikipedia://en/13314265).

A further development in this is a [bivariant](wikipedia://en/10987#Bifunctors) version of K-theory called [KK-theory](wikipedia://en/13314087), which has a composition product

$$KK(A,B)\times KK(B,C)\rightarrow KK(A,C)$$

of which the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) structure in ordinary K-theory is a special case. The product gives the structure of a [category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_%28topology%29) to KK. It has been related to [correspondences](wikipedia://en/57389807) of [algebraic varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_varieties).[^o5]
