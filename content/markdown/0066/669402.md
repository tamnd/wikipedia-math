# Zonohedron

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zonohedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **zonohedron** is a [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) that is [centrally symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_symmetry), every face of which is a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) that is centrally symmetric (a [zonogon](wikipedia://en/55312103)). Any zonohedron may equivalently be described as the [Minkowski sum](wikipedia://en/504105) of a set of line segments in three-dimensional space, or as a three-dimensional [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) of a [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783). Zonohedra were originally defined and studied by [E. S. Fedorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evgraf_Stepanovich_Fyodorov), a Russian [crystallographer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography). A zonohedron is three-dimensional; in any dimension, the Minkowski sum of line segments forms a [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885) known as a [zonotope](wikipedia://en/669406).

:::

## Tiling space {#Tiling_space}

The original motivation for studying zonohedra is that the [Voronoi diagram](wikipedia://en/177668) of any [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) forms a [convex uniform honeycomb](wikipedia://en/2961) in which the cells are zonohedra. Any zonohedron formed in this way can [tessellate](wikipedia://en/6008654) 3-dimensional space and is called a [primary parallelohedron](wikipedia://en/25207334). Each primary parallelohedron is combinatorially equivalent to one of five types: the [rhombohedron](wikipedia://en/2706406) (including the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285)), [hexagonal prism](wikipedia://en/2859668), [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654), [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631), and the [rhombo-hexagonal dodecahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombo-hexagonal_dodecahedron).

## From Minkowski sums {#From_Minkowski_sums}

*Image: Shapley–Folkman lemma.svg.* A zonotope is the Minkowski sum of line segments. The sixteen dark-red points (on the right) form the Minkowski sum of the four non-convex sets (on the left), each of which consists of a pair of red points. Their convex hulls (shaded pink) contain plus-signs (+): The right plus-sign is the sum of the left plus-signs.

Let $\{v_0, v_1, \dots\}$ be a collection of three-dimensional [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29). With each vector $v_i$ we may associate a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) $\{ x_i v_i \mid 0 \leq x_i \leq 1 \}$. The [Minkowski sum](wikipedia://en/504105) $\{ \textstyle \sum_i x_i v_i \mid 0 \leq x_i \leq 1 \}$ forms a zonohedron, and all zonohedra that contain the origin have this form. The vectors from which the zonohedron is formed are called its **generators**. This characterization allows the definition of zonohedra to be generalized to higher dimensions, giving zonotopes.

Each edge in a zonohedron is parallel to at least one of the generators, and has length equal to the sum of the lengths of the generators to which it is parallel. Therefore, by choosing a set of generators with no parallel pairs of vectors, and by setting all vector lengths equal, we may form an [equilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilateral) version of any combinatorial type of zonohedron.

By choosing sets of vectors with high degrees of symmetry, we can form in this way, zonohedra with at least as much symmetry. For instance, generators equally spaced around the equator of a sphere, together with another pair of generators through the poles of the sphere, form zonohedra in the form of [prism](wikipedia://en/283007) over regular $2k$-gons: the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), [hexagonal prism](wikipedia://en/2859668), [octagonal prism](wikipedia://en/3026569), [decagonal prism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decagonal_prism), [dodecagonal prism](wikipedia://en/3929468), etc. Generators parallel to the edges of an octahedron form a [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654), and generators parallel to the long diagonals of a cube form a [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631).[^o4]

The Minkowski sum of any two zonohedra is another zonohedron, generated by the union of the generators of the two given zonohedra. Thus, the Minkowski sum of a cube and a truncated octahedron forms the [truncated cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/285879), while the Minkowski sum of the cube and the rhombic dodecahedron forms the [truncated rhombic dodecahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_rhombic_dodecahedron). Both of these zonohedra are **simple** (three faces meet at each vertex), as is the [truncated small rhombicuboctahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truncated_small_rhombicuboctahedron) formed from the Minkowski sum of the cube, truncated octahedron, and rhombic dodecahedron.[^o4]

## From arrangements {#From_arrangements}

The [Gauss map](wikipedia://en/378881) of any convex polyhedron maps each face of the polygon to a point on the unit sphere, and maps each edge of the polygon separating a pair of faces to a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) arc connecting the corresponding two points. In the case of a zonohedron, the edges surrounding each face can be grouped into pairs of parallel edges, and when translated via the Gauss map any such pair becomes a pair of contiguous segments on the same great circle. Thus, the edges of the zonohedron can be grouped into **zones** of parallel edges, which correspond to the segments of a common great circle on the Gauss map, and the 1-[skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeleton_%28topology%29) of the zonohedron can be viewed as the [planar dual graph](wikipedia://en/24314) to an arrangement of great circles on the sphere. Conversely any arrangement of great circles may be formed from the Gauss map of a zonohedron generated by vectors perpendicular to the planes through the circles.

Any simple zonohedron corresponds in this way to a **simplicial arrangement**, one in which each face is a triangle. Simplicial arrangements of great circles correspond via central projection to simplicial [arrangements of lines](wikipedia://en/864149) in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). There are three known infinite families of simplicial arrangements, one of which leads to the prisms when converted to zonohedra, and the other two of which correspond to additional infinite families of simple zonohedra. There are also many sporadic examples that do not fit into these three families.[^o6]

It follows from the correspondence between zonohedra and arrangements, and from the [Sylvester–Gallai theorem](wikipedia://en/1052632) which (in its [projective dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_dual) form) proves the existence of crossings of only two lines in any arrangement, that every zonohedron has at least one pair of opposite [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) faces. (Squares, rectangles, and rhombuses count for this purpose as special cases of parallelograms.) More strongly, every zonohedron has at least six parallelogram faces, and every zonohedron has a number of parallelogram faces that is linear in its number of generators.[^o7]

## Types {#Types}

Any [prism](wikipedia://en/283007) over a regular polygon with an even number of sides forms a zonohedron. These prisms can be formed so that all faces are regular: two opposite faces are equal to the regular polygon from which the prism was formed, and these are connected by a sequence of square faces. Zonohedra of this type are the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), [hexagonal prism](wikipedia://en/2859668), [octagonal prism](wikipedia://en/3026569), [decagonal prism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decagonal_prism), [dodecagonal prism](wikipedia://en/3929468), etc.

In addition to this infinite family of regular-faced zonohedra, there are three [Archimedean solids](wikipedia://en/1847), all [omnitruncations](wikipedia://en/2537223#Convex_forms_and_fundamental_vertex_arrangements) of the regular forms:

- The [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654), with 6 square and 8 hexagonal faces. (Omnitruncated tetrahedron)
- The [truncated cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/285879), with 12 squares, 8 hexagons, and 6 octagons. (Omnitruncated cube)
- The [truncated icosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/286534), with 30 squares, 20 hexagons and 12 decagons. (Omnitruncated dodecahedron)

In addition, certain [Catalan solids](wikipedia://en/564393) (duals of Archimedean solids) are again zonohedra:

- [Kepler's rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631) is the dual of the [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280).
- The [rhombic triacontahedron](wikipedia://en/665025) is the dual of the [icosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/15023).

Others with congruent rhombic faces:

- [Bilinski's rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/50652823).
- [Rhombic icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3691443)
- [Rhombohedron](wikipedia://en/2706406)

There are infinitely many zonohedra with rhombic faces that are not all congruent to each other. They include:

## Dissections {#Dissections}

Every zonohedron with $n$ zones can be partitioned into $\tbinom{n}{3}$ [parallelepipeds](wikipedia://en/23975), each having three of the same zones, and with one parallelepiped for each triple of zones.[^o24]

The [Dehn invariant](wikipedia://en/338051) of any zonohedron is zero. This implies that any two zonohedra with the same [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) can be [dissected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissection_%28rearrangement%29) into each other. This means that it is possible to cut one of the two zonohedra into polyhedral pieces that can be reassembled into the other.[^o25]

## Zonohedrification {#Zonohedrification}

Zonohedrification is a process defined by [George W. Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Hart) for creating a zonohedron from another polyhedron.[^o28][^o29]

First the vertices of any seed polyhedron are considered vectors from the polyhedron center. These vectors create the zonohedron which we call the zonohedrification of the original polyhedron. If the seed polyhedron has [central symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_symmetry), opposite points define the same direction, so the number of zones in the zonohedron is half the number of vertices of the seed. For any two vertices of the original polyhedron, there are two opposite planes of the zonohedrification which each have two edges parallel to the vertex vectors.

## Zonotopes {#Zonotopes}

The [Minkowski sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_sum) of [line segments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_segments) in any dimension forms a type of [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) called a [zonotope](wikipedia://en/669406). Equivalently, a zonotope $Z$ generated by vectors $v_1,...,v_k\in\mathbb{R}^n$ is given by $Z = \{a_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_k v_k | \; \forall(j) a_j\in [0,1]\}$. Note that in the special case where $k \leq n$, the zonotope $Z$ is a (possibly degenerate) [parallelotope](wikipedia://en/25207334#Parallelotope).

## Volume {#Volume}

Zonohedra, and *n*-dimensional zonotopes in general, are noteworthy for admitting a simple analytic formula for their volume.[^o33]

Let $Z(S)$ be the zonotope $Z = \{a_1 v_1 + \cdots + a_k v_k | \; \forall(j) a_j\in [0,1]\}$ generated by a set of vectors $S = \{v_1,\dots,v_k\in\mathbb{R}^n\}$. Then the n-dimensional volume of $Z(S)$ is given by

$$\sum_{T\subset S \; : \; |T| = n} |\det(Z(T))|$$

The determinant in this formula makes sense because (as noted above) when the set $T$ has cardinality equal to the dimension $n$ of the ambient space, the zonotope is a parallelotope.

Note that when $k<n$, this formula simply states that the zonotope has n-volume zero.
