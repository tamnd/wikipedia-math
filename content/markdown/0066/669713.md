# Capillary wave

*Image: 2006-01-14 Surface waves.jpg.* Capillary waves (ripples) in water

*Image: Ripples Lifjord.jpg.* Ripples on Lifjord in [Øksnes Municipality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Øksnes_Municipality), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway)

*Image: Multy droplets impact.JPG.* Capillary waves produced by [droplet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droplet) impacts on the interface between water and air.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Capillary wave"}

A **capillary wave** is a [wave](wikipedia://en/33516) traveling along the [phase boundary](wikipedia://en/4742308) of a fluid, whose [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28mechanics%29) and [phase velocity](wikipedia://en/25098) are dominated by the effects of [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension).

:::

Capillary waves are common in [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature), and are often referred to as **ripples**. The [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) of capillary waves on water is typically less than a few centimeters, with a [phase speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_speed) in excess of 0.2–0.3 meter/second.

A longer wavelength on a fluid interface will result in **gravity–capillary waves** which are influenced by both the effects of surface tension and [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gravity), as well as by fluid [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia). Ordinary [gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_wave) have a still longer wavelength.

Light breezes upon the surface of water which stir up such small ripples are also sometimes referred to as 'cat's paws'. On the open ocean, much larger [ocean surface waves](wikipedia://en/710251) ([seas](wikipedia://en/710251) and [swells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swell_%28ocean%29)) may result from coalescence of smaller wind-caused ripple-waves.

## Dispersion relation {#Dispersion_relation}

The [dispersion relation](wikipedia://en/853778) describes the relationship between [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) and [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) in waves. Distinction can be made between pure capillary waves – fully dominated by the effects of surface tension – and gravity–capillary waves which are also affected by gravity.

### Capillary waves, proper {#Capillary_waves,_proper}

The dispersion relation for capillary waves is

$$\omega^2=\frac{\sigma}{\rho+\rho'}\, |k|^3,$$

where $\omega$ is the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency), $\sigma$ the [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension), $\rho$ the [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) of the heavier fluid, $\rho'$ the density of the lighter fluid and $k$ the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber). The [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) is $\lambda=\frac{2 \pi}{k}.$ For the boundary between fluid and vacuum (free surface), the dispersion relation reduces to

$$\omega^2=\frac{\sigma}{\rho}\, |k|^3.$$

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Gravity–capillary wave"}

### Gravity–capillary waves {#Gravity–capillary_waves}

*Image: Dispersion capillary.svg.* Dispersion of gravity–capillary waves on the surface of deep water (zero mass density of upper layer, $\rho'=0$). Phase and group velocity divided by $\scriptstyle \sqrt[4]{g\sigma/\rho}$ as a function of inverse relative wavelength $\scriptstyle \frac{1}{\lambda}\sqrt{\sigma/(\rho g)}$. Blue lines (A): phase velocity, Red lines (B): group velocity. Drawn lines: dispersion relation for gravity–capillary waves. Dashed lines: dispersion relation for deep-water gravity waves. Dash-dotted lines: dispersion relation valid for deep-water capillary waves.

When capillary waves are also affected substantially by gravity, they are called gravity–capillary waves. Their dispersion relation reads, for waves on the interface between two fluids of infinite depth:[^o6][^o7]

$$\omega^2=|k|\left( \frac{\rho-\rho'}{\rho+\rho'}g+\frac{\sigma}{\rho+\rho'}k^2\right),$$

where $g$ is the acceleration due to [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gravity), $\rho$ and $\rho'$ are the densities of the two fluids $(\rho > \rho')$. The factor $(\rho-\rho')/(\rho+\rho')$ in the first term is the [Atwood number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atwood_number).

#### Gravity wave regime {#Gravity_wave_regime}

Airy wave theory

For large wavelengths (small $k = 2\pi/\lambda$), only the first term is relevant and one has [gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_wave). In this limit, the waves have a [group velocity](wikipedia://en/12778) half the [phase velocity](wikipedia://en/25098): following a single wave's crest in a group one can see the wave appearing at the back of the group, growing and finally disappearing at the front of the group.

#### Capillary wave regime {#Capillary_wave_regime}

Shorter (large $k$) waves (e.g. 2 mm for the water–air interface), which are proper capillary waves, do the opposite: an individual wave appears at the front of the group, grows when moving towards the group center and finally disappears at the back of the group. Phase velocity is two thirds of group velocity in this limit.

#### Phase velocity minimum {#Phase_velocity_minimum}

Between these two limits is a point at which the dispersion caused by gravity cancels out the dispersion due to the capillary effect. At a certain wavelength, the group velocity equals the phase velocity, and there is no dispersion. At precisely this same wavelength, the phase velocity of gravity–capillary waves as a function of wavelength (or wave number) has a minimum. Waves with wavelengths much smaller than this critical wavelength $\lambda_{m}$ are dominated by surface tension, and much above by gravity. The value of this wavelength and the associated minimum phase speed $c_{m}$ are:[^o6]

$$\lambda_m = 2 \pi \sqrt{ \frac{\sigma}{(\rho-\rho') g}} \quad \text{and} \quad c_m = \sqrt{ \frac{2 \sqrt{ (\rho - \rho') g \sigma }}{\rho+\rho'} }.$$

For the [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air)–[water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) interface, $\lambda_{m}$ is found to be \1.7 cm, and $c_{m}$ is \0.23 m/s.[^o6]

If one drops a small stone or droplet into liquid, the waves then propagate outside an expanding circle of fluid at rest; this circle is a [caustic](wikipedia://en/3498698) which corresponds to the minimal group velocity.[^o11]

#### Derivation {#Derivation}

As [Richard Feynman](wikipedia://en/25523) put it, "*\[water waves\] that are easily seen by everyone and which are usually used as an example of waves in elementary courses \[...\] are the worst possible example \[...\]; they have all the complications that waves can have.*"[^o13] The derivation of the general dispersion relation is therefore quite involved.[^o14]

There are three contributions to the energy, due to gravity, to [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension), and to [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics). The first two are potential energies, and responsible for the two terms inside the parenthesis, as is clear from the appearance of $g$ and $\sigma$. For gravity, an assumption is made of the density of the fluids being constant (i.e., incompressibility), and likewise $g$ (waves are not high enough for gravitation to change appreciably). For surface tension, the deviations from planarity (as measured by derivatives of the surface) are supposed to be small. For common waves both approximations are good enough.

The third contribution involves the [kinetic energies](wikipedia://en/17327) of the fluids. It is the most complicated and calls for a [hydrodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics) framework. Incompressibility is again involved (which is satisfied if the speed of the waves is much less than the speed of sound in the media), together with the flow being [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational) – the flow is then [potential](wikipedia://en/58320). These are typically also good approximations for common situations.

The resulting equation for the potential (which is [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation)) can be solved with the proper boundary conditions. On one hand, the velocity must vanish well below the surface (in the "deep water" case, which is the one we consider, otherwise a more involved result is obtained, see [Ocean surface waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_surface_wave#Science_of_waves).) On the other, its vertical component must match the motion of the surface. This contribution ends up being responsible for the extra $k$ outside the parenthesis, which causes **all** regimes to be dispersive, both at low values of $k$, and high ones (except around the one value at which the two dispersions cancel out.)
