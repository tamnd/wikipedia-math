# Quantum logic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quantum logic"}

In the [mathematical study of logic](wikipedia://en/19636) and the [physical](wikipedia://en/22939) analysis of [quantum foundations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_foundations), **quantum logic** is a set of rules for manip­ulation of [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094) inspired by the structure of [quantum theory](wikipedia://en/25202). The formal system takes as its starting point an obs­ervation of [Garrett Birkhoff](wikipedia://en/1180916) and [John von&nbsp;Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von&nbsp;Neumann), that the structure of experimental tests in classical mechanics forms a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959), but the structure of experimental tests in quantum mechanics forms a much more complicated structure.

:::

A number of other logics have also been proposed to analyze quantum-mechanical phenomena, unfortunately also under the name of "quantum logic(s)". They are not the subject of this article. For discussion of the similarities and differences between quantum logic and some of these competitors, see .

Quantum logic has been proposed as the correct logic for propositional inference generally, most notably by the philosopher [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233), at least at one point in his career. This thesis was an important ingredient in Putnam's 1968 paper "[Is Logic Empirical?](wikipedia://en/1166402)" in which he analysed the [epistemological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology) status of the rules of propositional logic. Modern philosophers reject quantum logic as a basis for reasoning, because it lacks a [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808); a common alternative is the system of [linear logic](wikipedia://en/579675), of which quantum logic is a fragment.

Mathematically, quantum logic is formulated by weakening the [distributive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_law) for a Boolean algebra, resulting in an [ortho­complemented lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthocomplement). Quantum-mechanical [observables](wikipedia://en/294248) and [states](wikipedia://en/30876419) can be defined in terms of functions on or to the lattice, giving an alternate [formalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formalism_%28mathematics%29) for quantum computations.

## Introduction {#Introduction}

The most notable difference between quantum logic and [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) is the failure of the [propositional](wikipedia://en/18154) [distributive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributive_law):[^o3]

*p* and (*q* or *r*) = (*p* and *q*) or (*p* and *r*),

where the symbols *p*, *q* and *r* are propositional variables.

To illustrate why the distributive law fails, consider a particle moving on a line and (using some system of units where the [reduced Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_Planck_constant) is 1) let[^o4]

*p* = "the particle has [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) in the interval 0, +$\tfrac{1}{6}$"

*q* = "the particle is in the interval −1, 1"

*r* = "the particle is in the interval 1, 3"

We might observe that:

*p* and (*q* or *r*) = *true*

in other words, that the state of the particle is a weighted [superposition](wikipedia://en/82728) of momenta between 0 and +1/6 and positions between −1 and +3.

On the other hand, the propositions "*p* and *q*" and "*p* and *r*" each assert tighter restrictions on simultaneous values of position and momentum than are allowed by the [uncertainty principle](wikipedia://en/31883) (they each have uncertainty 1/3, which is less than the allowed minimum of 1/2). So there are no states that can support either proposition, and

(*p* and *q*) or (*p* and *r*) = *false*

## History and Philosophical Debate {#History_and_Philosophical_Debate}

In his classic 1932 treatise *[Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics](wikipedia://en/45154254)*, [John von&nbsp;Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von&nbsp;Neumann) noted that [projections](wikipedia://en/3909097) on a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) can be viewed as propositions about physical observables; that is, as potential *yes-or-no questions* an observer might ask about the state of a physical system, questions that could be settled by some measurement.[^n1] Principles for manipulating these quantum propositions were then called *quantum logic* by von Neumann and Birkhoff in a 1936 paper.[^n2]

[George Mackey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mackey), in his 1963 book (also called *Mathematical Foundations of Quantum Mechanics*), attempted to axiomatize quantum logic as the structure of an [ortho­complemented lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthocomplemented_lattice), and recognized that a physical observable could be *defined* in terms of quantum propositions. Although Mackey's presentation still assumed that the ortho­complemented lattice is the [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) of [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [linear subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) of a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) Hilbert space,[^n3] [Constantin Piron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constantin_Piron), Günther Ludwig and others later developed axiomatizations that do not assume an underlying Hilbert space.[^o9]

Inspired by [Hans Reichenbach](wikipedia://en/1546863)'s then-recent defence of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), the philosopher [Hilary Putnam](wikipedia://en/75233) popularized Mackey's work in two papers in 1968 and 1975,[^n4] in which he attributed the idea that anomalies associated to quantum measurements originate with a failure of logic itself to his coauthor, physicist [David Finkelstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Finkelstein).[^n5] Putnam hoped to develop a possible alternative to [hidden variables](wikipedia://en/211602) or [wavefunction collapse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction_collapse) in the problem of [quantum measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_measurement), but [Gleason's theorem](wikipedia://en/6796998) presents severe difficulties for this goal.[^n4][^n6] Later, Putnam retracted his views, albeit with much less fanfare,[^n4] but the damage had been done. While Birkhoff and von Neumann's original work only attempted to organize the calculations associated with the [Copenhagen interpretation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copenhagen_interpretation) of quantum mechanics, a school of researchers had now sprung up, either hoping that quantum logic would provide a viable hidden-variable theory, or obviate the need for one. Their work proved fruitless, and now lies in poor repute.[^n7]

Most philosophers would agree that quantum logic is not a competitor to [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868). It is far from evident (albeit true[^o17]) that quantum logic is a *logic*, in the sense of describing a process of reasoning, as opposed to a particularly convenient language to summarize the measurements performed by quantum apparatuses.[^n8][^n9] In particular, some modern [philosophers of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher_of_science) argue that quantum logic attempts to substitute metaphysical difficulties for unsolved problems in physics, rather than properly solving the physics problems.[^n10] [Tim Maudlin](wikipedia://en/37708243) writes that quantum "logic 'solves' the [\[measurement\] problem](wikipedia://en/773292) by making the problem impossible to state."[^n11]

Quantum logic remains in use among logicians[^n12] and interests are expanding through the recent development of [quantum computing](wikipedia://en/25220), which has engendered a proliferation of new logics for formal analysis of quantum protocols and algorithms (see also ).[^n13] The logic may also find application in (computational) linguistics.

## Algebraic structure {#Algebraic_structure}

Quantum logic can be axiomatized as the theory of propositions modulo the following identities:[^n14]

- *a* = ¬¬*a*
- ∨ is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) and [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative).
- There is a maximal element ⊤, and ⊤ = *b*∨¬*b* for any *b*.
- *a*∨¬(¬*a*∨*b*) = *a*.

("¬" is the traditional notation for "[not](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negation_%28logic%29)", "∨" the notation for "[or](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Or_%28logic%29)", and "∧" the notation for "[and](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/And_%28logic%29)".)

Some authors restrict to [orthomodular lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthomodular_lattice), which additionally satisfy the orthomodular law:[^o26]

- If ⊤ = ¬(¬*a*∨¬*b*)∨¬(*a*∨*b*) then *a* = *b*.

("⊤" is the traditional notation for [truth](wikipedia://en/29920) and ""⊥" the traditional notation for [falsity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deception).)

Alternative formulations include propositions derivable via a [natural deduction](wikipedia://en/51072),[^n12] [sequent calculus](wikipedia://en/252329)[^o28][^o29] or [tableaux](wikipedia://en/1027229) system.[^o30] Despite the relatively developed [proof&nbsp;theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof&nbsp;theory), quantum logic is not known to be [decidable](wikipedia://en/913118).[^n14]

## Quantum logic as the logic of observables {#Quantum_logic_as_the_logic_of_observables}

The remainder of this article assumes the reader is familiar with the [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) of [self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408) on a Hilbert space. However, the main ideas can be under­stood in the [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) case.

### Logic of classical mechanics {#Logic_of_classical_mechanics}

The [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) formulations of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) have three ingredients: [states](wikipedia://en/19555586), [observables](wikipedia://en/294248) and [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28mechanics%29). In the simplest case of a single particle moving in **R**<sup>3</sup>, the state space is the position–momentum space **R**<sup>6</sup>. An observable is some [real-valued function](wikipedia://en/22284647) *f* on the state space. Examples of observables are position, momentum or energy of a particle. For classical systems, the value *f*(*x*), that is the value of *f* for some particular system state *x*, is obtained by a process of measurement of *f*.

The [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094) concerning a classical system are generated from basic statements of the form

"Measurement of *f* yields a value in the interval \[*a*, *b*\] for some real numbers *a*, *b*."

through the conventional arithmetic operations and [pointwise limits](wikipedia://en/26551602). It follows easily from this characterization of propositions in classical systems that the corresponding logic is identical to the [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959) of [Borel subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_subset) of the state space. They thus obey the laws of [classical](wikipedia://en/55868) [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) (such as [de&nbsp;Morgan's laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De&nbsp;Morgan's_laws)) with the set operations of union and intersection corresponding to the [Boolean conjunctives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_operator_%28Boolean_algebra%29) and subset inclusion corresponding to [material implication](wikipedia://en/244516).

In fact, a stronger claim is true: they must obey the [infinitary logic](wikipedia://en/630478) $L_{\omega_{1},\omega}$.

We summarize these remarks as follows: The proposition system of a classical system is a lattice with a distinguished *orthocomplementation* operation: The lattice operations of *meet* and *join* are respectively set intersection and set union. The orthocomplementation operation is set complement. Moreover, this lattice is *sequentially complete*, in the sense that any sequence {*E*<sub>*i*</sub>}<sub>*i*∈**N**</sub> of elements of the lattice has a [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound), specifically the set-theoretic union:

$$\operatorname{LUB}(\{E_i\}) = \bigcup_{i=1}^\infty E_i\text{.}$$

### Propositional lattice of a quantum mechanical system {#Propositional_lattice_of_a_quantum_mechanical_system}

In the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) formulation of quantum mechanics as presented by von Neumann, a physical observable is represented by some (possibly [unbounded](wikipedia://en/455961)) densely defined [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408) *A* on a Hilbert space *H*. *A* has a [spectral decomposition](wikipedia://en/123495), which is a [projection-valued measure](wikipedia://en/1046155) E defined on the Borel subsets of **R**. In particular, for any bounded [Borel function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_function) *f* on **R**, the following extension of *f* to operators can be made:

$$f(A) = \int_{\mathbb{R}} f(\lambda) \, d \operatorname{E}(\lambda).$$

In case *f* is the indicator function of an interval \[*a*, *b*\], the operator *f*(*A*) is a self-adjoint projection onto the subspace of [generalized eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/1137612) of *A* with eigenvalue in *a*,*b*. That subspace can be interpreted as the quantum analogue of the classical proposition

- Measurement of *A* yields a value in the interval \[*a*, *b*\].

This suggests the following quantum mechanical replacement for the orthocomplemented lattice of propositions in classical mechanics, essentially Mackey's *Axiom VII*:

- The propositions of a quantum mechanical system correspond to the lattice of closed subspaces of *H*; the negation of a proposition *V* is the orthogonal complement *V*<sup>⊥</sup>.

The space *Q* of quantum propositions is also sequentially complete: any pairwise-disjoint sequence {*V*<sub>*i*</sub>}<sub>*i*</sub> of elements of *Q* has a least upper bound. Here disjointness of *W*<sub>1</sub> and *W*<sub>2</sub> means *W*<sub>2</sub> is a subspace of *W*<sub>1</sub><sup>⊥</sup>. The least upper bound of {*V*<sub>*i*</sub>}<sub>*i*</sub> is the closed internal [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687).

### Standard semantics {#Standard_semantics}

The standard semantics of quantum logic is that quantum logic is the logic of [projection operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_operator) in a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/20598932) or [pre-Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Hilbert_space), where an observable *p* is associated with the [set of quantum states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) for which *p* (when measured) has [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) 1. From there,

- *¬p* is the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) of *p* (since for those states, the probability of observing *p*, P(*p*) = 0),
- *p*∧*q* is the intersection of *p* and *q*, and
- *p*∨*q* = ¬(¬*p*∧¬*q*) refers to states that [superpose](wikipedia://en/82728) *p* and *q*.

This semantics has the nice property that the pre-Hilbert space is complete (i.e., Hilbert) if and only if the propositions satisfy the orthomodular law, a result known as the [Solèr theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solèr_theorem).[^o36] Although much of the development of quantum logic has been motivated by the standard semantics, it is not characterized by the latter; there are additional properties satisfied by that lattice that need not hold in quantum logic.[^n12]

## Differences with classical logic {#Differences_with_classical_logic}

The structure of *Q* immediately points to a difference with the partial order structure of a classical proposition system. In the classical case, given a proposition *p*, the equations

⊤ = *p*∨*q* and

⊥ = *p*∧*q*

have exactly one solution, namely the set-theoretic complement of *p*. In the case of the lattice of projections there are infinitely many solutions to the above equations (any closed, algebraic complement of *p* solves it; it need not be the orthocomplement).

More generally, [propositional valuation](wikipedia://en/17456938) has unusual properties in quantum logic. An orthocomplemented lattice admitting a [total](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_function) [lattice homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_Homomorphism) to $\{\perp,\top\}$ must be Boolean. A standard workaround is to study maximal partial homomorphisms *q* with a filtering property:

if *a*≤*b* and *q*(*a*) = ⊤, then *q*(*b*) = ⊤.[^n7]

### Failure of distributivity {#Failure_of_distributivity}

Expressions in quantum logic describe observables using a syntax that resembles classical logic. However, unlike classical logic, the distributive law *a* ∧ (*b* ∨ *c*) = (*a* ∧ *b*) ∨ (*a* ∧ *c*) fails when dealing with [noncommuting observables](wikipedia://en/294248#Incompatibility_of_observables_in_quantum_mechanics), such as position and momentum. This occurs because measurement affects the system, and measurement of whether a disjunction holds does not measure which of the disjuncts is true.

For example, consider a simple one-dimensional particle with position denoted by *x* and momentum by *p*, and define observables:

- *a* — |*p*| ≤ 1 (in some units)
- *b* — x ≤ 0
- *c* — x ≥ 0

Now, position and momentum are Fourier transforms of each other, and the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of a [square-integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-integrable) nonzero function with a [compact support](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_support) is [entire](wikipedia://en/10372) and hence does not have non-isolated zeroes. Therefore, there is no wave function that is both [normalizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizable_wave_function) in momentum space and vanishes on precisely *x* ≥ 0. Thus, *a* ∧ *b* and similarly *a* ∧ *c* are false, so (*a* ∧ *b*) ∨ (*a* ∧ *c*) is false. However, *a* ∧ (*b* ∨ *c*) equals *a*, which is certainly not false (there are states for which it is a viable [measurement outcome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_measurement)). Moreover: if the relevant Hilbert space for the particle's dynamics only admits momenta no greater than 1, then *a* is true.

To understand more, let *p*<sub>1</sub> and *p*<sub>2</sub> be the momentum functions (Fourier transforms) for the projections of the particle wave function to *x* ≤ 0 and *x* ≥ 0 respectively. Let |*p*<sub>i</sub>|↾<sub>≥1</sub> be the restriction of *p*<sub>i</sub> to momenta that are (in absolute value) ≥1.

(*a* ∧ *b*) ∨ (*a* ∧ *c*) corresponds to states with |*p*<sub>1</sub>|↾<sub>≥1</sub> = |*p*<sub>2</sub>|↾<sub>≥1</sub> = 0 (this holds even if we defined *p* differently so as to make such states possible; also, *a* ∧ *b* corresponds to |*p*<sub>1</sub>|↾<sub>≥1</sub>=0 and *p*<sub>2</sub>=0). Meanwhile, *a* corresponds to states with |*p*|↾<sub>≥1</sub> = 0. As an operator, *p* = *p*<sub>1</sub> + *p*<sub>2</sub>, and nonzero |*p*<sub>1</sub>|↾<sub>≥1</sub> and |*p*<sub>2</sub>|↾<sub>≥1</sub> might interfere to produce zero |*p*|↾<sub>≥1</sub>. Such interference is key to the richness of quantum logic and quantum mechanics.

## Relationship to quantum measurement {#Relationship_to_quantum_measurement}

### Mackey observables {#Mackey_observables}

Given a [orthocomplemented lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthocomplement) *Q*, a Mackey observable φ is a [countably additive homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_additive_measure) from the orthocomplemented lattice of Borel subsets of **R** to *Q*. In symbols, this means that for any sequence {*S*<sub>*i*</sub>}<sub>*i*</sub> of pairwise-disjoint Borel subsets of **R**, {φ(*S*<sub>*i*</sub>)}<sub>*i*</sub> are pairwise-orthogonal propositions (elements of *Q*) and

$$\varphi\left(\bigcup_{i=1}^\infty S_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \varphi(S_i).$$

Equivalently, a Mackey observable is a [projection-valued measure](wikipedia://en/1046155) on **R**.

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem** ([Spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495)). If *Q* is the lattice of closed subspaces of Hilbert *H*, then there is a bijective correspondence between Mackey observables and densely defined self-adjoint operators on *H*.

:::

### Quantum probability measures {#Quantum_probability_measures}

A *quantum probability measure* is a function P defined on *Q* with values in \[0,1\] such that P("⊥)=0, P(⊤)=1 and if {*E*<sub>*i*</sub>}<sub>*i*</sub> is a sequence of pairwise-orthogonal elements of *Q* then

$$\operatorname{P}\!\left(\bigvee_{i=1}^\infty E_i\right) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty \operatorname{P}(E_i).$$

Every quantum probability measure on the closed subspaces of a Hilbert space is induced by a [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) — a [nonnegative operator](wikipedia://en/454936) of [trace](wikipedia://en/43270#Generalizations) 1. Formally,

**Theorem**.[^o45] Suppose *Q* is the lattice of closed subspaces of a separable Hilbert space of complex dimension at least 3. Then for any quantum probability measure *P* on *Q* there exists a unique [trace class](wikipedia://en/192266) operator *S* such that

$$\operatorname{P}(E) = \operatorname{Tr}(S E)$$

for any self-adjoint projection *E* in *Q*.

## Relationship to other logics {#Relationship_to_other_logics}

Quantum logic embeds into [linear logic](wikipedia://en/579675)[^o47] and the [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365) *B*.[^n12] Indeed, modern logics for the analysis of quantum computation often begin with quantum logic, and attempt to graft desirable features of an extension of classical logic thereonto; the results then necessarily embed quantum logic.[^n15][^n16]

The orthocomplemented lattice of any set of quantum propositions can be embedded into a Boolean algebra, which is then amenable to classical logic.[^o51]

## Limitations {#Limitations}

Although many treatments of quantum logic assume that the underlying lattice must be orthomodular, such logics cannot handle multiple interacting quantum systems. In an example due to Foulis and Randall, there are orthomodular propositions with finite-dimensional Hilbert models whose pairing admits no orthomodular model.[^n6] Likewise, quantum logic with the orthomodular law falsifies the [deduction theorem](wikipedia://en/480010).[^n17]

Quantum logic admits no reasonable [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808); any [connective](wikipedia://en/18153) that is [monotone](wikipedia://en/693185) in a certain technical sense reduces the class of propositions to a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959).[^o55] Consequently, quantum logic struggles to represent the passage of time.[^o47] One possible workaround is the theory of [quantum filtrations](wikipedia://en/47616206) developed in the late 1970s and 1980s by [Belavkin](wikipedia://en/9717452).[^o56][^o57] It is known, however, that System [BV](wikipedia://en/693282), a [deep inference](wikipedia://en/1250664) fragment of [linear logic](wikipedia://en/579675) that is very close to quantum logic, can handle arbitrary [discrete spacetimes](wikipedia://en/44239711).[^o58]
