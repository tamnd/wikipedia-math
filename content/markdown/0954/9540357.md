# I'm entitled to my opinion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="I'm entitled to my opinion"}

"**I'm entitled to my opinion**" (or "**I have a right to my opinion**") is an [informal fallacy](wikipedia://en/1655450) in which someone dismisses arguments against their position by asserting that they have a right to hold their own particular viewpoint.[^o2][^o3] The statement exemplifies a [red herring](wikipedia://en/3197900) or [thought-terminating cliché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought-terminating_cliché). The fallacy is sometimes presented as "let's [agree to disagree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agree_to_disagree)".[^o4] Whether one has a particular entitlement or right is irrelevant to whether one's assertion is true or false. Where an objection to a belief is made, the assertion of the right to an opinion side-steps the usual steps of discourse of either asserting a [justification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justification_%28epistemology%29) of that belief, or an argument against the validity of the objection.[^o7] Such an assertion, however, can also be an assertion of one's own freedom from, or a refusal to participate in, the rules of [argumentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argumentation) and [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) at hand.[^o5]

:::

Philosopher [Patrick Stokes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Stokes_%28philosopher%29) has described the expression as problematic because it is often used to defend factually indefensible positions or to imply "an equal right to be heard on a matter in which only one of the two parties has the relevant expertise".[^o6] Further elaborating on Stokes' argument, philosopher David Godden argued that the claim that one is entitled to a view gives rise to certain obligations, such as the obligation to provide reasons for the view and to submit those reasons to contestation; Godden called these the principles of rational entitlement and rational responsibility, and he developed a classroom exercise for teaching these principles.[^o7]
