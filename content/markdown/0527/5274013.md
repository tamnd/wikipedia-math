# Higher category theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Higher category theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **higher category theory** is the part of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) at a *higher order*, which means that some equalities are replaced by explicit [arrows](wikipedia://en/27716891) in order to be able to explicitly study the structure behind those equalities. Higher category theory is often applied in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) (especially in [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831)), where one studies algebraic [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638) of [spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), such as the [fundamental](wikipedia://en/55264190) [weak ∞-groupoid](wikipedia://en/28662219).

:::

In higher category theory, the concept of higher categorical structures, such as ([∞-categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/∞-categories)), allows for a more robust treatment of [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), enabling one to capture finer homotopical distinctions, such as differentiating two [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) that have the same fundamental group but differ in their higher [homotopy groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_groups). This approach is particularly valuable when dealing with spaces with intricate topological features,[^o2] such as the [Eilenberg-MacLane space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eilenberg-MacLane_space).

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="4-category"}

## Strict higher categories {#Strict_higher_categories}

An ordinary [category](wikipedia://en/53759) has [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) and [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891), which are called 1-morphisms in the context of higher category theory. A [2-category](wikipedia://en/2740708) generalizes this by also including [2-morphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-morphism) between the 1-morphisms. Continuing this up to *n*-morphisms between (*n* − 1)-morphisms gives an *n*-category.

Just as the category known as **Cat**, which is the [category of small categories](wikipedia://en/2072741) and [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) is actually a 2-category with [natural transformations](wikipedia://en/45022) as its 2-morphisms, the category *n*-**Cat** of (small) *n*-categories is actually an (*n* + 1)-category.

An *n*-category is defined by induction on *n* by:

- A 0-category is a [set](wikipedia://en/26829),
- An (*n* + 1)-category is a category [enriched](wikipedia://en/142622) over the category *n*-**Cat**.

So a 1-category is just a ([locally small](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_small_category)) category.

The [monoidal](wikipedia://en/330604) structure of **Set** is the one given by the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) as tensor and a [singleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set) as unit. In fact any category with finite [products](wikipedia://en/237868) can be given a monoidal structure. The recursive construction of *n*-**Cat** works fine because if a category $C$ has finite products, the category of $C$-enriched categories has finite products too.

While this concept is too strict for some purposes in for example, [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831), where "weak" structures arise in the form of higher categories,[^o5] strict cubical higher homotopy groupoids have also arisen as giving a new foundation for algebraic topology on the border between [homology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_theory) and [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831); see the article [Nonabelian algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/61928215), referenced in the book below.

:::

## Weak higher categories {#Weak_higher_categories}

In weak *n*-categories, the associativity and identity conditions are no longer strict (that is, they are not given by equalities), but rather are satisfied up to an isomorphism of the next level. An example in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) is the composition of [paths](wikipedia://en/1189553), where the identity and association conditions hold only up to [reparameterization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reparameterization), and hence up to [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250), which is the 2-isomorphism for this 2-category. These *n*-isomorphisms must well behave between [hom-sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom-set) and expressing this is the difficulty in the definition of weak *n*-categories. Weak 2-categories, also called [bicategories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bicategory), were the first to be defined explicitly. A particularity of these is that a bicategory with one object is exactly a [monoidal category](wikipedia://en/330604), so that bicategories can be said to be "monoidal categories with many objects." Weak 3-categories, also called [tricategories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tricategory), and higher-level generalizations are increasingly harder to define explicitly. Several definitions have been given, and telling when they are equivalent, and in what sense, has become a new object of study in category theory.

## Quasi-categories {#Quasi-categories}

Weak Kan complexes, or quasi-categories, are [simplicial sets](wikipedia://en/1027784) satisfying a weak version of the Kan condition. [André Joyal](wikipedia://en/9157736) showed that they are a good foundation for higher category theory by constructing the [Joyal model structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joyal_model_structure) on the [category of simplicial sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_simplicial_sets), whose fibrant objects are exactly quasi-categories. Recently, in 2009, the theory has been systematized further by [Jacob Lurie](wikipedia://en/26663863) who simply calls them infinity categories, though the latter term is also a generic term for all models of (infinity, *k*) categories for any *k*.

## Simplicially enriched categories {#Simplicially_enriched_categories}

Simplicially enriched categories, or simplicial categories, are categories enriched over simplicial sets. However, when we look at them as a model for [(infinity, 1)-categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%28infinity,_1%29-category), then many categorical notions (e.g., [limits](wikipedia://en/39248)) do not agree with the corresponding notions in the sense of enriched categories. The same for other enriched models like topologically enriched categories.

## Topologically enriched categories {#Topologically_enriched_categories}

Topologically enriched categories (sometimes simply called topological categories) are categories enriched over some convenient category of topological spaces, e.g. the category of [compactly generated](wikipedia://en/899382) [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637).

## Segal categories {#Segal_categories}

These are models of higher categories introduced by Hirschowitz and Simpson in 1998,[^o11] partly inspired by results of Graeme Segal in 1974.
