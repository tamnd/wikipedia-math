# J. C. P. Miller

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="J. C. P. Miller"}

**Jeffrey Charles Percy Miller** (31 August 1906 – 24 April 1981) was an [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England) mathematician and [computing pioneer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing_pioneer). He worked in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) and on [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), particularly [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra), where **Miller's monster** is a nickname of the [great dirhombicosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2910030).

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Miller was born at [Isleworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isleworth), [Middlesex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middlesex) (now [West London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_London)), and educated at [St Paul's School, London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Paul's_School,_London) and [Trinity College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Cambridge) (BA 1928, MA 1931, PhD 1933).[^o3][^o4][^o5]

## Career {#Career}

He was an early member of the Computing Laboratory of the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge).[^o7] He contributed in computation to the construction and documentation of [mathematical tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_tables),[^o8] and by the proposal of certain [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775). [Miller's recurrence algorithm](wikipedia://en/60034541) is mentioned in the *[Handbook of Mathematical Functions](wikipedia://en/1206236)*.[^o9]

What Miller perceived was that in a second-order linear recurrence which has solutions sufficiently differentiated asymptotically, there is a solution that may be uniquely characterized by one initial value and a knowledge of its growth. This led to an algorithm for computing certain solutions of the equation which required only a scant knowledge of their pointwise values.[^o10]

In volume 2 of *[The Art of Computer Programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_Computer_Programming)*, [Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) attributes to Miller a basic technique on [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012), for recursive evaluation of coefficients of powers or more general functions.[^o11]

In the theory of [stellation](wikipedia://en/180253) of polyhedra, he made some influential suggestions to [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328). These became known as *Miller's rules*. The 1938 book on [the fifty-nine icosahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_fifty-nine_icosahedra) resulted, written by Coxeter and [Patrick du Val](wikipedia://en/10637467).[^o12] In the 1930s, Coxeter and Miller found 12 new [uniform polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_polyhedra), a step in the process of their complete classification in the 1950s.[^o13] Miller also made an early investigation into what is now known as the [Rule 90](wikipedia://en/16572515) [cellular automaton](wikipedia://en/54342).[^o14]

Miller was a Fellow of the [Royal Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Astronomical_Society) from 1929; his obituary in the Society's journal noted his early interest and work in astronomy – including papers on the effect of distribution of density on the period of pulsation in a star, and on the effect of opacity in the point-source stellar model – and observed that, "but for a serious illness that interrupted his university postgraduate years, there is little doubt that \[he\] would have continued to work in the field of astronomy and would have made notable contributions to it."[^o5]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

In 1934, Miller married Germaine Gough. They had three children (David, Alison and Jane). Germaine died in Cambridge in her 100th year in March 2010 and is buried at [St Andrew's Church, Chesterton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Andrew's_Church,_Chesterton), Cambridge.[^o16]
