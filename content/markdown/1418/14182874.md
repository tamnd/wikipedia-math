# Schuette–Nesbitt formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Schuette–Nesbitt formula"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Schuette–Nesbitt formula** is a generalization of the [inclusion–exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/342684). It is named after [Donald R. Schuette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_R._Schuette) and [Cecil J. Nesbitt](wikipedia://en/14373679).

:::

The [probabilistic](wikipedia://en/23542) version of the Schuette–Nesbitt [formula](wikipedia://en/164040) has practical applications in [actuarial science](wikipedia://en/189373), where it is used to calculate the [net single premium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_premium_valuation) for [life annuities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_annuity) and [life insurances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_insurance) based on the general symmetric status.

## Combinatorial versions {#Combinatorial_versions}

Consider a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $\Omega$ and [subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsets) $A_{1}, ..., A_{m}$. Let

::: {#o3 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$N(\omega)=\sum_{n=1}^m 1_{A_n}(\omega),\qquad \omega\in\Omega, \tag{1}$$

:::

denote the number of subsets to which $\omega \in \Omega$ belongs, where we use the [indicator functions](wikipedia://en/240790) of the sets $A_{1}, ..., A_{m}$. Furthermore, for each $k \in \{0, 1, ..., m\}$, let

::: {#o4 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$N_k(\omega)=\sum_{\scriptstyle J\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}\atop\scriptstyle|J|=k} 1_{\cap_{j\in J}A_j}(\omega),\qquad\omega\in\Omega, \tag{2}$$

:::

denote the number of [intersections](wikipedia://en/23476429) of exactly $k$ sets out of $A_{1}, ..., A_{m}$, to which $\omega$ belongs, where the intersection over the [empty index set](wikipedia://en/9566) is defined as $\Omega$, hence $N_{0} = 1_{\Omega}$. Let $V$ denote a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $R$ such as the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) (or more generally a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ with [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity)). Then, for every choice of $c_{0}, ..., c_{m} \in V$,

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$\sum_{n=0}^m 1_{\{N=n\}}c_n = \sum_{k=0}^m N_k\sum_{l=0}^k (-1)^{k-l}\binom klc_l, \tag{3}$$

:::

where $1_{\{N=n\}}$ denotes the indicator function of the set of all $\omega \in \Omega$ with $N(\omega) = n$, and $\textstyle\binom kl$ is a [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668). Equality ((3)) says that the two $V$-valued functions defined on $\Omega$ are the same.

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of ()"}

### Proof of ((3)) {#Proof_of_()}

We prove that ((3)) holds pointwise. Take $\omega \in \Omega$ and define $n = N(\omega)$. Then the left-hand side of ((3)) equals $c_{n}$. Let $I$ denote the set of all those indices $i \in \{1, ..., m\}$ such that $\omega \in A_{i}$, hence $I$ contains exactly $n$ indices. Given $J \subset \{1, ..., m\}$ with $k$ elements, then $\omega$ belongs to the intersection $\cap_{j\in J}A_{j}$ if and only if $J$ is a subset of $I$. By the combinatorial interpretation of the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668), there are $N_{k} =$ $\textstyle\binom nk$ such subsets (the binomial coefficient is zero for $k > n$). Therefore the right-hand side of ((3)) evaluated at $\omega$ equals

$$\sum_{k=0}^m \binom nk\sum_{l=0}^k (-1)^{k-l}\binom klc_l =\sum_{l=0}^m\underbrace{\sum_{k=l}^n (-1)^{k-l}\binom nk \binom kl}_{=:\,(*)} c_l,$$

where we used that the first binomial coefficient is zero for $k > n$. Note that the sum (\*) is empty and therefore defined as zero for $n < l$. Using the [factorial formula](wikipedia://en/4668#Factorial_formula) for the binomial coefficients, it follows that

$$\begin{aligned} (*) &=\sum_{k=l}^n (-1)^{k-l}\frac{n!}{k!\,(n-k)!}\,\frac{k!}{l!\,(k-l)!}\\ &=\underbrace{\frac{n!}{l!\,(n-l)!}}_{=\binom nl}\underbrace{\sum_{k=l}^n (-1)^{k-l}\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-k)!\,(k-l)!}}_{=:\,(**)}\\ \end{aligned}$$

Rewriting (\*\*) with the summation index $j = k - l$ und using the [binomial formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_formula) for the third equality shows that

$$\begin{aligned} (**) &=\sum_{j=0}^{n-l} (-1)^{j}\frac{(n-l)!}{(n-l-j)!\,j!}\\ &=\sum_{j=0}^{n-l} (-1)^{j}\binom{n-l}{j} =(1-1)^{n-l} =\delta_{ln}, \end{aligned}$$

which is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890). Substituting this result into the above formula and noting that $n$ choose $l$ equals $1$ for $l = n$, it follows that the right-hand side of ((3)) evaluated at $\omega$ also reduces to $c_{n}$.

:::

### Representation in the polynomial ring {#Representation_in_the_polynomial_ring}

As a special case, take for $V$ the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R[x]$ with the [indeterminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29) $x$. Then ((3)) can be rewritten in a more compact way as

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\sum_{n=0}^m 1_{\{N=n\}}x^n = \sum_{k=0}^m N_k(x-1)^k. \tag{4}$$

:::

This is an identity for two [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) whose coefficients depend on $\omega$, which is implicit in the notation.

Proof of ((4)) using ((3)): Substituting $c_{n} = x^{n}$ for $n \in \{0, ..., m\}$ into ((3)) and using the [binomial formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_formula) shows that

$$\sum_{n=0}^m 1_{\{N=n\}}x^n =\sum_{k=0}^m N_k\underbrace{\sum_{l=0}^k \binom kl(-1)^{k-l}x^l}_{=\,(x-1)^k},$$

which proves ((4)).

### Representation with shift and difference operators {#Representation_with_shift_and_difference_operators}

Consider the [linear](wikipedia://en/18102) [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) $E$ and the linear [difference operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_operator) $\Delta$, which we define here on the [sequence space](wikipedia://en/1701075) of $V$ by

$$\begin{aligned} E:V^{\mathbb{N}_0}&\to V^{\mathbb{N}_0},\\ E(c_0,c_1,c_2,c_3,\ldots)&\mapsto(c_1,c_2,c_3,\ldots),\\ \end{aligned}$$

and

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta:V^{\mathbb{N}_0}&\to V^{\mathbb{N}_0},\\ \Delta(c_0,c_1,c_2,c_3\ldots)&\mapsto(c_1-c_0,c_2-c_1,c_3-c_2,\ldots).\\ \end{aligned}$$

Substituting $x = E$ in ((4)) shows that

::: {#o11 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\sum_{n=0}^m 1_{\{N=n\}}E^n = \sum_{k=0}^m N_k\Delta^k, \tag{5}$$

:::

where we used that $\Delta = E - I$ with $I$ denoting the [identity operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_operator). Note that $E^{0}$ and $\Delta^{0}$ equal the identity operator $I$ on the sequence space, $E^{k}$ and $\Delta^{k}$ denote the $k$-fold [composition](wikipedia://en/195947).

::: {#o12 .proof cue=math-proof name="Direct proof of () by the operator method"}

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="✳"}

**Direct proof of ((5)) by the operator method.**

To prove ((5)), we first want to verify the equation

$$\sum_{n=0}^m 1_{\{N=n\}}E^n=\prod_{j=1}^m(1_{A_j^{\mathrm c}}I+1_{A_j}E) \tag{✳}$$

:::

involving [indicator functions](wikipedia://en/240790) of the sets $A_{1}, ..., A_{m}$ and their [complements](wikipedia://en/54347) with respect to $\Omega$. Suppose an $\omega$ from $\Omega$ belongs to exactly $k$ sets out of $A_{1}, ..., A_{m}$, where $k \in \{0, ..., m\}$, for simplicity of notation say that $\omega$ only belongs to $A_{1}, ..., A_{k}$. Then the left-hand side of ((✳)) is $E^{k}$. On the right-hand side of ((✳)), the first $k$ factors equal $E$, the remaining ones equal $I$, their product is also $E^{k}$, hence the formula ((✳)) is true.

Note that

$$\begin{aligned} 1_{A_j^{\mathrm c}}I+1_{A_j}E &=I-1_{A_j}I+1_{A_j}E\\ &=I+1_{A_j}(E-I)=I+1_{A_j}\Delta,\qquad j\in\{0,\ldots,m\}. \end{aligned}$$

Inserting this result into equation ((✳)) and expanding the product gives

$$\sum_{n=0}^m 1_{\{N=n\}}E^n =\sum_{k=0}^m\sum_{\scriptstyle J\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}\atop\scriptstyle|J|=k} 1_{\cap_{j\in J}A_j}\Delta^k,$$

because the product of indicator functions is the indicator function of the intersection. Using the definition ((2)), the result ((5)) follows.

:::

Let $(\Delta^{k}c)_{0}$ denote the 0th [component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_component) of the $k$-fold [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) $\Delta^{k}$ applied to $c = (c_{0}, c_{1}, ..., c_{m}, ...)$, where $\Delta^{0}$ denotes the identity. Then ((3)) can be rewritten in a more compact way as

::: {#o14 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$\sum_{n=0}^m 1_{\{N=n\}}c_n = \sum_{k=0}^m N_k(\Delta^k c)_0. \tag{6}$$

:::

## Probabilistic versions {#Probabilistic_versions}

Consider arbitrary [events](wikipedia://en/22961) $A_{1}, ..., A_{m}$ in a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) $(\Omega, F, \mathbb{P})$ and let $\mathbf{E}$ denote the [expectation operator](wikipedia://en/9653). Then $N$ from ((1)) is the [random number](wikipedia://en/25685) of these events which occur simultaneously. Using $N_{k}$ from ((2)), define

::: {#o16 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$S_k=\mathbb{E}[N_k]=\sum_{\scriptstyle J\subset\{1,\ldots,m\}\atop\scriptstyle|J|=k} \mathbb{P}\biggl(\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\biggr),\qquad k\in\{0,\ldots,m\}, \tag{7}$$

:::

where the intersection over the empty index set is again defined as $\Omega$, hence $S_{0} = 1$. If the ring $R$ is also an [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) over the real or complex numbers, then taking the expectation of the coefficients in ((4)) and using the notation from ((7)),

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="4'"}

$$\sum_{n=0}^m \mathbb{P}(N=n)x^n = \sum_{k=0}^m S_k(x-1)^k \tag{4'}$$

:::

in $R[x]$. If $R$ is the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of real numbers, then this is the [probability-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability-generating_function) of the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of $N$.

Similarly, ((5)) and ((6)) yield

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="5'"}

$$\sum_{n=0}^m \mathbb{P}(N=n)E^n=\sum_{k=0}^m S_k\Delta^k \tag{5'}$$

:::

and, for every sequence $c = (c_{0}, c_{1}, c_{2}, c_{3}, ..., c_{m}, ...)$,

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="6'"}

$$\sum_{n=0}^m \mathbb{P}(N=n)\,c_n=\sum_{k=0}^m S_k\,(\Delta^kc)_0. \tag{6'}$$

:::

The quantity on the left-hand side of ((6')) is the expected value of $c_{N}$.

## Remarks {#Remarks}

1. In [actuarial science](wikipedia://en/189373), the name *Schuette–Nesbitt formula* refers to equation ((6')), where $V$ denotes the set of real numbers.
1. The left-hand side of equation ((5')) is a [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) of the [powers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28mathematics%29) of the shift operator $E$, it can be seen as the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of random operator $E^{N}$. Accordingly, the left-hand side of equation ((6')) is the expected value of random component $c_{N}$. Note that both have a [discrete probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_probability_distribution) with finite [support](wikipedia://en/6591796), hence expectations are just the well-defined finite sums.
1. The probabilistic version of the [inclusion–exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/342684) can be derived from equation ((6')) by choosing the sequence $c = (0, 1, 1, ...)$: the left-hand side reduces to the probability of the event $\{N \ge 1\}$, which is the union of $A_{1}, ..., A_{m}$, and the right-hand side is $S_{1} - S_{2} + S_{3} - ... - (-1)^{m}S_{m}$, because $(\Delta^{0}c)_{0} = 0$ and $(\Delta^{k}c)_{0} = -(-1)^{k}$ for $k \in \{1, ..., m\}$.
1. Equations ((5)), ((5')), ((6)) and ((6')) are also true when the shift operator and the difference operator are considered on a subspace like the [$\ell^{p}$ spaces](wikipedia://en/45194#.E2.84.93p_spaces).
1. If desired, the formulae ((5)), ((5')), ((6)) and ((6')) can be considered in finite dimensions, because only the first $m + 1$ components of the sequences matter. Hence, represent the linear shift operator $E$ and the linear difference operator $\Delta$ as mappings of the $(m + 1)$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) into itself, given by the $(m + 1) \times (m + 1)$-[matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859)

$$E=\begin{pmatrix} 0&1&0&\cdots&0\\ 0&0&1&\ddots&\vdots\\ \vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\ 0&\cdots&0&0&1\\ 0&\cdots&0&0&0 \end{pmatrix}, \qquad \Delta=\begin{pmatrix} -1&1&0&\cdots&0\\ 0&-1&1&\ddots&\vdots\\ \vdots&\ddots&\ddots&\ddots&0\\ 0&\cdots&0&-1&1\\ 0&\cdots&0&0&-1 \end{pmatrix},$$

and let $I$ denote the $(m + 1)$-dimensional [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). Then ((6)) and ((6')) hold for every [vector](wikipedia://en/32370) $c = (c_{0}, c_{1}, ..., c_{m})^{T}$ in $(m + 1)$-dimensional Euclidean space, where the exponent $T$ in the definition of $c$ denotes the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844).

1. Equations ((5)) and ((5')) hold for an arbitrary linear operator $E$ as long as $\Delta$ is the difference of $E$ and the identity operator $I$.
1. The probabilistic versions ((4')), ((5')) and ((6')) can be generalized to every [finite measure space](wikipedia://en/4316185).

For textbook presentations of the probabilistic Schuette–Nesbitt formula ((6')) and their applications to actuarial science, cf. (Gerber 1997). Chapter 8, or (Bowers Gerber Hickman Jones 1997), Chapter 18 and the Appendix, pp. 577–578.

## History {#History}

For [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) events, the formula ((6')) appeared in a discussion of Robert P. White and T.N.E. Greville's paper by Donald R. Schuette and [Cecil J. Nesbitt](wikipedia://en/14373679), see (Schuette Nesbitt 1959). In the two-page note (Gerber 1979), Hans U. Gerber, called it Schuette–Nesbitt formula and generalized it to arbitrary events. Christian Buchta, see (Buchta 1994), noticed the combinatorial nature of the formula and published the elementary [combinatorial proof](wikipedia://en/741875) of ((3)).

Cecil J. Nesbitt, [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD), [F.S.A.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Actuaries#FSA_designation), M.A.A.A., received his [mathematical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_education) at the [University of Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Toronto) and the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in [Princeton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton,_New_Jersey). He taught [actuarial mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuarial_mathematics) at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan) from 1938 to 1980. He served the [Society of Actuaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_of_Actuaries) from 1985 to 1987 as Vice-President for Research and Studies. Professor Nesbitt died in 2001. (Short [CV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curriculum_vitae) taken from (Bowers Gerber Hickman Jones 1997), page xv.)

Donald Richard Schuette was a PhD student of C. Nesbitt, he later became professor at the [University of Wisconsin–Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin–Madison).

The probabilistic version of the Schuette–Nesbitt formula ((6')) generalizes much older formulae of [Waring](wikipedia://en/9723), which express the probability of the events $\{N = n\}$ and $\{N \ge n\}$ in terms of $S_{1}$, $S_{2}$, ..., $S_{m}$. More precisely, with $\textstyle\binom kn$ denoting the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668),

::: {#o28 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$\mathbb{P}(N=n)=\sum_{k=n}^m(-1)^{k-n}\binom kn S_k, \qquad n\in\{0,\ldots,m\}, \tag{8}$$

:::

and

::: {#o29 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

$$\mathbb{P}(N\ge n)=\sum_{k=n}^m(-1)^{k-n}\binom{k-1}{n-1}S_k,\qquad n\in\{1,\ldots,m\}, \tag{9}$$

:::

see (Feller 1968), Sections IV.3 and IV.5, respectively.

To see that these formulae are special cases of the probabilistic version of the Schuette–Nesbitt formula, note that by the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677)

$$\Delta^k=(E-I)^k=\sum_{j=0}^k\binom kj (-1)^{k-j}E^j,\qquad k\in\mathbb{N}_0.$$

Applying this operator identity to the sequence $c = (0, ..., 0, 1, 0, 0, ...)$ with $n$ leading zeros and noting that $(E^{j}c)_{0} = 1$ if $j = n$ and $(E^{j}c)_{0} = 0$ otherwise, the formula ((8)) for $\{N = n\}$ follows from ((6')).

Applying the identity to $c = (0, ..., 0, 1, 1, 1, ...)$ with $n$ leading zeros and noting that $(E^{j}c)_{0} = 1$ if $j \ge n$ and $(E^{j}c)_{0} = 0$ otherwise, equation ((6')) implies that

$$\mathbb{P}(N\ge n)=\sum_{k=n}^m S_k\sum_{j=n}^k\binom kj(-1)^{k-j}.$$

Expanding $(1 - 1)^{k}$ using the binomial theorem and using [equation (11) of the formulas involving binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668#Formulas_involving_binomial_coefficients), we obtain

$$\sum_{j=n}^k\binom kj(-1)^{k-j} =-\sum_{j=0}^{n-1}\binom kj(-1)^{k-j} =(-1)^{k-n}\binom{k-1}{n-1}.$$

Hence, we have the formula ((9)) for $\{N \ge n\}$.

## Applications {#Applications}

### In actuarial science {#In_actuarial_science}

::: {#o34 .problem cue=inline-head name="Problem"}

**Problem:** Suppose there are $m$ persons aged $x_{1}, ..., x_{m}$ with remaining random (but independent) lifetimes $T_{1}, ..., T_{m}$. Suppose the group signs a life insurance contract which pays them after $t$ years the amount $c_{n}$ if exactly $n$ persons out of $m$ are still alive after $t$ years. How high is the expected payout of this insurance contract in $t$ years?

**Solution:** Let $A_{j}$ denote the event that person $j$ survives $t$ years, which means that $A_{j} = \{T_{j} > t\}$. In [actuarial notation](wikipedia://en/852721) the probability of this event is denoted by ${}_{t} p_{x_{j}}$ and can be taken from a [life table](wikipedia://en/1614128). Use independence to calculate the probability of intersections. Calculate $S_{1}, ..., S_{m}$ and use the probabilistic version of the Schuette–Nesbitt formula ((6')) to calculate the expected value of $c_{N}$.

:::

### In probability theory {#In_probability_theory}

Let $\sigma$ be a [random permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_permutation) of the set $\{1, ..., m\}$ and let $A_{j}$ denote the event that $j$ is a [fixed point](wikipedia://en/449738) of $\sigma$, meaning that $A_{j} = \{\sigma(j) = j\}$. When the numbers in $J$, which is a subset of $\{1, ..., m\}$, are fixed points, then there are $(m - \lvert J\rvert)!$ ways to permute the remaining $m - \lvert J\rvert$ numbers, hence

$$\mathbb{P}\biggl(\bigcap_{j\in J}A_j\biggr)=\frac{(m-|J|)!}{m!}.$$

By the combinatorical interpretation of the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668), there are $\textstyle\binom mk$ different choices of a subset $J$ of $\{1, ..., m\}$ with $k$ elements, hence ((7)) simplifies to

$$S_k=\binom mk \frac{(m-k)!}{m!}=\frac1{k!}.$$

Therefore, using ((4')), the [probability-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability-generating_function) of the number $N$ of fixed points is given by

$$\mathbb{E}[x^N]=\sum_{k=0}^m\frac{(x-1)^k}{k!},\qquad x\in\mathbb{R}.$$

This is the [partial sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum) of the infinite series giving the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) at $x - 1$, which in turn is the [probability-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability-generating_function#Examples) of the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with parameter $1$. Therefore, as $m$ tends to [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824), the distribution of $N$ [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_in_distribution) to the Poisson distribution with parameter $1$.

:::
