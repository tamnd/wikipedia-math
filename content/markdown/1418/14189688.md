# Cardinal function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cardinal function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **cardinal function** (or **cardinal invariant**) is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that returns [cardinal numbers](wikipedia://en/6173).

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bounding number"}

## Cardinal functions in set theory {#Cardinal_functions_in_set_theory}

- The most frequently used cardinal function is the function that assigns to a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *A* its [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174), denoted by |*A*|.
- [Aleph numbers](wikipedia://en/293961) and [beth numbers](wikipedia://en/393359) can both be seen as cardinal functions defined on [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932).
- [Cardinal arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_arithmetic) operations are examples of functions from cardinal numbers (or pairs of them) to cardinal numbers.
- Cardinal characteristics of a (proper) [ideal](wikipedia://en/12633368) *I* of [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of *X* are:

$${\rm add}(I) = \min\{|\mathcal{A}| : \mathcal{A}\subseteq I \wedge \bigcup \mathcal{A} \notin I\}.$$

The "additivity" of *I* is the smallest number of sets from *I* whose [union](wikipedia://en/47949) is not in *I* any more. As any ideal is closed under finite unions, this number is always at least $\aleph_0$; if *I* is a [σ-ideal](wikipedia://en/1567410), then $\operatorname{add}(I) \ge \aleph_1.$

$$\operatorname{cov}(I) = \min\{|\mathcal{A}| : \mathcal{A} \subseteq I \wedge \bigcup \mathcal{A} = X\}.$$

The "covering number" of *I* is the smallest number of sets from *I* whose union is all of *X*. As *X* itself is not in *I*, we must have add(*I*) ≤ cov(*I*).

$$\operatorname{non}(I) = \min\{|A| : A \subseteq X\ \wedge\ A \notin I\},$$

The "uniformity number" of *I* (sometimes also written ${\rm unif}(I)$) is the size of the smallest set not in *I*. Assuming *I* contains all [singletons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set), add(*I*) ≤ non(*I*).

$${\rm cof}(I) = \min\{|\mathcal{B}| : \mathcal{B} \subseteq I \wedge \forall A \in I(\exists B \in \mathcal{B})(A\subseteq B)\}.$$

The "cofinality" of *I* is the [cofinality](wikipedia://en/6693) of the [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) (*I*, ⊆). It is easy to see that we must have non(*I*) ≤ cof(*I*) and cov(*I*) ≤ cof(*I*).

In the case that $I$ is an ideal closely related to the structure of the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438), such as the ideal of [Lebesgue null sets](wikipedia://en/21520#Lebesgue_measure) or the ideal of [meagre sets](wikipedia://en/226975), these cardinal invariants are referred to as [cardinal characteristics of the continuum](wikipedia://en/33911166).

- For a [preordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preordered_set) $(\mathbb{P},\sqsubseteq)$ the **bounding number** ${\mathfrak b}(\mathbb{P})$ and **dominating number** ${\mathfrak d}(\mathbb{P})$ are defined as

$${\mathfrak b}(\mathbb{P}) = \min\big\{|Y| : Y \subseteq \mathbb{P}\ \wedge\ (\forall x\in \mathbb{P})(\exists y\in Y)(y\not\sqsubseteq x)\big\},$$

$${\mathfrak d}(\mathbb{P}) = \min\big\{|Y| : Y \subseteq \mathbb{P}\ \wedge\ (\forall x\in \mathbb{P})(\exists y\in Y)(x\sqsubseteq y)\big\}.$$

- In [PCF theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCF_theory) the cardinal function $pp_\kappa(\lambda)$ is used.[^o4]

## Cardinal functions in topology {#Cardinal_functions_in_topology}

Cardinal functions are widely used in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) as a tool for describing various [topological properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_properties).[^o7][^o8] Below are some examples. (Note: some authors, arguing that "there are no finite cardinal numbers in [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649)",[^o9] prefer to define the cardinal functions listed below so that they never taken on finite cardinal numbers as values; this requires modifying some of the definitions given below, for example by adding "$\;\; + \;\aleph_0$" to the right-hand side of the definitions, etc.)

- Perhaps the simplest cardinal invariants of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ are its cardinality and the cardinality of its topology, denoted respectively by $|X|$ and $o(X).$
- The **[weight](wikipedia://en/59220#Weight_and_character)** $\operatorname{w}(X)$ of a topological space $X$ is the cardinality of the smallest [base](wikipedia://en/59220) for $X.$ When $\operatorname{w}(X) = \aleph_0$ the space $X$ is said to be *[second countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countable)*.
  - The **$\pi$-weight** of a space $X$ is the cardinality of the smallest $\pi$-base for $X.$ (A $\pi$-base is a set of non-[empty](wikipedia://en/9566) [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) whose supersets includes all opens.)
  - The **network weight** $\operatorname{nw}(X)$ of $X$ is the smallest cardinality of a network for $X.$ A *network* is a [family](wikipedia://en/723043) $\mathcal{N}$ of sets, for which, for all points $x$ and [open neighbourhoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_neighbourhood) $U$ containing $x,$ there exists $B$ in $\mathcal{N}$ for which $x \in B \subseteq U.$
- The **[character](wikipedia://en/59220#Weight_and_character)** of a topological space $X$ **at a point** $x$ is the cardinality of the smallest [local base](wikipedia://en/1714290) for $x.$ The **character** of space $X$ is $$\chi(X) = \sup \; \{\chi(x,X) : x\in X\}.$$ When $\chi(X) = \aleph_0$ the space $X$ is said to be *[first countable](wikipedia://en/904940)*.
- The **density** $\operatorname{d}(X)$ of a space $X$ is the cardinality of the smallest [dense subset](wikipedia://en/23523594) of $X.$ When $\rm{d}(X) = \aleph_0$ the space $X$ is said to be *[separable](wikipedia://en/27855)*.
- The **[Lindelöf number](wikipedia://en/310480#Generalisation)** $\operatorname{L}(X)$ of a space $X$ is the smallest infinite cardinality such that every [open cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cover) has a subcover of cardinality no more than $\operatorname{L}(X).$ When $\rm{L}(X) = \aleph_0$ the space $X$ is said to be a *[Lindelöf space](wikipedia://en/310480)*.
- The **cellularity** or **Suslin number** of a space $X$ is

$$\operatorname{c}(X) = \sup\{|\mathcal{U}| : \mathcal{U} \text{ is a family of mutually disjoint non-empty open subsets of } X\}.$$

  - The **hereditary cellularity** (sometimes called **spread**) is the least upper bound of cellularities of its subsets: $$s(X) = {\rm hc}(X) = \sup\{ {\rm c} (Y) : Y \subseteq X \}$$ or $$s(X) = \sup\{|Y|:Y \subseteq X \text{ with the subspace topology is discrete} \}$$ where "discrete" means that it is a [discrete topological space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topological_space).
- The **extent** of a space $X$ is $$e(X) = \sup\{|Y| : Y \subseteq X \text{ is closed and discrete}\}.$$ So $X$ has countable extent exactly when it has no [uncountable](wikipedia://en/32248) [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subset) discrete subset.
- The **tightness** $t(x, X)$ of a topological space $X$ **at a point** $x \in X$ is the smallest cardinal number $\alpha$ such that, whenever $x\in{\rm cl}_X(Y)$ for some subset $Y$ of $X,$ there exists a subset $Z$ of $Y$ with $|Z| \leq \alpha,$ such that $x\in \operatorname{cl}_X(Z).$ Symbolically, $$t(x, X) = \sup \left\{ \min \{|Z| : Z\subseteq Y\ \wedge\ x\in {\rm cl}_X(Z)\} : Y \subseteq X\ \wedge\ x \in {\rm cl}_X(Y)\right\}.$$ The **tightness of a space** $X$ is $$t(X) = \sup\{t(x, X) : x \in X\}.$$ When $t(X) = \aleph_0$ the space $X$ is said to be *[countably generated](wikipedia://en/7809399)* or *[countably tight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_tightness)*.
  - The **augmented tightness** of a space $X,$ $t^+(X)$ is the smallest [regular cardinal](wikipedia://en/374128) $\alpha$ such that for any $Y \subseteq X,$ $x\in{\rm cl}_X(Y)$ there is a subset $Z$ of $Y$ with cardinality less than $\alpha,$ such that $x\in{\rm cl}_X(Z).$

### Basic inequalities {#Basic_inequalities}

$$c(X) \leq d(X) \leq w(X) \leq o(X) \leq 2^{|X|}$$

$$e(X) \leq s(X)$$

$$\chi(X) \leq w(X)$$

$$\operatorname{nw}(X) \leq w(X) \text{ and } o(X) \leq 2^{\operatorname{nw}(X)}$$

## Cardinal functions in Boolean algebras {#Cardinal_functions_in_Boolean_algebras}

Cardinal functions are often used in the study of [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959).[^o12][^o13] We can mention, for example, the following functions:

- **Cellularity** $c(\mathbb{B})$ of a Boolean algebra $\mathbb{B}$ is the supremum of the cardinalities of [antichains](wikipedia://en/348973) in $\mathbb{B}$.
- **Length** ${\rm length}(\mathbb{B})$ of a Boolean algebra $\mathbb{B}$ is

$${\rm length}(\mathbb{B}) = \sup\big\{|A| : A \subseteq \mathbb{B} \text{ is a chain} \big\}$$

- **Depth** ${\rm depth}(\mathbb{B})$ of a Boolean algebra $\mathbb{B}$ is

$${\rm depth}(\mathbb{B}) = \sup\big\{|A| : A \subseteq \mathbb{B} \text{ is a well-ordered subset} \big\}.$$

- **Incomparability** ${\rm Inc}(\mathbb{B})$ of a Boolean algebra $\mathbb{B}$ is

$${\rm Inc}({\mathbb B}) = \sup\big\{|A| : A \subseteq \mathbb{B} \text{ such that } \forall a,b \in A \big(a \neq b\ \Rightarrow \neg (a\leq b\ \vee \ b \leq a)\big)\big\}.$$

- **Pseudo-weight** $\pi(\mathbb{B})$ of a Boolean algebra $\mathbb{B}$ is

$$\pi(\mathbb{B}) = \min\big\{|A| : A \subseteq \mathbb{B}\setminus \{0\} \text{ such that } \forall b \in B\setminus\{0\} \big(\exists a \in A\big)\big(a \leq b\big)\big\}.$$

## Cardinal functions in algebra {#Cardinal_functions_in_algebra}

Examples of cardinal functions in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) are:

- [Index of a subgroup](wikipedia://en/230581) *H* of *G* is the number of [cosets](wikipedia://en/98759).
- [Dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* is the cardinality of any [Hamel basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_basis) of *V*.
- More generally, for a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) *M* over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* we define rank ${\rm rank}(M)$ as the cardinality of any basis of this [module](wikipedia://en/276410).
- For a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) *W* of a vector space *V* we define [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) of *W* (with respect to *V*).
- For any [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) it is possible to consider the minimal cardinality of [generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generating_set) of the structure.
- For [algebraic](wikipedia://en/2125) [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634), [algebraic degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) and [separable degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_degree) are often employed (the algebraic degree equals the dimension of the extension as a vector space over the smaller field).
- For non-algebraic field extensions, [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) is likewise used.
