# Monte Carlo methods for option pricing

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Monte Carlo methods for option pricing"}

In [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680), a **Monte Carlo option model** uses [Monte Carlo methods](wikipedia://en/56098)[^o2] to calculate the value of an [option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_%28finance%29) with multiple sources of uncertainty or with complicated features.[^o4] The first application to option pricing was by [Phelim Boyle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phelim_Boyle) in 1977 (for [European options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_option)). In 1996, M. Broadie and P. Glasserman showed how to price [Asian options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_option) by Monte Carlo. An important development was the introduction in 1996 by Carriere of Monte Carlo methods for options with [early exercise features](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_style#American_and_European_options).

:::

## Methodology {#Methodology}

As [is standard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valuation_of_options), Monte Carlo valuation relies on [risk neutral valuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_pricing#Risk_neutral_valuation).[^o4] Here the price of the option is its [discounted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present_value) [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653); see [risk neutrality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk_neutrality) and [rational pricing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_pricing#Risk_neutral_valuation). The technique applied then, is (1) to generate a large number of possible, but [random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random), price paths for the [underlying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underlying) (or underlyings) via [simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation), and (2) to then calculate the associated [exercise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exercise_%28options%29) [value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_time_value#Intrinsic_value) (i.e. "payoff") of the option for each path. (3) These payoffs are then averaged and (4) discounted to today. This result is the value of the option.[^o5]

This approach, although relatively straightforward, allows for increasing complexity:

- An [option on equity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Option_%28finance%29) may be modelled with one source of uncertainty: the price of the underlying [stock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock) in question.[^o5] Here the price of the [underlying instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underlying_instrument) $\ S_t \,$ is usually modelled such that it follows a [geometric Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/203523) with constant drift $\mu \,$ and [volatility](wikipedia://en/11930108) $\sigma \,$. So: $dS_t = \mu S_t\,dt + \sigma S_t\,dW_t \,$, where $dW_t \,$ is found via a [random sampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sampling) from a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462); see [further](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black–Scholes#The_model) under [Black–Scholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black–Scholes). Since the underlying random process is the same, for enough price paths, the value of a [european option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_option) here should be [the same as under Black–Scholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_%28logic%29). More generally though, simulation is employed for [path dependent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_dependence) [exotic derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotic_derivatives), such as [Asian options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_options).
- In other cases, the source of uncertainty may be at a remove. For example, for [bond options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_option)[^o6] the underlying is a [bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_%28finance%29), but the source of uncertainty is the annualized [interest rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate) (i.e. the [short rate](wikipedia://en/1219048#The_short_rate)). Here, for each randomly generated [yield curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yield_curve) we observe a different [resultant bond price](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_valuation#Arbitrage-free_pricing_approach) on the option's exercise date; this bond price is then the input for the determination of the option's payoff. The same approach is used in valuing [swaptions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swaption),[^o7] where the value of the underlying [swap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swap_%28finance%29) is also a function of the evolving interest rate. (Whereas these options are more commonly valued using [lattice based models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_model_%28finance%29), as above, for path dependent [interest rate derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate_derivative) – such as [CMOs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collateralized_mortgage_obligation) – simulation is the *primary* technique employed.[^o8]) For the models used to simulate the interest-rate see [further](wikipedia://en/1219048#Particular_short-rate_models) under [Short-rate model](wikipedia://en/1219048); "to create realistic interest rate simulations" [Multi-factor short-rate models](wikipedia://en/1219048#Multi-factor_short-rate_models) are sometimes employed.[^o9] To apply simulation here, the analyst must first "calibrate" the model parameters, such that bond prices produced by the model [best fit](wikipedia://en/555425) observed market prices.
- Monte Carlo Methods allow for a [compounding in the uncertainty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_probability).[^o10] For example, where the underlying is denominated in a foreign currency, an additional source of uncertainty will be the [exchange rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rate): the underlying price and the exchange rate must be separately simulated and then combined to determine the value of the underlying in the local currency. In all such models, [correlation](wikipedia://en/157057) between the underlying sources of risk is also incorporated; see Cholesky decomposition#Monte Carlo simulation. Further complications, such as the impact of [commodity prices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodity_markets) or [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation) on the underlying, can also be introduced. Since simulation can accommodate complex problems of this sort, it is often used in analysing [real options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_options)[^o4] where management's decision at any point is a function of multiple underlying variables.
- Simulation can similarly be used to value options where the payoff depends on the value of multiple underlying assets[^o11] such as a [Basket option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basket_option) or [Rainbow option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow_option). Here, correlation between asset returns is likewise incorporated.[^o12]
- As required, Monte Carlo simulation can be used with any type of [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543), including changing distributions: the modeller is not limited to [normal](wikipedia://en/21462) or [log-normal](wikipedia://en/102476) returns;[^o19] see for example [Datar–Mathews method for real option valuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Datar–Mathews_method_for_real_option_valuation). Additionally, the [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895) of the underlying(s) may be specified so as to exhibit [jumps](wikipedia://en/2180750) or [mean reversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_reverting_process) or both; this feature makes simulation the primary valuation method applicable to [energy derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_derivative).[^o13] Further, some models even allow for (randomly) varying [statistical](wikipedia://en/413291) (and other) [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) of the sources of uncertainty. For example, in models incorporating [stochastic volatility](wikipedia://en/5740025), the [volatility](wikipedia://en/11930108) of the underlying changes with time; see [Heston model](wikipedia://en/10163132).

## Least Square Monte Carlo {#Least_Square_Monte_Carlo}

Least Square Monte Carlo is a technique for valuing early-exercise options (i.e. [Bermudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bermudan_option) or [American options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_option)). It was first introduced by Jacques Carriere in 1996.[^o15]

It is based on the iteration of a two step procedure:

- First, a [backward induction](wikipedia://en/2060912) process is performed in which a value is recursively assigned to every state at every timestep. The value is defined as the [least squares regression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_squares_regression) against market price of the option value at that [state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_prices) and time (-step). Option value for this regression is defined as the value of exercise possibilities (dependent on market price) plus the value of the timestep value which that exercise would result in (defined in the previous step of the process).[^o16]
- Secondly, when all states are valued for every timestep, the value of the option is calculated by moving through the timesteps and states by making an optimal decision on option exercise at every step on the hand of a price path and the value of the state that would result in. This second step can be done with multiple price paths to add a stochastic effect to the procedure.[^o15]

## Application {#Application}

As can be seen, Monte Carlo Methods are particularly useful in the valuation of options with multiple sources of uncertainty or with complicated features, which would make them difficult to value through a straightforward [Black–Scholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black–Scholes)-style or [lattice based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BOPM) computation. The technique is thus widely used in valuing path dependent structures like [lookback-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lookback_option) and [Asian options](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asian_option)[^o19] and in [real options analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_options_analysis).[^o4][^o10] Additionally, as above, the modeller is not limited as to the probability distribution assumed.[^o19]

Conversely, however, if an [analytical technique](wikipedia://en/585143) for valuing the option exists—or even a [numeric technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_methods), such as a (modified) [pricing tree](wikipedia://en/250074)[^o19]—Monte Carlo methods will usually be too slow to be competitive. They are, in a sense, a method of last resort;[^o19] see [further](wikipedia://en/1358940#Level_of_complexity) under [Monte Carlo methods in finance](wikipedia://en/1358940). With faster computing capability this computational constraint is less of a concern.
