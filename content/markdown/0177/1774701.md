# Uniformizable space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Uniformizable space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* is **uniformizable** if [there exists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/There_exists) a [uniform structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_structure) on *X* that [induces](wikipedia://en/32339#Topology_of_uniform_spaces) the topology of *X*. Equivalently, *X* is uniformizable if and only if it is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to a uniform space (equipped with the topology induced by the uniform structure).

:::

Any ([pseudo](wikipedia://en/68503))[metrizable space](wikipedia://en/19738) is uniformizable since the (pseudo)metric uniformity induces the (pseudo)metric topology. The converse fails: There are uniformizable spaces that are not (pseudo)metrizable. However, the topology of a uniformizable space can always be induced by a *[family](wikipedia://en/20696)* of [pseudometrics](wikipedia://en/68503); indeed, this is because any uniformity on a set *X* can be [defined](wikipedia://en/32339#Pseudometrics_definition) by a family of pseudometrics.

Showing that a space is uniformizable is much simpler than showing it is metrizable. In fact, uniformizability is equivalent to a common [separation axiom](wikipedia://en/40117302):

*A topological space is uniformizable if and only if it is [completely regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_regular).*

## Induced uniformity {#Induced_uniformity}

One way to construct a uniform structure on a topological space *X* is to take the [initial uniformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_uniformity) on *X* induced by *C*(*X*), the family of real-valued [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) on *X*. This is the coarsest uniformity on *X* for which all such functions are [uniformly continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_continuous). A subbase for this uniformity is given by the set of all [entourages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entourage_%28topology%29)

$$D_{f,\varepsilon} = \{(x,y)\in X\times X : |f(x) - f(y)| < \varepsilon\}$$

where *f* ∈ *C*(*X*) and *ε* > 0.

The uniform topology generated by the above uniformity is the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123) induced by the family *C*(*X*). In general, this topology will be [coarser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coarser_topology) than the given topology on *X*. The two topologies will coincide if and only if *X* is completely regular.

## Fine uniformity {#Fine_uniformity}

Given a uniformizable space *X* there is a finest uniformity on *X* compatible with the topology of *X* called the **fine uniformity** or **universal uniformity**. A uniform space is said to be **fine** if it has the fine uniformity generated by its uniform topology.

The fine uniformity is characterized by the [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245): any continuous function *f* from a fine space *X* to a uniform space *Y* is uniformly continuous. This implies that the [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) *F* : **CReg** → **Uni** that assigns to any completely regular space *X* the fine uniformity on *X* is [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) to the [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) sending a uniform space to its underlying completely regular space.

Explicitly, the fine uniformity on a completely regular space *X* is generated by all open neighborhoods *D* of the diagonal in *X* × *X* (with the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910)) such that there exists a sequence *D*<sub>1</sub>, *D*<sub>2</sub>, … of open neighborhoods of the diagonal with *D* = *D*<sub>1</sub> and $D_n\circ D_n\subseteq D_{n-1}$.

The uniformity on a completely regular space *X* induced by *C*(*X*) (see the previous section) is not always the fine uniformity.

:::
