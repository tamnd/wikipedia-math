# (B, N) pair

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="(B, N) pair"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **(*B*, *N*) pair** is a structure on [groups of Lie type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groups_of_Lie_type) that allows one to give uniform [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) of many results, instead of giving a large number of case-by-case proofs. Roughly speaking, it shows that all such [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) are similar to the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). They were introduced by the mathematician [Jacques Tits](wikipedia://en/219570), and are also sometimes known as **Tits systems**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **(*B*, *N*) pair** is a pair of [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) *B* and *N* of a group *G* such that the following axioms hold:

- *G* is generated by *B* and *N*.
- The [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429), *T*, of *B* and *N* is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of *N*.
- The group *W* = *N*/*T* is generated by a set *S* of elements of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) 2 such that
  - If *s* is an element of *S* and *w* is an element of *W* then *sBw* is contained in the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of *BswB* and *BwB*.
  - No element of *S* normalizes *B*.

The set *S* is uniquely determined by *B* and *N* and the pair (*W*, *S*) is a **[Coxeter system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_system)**.[^n1]

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

### Terminology {#Terminology}

BN pairs are closely related to [reductive groups](wikipedia://en/1088386) and the terminology in both subjects overlaps. The size of *S* is called the **rank**. We call

- *B* the (standard) **[Borel subgroup](wikipedia://en/588356)**,
- *T* the (standard) **[Cartan subgroup](wikipedia://en/4838153)**, and
- *W* the **[Weyl group](wikipedia://en/296332)**.

A subgroup of *G* is called

- **parabolic** if it contains a conjugate of *B*,
- **standard parabolic** if, in fact, it contains *B* itself, and
- a **Borel** (or **minimal parabolic**) if it is a conjugate of *B*.

## Examples {#Examples}

Abstract examples of (*B*, *N*) pairs arise from certain group actions.

- Suppose that *G* is any [doubly transitive permutation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_transitive_permutation_group) on a set *E* with more than 2 elements. We let *B* be the subgroup of *G* fixing a point *x*, and we let *N* be the subgroup fixing or exchanging 2 points *x* and *y*. The subgroup *T* is then the set of elements fixing both *x* and *y*, and *W* has order 2 and its nontrivial element is represented by anything exchanging *x* and *y*.
- Conversely, if *G* has a (*B*, *N*) pair of rank 1, then the action of *G* on the cosets of *B* is [doubly transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_transitive). So (*B*, *N*) pairs of rank 1 are more or less the same as doubly transitive actions on sets with more than 2 elements.

More concrete examples of (*B*, *N*) pairs can be found in reductive groups.

- Suppose that *G* is the general linear group GL<sub>*n*</sub>*K* over a field *K*. We take *B* to be the [upper triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859), *T* to be the [diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_matrices), and *N* to be the [monomial matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomial_matrices), i.e. matrices with exactly one non-zero element in each row and column. There are *n* − 1 generators, represented by the matrices obtained by swapping two adjacent rows of a diagonal matrix. The Weyl group is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on *n* letters.
- More generally, if **G** is a reductive group over a field *K* then the group *G* = **G**(*K*) has a (*B*, *N*) pair in which
  - *B* = **P**(*K*), where **P** is a minimal parabolic subgroup of **G**, and
  - *N* = **N**(*K*), where **N** is the normalizer of a split maximal torus contained in **P**.[^n2]
- In particular, any [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) [group of Lie type](wikipedia://en/1691490) has the structure of a (*B*, *N*) pair.
  - Over the [field of two elements](wikipedia://en/11137263), the Cartan subgroup is trivial in this example.
- A semisimple simply-connected [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) over a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) has a (*B*, *N*) pair where *B* is an [Iwahori subgroup](wikipedia://en/32092579).

## Properties {#Properties}

### Bruhat decomposition {#Bruhat_decomposition}

The [Bruhat decomposition](wikipedia://en/1139142) states that *G = BWB*. More precisely, the [double cosets](wikipedia://en/982771) *B*\\*G*/*B* are represented by a [set of lifts](wikipedia://en/897733) of *W* to *N*.[^n3]

### Parabolic subgroups {#Parabolic_subgroups}

Every parabolic subgroup equals its [normalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalizer) in *G*.[^n4]

Every standard parabolic is of the form *BW*(*X*)*B* for some subset *X* of *S*, where *W*(*X*) denotes the Coxeter subgroup generated by *X*. Moreover, two standard parabolics are conjugate [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) their sets *X* are the same. Hence there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between subsets of *S* and standard parabolics.[^n5] More generally, this bijection extends to conjugacy classes of parabolic subgroups.[^n4]

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Tits's simplicity theorem"}

### Tits's simplicity theorem {#Tits's_simplicity_theorem}

BN-pairs can be used to prove that many groups of Lie type are [simple](wikipedia://en/47600) modulo their [centers](wikipedia://en/7125). More precisely, if *G* has a *BN*-pair such that *B* is a [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012), the intersection of all conjugates of *B* is trivial, and the set of generators of *W* cannot be decomposed into two [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) commuting sets, then *G* is simple whenever it is a [perfect group](wikipedia://en/585271). In practice all of these conditions except for *G* being perfect are easy to check. Checking that *G* is perfect needs some slightly messy calculations (and in fact there are a few small groups of Lie type which are not perfect). But showing that a group is perfect is usually far easier than showing it is simple.
