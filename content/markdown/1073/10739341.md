# Dependency relation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dependency relation"}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), in particular in [concurrency theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurrency_%28computer_science%29), a **dependency relation** is a [symmetric](wikipedia://en/200459) and [reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458)[^o2] [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) on a finite domain $\Sigma$;[^o2] i.e. a finite [tolerance relation](wikipedia://en/26014321). That is, it is a finite set of [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) $D$, such that

:::

- If $(a,b)\in D$ then $(b,a) \in D$ (symmetric)
- If $a \in \Sigma$, then $(a,a) \in D$ (reflexive)

In general, dependency relations are not [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463); thus, they generalize the notion of an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) by discarding transitivity.

$\Sigma$ is also called the [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet_%28computer_science%29) on which $D$ is defined. The **independency** induced by $D$ is the binary relation $I$

$$I = (\Sigma \times \Sigma) \setminus D$$

That is, the independency is the set of all ordered pairs that are not in $D$. The independency relation is symmetric and irreflexive. Conversely, given any symmetric and irreflexive relation $I$ on a finite alphabet, the relation

$$D = (\Sigma \times \Sigma) \setminus I$$

is a dependency relation.

The pair $(\Sigma, D)$ is called the **concurrent alphabet**.[^o3] The pair $(\Sigma, I)$ is called the **independency alphabet** or **reliance alphabet**, but this term may also refer to the triple $(\Sigma, D, I)$ (with $I$ induced by $D$).[^o4] Elements $x,y \in \Sigma$ are called **dependent** if $xDy$ holds, and **independent**, else (i.e. if $xIy$ holds).[^o2]

Given a reliance alphabet $(\Sigma, D, I)$, a symmetric and irreflexive relation $\doteq$ can be defined on the [free monoid](wikipedia://en/523166) $\Sigma^*$ of all possible strings of finite length by: $x a b y \doteq x b a y$ for all strings $x, y \in \Sigma^*$ and all independent symbols $a, b \in I$. The [equivalence closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_closure) of $\doteq$ is denoted $\equiv$ or $\equiv_{(\Sigma, D, I)}$ and called $(\Sigma, D, I)$-equivalence. Informally, $p \equiv q$ holds if the string $p$ can be transformed into $q$ by a finite sequence of swaps of adjacent independent symbols. The [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of $\equiv$ are called [traces](wikipedia://en/10739141),[^o2] and are studied in [trace theory](wikipedia://en/10815131).

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Relación de dependencia.svg.*

Given the alphabet $\Sigma=\{a,b,c\}$, a possible dependency relation is $D = \{ (a,b),\, (b,a),\, (a,c),\, (c,a),\, (a,a),\, (b,b),\, (c,c) \}$, see picture.

The corresponding independency is $I=\{(b,c),\,(c,b)\}$. Then e.g. the symbols $b,c$ are independent of one another, and e.g. $a,b$ are dependent. The string $a c b b a$ is equivalent to $a b c b a$ and to $a b b c a$, but to no other string.
