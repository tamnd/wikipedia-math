# Catenoid

*Image: Catenoid.svg.* A catenoid

*Image: Catenoid.gif.* A catenoid obtained from the rotation of a catenary

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Catenoid"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **catenoid** is a type of [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970), arising by rotating a [catenary](wikipedia://en/7163) curve about an axis (a [surface of revolution](wikipedia://en/329549)).[^o2] It is a [minimal surface](wikipedia://en/276734), meaning that it occupies the least area when bounded by a closed space.[^o3] It was formally described in 1744 by the mathematician [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902).

:::

[Soap film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_film) attached to twin circular rings will take the shape of a catenoid.[^o3] Because they are members of the same [associate family](wikipedia://en/37016612) of surfaces, a catenoid can be bent into a portion of a [helicoid](wikipedia://en/684207), and vice versa.

## Geometry {#Geometry}

The catenoid was the first non-trivial minimal [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) in 3-dimensional Euclidean space to be discovered apart from the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29). The catenoid is obtained by rotating a catenary about its [directrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directrix_%28conic_section%29).[^o3] It was found and proved to be minimal by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in 1744.[^o5][^o6]

Early work on the subject was published also by [Jean Baptiste Meusnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Baptiste_Meusnier).[^o7][^o6] There are only two [minimal surfaces of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_surfaces_of_revolution) ([surfaces of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfaces_of_revolution) which are also minimal surfaces): the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) and the catenoid.[^o8]

The catenoid may be defined by the following parametric equations:

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\begin{aligned} x &= c \cosh \frac{v}{c} \cos u \\ y &= c \cosh \frac{v}{c} \sin u \\ z &= v \end{aligned} \tag{1}$$

:::

where $u \in [-\pi, \pi)$ and $v \in \mathbb{R}$ and $c$ is a non-zero real constant.

In cylindrical coordinates:

$$\rho =c \cosh \frac{z}{c},$$

where $c$ is a real constant.

A physical model of a catenoid can be formed by dipping two [circular](wikipedia://en/6220) rings into a soap solution and slowly drawing the circles apart.

The catenoid may be also defined approximately by the [stretched grid method](wikipedia://en/33932698) as a facet 3D model.

## Helicoid transformation {#Helicoid_transformation}

*Image: helicatenoid.gif.* Deformation of a right-handed [helicoid](wikipedia://en/684207) into a left-handed one and back again via a catenoid

Because they are members of the same [associate family](wikipedia://en/37016612) of surfaces, one can bend a catenoid into a portion of a [helicoid](wikipedia://en/684207) without stretching. In other words, one can make a (mostly) [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) and [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) deformation of a catenoid to a portion of the [helicoid](wikipedia://en/684207) such that every member of the deformation family is [minimal](wikipedia://en/276734) (having a [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) of zero). A [parametrization](wikipedia://en/576108) of such a deformation is given by the system

$$\begin{aligned} x(u,v) &= \sin \theta \,\cosh v \,\cos u + \cos \theta \,\sinh v \,\sin u \\ y(u,v) &= \sin \theta \,\cosh v \,\sin u - \cos \theta \,\sinh v \,\cos u \\ z(u,v) &= v \sin \theta + u \cos \theta \end{aligned}$$

for $(u,v) \in (-\pi, \pi] \times (-\infty, \infty)$, with deformation parameter $-\pi < \theta \le \pi$, where:

- $\theta = \pi$ corresponds to a right-handed helicoid,
- $\theta = \pm \pi / 2$ corresponds to a catenoid, and
- $\theta = 0$ corresponds to a left-handed helicoid.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="The critical catenoid conjecture"}

## The critical catenoid conjecture {#The_critical_catenoid_conjecture}

A *critical* catenoid is a catenoid in the unit ball that meets the boundary sphere orthogonally. Up to rotation about the origin, it is given by rescaling (Eq. 1) with $c=1$ by a factor $(\rho_0\cosh\rho_0)^{-1}$, where $\rho_0\tanh\rho_0=1$. It is an embedded annular solution of the [free boundary problem](wikipedia://en/32070938) for the area functional in the unit ball and the *critical catenoid conjecture* states that it is the unique such annulus.

The similarity of the critical catenoid conjecture to [Hsiang-Lawson's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hsiang-Lawson's_conjecture) on the Clifford torus in the 3-sphere, which was proven by [Simon Brendle](wikipedia://en/36493319) in 2012,[^o13] has driven interest in the conjecture,[^o14][^o19] as has its relationship to the Steklov eigenvalue problem.[^o15]

Nitsche proved in 1985 that the only immersed minimal disk in the unit ball with free boundary is an equatorial totally geodesic disk.[^o16] Nitsche also claimed without proof in the same paper that any free boundary constant mean curvature annulus in the unit ball is rotationally symmetric, and hence a catenoid or a parallel surface. Non-embedded counterexamples to Nitsche’s claim have since been constructed.[^o17][^o18]

The critical catenoid conjecture is stated in the embedded case by Fraser and Li[^o19] and has been proven by McGrath with the extra assumption that the annulus is reflection invariant through coordinate planes,[^o20] and by Kusner and McGrath when the annulus has antipodal symmetry.[^o21]

As of 2025 the full conjecture remains open.
