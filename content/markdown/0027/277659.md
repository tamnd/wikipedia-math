# Sainte-Laguë method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sainte-Laguë method"}

The **Sainte-Laguë method** (), also called the **Webster method** or the **Schepers method** (), is a [highest averages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_averages_method) [apportionment method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apportionment_method) for allocating seats in a parliament among [federal states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_states), or among parties in a [party-list proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party-list_proportional_representation) system. The Sainte-Laguë method shows a more equal [seats-to-votes ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seats-to-votes_ratio) for different sized parties[^o7] among apportionment methods.

:::

The method was first described in 1832 by American statesman and senator [Daniel Webster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Webster). In 1842, the method was adopted for proportional allocation of seats in [United States congressional apportionment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_congressional_apportionment) (Act of 25 June 1842, ch 46, 5 Stat. 491). The same method was independently invented in 1910 by the French mathematician [André Sainte-Laguë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Sainte-Laguë), and in 1980 by German physicist Hans Schepers (physicist).[^o2]

It is used for party-list [proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_representation) in [Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Ecuador), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Germany), [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Indonesia), [Latvia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Latvia) and [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Nepal),[^o3] as well as in modified form in [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Norway) and [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Sweden). It is also used as part of [MMP in New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MMP_in_New_Zealand).

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Proportional [electoral systems](wikipedia://en/29066482) attempt to distribute seats in proportion to the votes for each political party, e.g. a party with 30% of votes would receive 30% of seats. Exact proportionality is not possible because only whole seats can be distributed. Different [apportionment methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apportionment_%28politics%29), of which the Sainte-Laguë method is one, exist to distribute the seats according to the votes. Different apportionment methods show different levels of proportionality, [apportionment paradoxes](wikipedia://en/491556) and [political fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_fragmentation). The Sainte-Laguë method minimizes the average seats-to-votes ratio deviation[^o5] and empirically shows the best proportionality behavior[^o6] and more equal [seats-to-votes ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seats-to-votes_ratio) for different sized parties[^o7] among apportionment methods. Among other common methods, the [D'Hondt method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Hondt_method) favours large [parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_party) and [coalitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_coalition) over small parties.[^o7][^o8][^o9][^o10] While favoring large parties reduces [political fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_fragmentation), this can be achieved with [electoral thresholds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_threshold) as well. The Sainte-Laguë method shows fewer [apportionment paradoxes](wikipedia://en/491556) compared to [largest remainder methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_remainder_method)[^o11] such as the [Hare quota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hare_quota) and other [highest averages methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_averages_method) such as d'Hondt method.[^o12]

## Description {#Description}

After all the votes have been tallied, successive [quotients](wikipedia://en/332640) are calculated for each party. The formula for the quotient is[^o14]

$$\text{quotient} = \frac{V}{2s+1}$$

where:

- *V* is the total number of votes that party received, and
- *s* is the number of seats that have been allocated so far to that party, initially 0 for all parties.

Whichever party has the highest quotient gets the next seat allocated, and their quotient is recalculated. The process is repeated until all seats have been allocated.

The Webster/Sainte-Laguë method does not ensure that a party receiving more than half the votes will win at least half the seats, which can happen when a party with just over half the vote gets "rounded down" to under half the seats.[^o15] It also does not ensure that a party with a minority of the vote will not win a majority of the seats, for roughly the same reason.

Often there is an [electoral threshold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_threshold); that is, in order to be allocated seats, a minimum percentage of votes must be gained.

### Modified Sainte-Laguë method {#Modified_Sainte-Laguë_method}

To reduce [political fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_fragmentation), some countries, e.g. [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepal), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway) and [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden), change the quotient formula for parties with no seats (*s* = 0). These countries changed the quotient from *V*/1 to *V*/1.4, though from the general 2018 elections onwards, Sweden has been using *V*/1.2.[^o23] That is, the modified method changes the sequence of divisors used in this method from (1, 3, 5, 7, ...) to (1.4, 3, 5, 7, ...). This makes it more difficult for parties to earn only one seat, compared to the unmodified Sainte-Laguë's method. With the modified method, such small parties do not get any seats; these seats are instead given to a larger party.[^o14]

Norway further amends this system by utilizing a two-tier proportionality. The number of members to be returned from each of Norway's 19 constituencies (former counties) depends on the population and area of the county; each inhabitant counts one point, while each km<sup>2</sup> counts 1.8 points. Furthermore, one seat from each constituency is allocated according to the national distribution of votes.[^o24]

:::

### Threshold for seats {#Threshold_for_seats}

An [election threshold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_threshold) can be set to reduce [political fragmentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_fragmentation), and any list party which does not receive at least a specified percentage of list votes will not be allocated any seats, even if it received enough votes to have otherwise receive a seat. Examples of countries using the Sainte-Laguë method with a threshold are Germany and New Zealand (5%), although the threshold does not apply if a party wins at least one electorate seat in New Zealand or three electorate seats in Germany. Sweden uses a modified Sainte-Laguë method with a 4% threshold, and a 12% threshold in individual constituencies (i.e. a political party can gain representation with a minuscule representation on the national stage, if its vote share in at least one constituency exceeded 12%). Norway has a threshold of 4% to qualify for [leveling seats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leveling_seats) that are allocated according to the national distribution of votes. This means that even though a party is below the threshold of 4% nationally, they can still get seats from constituencies in which they are particularly popular.

## Usage by country {#Usage_by_country}

The Webster/Sainte-Laguë method is currently used in [Bosnia and Herzegovina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosnia_and_Herzegovina), [Ecuador](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecuador), [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia),[^o27] [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq),[^o28][^o29] [Latvia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latvia), [Nepal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nepal),[^o30] [New Zealand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Zealand), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway) and [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden).

In [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) it is used on the federal level for the [Bundestag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundestag), and on the state level for the [legislatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landtag) of [Baden-Württemberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landtag_of_Baden-Württemberg), [Bavaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landtag_of_Bavaria), [Bremen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bürgerschaft_of_Bremen), [Hamburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elections_in_Hamburg), [North Rhine-Westphalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landtag_of_North_Rhine-Westphalia), [Rhineland-Palatinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landtag_of_Rhineland-Palatinate), [Saxony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landtag_of_Saxony) and [Schleswig-Holstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landtag_of_Schleswig-Holstein).[^o31] To correct for the deficiency where a party can win a majority of votes but not a majority of seats, in federal elections the law provides such a party will receive extra seats until it has a majority of one.[^o32]

Some cantons in Switzerland use the Sainte-Laguë method for [biproportional apportionment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biproportional_apportionment) between electoral districts and for votes to seats allocation.[^o33]

## History {#History}

Webster proposed the method in the United States Congress in 1832 for proportional allocation of seats in [United States congressional apportionment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_congressional_apportionment). In 1842 the method was adopted (Act of June 25, 1842, ch 46, 5 Stat. 491). It was then replaced by [Hamilton method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_remainder_method) and in 1911 the Webster method was reintroduced.[^o52]

Webster and Sainte-Laguë methods should be treated as two methods with the same result, because the Webster method is used for allocating seats based on states' population, and the Sainte-Laguë based on parties' votes.[^o53] Webster invented his method for legislative apportionment (allocating legislative seats to regions based on their share of the population) rather than elections (allocating legislative seats to parties based on their share of the votes) but this makes no difference to the calculations in the method.

Webster's method is defined in terms of a quota as in the [largest remainder method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_remainder_method); in this method, the quota is called a "divisor". For a given value of the divisor, the population count for each region is divided by this divisor and then rounded to give the number of legislators to allocate to that region. In order to make the total number of legislators come out equal to the target number, the divisor is adjusted to make the sum of allocated seats after being rounded give the required total.

One way to determine the correct value of the divisor would be to start with a very large divisor, so that no seats are allocated after rounding. Then the divisor may be successively decreased until one seat, two seats, three seats and finally the total number of seats are allocated. The number of allocated seats for a given region increases from *s* to *s* + 1 exactly when the divisor equals the population of the region divided by *s* + 1/2, so at each step the next region to get a seat will be the one with the largest value of this quotient. That means that this successive adjustment method for implementing Webster's method allocates seats in the same order to the same regions as the Sainte-Laguë method would allocate them.

In 1980 the German physicist Hans Schepers, at the time Head of the Data Processing Group of the German Bundestag, suggested that the distribution of seats according to d'Hondt be modified to avoid putting smaller parties at a disadvantage.[^o2] German media started using the term Schepers Method and later German literature usually calls it Sainte-Laguë/Schepers.[^o2]

## Properties {#Properties}

When apportioning seats in [proportional representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportional_representation), it is particularly important to avoid bias between large parties and small parties to avoid [strategic voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_voting). [André Sainte-Laguë](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Sainte-Laguë) showed theoretically that the Sainte-Laguë method shows the lowest average bias in [apportionment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apportionment_%28politics%29),[^o5] confirmed by different theoretical and empirical ways.[^o6][^o52] The [European Parliament (Representation) Act 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Parliament_%28Representation%29_Act_2003) stipulates each region must be allocated at least 3 seats and that the ratio of electors to seats is as nearly as possible the same for each, the Commission found the Sainte-Laguë method produced the smallest [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590) when compared to the [D'Hondt method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Hondt_method) and Hare quota.[^o56][^o57]

### Proportionality under Sainte-Laguë method {#Proportionality_under_Sainte-Laguë_method}

The [seats-to-votes ratio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seats-to-votes_ratio) $a_i$ for a political party $i$ is the ratio between the fraction of seats $s_i$ and the fraction of votes $v_i$ for that party:

$$a_i = \frac{s_i}{v_i}$$

The Sainte-Laguë method approximates proportionality by optimizing the seats-to-votes ratio among all parties $i$ with the [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) approach. First, the difference between the seats-to-votes ratio for a party and the ideal seats-to-votes ratio is calculated and squared to obtain the error for the party $i$. To achieve equal representation of each voter, the ideal ratio of seats share to votes share is $1$.

$$\text{error}_i = (a_i-a_\text{ideal})^2=\left(\frac{s_i}{v_i}-1\right)^2$$

Second, the error for each party is weighted according to the vote share of each party to represent each voter equally. In the last step, the errors for each party are summed up. This error is identical to the [Sainte-Laguë Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sainte-Laguë_Index).

$$\text{error} = \sum_i v_i\text{error}_i=\sum_i {v_i\left(\frac{s_i}{v_i}-1\right)^2}$$

It was shown[^o59] that this error is minimized by the Sainte-Laguë method.

:::

## Comparison to other methods {#Comparison_to_other_methods}

The method belongs to the class of [*highest-averages methods*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_averages_method). It is similar to the [Jefferson/D'Hondt method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Hondt_method), but uses different divisors. The Jefferson/D'Hondt method favors larger parties while the Webster/Sainte-Laguë method does not.[^o14] The Webster/Sainte-Laguë method is generally seen as more proportional, but risks an outcome where a party with more than half the votes can win fewer than half the seats.[^o61]

When there are two parties, the Webster method is the unique divisor method which is identical to the [Hamilton method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Largest_remainder_method).[^o62]
