# Cokernel

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cokernel"}

The **cokernel** of a [linear mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_mapping) of [vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_spaces) $f : X \to Y$ is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) $Y / \operatorname{im}(f)$ of the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) of $f$ by the image of $f$. The dimension of the cokernel is called the *corank* of $f$.

:::

Cokernels are [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) to the [kernels of category theory](wikipedia://en/142616), hence the name: the kernel is a [subobject](wikipedia://en/540476) of the domain (it maps to the domain), while the cokernel is a [quotient object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_object) of the codomain (it maps from the codomain).

Intuitively, given an equation $f(x) = y$ that one is seeking to solve, the cokernel measures the *constraints* that $y$ must satisfy for this equation to have a solution – the obstructions to a solution – while the kernel measures the *degrees of freedom* in a solution, if one exists. This is elaborated in intuition, below.

More generally, the cokernel of a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) $f : X \to Y$ in some [category](wikipedia://en/5869) (e.g. a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) between [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) or a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) between [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932)) is an object $Q$ and a morphism $q : Y \to Q$ such that the composition $q f$ is the [zero morphism](wikipedia://en/320638) of the category, and furthermore $q$ is [universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_mapping_property) with respect to this property. Often the map $q$ is understood, and $Q$ itself is called the cokernel of $f$.

In many situations in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), such as for [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) or [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), the cokernel of the [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) $f : X \to Y$ is the [quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set) of $Y$ by the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of $f$. In [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) settings, such as with bounded linear operators between Hilbert spaces, one typically has to take the [closure](wikipedia://en/293802) of the image before passing to the quotient.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

One can define the cokernel in the general framework of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869). In order for the definition to make sense the category in question must have [zero morphisms](wikipedia://en/320638). The **cokernel** of a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) $f : X \to Y$ is defined as the [coequalizer](wikipedia://en/431940) of $f$ and the zero morphism $0_{XY} : X \to Y$.

Explicitly, this means the following. The cokernel of $f : X \to Y$ is an object $Q$ together with a morphism $q : Y \to Q$ such that the diagram

[commutes](wikipedia://en/138484). Moreover, the morphism $q$ must be [universal](wikipedia://en/32245) for this diagram, i.e. any other such $q' : Y \to Q'$ can be obtained by composing $q$ with a unique morphism $u : Q \to Q'$:

*Image: Cokernel-02.png.*

As with all universal constructions the cokernel, if it exists, is unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a unique [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), or more precisely: if $q : Y \to Q$ and $q' : Y \to Q'$ are two cokernels of $f : X \to Y$, then there exists a unique isomorphism $u : Q \to Q'$ with $q\boldsymbol{ = u q}$.

Like all coequalizers, the cokernel $q : Y \to Q$ is necessarily an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539). Conversely an epimorphism is called *[normal](wikipedia://en/142626)* (or *conormal*) if it is the cokernel of some morphism. A category is called *conormal* if every epimorphism is normal (e.g. the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092) is conormal).

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

In the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), the cokernel of a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $f : G \to H$ is the [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) of $H$ by the [normal closure](wikipedia://en/153099) of the image of $f$. In the case of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), since every [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) is normal, the cokernel is just $H$ [modulo](wikipedia://en/25977) the image of $f$:

$$\operatorname{coker}(f) = H / \operatorname{im}(f).$$

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

### Special cases {#Special_cases}

In a [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521), it makes sense to add and subtract morphisms. In such a category, the [coequalizer](wikipedia://en/431940) of two morphisms $f$ and $g$ (if it exists) is just the cokernel of their difference:

$$\operatorname{coeq}(f, g) = \operatorname{coker}(g - f).$$

In an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) (a special kind of preadditive category) the [image](wikipedia://en/342899) and [coimage](wikipedia://en/632685) of a morphism $f$ are given by

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{im}(f) &= \ker(\operatorname{coker} f), \\ \operatorname{coim}(f) &= \operatorname{coker}(\ker f). \end{aligned}$$

In particular, every abelian category is normal (and conormal as well). That is, every [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) $m$ can be written as the kernel of some morphism. Specifically, $m$ is the kernel of its own cokernel:

$$m = \ker(\operatorname{coker}(m))$$

:::

:::

## Intuition {#Intuition}

The cokernel can be thought of as the space of *constraints* that an equation must satisfy, as the space of *obstructions*, just as the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) is the space of *solutions.*

Formally, one may connect the kernel and the cokernel of a map $T: V \to W$ by the [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215)

$$0 \to \ker T \to V \overset T \longrightarrow W \to \operatorname{coker} T \to 0.$$

These can be interpreted thus: given a linear equation $T(v) = w$ to solve,

- the kernel is the space of *solutions* to the *homogeneous* equation $T(v) = 0$, and its dimension is the number of *degrees of freedom* in solutions to $T(v) = w$, if they exist;
- the cokernel is the space of *constraints* on *w* that must be satisfied if the equation is to have a solution, and its dimension is the number of independent constraints that must be satisfied for the equation to have a solution.

The dimension of the cokernel plus the dimension of the image (the rank) add up to the dimension of the target space, as the dimension of the quotient space $W / T(V)$ is simply the dimension of the space *minus* the dimension of the image.

As a simple example, consider the map $T: \mathbf{R}^{2} \to \mathbf{R}^{2}$, given by $T(x, y) = (0, y)$. Then for an equation $T(x, y) = (a, b)$ to have a solution, we must have $a = 0$ (one constraint), and in that case the solution space is $(x, b)$, or equivalently, $(0, b) + (x, 0)$, (one degree of freedom). The kernel may be expressed as the subspace $(x, 0) \subseteq V$: the value of $x$ is the freedom in a solution. The cokernel may be expressed via the real valued map $W: (a, b) \to (a)$: given a vector $(a, b)$, the value of $a$ is the *obstruction* to there being a solution.

Additionally, the cokernel can be thought of as something that "detects" [surjections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection) in the same way that the kernel "detects" [injections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injection_%28mathematics%29). A map is injective if and only if its kernel is trivial, and a map is surjective if and only if its cokernel is trivial, or in other words, if $W = \operatorname{im}(T)$.
