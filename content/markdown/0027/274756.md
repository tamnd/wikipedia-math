# John Machin

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="John Machin"}

**John Machin** (bapt. c. 1686 – June 9, 1751)[^o3] was a professor of [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) at [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College), London. He is best known for developing a quickly [converging series](wikipedia://en/1743431) for [pi](wikipedia://en/23601) in 1706 and using it to compute pi to 100 decimal places.

:::

## History {#History}

John Machin served as secretary of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society) from 1718 to 1747. He was also a member of the commission which decided the [Calculus priority dispute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_and_Newton_calculus_controversy) between [Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) and [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) in 1712.[^n1]

On 16 May 1713 he succeeded [Alexander Torriano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Torriano) as professor of astronomy in [Gresham College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gresham_College), and held the post until his death, which occurred in London on 9 June 1751. Machin enjoyed a high mathematical reputation. In 1706, Machin computed the value of π with the formula given below to one hundred decimal places. His ingenious quadrature of the circle was later investigated by Charles Hutton. A mass of his manuscripts is preserved by the [Royal Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Astronomical_Society); and writing to [William Jones](wikipedia://en/239126) in 1727, he asserted his claim to the parliamentary reward of £10,000 for amending the lunar tables.[^n1]

In 1728, he was listed as one of the subscribers to the *[Cyclopaedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclopaedia,_or_Universal_Dictionary_of_Arts_and_Sciences)* of [Ephraim Chambers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephraim_Chambers).[^o7]

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula"}

## Formula {#Formula}

Machin's formula[^o10] (for which the [derivation](wikipedia://en/2724287#Derivation) is straightforward) is:

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 4 \arctan \frac{1}{5} - \arctan \frac{1}{239}$$

The benefit of the new formula, a variation on the [Gregory–Leibniz series](wikipedia://en/1301559) ($\tfrac{\pi}{4}$ = arctan 1), was that it had a significantly increased rate of convergence, which made it a much more practical method of calculation.

To compute π to 100 decimal places, he combined his formula with the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) expansion for the inverse tangent. ([Brook Taylor](wikipedia://en/210429) was Machin's contemporary in [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge).) Machin's formula remained the primary tool of pi-hunters for centuries (well into the computer era).

Several other [Machin-like formulae](wikipedia://en/2724287) are known.
