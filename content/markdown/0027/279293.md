# Mathematics and architecture

*Image: 30 St Mary Axe from Leadenhall Street.jpg.* "The Gherkin",[^o128] [30 St Mary Axe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30_St_Mary_Axe), London, completed 2003, is a [parametrically designed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_design) [solid of revolution](wikipedia://en/329400).

*Image: Kandariya mahadeva temple.jpg.* Detail of [Kandariya Mahadeva Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandariya_Mahadeva_Temple) (c. 1030), [Khajuraho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khajuraho), India, showing religious architecture with a [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913)-like structure of many parts that resemble the whole.[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematics and architecture"}

**Mathematics and architecture** are related, since [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), [like some other arts](wikipedia://en/22468661), uses [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) for several reasons. Apart from the mathematics needed when engineering [buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Building), architects use [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446): to define the spatial form of a building; from the [Pythagoreans](wikipedia://en/291170) of the sixth century BC onwards, to create [architectural forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_form) considered harmonious, and thus to lay out buildings and their surroundings according to mathematical, [aesthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic) and sometimes religious principles; to decorate buildings with mathematical objects such as [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671); and to meet environmental goals, such as to minimise wind speeds around the bases of tall buildings.

:::

In [ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_architecture), [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_architecture), [India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture_of_India), and the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture), buildings including [pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_pyramids), temples, mosques, palaces and [mausoleums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausoleum) were laid out with specific proportions for religious reasons. In Islamic architecture, [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) shapes and [geometric tiling patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_geometric_patterns) are used to decorate buildings, both inside and outside. Some Hindu temples have a [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913)-like structure where parts resemble the whole, conveying a message about the infinite in [Hindu cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_cosmology). In [Chinese architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_architecture), the [tulou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulou) of [Fujian province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujian_province) are circular, communal defensive structures. In the twenty-first century, mathematical ornamentation is again being used to cover public buildings.

In [Renaissance architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture), [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) and proportion were deliberately emphasized by architects such as [Leon Battista Alberti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Battista_Alberti), [Sebastiano Serlio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastiano_Serlio) and [Andrea Palladio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Palladio), influenced by [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius)'s *[De architectura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_architectura)* from [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome) and the arithmetic of the Pythagoreans from ancient Greece. At the end of the nineteenth century, [Vladimir Shukhov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Shukhov) in [Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia) and [Antoni Gaudí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoni_Gaudí) in [Barcelona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcelona) pioneered the use of [hyperboloid structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_structures); in the [Sagrada Família](wikipedia://en/59545), Gaudí also incorporated [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/14052) [paraboloids](wikipedia://en/145845), tessellations, [catenary arches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catenary_arch), [catenoids](wikipedia://en/273329), [helicoids](wikipedia://en/684207), and [ruled surfaces](wikipedia://en/705158). In the twentieth century, styles such as [modern architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture) and [Deconstructivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deconstructivism) explored different geometries to achieve desired effects. [Minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734) have been exploited in tent-like roof coverings as at [Denver International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denver_International_Airport), while [Richard Buckminster Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Buckminster_Fuller) pioneered the use of the strong [thin-shell structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-shell_structure) known as [geodesic domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_dome).

## Connected fields {#Connected_fields}

*Image: CdM, presunto autoritratto di leon battista alberti, white ground.jpg.* In the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), an [architect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architect) like [Leon Battista Alberti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Battista_Alberti) was expected to be knowledgeable in many disciplines, including [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) and [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446).

The architects Michael Ostwald and [Kim Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kim_Williams_%28architect%29), considering the relationships between [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), note that the fields as commonly understood might seem to be only weakly connected, since architecture is a profession concerned with the practical matter of making buildings, while mathematics is the pure [study of number](wikipedia://en/21527) and other abstract objects. But, they argue, the two are strongly connected, and have been since [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity). In ancient Rome, [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius) described an architect as a man who knew enough of a range of other disciplines, primarily [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), to enable him to oversee skilled artisans in all the other necessary areas, such as masons and carpenters. The same applied in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), where graduates learnt [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), geometry and [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) alongside the basic syllabus of grammar, logic, and rhetoric (the [trivium](wikipedia://en/30666)) in elegant halls made by master builders who had guided many craftsmen. A master builder at the top of his profession was given the title of architect or engineer. In the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), the [quadrivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrivium) of arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy became an extra syllabus expected of the [Renaissance man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_man) such as [Leon Battista Alberti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Battista_Alberti). Similarly in England, Sir [Christopher Wren](wikipedia://en/52946), known today as an architect, was firstly a noted astronomer.[^o4]

Williams and Ostwald, further overviewing the interaction of mathematics and architecture since 1500 according to the approach of the German sociologist [Theodor Adorno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodor_W._Adorno), identify three tendencies among architects, namely: to be *revolutionary*, introducing wholly new ideas; *reactionary*, failing to introduce change; or *[revivalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Revivalism_%28architecture%29)*, actually going backwards. They argue that architects have avoided looking to mathematics for inspiration in revivalist times. This would explain why in revivalist periods, such as the [Gothic Revival](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_Revival_architecture) in 19th century England, architecture had little connection to mathematics. Equally, they note that in reactionary times such as the Italian [Mannerism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mannerism) of about 1520 to 1580, or the 17th century [Baroque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baroque) and [Palladian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladian) movements, mathematics was barely consulted. In contrast, the revolutionary early 20th-century movements such as [Futurism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futurism) and [Constructivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivist_architecture) actively rejected old ideas, embracing mathematics and leading to [Modernist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernist) architecture. Towards the end of the 20th century, too, [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) geometry was quickly seized upon by architects, as was [aperiodic tiling](wikipedia://en/868145), to provide interesting and attractive coverings for buildings.[^o5]

Architects use mathematics for several reasons, leaving aside the necessary use of mathematics in the [engineering of buildings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architectural_engineering).[^o6] Firstly, they [use geometry](wikipedia://en/19315523) because it defines the spatial form of a building.[^o7] Secondly, they use mathematics to design forms that are [considered beautiful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic) or harmonious.[^o8] From the time of the [Pythagoreans](wikipedia://en/291170) with their religious philosophy of number,[^o9] architects in [ancient Greece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_architecture), [ancient Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome), the [Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_architecture) and the [Italian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_architecture) have chosen the [proportions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proportion_%28architecture%29) of the built environment – buildings and their designed surroundings – according to mathematical as well as aesthetic and sometimes religious principles.[^o15][^o81][^o86][^o70] Thirdly, they may use mathematical objects such as [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671) to decorate buildings.[^o10][^o11] Fourthly, they may use mathematics in the form of computer modelling to meet environmental goals, such as to minimise whirling air currents at the base of tall buildings.[^o128]

## Secular aesthetics {#Secular_aesthetics}

### Ancient Rome {#Ancient_Rome}

*Image: Greekhse1.jpg.* Plan of a Greek house by [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius)

#### Vitruvius {#Vitruvius}

*Image: Giovanni Paolo Panini - Interior of the Pantheon, Rome - Google Art Project.jpg.* The interior of the [Pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_%28Rome%29) by [Giovanni Paolo Panini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Paolo_Panini), 1758

The influential ancient Roman architect Vitruvius argued that the design of a building such as a temple depends on two qualities, proportion and *symmetria*. Proportion ensures that each part of a building relates harmoniously to every other part. *Symmetria* in Vitruvius's usage means something closer to the English term [modularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modularity) than [mirror symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873), as again it relates to the assembling of (modular) parts into the whole building. In his Basilica at [Fano](wikipedia://en/815833), he uses ratios of small integers, especially the [triangular numbers](wikipedia://en/213915) (1, 3, 6, 10, ...) to proportion the structure into [(Vitruvian) modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvian_module). Thus the Basilica's width to length is 1:2; the aisle around it is as high as it is wide, 1:1; the columns are five feet thick and fifty feet high, 1:10.[^o15]

*Image: Dehio 1 Pantheon Floor plan.jpg.* Floor plan of the Pantheon

Vitruvius named three qualities required of architecture in his *[De architectura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_architectura)*, c. 15 B.C.: [firmness, usefulness (or "Commodity" in Henry Wotton's 17th century English), and delight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firmness,_commodity,_and_delight). These can be used as categories for classifying the ways in which mathematics is used in architecture. Firmness encompasses the use of mathematics to ensure a building stands up, hence the mathematical tools used in design and to support construction, for instance to ensure stability and to model performance. Usefulness derives in part from the effective application of mathematics, reasoning about and analysing the spatial and other relationships in a design. Delight is an attribute of the resulting building, resulting from the embodying of mathematical relationships in the building; it includes aesthetic, sensual and intellectual qualities.[^o16]

#### The Pantheon {#The_Pantheon}

The [Pantheon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pantheon_%28Rome%29) in Rome has survived intact, illustrating classical Roman structure, proportion, and decoration. The main structure is a dome, the apex left open as a circular [oculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oculus_%28architecture%29) to let in light; it is fronted by a short colonnade with a triangular pediment. The height to the oculus and the diameter of the interior circle are the same, \43.3 m, so the whole interior would fit exactly within a cube, and the interior could house a sphere of the same diameter.[^o18] These dimensions make more sense when expressed in [ancient Roman units of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_units_of_measurement): The dome spans 150 [Roman feet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_foot)); the oculus is 30 Roman feet in diameter; the doorway is 40 Roman feet high.[^o19] The Pantheon remains the world's largest unreinforced concrete dome.[^o20]

### Renaissance {#Renaissance}

*Image: Santa Maria Novella.jpg.* Facade of [Santa Maria Novella](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Maria_Novella), [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence), 1470. The frieze (with squares) and above is by [Leon Battista Alberti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Battista_Alberti).

The first Renaissance treatise on architecture was Leon Battista Alberti's 1450 [De re aedificatoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_re_aedificatoria) (On the Art of Building); it became the first printed book on architecture in 1485. It was partly based on Vitruvius's *De architectura* and, via Nicomachus, Pythagorean arithmetic. Alberti starts with a cube, and derives ratios from it. Thus the diagonal of a face gives the ratio 1:$\sqrt{2}$, while the diameter of the sphere which circumscribes the cube gives 1:$\sqrt{3}$.[^o22][^o23] Alberti also documented [Filippo Brunelleschi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filippo_Brunelleschi)'s discovery of [linear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_perspective), developed to enable the design of buildings which would look beautifully proportioned when viewed from a convenient distance.[^o70]

*Image: Houghton Typ 525.69.781 - Serlio, 69.jpg.* Architectural perspective of a stage set by [Sebastiano Serlio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastiano_Serlio), 1569[^o24]

The next major text was [Sebastiano Serlio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sebastiano_Serlio)'s *Regole generali d'architettura* (General Rules of Architecture); the first volume appeared in Venice in 1537; the 1545 volume (books 1 and 2) covered geometry and [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405). Two of Serlio's methods for constructing perspectives were wrong, but this did not stop his work being widely used.[^o25]

*Image: Villa Pisani.jpg.* [Andrea Palladio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Palladio)'s plan and elevation of the [Villa Pisani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Pisani_%28Bagnolo%29)

In 1570, [Andrea Palladio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrea_Palladio) published the influential *[I quattro libri dell'architettura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_quattro_libri_dell'architettura)* (The Four Books of Architecture) in [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice). This widely printed book was largely responsible for spreading the ideas of the [Italian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Renaissance) throughout Europe, assisted by proponents like the English diplomat Henry Wotton with his 1624 *The Elements of Architecture*.[^o26] The proportions of each room within the villa were calculated on simple mathematical ratios like 3:4 and 4:5, and the different rooms within the house were interrelated by these ratios. Earlier architects had used these formulas for balancing a single symmetrical facade; however, Palladio's designs related to the whole, usually square, villa.[^o27] Palladio permitted a range of ratios in the *Quattro libri*, stating:[^o28][^o29]

There are seven types of room that are the most beautiful and well proportioned and turn out better: they can be made circular, though these are rare; or square; or their length will equal the diagonal of the square of the breadth; or a square and a third; or a square and a half; or a square and two-thirds; or two squares.

In 1615, [Vincenzo Scamozzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Scamozzi) published the late Renaissance treatise *L'idea dell'architettura universale* (The Idea of a Universal Architecture).[^o30] He attempted to relate the design of cities and buildings to the ideas of Vitruvius and the Pythagoreans, and to the more recent ideas of Palladio.[^o31]

### Nineteenth century {#Nineteenth_century}

*Image: Adziogol hyperboloid Lighthouse by Vladimir Shukhov 1911.jpg.* [Hyperboloid lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_structure) [lighthouse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adziogol_Lighthouse) by [Vladimir Shukhov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Shukhov), [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine), 1911

[Hyperboloid structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_structure) were used starting towards the end of the nineteenth century by [Vladimir Shukhov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Shukhov) for masts, lighthouses and cooling towers. Their striking shape is both aesthetically interesting and strong, using structural materials economically. [Shukhov's first hyperboloidal tower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shukhov_Tower_in_Polibino) was exhibited in [Nizhny Novgorod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nizhny_Novgorod) in 1896.[^o33][^o34][^o35]

### Twentieth century {#Twentieth_century}

*Image: Rietveld Schröderhuis HayKranen-7.JPG.* [De Stijl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Stijl)'s sliding, intersecting planes: the [Rietveld Schröder House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rietveld_Schröder_House), 1924

The early twentieth century movement [Modern architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_architecture), pioneered by Russian [Constructivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivism_%28art%29),[^o37] used rectilinear [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9417) (also called [Cartesian](wikipedia://en/7706)) geometry. In the [De Stijl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Stijl) movement, the horizontal and the vertical were seen as constituting the universal. The architectural form consists of putting these two directional tendencies together, using roof planes, wall planes and balconies, which either slide past or intersect each other, as in the 1924 [Rietveld Schröder House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rietveld_Schröder_House) by [Gerrit Rietveld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerrit_Rietveld).[^o38]

*Image: Raoul Heinrich Francé Poppy and Pepperpot from Die Pflanze als erfinder 1920.jpeg.* [Raoul Heinrich Francé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoul_Heinrich_Francé)'s [poppy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poppy) and [pepperpot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pepper_shaker) ([biomimetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomimetics)) image from *Die Pflanze als Erfinder*, 1920

Modernist architects were free to make use of curves as well as planes. [Charles Holden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Holden)'s 1933 [Arnos station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnos_Grove_tube_station) has a circular ticket hall in brick with a flat concrete roof. In 1938, the [Bauhaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bauhaus) painter [László Moholy-Nagy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/László_Moholy-Nagy) adopted [Raoul Heinrich Francé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raoul_Heinrich_Francé)'s seven [biotechnical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomimetics) elements, namely the crystal, the sphere, the cone, the plane, the (cuboidal) strip, the (cylindrical) rod, and the spiral, as the supposed basic building blocks of architecture inspired by nature.[^o40][^o41]

[Le Corbusier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Le_Corbusier) proposed an [anthropometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropometric) [scale](wikipedia://en/49526) of proportions in architecture, the [Modulor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulor), based on the supposed height of a man.[^o42] Le Corbusier's 1955 [Chapelle Notre-Dame du Haut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre-Dame_du_Haut) uses free-form curves not describable in mathematical formulae. The shapes are said to be evocative of natural forms such as the [prow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prow) of a ship or praying hands.[^o43] The design is only at the largest scale: there is no hierarchy of detail at smaller scales, and thus no fractal dimension; the same applies to other famous twentieth-century buildings such as the [Sydney Opera House](wikipedia://en/28222), [Denver International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denver_International_Airport), and the [Guggenheim Museum, Bilbao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guggenheim_Museum,_Bilbao).[^o53]

[Contemporary architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contemporary_architecture), in the opinion of the 90 leading architects who responded to a 2010 [World Architecture Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Architecture_Survey), is extremely diverse; the best was judged to be [Frank Gehry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Gehry)'s Guggenheim Museum, Bilbao.[^o44]

*Image: DIA Airport Roof.jpg.* The [minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734) of the [fabric roof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabric_structure) of [Denver International Airport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denver_International_Airport), completed in 1995, evoke [Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado)'s snow-capped mountains and the [teepee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teepee) tents of [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States).

Denver International Airport's terminal building, completed in 1995, has a [fabric roof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabric_structure) supported as a [minimal surface](wikipedia://en/276734) (i.e., its [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) is zero) by steel cables. It evokes [Colorado](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colorado)'s snow-capped mountains and the [teepee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teepee) tents of [Native Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Native_Americans_in_the_United_States).[^o45][^o46]

The architect [Richard Buckminster Fuller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Buckminster_Fuller) is famous for designing strong [thin-shell structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thin-shell_structure) known as [geodesic domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_dome). The [Montréal Biosphère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montréal_Biosphère) dome is 61 m high; its diameter is 76 m.[^o47]

Sydney Opera House has a dramatic roof consisting of soaring white vaults, reminiscent of ship's sails; to make them possible to construct using standardized components, the vaults are all composed of triangular sections of spherical shells with the same radius. These have the required uniform [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) in every direction.[^o48]

The late twentieth century movement [Deconstructivism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deconstructivism) creates deliberate disorder with what [Nikos Salingaros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikos_Salingaros) in *[A Theory of Architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Theory_of_Architecture)* calls random forms[^o49] of high complexity[^o50] by using non-parallel walls, superimposed grids and complex 2-D surfaces, as in Frank Gehry's [Disney Concert Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disney_Concert_Hall) and Guggenheim Museum, Bilbao.[^o51][^o52] Until the twentieth century, architecture students were obliged to have a grounding in mathematics. Salingaros argues that first "overly simplistic, politically-driven" [Modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism) and then "anti-scientific" Deconstructivism have effectively separated architecture from mathematics. He believes that this "reversal of mathematical values" is harmful, as the "pervasive aesthetic" of non-mathematical architecture trains people "to reject mathematical information in the built environment"; he argues that this has negative effects on society.[^o53]

## Religious principles {#Religious_principles}

### Ancient Egypt {#Ancient_Egypt}

*Image: Mathematical Pyramid.svg.* Base:hypotenuse (b:a) ratios for pyramids like the [Great Pyramid of Giza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza) could be: 1:φ ([Kepler triangle](wikipedia://en/13938136)), 3:5 ([3:4:5 triangle](wikipedia://en/2278116)), or 1:4/π

The [pyramids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid) of [ancient Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) are [tombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_funerary_practices) constructed with mathematical proportions, but which these were, and whether the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) was used, are debated. The ratio of the slant height to half the base length of the [Great Pyramid of Giza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Pyramid_of_Giza) is less than 1% from the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).[^o57] If this was the design method, it would imply the use of [Kepler's triangle](wikipedia://en/13938136) (face angle 51°49'),[^o57][^o58] but according to many [historians of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science), the golden ratio was not known until the time of the [Pythagoreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans).[^o59]

The proportions of some pyramids may have also been based on the [3:4:5 triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_right_triangles) (face angle 53°8'), known from the [Rhind Mathematical Papyrus](wikipedia://en/9210114) (c. 1650–1550 BC); this was first conjectured by historian [Moritz Cantor](wikipedia://en/307975) in 1882.[^o60] It is known that right angles were laid out accurately in ancient Egypt using [knotted cords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knotted_cord) for measurement,[^o60] that [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) recorded in *[Isis and Osiris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Iside_et_Osiride)* (c. 100 AD) that the Egyptians admired the 3:4:5 triangle,[^o60] and that a scroll from before 1700 BC demonstrated basic [square](wikipedia://en/659942) formulas.[^o61] Historian Roger L. Cooke observes that "It is hard to imagine anyone being interested in such conditions without knowing the Pythagorean theorem," but also notes that no Egyptian text before 300 BC actually mentions the use of the theorem to find the length of a triangle's sides, and that there are simpler ways to construct a right angle. Cooke concludes that Cantor's conjecture remains uncertain; he guesses that the ancient Egyptians probably knew the Pythagorean theorem, but "there is no evidence that they used it to construct right angles."[^o60]

### Ancient India {#Ancient_India}

*Image: Virupaksha Temple,Hampi.JPG.* [Gopuram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gopuram) of the [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu) [Virupaksha Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virupaksha_Temple,_Hampi) has a [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913)-like structure where the parts resemble the whole.

[Vaastu Shastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaastu_Shastra), the ancient [Indian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/India) canons of architecture and town planning, employs symmetrical drawings called [mandalas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mandala). Complex calculations are used to arrive at the dimensions of a building and its components. The designs are intended to integrate architecture with nature, the relative functions of various parts of the structure, and ancient beliefs utilizing geometric patterns ([yantra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yantra)), symmetry and [directional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direction_%28geometry,_geography%29) alignments.[^o63][^o64] However, early builders may have come upon mathematical proportions by accident. The mathematician Georges Ifrah notes that simple "tricks" with string and stakes can be used to lay out geometric shapes, such as ellipses and right angles.[^o70][^o65]

*Image: Plan of Meenakshi Amman Temple Madurai India.jpg.* Plan of [Meenakshi Amman Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meenakshi_Amman_Temple), [Madurai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madurai), from the 7th century onwards. The four gateways (numbered I-IV) are tall [gopurams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gopuram).

The mathematics of [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals) has been used to show that the reason why existing buildings have universal appeal and are visually satisfying is because they provide the viewer with a sense of scale at different viewing distances. For example, in the tall [gopuram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gopuram) gatehouses of [Hindu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu) temples such as the [Virupaksha Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virupaksha_Temple,_Hampi) at [Hampi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hampi) built in the seventh century, and others such as the [Kandariya Mahadev Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kandariya_Mahadev_Temple) at [Khajuraho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khajuraho), the parts and the whole have the same character, with [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) in the range 1.7 to 1.8. The cluster of smaller towers (*shikhara*, lit. 'mountain') about the tallest, central, tower which represents the holy [Mount Kailash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mount_Kailash), abode of Lord [Shiva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shiva), depicts the endless repetition of universes in [Hindu cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu_cosmology).[^o1][^o66] The religious studies scholar William J. Jackson observed of the pattern of towers grouped among smaller towers, themselves grouped among still smaller towers, that:

The ideal form gracefully artificed suggests the infinite rising levels of existence and consciousness, expanding sizes rising toward transcendence above, and at the same time housing the sacred deep within.[^o66][^o67]

The [Meenakshi Amman Temple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meenakshi_Amman_Temple) is a large complex with multiple shrines, with the streets of [Madurai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madurai) laid out concentrically around it according to the shastras. The four gateways are tall towers ([gopurams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gopuram)) with fractal-like repetitive structure as at Hampi. The enclosures around each shrine are rectangular and surrounded by high stone walls.[^o68]

### Ancient Greece {#Ancient_Greece}

*Image: The Parthenon Athens.jpg.* The [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon) was designed using [Pythagorean](wikipedia://en/291170) ratios.

[Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275) (c. 569 – c. 475 B.C.) and his followers, the Pythagoreans, held that "all things are numbers". They observed the harmonies produced by notes with specific small-integer ratios of frequency, and argued that buildings too should be designed with such ratios. The Greek word *symmetria* originally denoted the harmony of architectural shapes in precise ratios from a building's smallest details right up to its entire design.[^o70]

The [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon) is \69.5 m long, \30.9 m wide and \13.7 m high to the cornice. This gives a ratio of width to length of 4:9, and the same for height to width. Putting these together gives height:width:length of 16:36:81, or to the delight[^o71] of the Pythagoreans 4<sup>2</sup>:6<sup>2</sup>:9<sup>2</sup>. This sets the module as 0.858 m. A 4:9 rectangle can be constructed as three contiguous rectangles with sides in the ratio 3:4. Each half-rectangle is then a convenient 3:4:5 right triangle, enabling the angles and sides to be checked with a suitably knotted rope. The inner area (naos) similarly has 4:9 proportions (\21.44 m wide by 48.3 m long); the ratio between the diameter of the outer columns, \1.905 m, and the spacing of their centres, \4.293 m, is also 4:9.[^o70]

*Image: Parthenon-top-view.svg.* Floor plan of the Parthenon

The Parthenon is considered by authors such as [John Julius Norwich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Julius_Norwich) "the most perfect Doric temple ever built".[^o72] Its elaborate architectural refinements include "a subtle correspondence between the curvature of the stylobate, the taper of the [naos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naos_%28architecture%29) walls and the *entasis* of the columns".[^o72] *[Entasis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entasis)* refers to the subtle diminution in diameter of the columns as they rise. The stylobate is the platform on which the columns stand. As in other classical Greek temples,[^o73] the platform has a slight parabolic upward curvature to shed rainwater and reinforce the building against earthquakes. The columns might therefore be supposed to lean outwards, but they actually lean slightly inwards so that if they carried on, they would meet about a kilometre and a half above the centre of the building; since they are all the same height, the curvature of the outer stylobate edge is transmitted to the [architrave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architrave) and roof above: "all follow the rule of being built to delicate curves".[^o74]

The golden ratio was known in 300 B.C., when [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) described the method of geometric construction.[^o75] It has been argued that the golden ratio was used in the design of the Parthenon and other ancient Greek buildings, as well as sculptures, paintings, and vases.[^o76] More recent authors such as [Nikos Salingaros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikos_Salingaros), however, doubt all these claims.[^o77] Experiments by the computer scientist George Markowsky failed to find any preference for the [golden rectangle](wikipedia://en/219374).[^o78]

### Islamic architecture {#Islamic_architecture}

*Image: Selimiye... - panoramio.jpg.* [Selimiye Mosque, Edirne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selimiye_Mosque,_Edirne), 1569–1575

The historian of Islamic art Antonio Fernandez-Puertas suggests that the [Alhambra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhambra), like the [Great Mosque of Cordoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Cordoba),[^o80] was designed using the [Hispano-Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) foot or *codo* of about \0.62 m. In the palace's [Court of the Lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_the_Lions), the proportions follow a series of [surds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surd_%28mathematics%29). A rectangle with sides 1 and $\sqrt{2}$ has (by [Pythagoras's theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034)) a diagonal of $\sqrt{3}$, which describes the right triangle made by the sides of the court; the series continues with $\sqrt{4}$ (giving a 1:2 ratio), $\sqrt{5}$ and so on. The decorative patterns are similarly proportioned, $\sqrt{2}$ generating squares inside circles and eight-pointed stars, $\sqrt{3}$ generating six-pointed stars. There is no evidence to support earlier claims that the golden ratio was used in the Alhambra.[^o81][^o82] The [Court of the Lions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court_of_the_Lions) is bracketed by the Hall of Two Sisters and the Hall of the Abencerrajes; a regular [hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733) can be drawn from the centres of these two halls and the four inside corners of the Court of the Lions.[^o83]

The [Selimiye Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selimiye_Mosque,_Edirne) in [Edirne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edirne), Turkey, was built by [Mimar Sinan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mimar_Sinan) to provide a space where the [mihrab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mihrab) could be seen from anywhere inside the building. The very large central space is accordingly arranged as an octagon, formed by eight enormous pillars, and capped by a circular dome of \31.25 m diameter and 43 m high. The octagon is formed into a square with four semidomes, and externally by four exceptionally tall minarets, 83 m tall. The building's plan is thus a circle, inside an octagon, inside a square.[^o84]

### Mughal architecture {#Mughal_architecture}

*Image: TajMahalbyAmalMongia.jpg.* The [Taj Mahal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taj_Mahal) mausoleum with part of the complex's gardens at [Agra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agra)

[Mughal architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_architecture), as seen in the abandoned imperial city of [Fatehpur Sikri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatehpur_Sikri) and the [Taj Mahal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taj_Mahal) complex, has a distinctive mathematical order and a strong aesthetic based on symmetry and harmony.[^o86][^o87]

The Taj Mahal exemplifies Mughal architecture, both representing [paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paradise)[^o88] and displaying the [Mughal Emperor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Mughal_emperors) [Shah Jahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shah_Jahan)'s power through its scale, symmetry and costly decoration. The white marble [mausoleum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mausoleum), decorated with [pietra dura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pietra_dura), the great gate (*Darwaza-i rauza*), other buildings, the gardens and paths together form a unified hierarchical design. The buildings include a [mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosque) in red sandstone on the west, and an almost identical building, the Jawab or 'answer' on the east to maintain the bilateral symmetry of the complex. The formal [charbagh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charbagh) ('fourfold garden') is in four parts, symbolising the four [rivers of Paradise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rivers_of_Paradise), and offering views and reflections of the mausoleum. These are divided in turn into 16 parterres.[^o89]

*Image: Taj site plan.png.* Site plan of the [Taj Mahal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taj_Mahal) complex. The great gate is at the right, the mausoleum in the centre, bracketed by the mosque (below) and the jawab. The plan includes squares and [octagons](wikipedia://en/314575).

The Taj Mahal complex was laid out on a grid, subdivided into smaller grids. The historians of architecture Koch and Barraud agree with the traditional accounts that give the width of the complex as 374 Mughal yards or [gaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaz_%28measure%29), the main area being three 374-gaz squares. These were divided in areas like the bazaar and caravanserai into 17-gaz modules; the garden and terraces are in modules of 23 gaz, and are 368 gaz wide (16 x 23). The mausoleum, mosque and guest house are laid out on a grid of 7 gaz. Koch and Barraud observe that if an octagon, used repeatedly in the complex, is given sides of 7 units, then it has a width of 17 units, which may help to explain the choice of ratios in the complex.[^o90]

### Christian architecture {#Christian_architecture}

The [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) [patriarchal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecumenical_Patriarch_of_Constantinople) [basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica) of [Haghia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haghia_Sophia) in [Byzantium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantium) (now [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul)), first constructed in 537 (and twice rebuilt), was for a thousand years the largest cathedral ever built. It inspired many later buildings including [Sultan Ahmed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sultan_Ahmed_Mosque) and other mosques in the city. The [Byzantine architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_architecture) includes a nave crowned by a circular dome and two half-domes, all of the same diameter (31 m), with a further five smaller half-domes forming an [apse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apse) and four rounded corners of a vast rectangular interior.[^o92] This was interpreted by mediaeval architects as representing the mundane below (the square base) and the divine heavens above (the soaring spherical dome).[^o93] The emperor [Justinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justinian) used two geometers, [Isidore of Miletus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isidore_of_Miletus) and [Anthemius of Tralles](wikipedia://en/1873) as architects; Isidore compiled the works of [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) on [solid geometry](wikipedia://en/507960), and was influenced by him.[^o70][^o94]

*Image: Hagia-Sophia-Grundriss.jpg.* [Haghia Sophia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haghia_Sophia), Istanbula) Plan of gallery (upper half)b) Plan of the ground floor (lower half)

The importance of water [baptism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptism) in Christianity was reflected in the scale of [baptistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptistry) architecture. The oldest, the [Lateran Baptistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lateran_Baptistry) in Rome, built in 440,[^o95] set a trend for octagonal baptistries; the [baptismal font](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baptismal_font) inside these buildings was often octagonal, though Italy's largest [baptistry, at Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisa_Baptistry), built between 1152 and 1363, is circular, with an octagonal font. It is \54.86 m high, with a diameter of \34.13 m (a ratio of 8:5).[^o96] [Saint Ambrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Ambrose) wrote that fonts and baptistries were octagonal "because on the eighth day, by rising, Christ loosens the bondage of death and receives the dead from their graves."[^o97][^o98] [Saint Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Augustine) similarly described the eighth day as "everlasting ... hallowed by the [resurrection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resurrection) of Christ".[^o98][^o99] The octagonal [Baptistry of Saint John, Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence_Baptistery), built between 1059 and 1128, is one of the oldest buildings in that city, and one of the last in the direct tradition of classical antiquity; it was extremely influential in the subsequent Florentine Renaissance, as major architects including [Francesco Talenti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Talenti), Alberti and Brunelleschi used it as the model of classical architecture.[^o100]

The number five is used "exuberantly"[^o127] in the 1721 [Pilgrimage Church of St John of Nepomuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilgrimage_Church_of_St_John_of_Nepomuk) at Zelená hora, near [Žďár nad Sázavou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Žďár_nad_Sázavou) in the Czech republic, designed by [Jan Blažej Santini Aichel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Santini_Aichel). The nave is circular, surrounded by five pairs of columns and five oval domes alternating with ogival apses. The church further has five gates, five chapels, five altars and five stars; a legend claims that when [Saint John of Nepomuk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_John_of_Nepomuk) was martyred, five stars appeared over his head.[^o127][^o101] The fivefold architecture may also symbolise the [five wounds of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Five_wounds_of_Christ) and the five letters of "Tacui" (Latin: "I kept silence" \[about secrets of the [confessional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessional)\]).[^o102]

[Antoni Gaudí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antoni_Gaudí) used a wide variety of geometric structures, some being minimal surfaces, in the [Sagrada Família](wikipedia://en/59545), [Barcelona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcelona), started in 1882 (and not completed as of 2023). These include hyperbolic [paraboloids](wikipedia://en/145845) and [hyperboloids of revolution](wikipedia://en/145844),[^o105] tessellations, [catenary arches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catenary_arch), [catenoids](wikipedia://en/273329), [helicoids](wikipedia://en/684207), and [ruled surfaces](wikipedia://en/705158). This varied mix of geometries is creatively combined in different ways around the church. For example, in the Passion Façade of Sagrada Família, Gaudí assembled stone "branches" in the form of hyperbolic paraboloids, which overlap at their tops (directrices) without, therefore, meeting at a point. In contrast, in the colonnade there are hyperbolic paraboloidal surfaces that smoothly join other structures to form unbounded surfaces. Further, Gaudí exploits [natural patterns](wikipedia://en/35659147), themselves mathematical, with [columns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column) derived from the shapes of [trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree), and [lintels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lintel) made from unmodified [basalt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basalt) naturally cracked (by cooling from molten rock) into [hexagonal columns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_places_with_columnar_jointed_volcanics).[^o103][^o104][^o105]

The 1971 [Cathedral of Saint Mary of the Assumption, San Francisco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Saint_Mary_of_the_Assumption_%28San_Francisco,_California%29) has a [saddle roof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddle_roof) composed of eight segments of hyperbolic paraboloids, arranged so that the bottom horizontal cross section of the roof is a square and the top cross section is a [Christian cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_cross). The building is a square \77.7 m on a side, and \57.9 m high.[^o106] The 1970 [Cathedral of Brasília](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Brasília) by [Oscar Niemeyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Niemeyer) makes a different use of a hyperboloid structure; it is constructed from 16 identical concrete beams, each weighing 90 tonnes, arranged in a circle to form a hyperboloid of revolution, the white beams creating a shape like hands praying to heaven. Only the dome is visible from outside: most of the building is below ground.[^o107][^o108][^o109][^o110]

Several medieval [churches in Scandinavia are circular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nordic_round_churches), including four on the Danish island of [Bornholm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornholm). One of the oldest of these, [Østerlars Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Østerlars_Church) from c. 1160, has a circular nave around a massive circular stone column, pierced with arches and decorated with a fresco. The circular structure has three storeys and was apparently fortified, the top storey having served for defence.[^o111] [^o112]

## Mathematical decoration {#Mathematical_decoration}

### Islamic architectural decoration {#Islamic_architectural_decoration}

Islamic buildings are often decorated with [geometric patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_geometric_patterns) which typically make use of several mathematical [tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tessellations), formed of ceramic tiles ([girih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girih), [zellige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zellige)) that may themselves be plain or decorated with stripes.[^o70] Symmetries such as stars with six, eight, or multiples of eight points are used in Islamic patterns. Some of these are based on the "Khatem Sulemani" or Solomon's seal motif, which is an eight-pointed star made of two squares, one rotated 45 degrees from the other on the same centre.[^o115] Islamic patterns exploit many of the 17 possible [wallpaper groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallpaper_groups); as early as 1944, Edith Müller showed that the Alhambra made use of 11 wallpaper groups in its decorations, while in 1986 [Branko Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) claimed to have found 13 wallpaper groups in the Alhambra, asserting controversially that the remaining four groups are not found anywhere in Islamic ornament.[^o115]

### Modern architectural decoration {#Modern_architectural_decoration}

Towards the end of the 20th century, novel mathematical constructs such as fractal geometry and aperiodic tiling were seized upon by architects to provide interesting and attractive coverings for buildings.[^o5] In 1913, the Modernist architect [Adolf Loos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Loos) had declared that "Ornament is a crime",[^o117] influencing architectural thinking for the rest of the 20th century. In the 21st century, architects are again starting to explore the use of [ornament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ornament_%28art%29). 21st century ornamentation is extremely diverse. Henning Larsen's 2011 [Harpa Concert and Conference Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harpa_Concert_and_Conference_Centre), Reykjavik has what looks like a crystal wall of rock made of large blocks of glass.[^o117] Foreign Office Architects' 2010 [Ravensbourne College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ravensbourne_College), London is tessellated decoratively with 28,000 anodised aluminium tiles in red, white and brown, interlinking circular windows of differing sizes. The tessellation uses three types of tile, an equilateral triangle and two irregular pentagons.[^o118][^n1] Kazumi Kudo's [Kanazawa Umimirai Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanazawa_Umimirai_Library) creates a decorative grid made of small circular blocks of glass set into plain concrete walls.[^o117]

## Defence {#Defence}

### Europe {#Europe}

The architecture of [fortifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortification) evolved from [medieval fortresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_fortification), which had high masonry walls, to low, symmetrical [star forts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_fort) able to resist [artillery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artillery) bombardment between the mid-fifteenth and nineteenth centuries. The geometry of the star shapes was dictated by the need to avoid dead zones where attacking infantry could shelter from defensive fire; the sides of the projecting points were angled to permit such fire to sweep the ground, and to provide crossfire (from both sides) beyond each projecting point. Well-known architects who designed such defences include [Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo), [Baldassare Peruzzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldassare_Peruzzi), [Vincenzo Scamozzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Scamozzi) and [Sébastien Le Prestre de Vauban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sébastien_Le_Prestre_de_Vauban).[^o121][^o122]

The architectural historian [Siegfried Giedion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siegfried_Giedion) argued that the star-shaped fortification had a formative influence on the patterning of the Renaissance [ideal city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urban_planning): "The Renaissance was hypnotized by one city type which for a century and a half—from Filarete to Scamozzi—was impressed upon all utopian schemes: this is the star-shaped city."[^o123]

### China {#China}

*Image: HakkaYongding.jpg.* A [tulou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulou) in [Yongding County](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongding_County), [Fujian province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujian_province)

In [Chinese architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_architecture), the [tulou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tulou) of [Fujian province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fujian_province) are circular, communal defensive structures with mainly blank walls and a single iron-plated wooden door, some dating back to the sixteenth century. The walls are topped with roofs that slope gently both outwards and inwards, forming a ring. The centre of the circle is an open cobbled courtyard, often with a well, surrounded by timbered galleries up to five stories high.[^o125]

## Environmental goals {#Environmental_goals}

*Image: Yakhchal of Yazd province.jpg.* [Yakhchal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakhchal) in [Yazd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazd), Iran

Architects may also select the form of a building to meet environmental goals.[^o127] For example, [Foster and Partners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foster_and_Partners)' [30 St Mary Axe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/30_St_Mary_Axe), London, known as "The Gherkin" for its [cucumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cucumber)-like shape, is a [solid of revolution](wikipedia://en/329400) designed using [parametric modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_aided_design). Its geometry was chosen not purely for aesthetic reasons, but to minimise whirling air currents at its base. Despite the building's apparently curved surface, all the panels of glass forming its skin are flat, except for the lens at the top. Most of the panels are [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278), as they can be cut from rectangular glass with less wastage than triangular panels.[^o128]

The traditional [yakhchal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakhchal) (ice pit) of [Persia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran) functioned as an [evaporative cooler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporative_cooler). Above ground, the structure had a domed shape, but had a subterranean storage space for ice and sometimes food as well. The subterranean space and the thick heat-resistant construction insulated the storage space year round. The internal space was often further cooled with [windcatchers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windcatcher).[^o129]
