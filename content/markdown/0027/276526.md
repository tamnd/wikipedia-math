# Fiber bundle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fiber bundle"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and particularly [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a **fiber bundle** ([*Commonwealth English*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_in_the_Commonwealth_of_Nations): **fibre bundle**) is a [space](wikipedia://en/5308894) that is locally a [product space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space), but globally may have a different [topological structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_structure). Specifically, the similarity between a space $E$ and a product space $B \times F$ is defined using a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873) [map](wikipedia://en/516931), $\pi : E \to B,$ that in small regions of $E$ behaves just like a projection from corresponding regions of $B \times F$ to $B.$ The map $\pi,$ called the **[projection](wikipedia://en/3909097)** or [**submersion**](wikipedia://en/356413) of the bundle, is regarded as part of the structure of the bundle. The space $E$ is known as the **total space** of the fiber bundle, $B$ as the **base space**, and $F$ the **fiber**.

:::

In the *[trivial](wikipedia://en/193053)* case, $E$ is just $B \times F,$ and the map $\pi$ is just the projection from the product space to the first factor. This is called a **trivial bundle**. Examples of non-trivial fiber bundles include the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817) and [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412), as well as nontrivial [covering spaces](wikipedia://en/250323). Fiber bundles, such as the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) and other more general [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524), play an important role in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562), as do [principal bundles](wikipedia://en/310950).

Mappings between total spaces of fiber bundles that "commute" with the projection maps are known as [bundle maps](wikipedia://en/7220589), and the [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of fiber bundles forms a [category](wikipedia://en/5869) with respect to such mappings. A bundle map from the base space itself (with the [identity mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_mapping) as projection) to $E$ is called a [section](wikipedia://en/367621) of $E.$ Fiber bundles can be specialized in a number of ways, the most common of which is requiring that the [transition maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_map) between the local trivial patches lie in a certain [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315), known as the **structure group**, acting on the fiber $F$.

## History {#History}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the terms ***fiber*** (German: *Faser*) and ***fiber space*** (*gefaserter Raum*) appeared for the first time in a paper by [Herbert Seifert](wikipedia://en/637024) in 1933,[^o3][^o4][^o5] but his definitions are limited to a very special case. The main difference from the present day conception of a fiber space, however, was that for Seifert what is now called the **base space** (topological space) of a fiber (topological) space *E* was not part of the structure, but derived from it as a quotient space of *E*. The first definition of **fiber space** was given by [Hassler Whitney](wikipedia://en/391794) in 1935[^o6] under the name **sphere space**, but in 1940 Whitney changed the name to **sphere bundle**.[^o7]

The theory of fibered spaces, of which [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524), [principal bundles](wikipedia://en/310950), topological [fibrations](wikipedia://en/316826) and [fibered manifolds](wikipedia://en/3247635) are a special case, is attributed to [Herbert Seifert](wikipedia://en/637024), [Heinz Hopf](wikipedia://en/543082), [Jacques Feldbau](wikipedia://en/34779188),[^o8] Whitney, [Norman Earl Steenrod](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norman_Earl_Steenrod), [Charles Ehresmann](wikipedia://en/605108),[^o9][^o10][^o11] [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483),[^o12] and others.

Fiber bundles became their own object of study in the period 1935–1940. The first general definition appeared in the works of Whitney.[^o13]

Whitney came to the general definition of a fiber bundle from his study of a more particular notion of a [sphere bundle](wikipedia://en/48880896),[^o14] that is a fiber bundle whose fiber is a sphere of arbitrary [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398).[^o15]

::: {#o17 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A fiber bundle is a 4-tuple $(E,\, B,\, \pi,\, F),$ where $E, B,$ and $F$ are [topological spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_spaces) and $\pi : E \to B$ is a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29) [surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection) satisfying a *local triviality* condition outlined below. The space $B$ is called the **base space** of the bundle, $E$ the **total space**, and $F$ the **fiber**. The map $\pi$ is called the **projection map** (or **bundle projection**). We shall assume in what follows that the base space $B$ is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233).

We require that for every $x \in B$, there is an open [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28topology%29) $U \subseteq B$ of $x$ (which will be called a trivializing neighborhood) such that there is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) $\varphi : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times F$ (where $\pi^{-1}(U)$ is given the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926), and $U \times F$ is the product space) in such a way that $\pi$ agrees with the projection onto the first factor. That is, the following diagram should [commute](wikipedia://en/138484):

*Image: Fibre bundle local trivial.svg.* Local triviality condition

where $\operatorname{proj}_1 : U \times F \to U$ is the natural projection. The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all $\left\{\left(U_i,\, \varphi_i\right)\right\}$ is called a **local trivialization** of the bundle.

Thus for any $p \in B$, the [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) $\pi^{-1}(\{p\})$ is homeomorphic to $F$ (since this is true of $\operatorname{proj}_1^{-1}(\{p\})$) and is called the **fiber over $p$**. Every fiber bundle $\pi : E \to B$ is an [open map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map), since projections of products are open maps. Therefore $B$ carries the [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) determined by the map $\pi.$

A fiber bundle $(E,\, B,\, \pi,\, F)$ is often denoted

$$\begin{matrix} {} \\ F \longrightarrow E\ \xrightarrow{\,\ \pi\ }\ B \\ {} \end{matrix}$$

that, in analogy with a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence), indicates which space is the fiber, total space and base space, as well as the map from total to base space.

A **smooth fiber bundle** is a fiber bundle in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold). That is, $E$, $B$, and $F$ are required to be smooth manifolds and all the [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) above are required to be [smooth maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map).

## Examples {#Examples}

::: {#o21 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Trivial bundle"}

### Trivial bundle {#Trivial_bundle}

Let $E = B \times F$ and let $\pi : E \to B$ be the projection onto the first factor. Then $\pi$ is a fiber bundle (of $F$) over $B.$ Here $E$ is not just locally a product but *globally* one. Any such fiber bundle is called a **trivial bundle**. Any fiber bundle over a [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414) [CW-complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CW-complex) is trivial.

:::

### Nontrivial bundles {#Nontrivial_bundles}

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Möbius bundle"}

#### Möbius strip {#Möbius_strip}

*Image: Moebius Surface 1 Display Small.png.* The Möbius strip is a nontrivial bundle over the circle.

Perhaps the simplest example of a nontrivial bundle $E$ is the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817). It has the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) that runs lengthwise along the center of the strip as a base $B$ and a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) for the fiber $F$, so the Möbius strip is a bundle of the line segment over the circle. A [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $U$ of $\pi(x) \in B$ (where $x \in E$) is an [arc](wikipedia://en/5578920); in the picture, this is the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) of one of the squares. The [preimage](wikipedia://en/579311) $\pi^{-1}(U)$ in the picture is a (somewhat twisted) slice of the strip four squares wide and one long (i.e. all the points that project to $U$).

A homeomorphism ($\varphi$ in ) exists that maps the preimage of $U$ (the trivializing neighborhood) to a slice of a cylinder: curved, but not twisted. This pair locally trivializes the strip. The corresponding trivial bundle $B\times F$ would be a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29), but the Möbius strip has an overall "twist". This twist is visible only globally; locally the Möbius strip and the cylinder are identical (making a single vertical cut in either gives the same space).

:::

#### Klein bottle {#Klein_bottle}

A similar nontrivial bundle is the [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412), which can be viewed as a "twisted" circle bundle over another circle. The corresponding non-twisted (trivial) bundle is the 2-[torus](wikipedia://en/74800), $S^1 \times S^1$.

### Covering map {#Covering_map}

A **[covering space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_map)** is a fiber bundle such that the bundle projection is a [local homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/248582). It follows that the fiber is a [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061).

### Vector and principal bundles {#Vector_and_principal_bundles}

A special class of fiber bundles, called **[vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524)**, are those whose fibers are [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) (to qualify as a vector bundle the structure group of the bundle—see below—must be a [linear group](wikipedia://en/113564)). Important examples of vector bundles include the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) and [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) of a smooth manifold. From any vector bundle, one can construct the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) of [bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28mathematics%29), which is a principal bundle (see below).

Another special class of fiber bundles, called **[principal bundles](wikipedia://en/310950)**, are bundles on whose fibers a [free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_action) and [transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_action) [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) by a group $G$ is given, so that each fiber is a [principal homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363441). The bundle is often specified along with the group by referring to it as a principal $G$-bundle. The group $G$ is also the structure group of the bundle. Given a [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) $\rho$ of $G$ on a vector space $V$, a vector bundle with $\rho(G) \subseteq \text{Aut}(V)$ as a structure group may be constructed, known as the [associated bundle](wikipedia://en/398874).

::: {#o29 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Sphere bundle (0)"}

### Sphere bundles {#Sphere_bundles}

A **sphere bundle** is a fiber bundle whose fiber is an [*n*-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere). Given a vector bundle $E$ with a [metric](wikipedia://en/195795) (such as the tangent bundle to a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652)) one can construct the associated **unit sphere bundle**, for which the fiber over a point $x$ is the set of all [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) in $E_x$. When the vector bundle in question is the tangent bundle $TM$, the unit sphere bundle is known as the **[unit tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/7932644)**.

A sphere bundle is partially characterized by its [Euler class](wikipedia://en/2932393), which is a degree $n + 1$ [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) class in the total space of the bundle. In the case $n = 1$ the sphere bundle is called a [circle bundle](wikipedia://en/2794417) and the Euler class is equal to the first [Chern class](wikipedia://en/294349), which characterizes the topology of the bundle completely. For any $n$, given the Euler class of a bundle, one can calculate its cohomology using a [long exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence) called the [Gysin sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gysin_sequence).

:::

### Mapping tori {#Mapping_tori}

If $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) and $f : X \to X$ is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) then the [mapping torus](wikipedia://en/21034384) $M_f$ has a natural structure of a fiber bundle over the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) with fiber $X.$ Mapping tori of homeomorphisms of [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865) are of particular importance in [3-manifold topology](wikipedia://en/1018257).

### Quotient spaces {#Quotient_spaces}

If $G$ is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) and $H$ is a [closed subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subgroup), then under some circumstances, the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) $G/H$ together with the quotient map $\pi : G \to G/H$ is a fiber bundle, whose fiber is the topological space $H$. A [necessary and sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) for ($G,\, G/H,\, \pi,\, H$) to form a fiber bundle is that the mapping $\pi$ admits local cross-sections (Steenrod 1951, §7).

The most general conditions under which the [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map) will admit local cross-sections are not known, although if $G$ is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) and $H$ a closed subgroup (and thus a [Lie subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_subgroup) by [Cartan's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subgroup_theorem)), then the quotient map is a fiber bundle. One example of this is the [Hopf fibration](wikipedia://en/580384), $S^3 \to S^2$, which is a fiber bundle over the sphere $S^2$ whose total space is $S^3$. From the perspective of Lie groups, $S^3$ can be identified with the [special unitary group](wikipedia://en/173997) $SU(2)$. The [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) subgroup of [diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_matrices) is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic_group) to the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) $U(1)$, and the quotient $SU(2)/U(1)$ is [diffeomorphic](wikipedia://en/8564) to the sphere.

More generally, if $G$ is any topological group and $H$ a closed subgroup that also happens to be a Lie group, then $G \to G/H$ is a fiber bundle.

:::

## Sections {#Sections}

A **section** (or **cross section**) of a fiber bundle $\pi$ is a continuous map $f : B \to E$ such that $\pi(f(x)) = x$ [for all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_all) *x* in *B*. Since bundles do not in general have globally defined sections, one of the purposes of the theory is to account for their existence. The [obstruction](wikipedia://en/2179639) to the existence of a section can often be measured by a cohomology class, which leads to the theory of [characteristic classes](wikipedia://en/363530) in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).

The most well-known example is the [hairy ball theorem](wikipedia://en/485168), where the [Euler class](wikipedia://en/2932393) is the obstruction to the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of the [2-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-sphere) having a nowhere vanishing section.

Often one would like to define sections only locally (especially when global sections do not exist). A **local section** of a fiber bundle is a continuous map $f : U \to E$ where *U* is an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) in *B* and $\pi(f(x)) = x$ for all *x* in *U*. If $(U,\, \varphi)$ is a local trivialization [chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chart_%28mathematics%29) then local sections always exist over *U*. Such sections are in [1-1 correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-1_Correspondence) with continuous maps $U \to F$. Sections form a [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466).

::: {#o35 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Structure group"}

## Structure groups and transition functions {#Structure_groups_and_transition_functions}

Fiber bundles often come with a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of symmetries that describe the matching conditions between overlapping local trivialization charts. Specifically, let *G* be a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) that [acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) continuously on the fiber space *F* on the left. We lose nothing if we require *G* to act [faithfully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_action) on *F* so that it may be thought of as a group of [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660) of *F*. A ***G*-[atlas](wikipedia://en/47436)** for the bundle $(E, B, \pi, F)$ is a set of local trivialization charts $\{(U_k,\, \varphi_k)\}$ such that for any $\varphi_i,\varphi_j$ for the overlapping charts $(U_i,\, \varphi_i)$ and $(U_j,\, \varphi_j)$ the function

$$\varphi_i\varphi_j^{-1} : \left(U_i \cap U_j\right) \times F \to \left(U_i \cap U_j\right) \times F$$

is given by

$$\varphi_i\varphi_j^{-1}(x,\, \xi) = \left(x,\, t_{ij}(x)\xi\right)$$

where $t_{ij} : U_i \cap U_j \to G$ is a continuous map called a **transition function**. Two *G*-atlases are equivalent if their union is also a *G*-atlas. A ***G*-bundle** is a fiber bundle with an equivalence class of *G*-atlases. The group *G* is called the **structure group** of the bundle. This is related to the [gauge group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_group) in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), which is the group of automorphisms of the principal *G*-bundle that leave the base space unchanged.

In the smooth category, a *G*-bundle is a smooth fiber bundle where *G* is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) and the corresponding action on *F* is smooth and the transition functions are all smooth maps.

The transition functions $t_{ij}$ satisfy the following conditions

1. $$t_{ii}(x) = 1\,$$
1. $$t_{ij}(x) = t_{ji}(x)^{-1}\,$$
1. $$t_{ik}(x) = t_{ij}(x)t_{jk}(x).\,$$

The third condition applies on triple overlaps *U<sub>i</sub>* ∩ *U<sub>j</sub>* ∩ *U<sub>k</sub>* and is called the (Čech) **[cocycle](wikipedia://en/856614#Cocycle) condition** (see also [Čech cohomology](wikipedia://en/856614)). The importance of this is that the transition functions determine the fiber bundle (if one assumes the cocycle condition).

A [principal *G*-bundle](wikipedia://en/310950) is a *G*-bundle where the fiber *F* is a [principal homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363441) for the left action of *G* itself (equivalently, one can specify that the action of *G* on the fiber *F* is free and transitive, i.e. [regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Regular)). In this case, it is often a matter of convenience to identify *F* with *G* and so obtain a (right) action of *G* on the principal bundle.

:::

## Bundle maps {#Bundle_maps}

It is useful to have notions of a mapping between two fiber bundles. Suppose that $M$ and $N$ are base spaces, and $\pi_E : E \to M$ and $\pi_F : F \to N$ are fiber bundles over $M$ and $N$, respectively. A **bundle map** or **bundle morphism** consists of a pair of continuous[^o37] functions

$$\varphi : E \to F,\quad f : M \to N$$

such that $\pi_F\circ \varphi = f \circ \pi_E$. That is, the following diagram is [commutative](wikipedia://en/138484):

*Image: BundleMorphism-04.svg.*

For fiber bundles with structure group $G$ and whose total spaces are (right) $G$-spaces (such as a principal bundle), bundle [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) are also required to be $G$-[equivariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivariant) on the fibers. This means that $\varphi : E \to F$ is also $G$-morphism from one $G$-space to another, that is, $\varphi(xs) = \varphi(x)s$ for all $x \in E$ and $s \in G$.

In case the base spaces $M$ and $N$ coincide, then a bundle morphism over $M$ from the fiber bundle $\pi_E : E \to M$ to $\pi_F : F \to M$ is a map $\varphi : E \to F$ such that $\pi_E = \pi_F \circ \varphi$. This means that the bundle map $\varphi : E \to F$ [covers](wikipedia://en/317552) the identity of $M$. That is, $f \equiv \mathrm{id}_{M}$ and the following diagram commutes:

*Image: BundleMorphism-03.svg.*

Assume that both $\pi_E : E \to M$ and $\pi_F : F \to M$ are defined over the same base space $M$. A bundle [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) is a bundle map $(\varphi,\, f)$ between $\pi_E : E \to M$ and $\pi_F : F \to M$ such that $f \equiv \mathrm{id}_M$ and such that $\varphi$ is also a homeomorphism.[^o38]

## Differentiable fiber bundles {#Differentiable_fiber_bundles}

In the category of [differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219), fiber bundles arise naturally as [submersions](wikipedia://en/356413) of one manifold to another. Not every (differentiable) submersion $f : M \to N$ from a differentiable manifold *M* to another differentiable manifold *N* gives rise to a differentiable fiber bundle. For one thing, the map must be surjective, and $(M, N, f)$ is called a [fibered manifold](wikipedia://en/3247635). However, this necessary condition is not quite sufficient, and there are a variety of sufficient conditions in common use.

If *M* and *N* are [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [connected](wikipedia://en/308815), then any submersion $f : M \to N$ gives rise to a fiber bundle in the sense that there is a fiber space *F* diffeomorphic to each of the fibers such that $(E, B, \pi, F) = (M, N, f, F)$ is a fiber bundle. (Surjectivity of $f$ follows by the assumptions already given in this case.) More generally, the assumption of compactness can be relaxed if the submersion $f : M \to N$ is assumed to be a surjective [proper map](wikipedia://en/1190262), meaning that $f^{-1}(K)$ is compact for every compact [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) *K* of *N*. Another sufficient condition, due to (Ehresmann 1951), is that if $f : M \to N$ is a surjective [submersion](wikipedia://en/356413) with *M* and *N* [differentiable manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219) such that the preimage $f^{-1}\{x\}$ is compact and connected for all $x \in N,$ then $f$ admits a [compatible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compatible_%28algebra%29) fiber bundle structure (Michor 2008, §17).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

- The notion of a [bundle](wikipedia://en/827658) applies to many more categories in mathematics, at the expense of appropriately modifying the local triviality condition; cf. [principal homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363441) and [torsor (algebraic geometry)](wikipedia://en/41910974).
- In topology, a [fibration](wikipedia://en/316826) is a mapping $\pi : E \to B$ that has certain [homotopy-theoretic](wikipedia://en/61994831) properties in common with fiber bundles. Specifically, under mild technical assumptions a fiber bundle always has the [homotopy lifting property](wikipedia://en/522786) or homotopy covering property (see (Steenrod 1951, 11.7) for details). This is the defining property of a fibration.
- A section of a fiber bundle is a "function whose output [range](wikipedia://en/275991) is continuously dependent on the input." This property is formally captured in the notion of [dependent type](wikipedia://en/1949487).

:::
