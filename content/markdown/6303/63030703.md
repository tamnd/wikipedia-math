# Matching in hypergraphs

*Image: Hypergraph matchings.svg.* The red set of edges is a **perfect matching**, because it contains every vertex of the hypergraph. The yellow set is a **maximum-cardinality matching**, because it contains the most edges possible for a matching in this hypergraph, and the **matching number** is therefore 3. In a regular graph, if one has a perfect matching, that same matching is definitionally the maximum-cardinality matching for the graph, but in a hypergraph, where the number of vertices connected by an edge is variable, they can be 2 distinct matchings, as shown here.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Matching in hypergraphs"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **matching** in a [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535) is a set of [hyperedges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperedges), in which every two hyperedges are [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359). It is an extension of the notion of [matching in a graph](wikipedia://en/581797). [^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Recall that a [hypergraph](wikipedia://en/232535) $H$ is a pair $(V, E)$, where $V$ is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) and $E$ is a set of [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of $V$ called *hyperedges*. Each hyperedge may contain one or more vertices.

A **matching** in $H$ is a subset $M$ of $E$, such that every two hyperedges $e_{1}$ and $e_{2}$ in $M$ have an empty intersection (have no vertex in common).

The **matching number** of a hypergraph $H$ is the largest size of a matching in $H$. It is often denoted by $\nu(H)$.[^o2] [^o6]

As an example, let $V$ be the set $\{1,2,3,4,5,6,7\}.$ Consider a 3-uniform hypergraph on $V$ (a hypergraph in which each hyperedge contains exactly 3 vertices). Let $H$ be a 3-uniform hypergraph with 4 hyperedges:

$$\{ \{1,2,3\}, \{1,4,5\}, \{4,5,6\}, \{2,3,6\} \}$$

Then $H$ admits several matchings of size 2, for example:

$$\{ \{1,2,3\}, \{4,5,6\} \}$$

$$\{ \{1,4,5\}, \{2,3,6\} \}$$

However, in any subset of 3 hyperedges, at least two of them intersect, so there is no matching of size 3. Hence, the matching number of $H$ is 2.

:::

## Intersecting hypergraph {#Intersecting_hypergraph}

A hypergraph $H = (V, E)$ is called *intersecting* if every two hyperedges in $E$ have a vertex in common. A hypergraph $H$ is intersecting [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it has no matching with two or more hyperedges, if and only if $\nu(H) = 1$.[^o11]

## Matching in a graph as a special case {#Matching_in_a_graph_as_a_special_case}

A graph without [self-loops](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-loop) is just a 2-uniform hypergraph: each edge can be considered as a set of the two vertices that it connects. For example, this 2-uniform hypergraph represents a graph with 4 vertices $\{1,2,3,4\}$ and 3 edges:

$$\{ \{1,3\}, \{1,4\}, \{2,4\} \}$$

By the above definition, a matching in a graph is a set $M$ of edges, such that each two edges in $M$ have an empty intersection. This is equivalent to saying that no two edges in $M$ are adjacent to the same vertex; this is exactly the definition of a [matching in a graph](wikipedia://en/581797).

## Fractional matching {#Fractional_matching}

A **[fractional matching](wikipedia://en/64410778)** in a hypergraph is a function that assigns a fraction in $[0,1]$ to each hyperedge, such that for every vertex $v$ in $V$, the sum of fractions of hyperedges containing $v$ is at most 1. A matching is a special case of a fractional matching in which all fractions are either 0 or 1. The *size* of a fractional matching is the sum of fractions of all hyperedges.

The **fractional matching number** of a hypergraph $H$ is the largest size of a fractional matching in $H$. It is often denoted by $\nu*(H)$.[^o6]

Since a matching is a special case of a fractional matching, for every hypergraph $H$:

> Matching-number($H$) ≤ fractional-matching-number($H$)

Symbolically, this principle is written:

$$\nu(H) \leq \nu^*(H)$$

In general, the fractional matching number may be larger than the matching number. A theorem by [Zoltán Füredi](wikipedia://en/22730000)[^o11] provides upper bounds on the fractional-matching-number($H$) / matching-number($H$) ratio:

- If each hyperedge in $H$ contains at most $r$ vertices, then

$$\frac{\nu^*(H)}{ \nu (H)} \leq r-1+ \frac{1}{r}.$$

In particular, in a simple graph:[^o12]

$$\frac{\nu^*(H)}{ \nu (H)} \leq \frac{3}{2}.$$

- The inequality is sharp: Let $H_{r}$ be the $r$-uniform [finite projective plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_projective_plane). Then $\nu(H_{r}) = 1$ since every two hyperedges intersect, and $\nu*(H_{r}) = r - 1 + \tfrac{1}{r}$ by the fractional matching that assigns a weight of $\tfrac{1}{r}$ to each hyperedge (it is a matching since each vertex is contained in $r$ hyperedges, and its size is $r - 1 + \tfrac{1}{r}$ since there are $r^{2} - r + 1$ hyperedges). Therefore the ratio is exactly $r - 1 + \tfrac{1}{r}$.
- If $r$ is such that the $r$-uniform [finite projective plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_projective_plane) does not exist (for example, $r = 7$), then a stronger inequality holds:

$$\frac{\nu^*(H)}{\nu (H)} \leq r-1.$$

- If $H$ is $r$-partite (the vertices are partitioned into $r$ parts and each hyperedge contains a vertex from each part), then:

$$\frac{\nu^*(H)}{\nu (H)} \leq r-1.$$

In particular, in a bipartite graph, $\nu*(H) = \nu(H)$. This was proved by [András Gyárfás](wikipedia://en/614969).[^o11]

- The inequality is sharp: Let $H_{r-}$ be the [truncated projective plane](wikipedia://en/64551468) of order $r - 1$. Then $\nu(H_{r-}) = 1$ since every two hyperedges intersect, and $\nu*(H_{r-}) = r - 1$ by the fractional matching that assigns a weight of $\tfrac{1}{r}$ to each hyperedge (there are $r^{2} - r$ hyperedges).

:::

## Perfect matching {#Perfect_matching}

A matching $M$ is called **perfect** if every vertex $v$ in $V$ is contained in *exactly* one hyperedge of $M$. This is the natural extension of the notion of [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) in a graph.

A fractional matching $M$ is called **perfect** if for every vertex $v$ in $V$, the sum of fractions of hyperedges in $M$ containing $v$ is *exactly* 1.

Consider a hypergraph $H$ in which each hyperedge contains at most $n$ vertices. If $H$ admits a perfect fractional matching, then its fractional matching number is at least $\tfrac{\lvert V\rvert}{n}$. If each hyperedge in $H$ contains exactly $n$ vertices, then its fractional matching number is at exactly $\tfrac{\lvert V\rvert}{n}$.[^o14] This is a generalization of the fact that, in a graph, the size of a perfect matching is $\tfrac{\lvert V\rvert}{2}$.

Given a set $V$ of vertices, a collection $E$ of subsets of $V$ is called *balanced* if the hypergraph $(V,E)$ admits a perfect fractional matching.

For example, if $V = \{1,2,3,a,b,c\}$ and $E = \{ \{1,a\}, \{2,a\}, \{1,b\}, \{2,b\}, \{3,c\} \},$ then $E$ is balanced, with the perfect fractional matching $\{ 1/2, 1/2, 1/2, 1/2, 1 \}.$

There are various sufficient conditions for the existence of a perfect matching in a hypergraph:

- [Hall-type theorems for hypergraphs](wikipedia://en/64398432) - presents sufficient conditions analogous to [Hall's marriage theorem](wikipedia://en/186969), based on sets of neighbors.
- [Perfect matching in high-degree hypergraphs](wikipedia://en/64555429) - presents sufficient conditions analogous to [Dirac's theorem on Hamiltonian cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac's_theorem_on_Hamiltonian_cycles), based on degree of vertices.
- [Keevash](wikipedia://en/41707901) and Mycroft developed a geometric theory for hypergraph matching.[^o15]

## Balanced set-family {#Balanced_set-family}

A [set-family](wikipedia://en/723043) $E$ over a ground set $V$ is called *balanced* (with respect to $V$) if the hypergraph $H = (V, E)$ admits a perfect fractional matching.[^o14]

For example, consider the vertex set $V = \{1,2,3,a,b,c\}$ and the edge set $E = \{1-a, 2-a, 1-b, 2-b, 3-c\}.$ $E$ is balanced, since there is a perfect fractional matching with weights $\{1/2, 1/2, 1/2, 1/2, 1\}.$

## Computing a maximum matching {#Computing_a_maximum_matching}

The problem of finding a maximum-cardinality matching in a hypergraph, thus calculating $\nu(H)$, is NP-hard even for 3-uniform hypergraphs (see [3-dimensional matching](wikipedia://en/22261908)). This is in contrast to the case of simple (2-uniform) graphs in which computing a [maximum-cardinality matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_cardinality_matching) can be done in polynomial time.

## Matching and covering {#Matching_and_covering}

A [*vertex-cover in a hypergraph*](wikipedia://en/64318108) $H = (V, E)$ is a subset $T$ of $V$, such that every hyperedge in $E$ contains at least one vertex of $T$ (it is also called a [**transversal**](wikipedia://en/897733) or a **[hitting set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitting_set)**, and is equivalent to a [set cover](wikipedia://en/870399)). It is a generalization of the notion of a [vertex cover](wikipedia://en/562782) in a graph.

The **vertex-cover number** of a hypergraph $H$ is the smallest size of a vertex cover in $H$. It is often denoted by $\tau(H)$,[^o2] for transversal.

A **fractional vertex-cover** is a function assigning a weight to each vertex in $V$, such that for every hyperedge $e$ in $E$, the sum of fractions of vertices in $e$ is at least 1. A vertex cover is a special case of a fractional vertex cover in which all weights are either 0 or 1. The *size* of a fractional vertex-cover is the sum of fractions of all vertices.

The **fractional vertex-cover number** of a hypergraph $H$ is the smallest size of a fractional vertex-cover in $H$. It is often denoted by $\tau*(H)$.

Since a vertex-cover is a special case of a fractional vertex-cover, for every hypergraph $H$:

> fractional-vertex-cover-number ($H$) ≤ vertex-cover-number ($H$).

[Linear programming duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_programming_duality) implies that, for every hypergraph $H$:

> fractional-matching-number ($H$) = fractional-vertex-cover-number($H$).

Hence, for every hypergraph $H$:[^o11]

$$\nu(H) \leq \nu^*(H) = \tau^*(H)\leq \tau(H)$$

If the size of each hyperedge in $H$ is at most $r$ then the union of all hyperedges in a maximum matching is a vertex-cover (if there was an uncovered hyperedge, we could have added it to the matching). Therefore:

$$\tau(H)\leq r\cdot \nu(H).$$

This inequality is tight: equality holds, for example, when $V$ contains $r\cdot\nu(H) + r - 1$ vertices and $E$ contains all subsets of $r$ vertices.

However, in general $\tau*(H) < r\cdot\nu(H)$, since $\nu*(H) < r\cdot\nu(H)$; see Fractional matching above.

*[Ryser's conjecture](wikipedia://en/64550992)* says that, in every $r$-partite $r$-uniform hypergraph:

$$\tau (H)\leq (r-1) \nu(H).$$

Some special cases of the conjecture have been proved; see [Ryser's conjecture](wikipedia://en/64550992).

## Kőnig's property {#Kőnig's_property}

A hypergraph has the **Kőnig property** if its maximum matching number equals its minimum vertex-cover number, namely if $\nu(H) = \tau(H)$. The [Kőnig-Egerváry theorem](wikipedia://en/5465118) shows that every [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) has the Kőnig property. To extend this theorem to hypergraphs, we need to extend the notion of bipartiteness to hypergraphs.[^o2]

A natural generalization is as follows. A hypergraph is called **2-colorable** if its vertices can be 2-colored so that every hyperedge (of size at least 2) contains at least one vertex of each color. An alternative term is [Property B](wikipedia://en/915558). A simple graph is bipartite iff it is 2-colorable. However, there are 2-colorable hypergraphs without Kőnig's property. For example, consider the hypergraph with $V = \{1,2,3,4\}$ with all triplets $E = \{ \{1,2,3\} , \{1,2,4\} , \{1,3,4\} , \{2,3,4\} \}.$ It is 2-colorable, for example, we can color $\{1,2\}$ blue and $\{3,4\}$ white. However, its matching number is 1 and its vertex-cover number is 2.

A stronger generalization is as follows. Given a hypergraph $H = (V, E)$ and a subset $V'$ of $V$, the **restriction** of $H$ to $V'$ is the hypergraph whose vertices are $V$, and for every hyperedge $e$ in $E$ that intersects $V'$, it has a hyperedge $e'$ that is the intersection of $e$ and $V'$. A hypergraph is called **[balanced](wikipedia://en/64435862)** if all its restrictions are *essentially 2-colorable*, meaning that we ignore singleton hyperedges in the restriction.[^o20] A simple graph is bipartite iff it is balanced.

A simple graph is bipartite iff it has no odd-length cycles. Similarly, a hypergraph is balanced iff it has no odd-length *circuits*. A circuit of length $k$ in a hypergraph is an alternating sequence $(v_{1}, e_{1}, v_{2}, e_{2}, \ldots, v_{k}, e_{k}, v_{k}{}_{+1} = v_{1})$, where the $v_{i}$ are distinct vertices and the $e_{i}$ are distinct hyperedges, and each hyperedge contains the vertex to its left and the vertex to its right. The circuit is called *unbalanced* if each hyperedge contains no other vertices in the circuit. [Claude Berge](wikipedia://en/5893760) proved that a hypergraph is balanced if and only if it does not contain an unbalanced odd-length circuit. Every balanced hypergraph has Kőnig's property.[^o21][^o2]

The following are equivalent:[^o2]

- Every partial hypergraph of $H$ (i.e., a hypergraph derived from $H$ by deleting some hyperedges) has the Kőnig property.
- Every partial hypergraph of $H$ has the property that its maximum degree equals its minimum [edge coloring](wikipedia://en/690647) number.
- $H$ has the [Helly property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helly_property), and the [intersection graph](wikipedia://en/7726759) of $H$ (the simple graph in which the vertices are $E$ and two elements of $E$ are linked if and only if they intersect) is a [perfect graph](wikipedia://en/670531).

## Matching and packing {#Matching_and_packing}

The problem of **[set packing](wikipedia://en/2017636)** is equivalent to hypergraph matching.

A **[vertex-packing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_packing)** in a (simple) graph is a subset $P$ of its vertices, such that no two vertices in $P$ are adjacent.

The problem of finding a maximum vertex-packing in a graph is equivalent to the problem of finding a maximum matching in a hypergraph:[^o2]

- Given a hypergraph $H = (V, E)$, define its *intersection graph* $\operatorname{Int}(H)$ as the simple graph whose vertices are $E$ and whose edges are pairs $(e_{1},e_{2})$ such that $e_{1}$, $e_{2}$ have a vertex in common. Then every matching in $H$ is a vertex-packing in $\operatorname{Int}(H)$ and vice versa.
- Given a graph $G = (V' , E' )$, define its *star hypergraph* $\operatorname{St}(G)$ as the hypergraph whose vertices are $E'$ and whose hyperedges are the [stars](wikipedia://en/19310129) of the vertices of $G$ (i.e., for each vertex $v'$ in $V'$, there is a hyperedge in $\operatorname{St}(G)$ that contains all edges in $E'$ that are adjacent to $v'$). Then every vertex-packing in $G$ is a matching in $\operatorname{St}(G)$ and vice versa.
- Alternatively, given a graph $G = (V' , E' )$, define its *clique hypergraph* $\operatorname{Cl}(G)$ as the hypergraph whose vertices are the [cliques](wikipedia://en/524466) of $G$, and for each vertex $v'$ in $V'$, there is a hyperedge in $\operatorname{Cl}(G)$ containing all cliques in $G$ that contain $v'$. Then again, every vertex-packing in $G$ is a matching in $\operatorname{Cl}(G)$ and vice versa. Note that $\operatorname{Cl}(G)$ cannot be constructed from $G$ in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time), so it cannot be used as a reduction for proving NP-hardness. But it has some theoretical uses.
