# Complemented subspace

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complemented subspace"}

In the branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) called [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a **complemented subspace** of a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $X,$ is a [vector subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subspace) $M$ for which there exists some other vector subspace $N$ of $X,$ called its (**topological**) **complement** in $X$, such that $X$ is the [direct sum $M \oplus N$ in the category of topological vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_topological_vector_spaces). Formally, topological direct sums strengthen the [algebraic direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) by requiring certain maps be continuous; the result retains many nice properties from the operation of direct sum in finite-dimensional vector spaces.

:::

Every finite-dimensional subspace of a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) is complemented, but other subspaces may not. In general, classifying all complemented subspaces is a difficult problem, which has been solved only for [some well-known Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/17542489).

The concept of a complemented subspace is analogous to, but distinct from, that of a [set complement](wikipedia://en/54347). The set-theoretic complement of a vector subspace is never a complementary subspace.

## Preliminaries: definitions and notation {#Preliminaries:_definitions_and_notation}

If $X$ is a vector space and $M$ and $N$ are [vector subspaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subspace) of $X$ then there is a well-defined addition map

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} S :\;&& M \times N &&\;\to \;& X \\ && (m, n) &&\;\mapsto\;& m + n \\ \end{alignedat}$$

The map $S$ is a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) in the [category of vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_vector_spaces) — that is to say, [linear](wikipedia://en/18102).

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Algebraic complement"}

### Algebraic direct sum {#Algebraic_direct_sum}

The vector space $X$ is said to be the algebraic [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) (or direct sum in the category of vector spaces) $M\oplus N$ when any of the following equivalent conditions are satisfied:

1. The addition map $S : M \times N \to X$ is a [vector space isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space_isomorphism).[^n1][^o6]
1. The addition map is bijective.
1. $M \cap N = \{0\}$ and $M + N = X$; in this case $N$ is called an **algebraic complement** or **supplement** to $M$ in $X$ and the two subspaces are said to be **complementary** or **supplementary**.[^o6][^o7]

When these conditions hold, the inverse $S^{-1} : X \to M \times N$ is well-defined and can be written in terms of coordinates as

$$S^{-1} = \left(P_M, P_N\right)\text{.}$$

The first coordinate $P_M : X \to M$ is called the canonical projection of $X$ onto $M$; likewise the second coordinate is the canonical projection onto $N.$[^n2]

Equivalently, $P_M(x)$ and $P_N(x)$ are the unique vectors in $M$ and $N,$ respectively, that satisfy

$$x = P_M(x) + P_N(x)\text{.}$$

As maps,

$$P_M + P_N = \operatorname{Id}_X, \qquad \ker P_M = N, \qquad \text{ and } \qquad \ker P_N = M$$

where $\operatorname{Id}_X$ denotes the [identity map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) on $X$.[^o6]

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Suppose that the vector space $X$ is the algebraic direct sum of $M\oplus N$. In the category of vector spaces, finite [products](wikipedia://en/237868) and [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889) coincide: algebraically, $M \oplus N$ and $M \times N$ are indistinguishable. Given a problem involving elements of $X$, one can break the elements down into their components in $M$ and $N$, because the projection maps defined above act as inverses to the natural inclusion of $M$ and $N$ into $X$. Then one can solve the problem in the vector subspaces and recombine to form an element of $X$.

In the category of [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752), that algebraic decomposition becomes less useful. The definition of a topological vector space requires the addition map $S$ to be continuous; its inverse $S^{-1} : X \to M \times N$ may not be.[^n1] The [categorical definition of a direct sum](wikipedia://en/310889), however, requires $P_M$ and $P_N$ to be morphisms — that is, *continuous* linear maps.

The space $X$ is the **topological direct sum** of $M$ and $N$ if (and only if) any of the following equivalent conditions hold:

1. The addition map $S : M \times N \to X$ is a [TVS-isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVS-isomorphism) (that is, a surjective [linear](wikipedia://en/18102) [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660)).[^n1]
1. $X$ is the algebraic direct sum of $M$ and $N$ and also any of the following equivalent conditions: The inverse of the addition map $S^{-1} : X \to M \times N$ is continuous.; Both canonical projections $P_M : X \to M$ and $P_N : X \to N$ are continuous.; At least one of the canonical projections $P_M$ and $P_N$ is continuous.; The canonical [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map) $p : N \to X / M; p(n)=n+M$ is an isomorphism of topological vector spaces (i.e. a linear homeomorphism).[^o6]
1. $X$ is the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/310889) of $M$ and $N$ in the category of topological vector spaces.
1. The map $S$ is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) and [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map).
1. When considered as [additive](wikipedia://en/332229) [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315), $X$ is the [topological direct sum of the subgroups](wikipedia://en/43854082) $M$ and $N.$

The topological direct sum is also written $X = M \oplus N$; whether the sum is in the topological or algebraic sense is usually clarified through [context](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context_%28language_use%29).

::: {#o13 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Every topological direct sum is an algebraic direct sum $X = M \oplus N$; the converse is not guaranteed. Even if both $M$ and $N$ are closed in $X$, $S^{-1}$ may *still* fail to be continuous. $N$ is a (topological) **complement** or **supplement** to $M$ if it avoids that pathology — that is, if, topologically, $X = M \oplus N$. (Then $M$ is likewise complementary to $N$.)[^n1] Condition 2(d) above implies that any topological complement of $M$ is isomorphic, as a topological vector space, to the [quotient vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_vector_space) $X / M$.

$M$ is called **complemented** if it has a topological complement $N$ (and **uncomplemented** if not). The choice of $N$ can matter quite strongly: every complemented vector subspace $M$ has algebraic complements that do not complement $M$ topologically.

Because a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) between two [normed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) (or [Banach](wikipedia://en/3989)) spaces is [bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_map) if and only if it is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_linear_map), the definition in the categories of normed (resp. [Banach](wikipedia://en/3989)) spaces is the same as in topological vector spaces.

### Equivalent characterizations {#Equivalent_characterizations}

The vector subspace $M$ is complemented in $X$ if and only if any of the following holds:[^n1]

- There exists a continuous linear map $P_M : X \to X$ with [image](wikipedia://en/579311) $P_M(X) = M$ such that $P \circ P = P$. That is, $P_M$ is a [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [linear](wikipedia://en/18102) [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) onto $M$. (In that case, algebraically $X=M\oplus\ker{P}$, and it is the continuity of $P_M$ that implies that this is a complement.)
- For every TVS $Y,$ the [restriction map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restriction_map) $R : L(X; Y) \to L(M; Y); R(u)=u|_M$ is surjective.[^n3]

If in addition $X$ is [Banach](wikipedia://en/3989), then an equivalent condition is

- $M$ is [closed](wikipedia://en/42309) in $X$, there exists another closed subspace $N\subseteq X$, and $S$ is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/3989#Linear_operators,_isomorphisms) from the [abstract direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_Banach_spaces) $M \oplus N$ to $X$.

:::

::: {#o19 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- If $Y$ is a measure space and $X\subseteq Y$ has positive measure, then $L^p(X)$ is complemented in $L^p(Y)$.
- $c_0$, the space of sequences converging to $0$, is complemented in $c$, the space of convergent sequences.
- By [Lebesgue decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_decomposition), $L^1([0,1])$ is complemented in $\mathrm{rca}([0,1])\cong C([0,1])^*$.

:::

## Sufficient conditions {#Sufficient_conditions}

For any two topological vector spaces $X$ and $Y$, the subspaces $X \times \{0\}$ and $\{0\} \times Y$ are topological complements in $X \times Y$.

Every algebraic complement of $\overline{\{0\}}$, the closure of $0$, is also a topological complement. This is because $\overline{\{0\}}$ has the [indiscrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_topology), and so the algebraic projection is continuous.[^n4]

If $X=M\oplus N$ and $A:X\to Y$ is surjective, then $Y=AM\oplus AN$.[^o6]

### Finite dimension {#Finite_dimension}

Suppose $X$ is Hausdorff and [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) and $Y$ a [free topological vector subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_vector_space): for some set $I$, we have $Y\cong\mathbb{K}^I$ (as a t.v.s.). Then $Y$ is a closed and complemented vector subspace of $X$.[^o23] In particular, any finite-dimensional subspace of $X$ is complemented.[^n5]

In arbitrary topological vector spaces, a finite-dimensional vector subspace $Y$ is topologically complemented if and only if for every non-zero $y\in Y$, there exists a continuous linear functional on $X$ that [separates](wikipedia://en/13972359) $y$ from $0$.[^n1] For an example in which this fails, see .

### Finite codimension {#Finite_codimension}

Not all finite-[codimensional](wikipedia://en/525149) vector subspaces of a TVS are closed, but those that are, do have complements.[^n5][^o28]

### Hilbert spaces {#Hilbert_spaces}

In a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), the [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) $M^{\bot}$ of any closed vector subspace $M$ is always a topological complement of $M$. This property characterizes Hilbert spaces within the class of [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989): every infinite dimensional, non-Hilbert Banach space contains a closed uncomplemented subspace, a deep theorem of [Joram Lindenstrauss](wikipedia://en/20653945) and [Lior Tzafriri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lior_Tzafriri).[^o30][^o7]

### Fréchet spaces {#Fréchet_spaces}

Let $X$ be a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) over the field $\mathbb{K}$. Then the following are equivalent:[^n6]

1. $X$ is not normable (that is, any continuous norm does not generate the topology)
1. $X$ contains a vector subspace TVS-isomorphic to $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}.$
1. $X$ contains a complemented vector subspace TVS-isomorphic to $\mathbb{K}^{\mathbb{N}}$.

## Properties; examples of uncomplemented subspaces {#Properties;_examples_of_uncomplemented_subspaces}

A complemented (vector) subspace of a Hausdorff space $X$ is necessarily a [closed subset](wikipedia://en/47279) of $X$, as is its complement.[^n1][^o50]

From the existence of [Hamel bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_bases), every infinite-dimensional Banach space contains unclosed linear subspaces.[^o35] Since any complemented subspace is closed, none of those subspaces is complemented.

Likewise, if $X$ is a [complete TVS](wikipedia://en/53695856) and $X / M$ is not complete, then $M$ has no topological complement in $X.$[^n7]

## Applications {#Applications}

If $A : X \to Y$ is a continuous linear [surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection), then the following conditions are equivalent:

1. The kernel of $A$ has a topological complement.
1. There exists a "right inverse": a continuous linear map $B : Y \to X$ such that $AB = \mathrm{Id}_Y$, where $\operatorname{Id}_Y : Y \to Y$ is the identity map.[^n3]

(Note: This claim is an erroneous exercise given by Trèves. Let $X$ and $Y$ both be $\mathbb{R}$ where $X$ is endowed with the usual topology, but $Y$ is endowed with the trivial topology. The identity map $X \to Y$ is then a continuous, linear bijection but its inverse is not continuous, since $X$ has a finer topology than $Y$. The kernel $\{0\}$ has $X$ as a topological complement, but we have just shown that no continuous right inverse can exist. If $A: X \to Y$ is also open (and thus a TVS homomorphism) then the claimed result holds.)

### The Method of Decomposition {#The_Method_of_Decomposition}

Topological vector spaces admit the following [Cantor-Schröder-Bernstein–type theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor-Schroder-Bernstein_theorem):

Let $X$ and $Y$ be TVSs such that $X = X \oplus X$ and $Y = Y \oplus Y.$ Suppose that $Y$ contains a complemented copy of $X$ and $X$ contains a complemented copy of $Y.$ Then $X$ is TVS-isomorphic to $Y.$

The "self-splitting" assumptions that $X = X \oplus X$ and $Y = Y \oplus Y$ cannot be removed: [Tim Gowers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Gowers) showed in 1996 that there exist non-isomorphic [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) $X$ and $Y$, each complemented in the other.[^n8]

:::

## In classical Banach spaces {#In_classical_Banach_spaces}

Understanding the complemented subspaces of an arbitrary Banach space $X$ [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) isomorphism is a classical problem that has motivated much work in basis theory, particularly the development of [absolutely summing operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_summing_operator). The problem remains open for a variety of important Banach spaces, most notably the space $L_1[0,1]$.[^o43]

For some Banach spaces the question is closed. Most famously, if $1 \leq p \leq \infty$ then the only complemented infinite-dimensional subspaces of $\ell_p$ are isomorphic to $\ell_p,$ and the same goes for $c_0.$ Such spaces are called prime (when their only infinite-dimensional complemented subspaces are isomorphic to the original). These are not the only prime spaces, however.[^o43]

The spaces $L_p[0,1]$ are not prime whenever $p \in (1, 2) \cup (2, \infty);$ in fact, they admit uncountably many non-isomorphic complemented subspaces.[^o43]

The spaces $L_2[0,1]$ and $L_{\infty}[0,1]$ are isomorphic to $\ell_2$ and $\ell_{\infty},$ respectively, so they are indeed prime.[^o43]

The space $L_1[0,1]$ is not prime, because it contains a complemented copy of $\ell_1$. No other complemented subspaces of $L_1[0,1]$ are currently known.[^o43]

::: {#o45 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Indecomposable Banach space"}

## Indecomposable Banach spaces {#Indecomposable_Banach_spaces}

An infinite-dimensional Banach space is called **indecomposable** whenever its only complemented subspaces are either finite-dimensional or -codimensional. Because a finite-[codimensional](wikipedia://en/525149) subspace of a Banach space $X$ is always isomorphic to $X,$ indecomposable Banach spaces are prime.

The most well-known example of indecomposable spaces are in fact hereditarily indecomposable, which means every infinite-dimensional subspace is also indecomposable.[^o46]

:::
