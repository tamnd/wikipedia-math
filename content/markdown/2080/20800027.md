# Slip line field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Slip line field"}

In [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science) and [soil mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_mechanics), a **slip line field** or **slip line field theory** is a technique often used to analyze the [stresses](wikipedia://en/228107) and [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force) involved in the major [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28engineering%29) of [metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal) or [soils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil). In essence, in some problems including plane [strain](wikipedia://en/19194778) and plane stress elastic-plastic problems, elastic part of the material prevent unrestrained plastic flow but in many metal-forming processes, such as [rolling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rolling_%28metalworking%29), [drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drawing_%28manufacturing%29), gorging, etc., large unrestricted plastic flows occur except for many small elastic zones. In effect we are concerned with a rigid-plastic material under condition of plane strain.[^o2] it turns out that the simplest way of solving stress equations is to express them in terms of a coordinate system that is along potential slip (or failure) surfaces. It is for this reason that this type of analysis is termed slip line analysis or the theory of slip line fields in the literature.[^o3][^o4]

:::

## History {#History}

The slip-line theory was co-developed by [Hilda Geiringer](wikipedia://en/7553047) in the early 1930s.[^o6] She developed the **Geiringer equations**, which simplify the process of calculating the deformation.[^o6]
