# Orbital plane

*Image: Orbit1.svg.* An orbital plane as viewed relative to a [plane of reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_of_reference).

*Image: Conic sections with plane.svg.* An orbital plane can also be seen in relative to [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673), in which the orbital path is defined as the intersection between a plane and a cone. [Parabolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_trajectory) (1) and [hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_trajectory) (3) orbits are [escape](wikipedia://en/37913) orbits, whereas [elliptical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptical_orbit) and circular orbits (2) are captive.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orbital plane"}

The **orbital plane** of a revolving body is the [geometric plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) in which its [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) lies. Three non-[collinear](wikipedia://en/946975) points in space suffice to determine an orbital plane. A common example would be the positions of the centers of a [massive body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primary_%28astronomy%29) (host) and of an [orbiting celestial body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_body) at two different times/points of its orbit.

:::

The orbital plane is defined in relation to a [reference plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_plane) by two [parameters](wikipedia://en/98663): [inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclination) (*i*) and [longitude of the ascending node](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_of_the_ascending_node) (Ω).

By definition, the reference plane for the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) is usually considered to be [Earth's orbital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit) plane, which defines the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264), the circular path on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) that the Sun appears to follow over the course of a year.

In other cases, for instance a [moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_satellite) or artificial [satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite) orbiting another planet, it is convenient to define the inclination of the object's orbit as the angle between its orbital plane and the planet's [equatorial plane](wikipedia://en/153681).

The coordinate system defined that uses the orbital plane as the $xy$ plane is known as the [perifocal coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perifocal_coordinate_system).

## Artificial satellites around the Earth {#Artificial_satellites_around_the_Earth}

For launch vehicles and artificial satellites, the orbital plane is a defining parameter of an orbit; as in general, it will take a very large amount of [propellant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propellant) to change the orbital plane of an object. Other parameters, such as the [orbital period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period), the [eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity) of the orbit and the phase of the orbit are more easily changed by propulsion systems.

Orbital planes of satellites are perturbed by the non-spherical nature of the [Earth's gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_gravity). This causes the orbital plane of the satellite's orbit to slowly rotate around the Earth, depending on the angle the plane makes with the Earth's equator. For planes that are at a critical angle this can mean that the plane will track the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) around the Earth, forming a [Sun-synchronous orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-synchronous_orbit).

A launch vehicle's [launch window](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Launch_window) is usually determined by the times when the target orbital plane intersects the launch site.
