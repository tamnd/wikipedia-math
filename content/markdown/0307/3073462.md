# Elliptic surface

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Elliptic surface"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **elliptic surface** is a surface that has an elliptic [fibration](wikipedia://en/316826), in other words a [proper morphism](wikipedia://en/603782) with connected [fibers](wikipedia://en/6603892) to an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) such that almost all fibers are [smooth](wikipedia://en/35033819) curves of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) 1. (Over an algebraically closed field such as the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), these fibers are [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225), perhaps without a chosen origin.) This is equivalent to the [generic fiber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generic_fiber) being a smooth curve of genus one. This follows from [proper base change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_base_change_theorem).

:::

The surface and the base curve are assumed to be non-singular ([complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742) or [regular schemes](wikipedia://en/43016199), depending on the context). The fibers that are not elliptic curves are called the **singular fibers** and were classified by [Kunihiko Kodaira](wikipedia://en/646326). Both elliptic and singular fibers are important in [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), especially in [F-theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-theory).

Elliptic surfaces form a large class of surfaces that contains many of the interesting examples of surfaces, and are relatively well understood in the theories of complex manifolds and [smooth](wikipedia://en/2119219) [4-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1362795). They are similar to (have analogies with, that is), elliptic curves over [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The product of any elliptic curve with any curve is an elliptic surface (with no singular fibers).
- All surfaces of [Kodaira dimension](wikipedia://en/1866873) 1 are elliptic surfaces.
- Every complex [Enriques surface](wikipedia://en/3094760) is elliptic, and has an elliptic fibration over the projective line.
- [Kodaira surfaces](wikipedia://en/21256202)
- [Dolgachev surfaces](wikipedia://en/22729603)
- [Shioda modular surfaces](wikipedia://en/33530042)

:::

## Kodaira's table of singular fibers {#Kodaira's_table_of_singular_fibers}

Most of the fibers of an elliptic fibration are (non-singular) elliptic curves. The remaining fibers are called singular fibers: there are a finite number of them, and each one consists of a union of rational curves, possibly with singularities or non-zero multiplicities (so the fibers may be non-reduced schemes). Kodaira and Néron independently classified the possible fibers, and [Tate's algorithm](wikipedia://en/14795246) can be used to find the type of the fibers of an elliptic curve over a number field.

The following table lists the possible fibers of a [minimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enriques-Kodaira_classification#Minimal_models_and_blowing_up) elliptic fibration. ("Minimal" means roughly one that cannot be factored through a "smaller" one; precisely, the singular fibers should contain no smooth rational curves with self-intersection number −1.) It gives:

- Kodaira's symbol for the fiber,
- [André Néron](wikipedia://en/9175241)'s symbol for the fiber,
- The number of irreducible components of the fiber (all rational except for type I<sub>0</sub>)
- The intersection matrix of the components. This is either a 1×1 [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888), or an [affine Cartan matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_Cartan_matrix), whose [Dynkin diagram](wikipedia://en/277109) is given.
- The multiplicities of each fiber are indicated in the Dynkin diagram.

::: {#o5 .table cue=table}

This table can be found as follows. Geometric arguments show that the intersection matrix of the components of the fiber must be negative semidefinite, connected, symmetric, and have no diagonal entries equal to −1 (by minimality). Such a matrix must be 0 or a multiple of the Cartan matrix of an affine Dynkin diagram of type [ADE](wikipedia://en/648042).

The intersection matrix determines the fiber type with three exceptions:

- If the intersection matrix is 0 the fiber can be either an elliptic curve (type I<sub>0</sub>), or have a double point (type I<sub>1</sub>), or a cusp (type II).
- If the intersection matrix is affine A<sub>1</sub>, there are 2 components with intersection multiplicity 2. They can meet either in 2 points with order 1 (type I<sub>2</sub>), or at one point with order 2 (type III).
- If the intersection matrix is affine A<sub>2</sub>, there are 3 components each meeting the other two. They can meet either in pairs at 3 distinct points (type I<sub>3</sub>), or all meet at the same point (type IV).

## Monodromy {#Monodromy}

The [monodromy](wikipedia://en/251250) around each singular fiber is a well-defined [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176) in the group SL(2,**Z**) of 2 × 2 integer matrices with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) 1. The monodromy describes the way the first [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) group of a smooth fiber (which is isomorphic to **Z**<sup>2</sup>) changes as we go around a singular fiber. Representatives for these conjugacy classes associated to singular fibers are given by:[^o7]

::: {#o8 .table cue=table}

| Fiber | Intersection matrix | Monodromy | *j*-invariant | Group structure on smooth locus |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| I<sub>ν</sub> | affine A<sub>ν-1</sub> | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & \nu \\ 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ | $\infty$ | $\mathbf{Z}/\nu \times \mathbf{C}^*$ |
| II | 0 | $\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | 0 | $\mathbf{C}$ |
| III | affine A<sub>1</sub> | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | 1728 | $\mathbf{Z}/2 \times \mathbf{C}$ |
| IV | affine A<sub>2</sub> | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ -1 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ | 0 | $\mathbf{Z}/3 \times \mathbf{C}$ |
| I<sub>0</sub><sup>\*</sup> | affine D<sub>4</sub> | $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & 0 \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ | in $\mathbf{C}$ | $(\mathbf{Z}/2)^2 \times \mathbf{C}$ |
| I<sub>ν</sub><sup>\*</sup> (ν≥1) | affine D<sub>4+ν</sub> | $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & -\nu \\ 0 & -1 \end{pmatrix}$ | $\infty$ | $(\mathbf{Z}/2)^2 \times \mathbf{C}$ if ν is even, $\mathbf{Z}/4\times\mathbf{C}$ if ν is odd |
| IV<sup>\*</sup> | affine E<sub>6</sub> | $\begin{pmatrix} -1 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | 0 | $\mathbf{Z}/3 \times \mathbf{C}$ |
| III<sup>\*</sup> | affine E<sub>7</sub> | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix}$ | 1728 | $\mathbf{Z}/2 \times \mathbf{C}$ |
| II<sup>\*</sup> | affine E<sub>8</sub> | $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & -1 \\ 1 & 1 \end{pmatrix}$ | 0 | $\mathbf{C}$ |

:::

For singular fibers of type II, III, IV, I<sub>0</sub><sup>\*</sup>, IV<sup>\*</sup>, III<sup>\*</sup>, or II<sup>\*</sup>, the monodromy has finite order in SL(2,**Z**). This reflects the fact that an elliptic fibration has [potential good reduction](wikipedia://en/35945175) at such a fiber. That is, after a ramified finite covering of the base curve, the singular fiber can be replaced by a smooth elliptic curve. Which smooth curve appears is described by the [j-invariant](wikipedia://en/451988) in the table. Over the complex numbers, the curve with *j*-invariant 0 is the unique elliptic curve with automorphism group of order 6, and the curve with *j*-invariant 1728 is the unique elliptic curve with automorphism group of order 4. (All other elliptic curves have automorphism group of order 2.)

For an elliptic fibration with a [section](wikipedia://en/367621), called a **Jacobian elliptic fibration**, the smooth locus of each fiber has a group structure. For singular fibers, this group structure on the smooth locus is described in the table, assuming for convenience that the base field is the complex numbers. (For a singular fiber with intersection matrix given by an affine Dynkin diagram $\tilde{\Gamma}$, the group of components of the smooth locus is isomorphic to the center of the simply connected simple Lie group with Dynkin diagram $\Gamma$, as listed [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_simple_Lie_groups#List).) Knowing the group structure of the singular fibers is useful for computing the [Mordell-Weil group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell-Weil_theorem) of an elliptic fibration (the group of sections), in particular its torsion subgroup.

::: {#o10 .statement cue=named-heading name="Canonical bundle formula"}

## Canonical bundle formula {#Canonical_bundle_formula}

To understand how elliptic surfaces fit into the [classification of surfaces](wikipedia://en/3029260), it is important to compute the [canonical bundle](wikipedia://en/734893) of a minimal elliptic surface *f*: *X* → *S*. Over the complex numbers, Kodaira proved the following **canonical bundle formula**:[^o11]

$$K_X=f^*(L)\otimes O_S\big(\sum_i (m_i-1)D_i\big).$$

Here the multiple fibers of *f* (if any) are written as $f^*(p_i)=m_iD_i$, for an integer *m*<sub>*i*</sub> at least 2 and a divisor *D*<sub>*i*</sub> whose coefficients have greatest common divisor equal to 1, and *L* is some line bundle on the smooth curve *S*. If *S* is projective (or equivalently, compact), then the [degree](wikipedia://en/1693055#Divisors_on_a_Riemann_surface) of *L* is determined by the [holomorphic Euler characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_Euler_characteristic) of *X* and *S*: deg(*L*) = χ(*X*,*O*<sub>*X*</sub>) − 2χ(*S*,*O*<sub>*S*</sub>). The canonical bundle formula implies that *K*<sub>*X*</sub> is **Q**-linearly equivalent to the pullback of some **Q**-divisor on *S*; it is essential here that the elliptic surface *X* → *S* is minimal.

Building on work of [Kenji Ueno](wikipedia://en/48043755), Takao Fujita (1986) gave a useful variant of the canonical bundle formula, showing how *K*<sub>*X*</sub> depends on the variation of the smooth fibers.[^o12] Namely, there is a **Q**-linear equivalence

$$K_X\sim_{\bf Q}f^*(K_S+B_S+M_S),$$

where the **discriminant divisor** *B*<sub>*S*</sub> is an explicit effective **Q**-divisor on *S* associated to the singular fibers of *f*, and the **moduli divisor** *M*<sub>*S*</sub> is $(1/12)j^*O(1)$, where *j*: *S* → **P**<sup>1</sup> is the function giving the [*j*-invariant](wikipedia://en/451988) of the smooth fibers. (Thus *M*<sub>*S*</sub> is a **Q**-linear equivalence class of **Q**-divisors, using the identification between the [divisor class group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor_class_group) Cl(*S*) and the [Picard group](wikipedia://en/1850134) Pic(*S*).) In particular, for *S* projective, the moduli divisor *M*<sub>*S*</sub> has nonnegative degree, and it has degree zero if and only if the elliptic surface is isotrivial, meaning that all the smooth fibers are isomorphic.

The discriminant divisor in Fujita's formula is defined by

$$B_S=\sum_{p\in S}(1-c(p))[p],$$

where *c*(*p*) is the **log canonical threshold** $\text{lct}(X,f^*(p))$. This is an explicit rational number between 0 and 1, depending on the type of singular fiber. Explicitly, the lct is 1 for a smooth fiber or type $I_{\nu}$, and it is 1/*m* for a multiple fiber ${}_mI_{\nu}$, 1/2 for $I_{\nu}^*$, 5/6 for II, 3/4 for III, 2/3 for IV, 1/3 for IV\*, 1/4 for III\*, and 1/6 for II\*.

The canonical bundle formula (in Fujita's form) has been generalized by [Yujiro Kawamata](wikipedia://en/47502077) and others to families of [Calabi–Yau varieties](wikipedia://en/188932) of any dimension.[^o13]

:::

## Logarithmic transformations {#Logarithmic_transformations}

Logarithmic transformation

A **logarithmic transformation** (of order *m* with center *p*) of an elliptic surface or fibration turns a fiber of multiplicity 1 over a point *p* of the base space into a fiber of multiplicity *m*. It can be reversed, so fibers of high multiplicity can all be turned into fibers of multiplicity 1, and this can be used to eliminate all multiple fibers.

Logarithmic transformations can be quite violent: they can change the Kodaira dimension, and can turn algebraic surfaces into non-algebraic surfaces.

::: {#o15 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example:** Let *L* be the lattice **Z**+i**Z** of **C**, and let *E* be the elliptic curve **C**/*L*. Then the projection map from *E*×**C** to **C** is an elliptic fibration. We will show how to replace the fiber over 0 with a fiber of multiplicity 2.

There is an automorphism of *E*×**C** of order 2 that maps (*c*,*s*) to (*c*+1/2, *−s*). We let *X* be the quotient of *E*×**C** by this group action. We make *X* into a fiber space over **C** by mapping (*c*,*s*) to *s*<sup>2</sup>. We construct an isomorphism from *X* minus the fiber over 0 to *E*×**C** minus the fiber over 0 by mapping (*c*,*s*) to (*c*-log(*s*)/2πi,*s*<sup>2</sup>). (The two fibers over 0 are non-isomorphic elliptic curves, so the fibration *X* is certainly not isomorphic to the fibration *E*×**C** over all of **C**.)

Then the fibration *X* has a fiber of multiplicity 2 over 0, and otherwise looks like *E*×**C**. We say that *X* is obtained by applying a logarithmic transformation of order 2 to *E*×**C** with center 0.

:::
