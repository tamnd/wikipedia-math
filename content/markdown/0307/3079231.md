# Carothers equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carothers equation"}

In [step-growth polymerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Step-growth_polymerization), the **Carothers equation** (or **Carothers' equation**) gives the [degree of polymerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_polymerization), $X_{n}$, for a given fractional [monomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomer) conversion, $p$.

:::

There are several versions of this equation, proposed by [Wallace Carothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_Carothers), who invented [nylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nylon) in 1935.

## Linear polymers: two monomers in equimolar quantities {#Linear_polymers:_two_monomers_in_equimolar_quantities}

The simplest case refers to the formation of a strictly linear polymer by the reaction (usually by condensation) of two [monomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomer) in equimolar quantities. An example is the synthesis of [nylon-6,6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nylon-6,6) whose formula is \[\\sNH\\s(CH2)6\\sNH\\sCO\\s(CH2)4\\sCO\\s\]\_{*n*} from one mole of [hexamethylenediamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexamethylenediamine), H2N(CH2)6NH2, and one mole of [adipic acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adipic_acid), HOOC\\s(CH2)4\\sCOOH. For this case[^o3][^o4]

$$\bar{X}_n=\frac{1}{1-p}$$

In this equation

- $\bar{X}_n$ is the [number-average](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_mass_distribution#Number_average_molecular_weight) value of the [degree of polymerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_of_polymerization), equal to the average number of monomer units in a polymer [molecule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule). For the example of nylon-6,6 $\bar{X}_n = 2n$ ($n$ diamine units and $n$ diacid units).
- $p=\tfrac{N_0-N}{N_0}$ is the extent of reaction (or conversion to polymer), defined by
  - $N_0$ is the number of molecules present initially as monomer
  - $N$ is the number of molecules present after time $t$. The total includes all degrees of polymerization: monomers, [oligomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oligomer) and polymers.

This equation shows that a high monomer [conversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversion_%28chemistry%29) is required to achieve a high degree of polymerization. For example, a monomer conversion, $p$, of 98% is required for $\bar{X}_n$ = 50, and $p$ = 99% is required for $\bar{X}_n$ = 100.

## Linear polymers: one monomer in excess {#Linear_polymers:_one_monomer_in_excess}

If one monomer is present in [stoichiometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometry) excess, then the equation becomes[^o6]

$$\bar{X}_n=\frac{1+r}{1+r-2rp}$$

- *r* is the stoichiometric ratio of reactants, the excess reactant is conventionally the denominator so that r \< 1. If neither monomer is in excess, then r = 1 and the equation reduces to the equimolar case above.

The effect of the excess reactant is to reduce the degree of polymerization for a given value of p. In the limit of complete conversion of the [limiting reagent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiting_reagent) monomer, p → 1 and

$$\bar{X}_n\to\frac{1+r}{1-r}$$

Thus for a 1% excess of one monomer, r = 0.99 and the limiting degree of polymerization is 199, compared to infinity for the equimolar case. An excess of one reactant can be used to control the degree of polymerization.

## Branched polymers: multifunctional monomers {#Branched_polymers:_multifunctional_monomers}

The **functionality** of a monomer molecule is the number of functional groups which participate in the polymerization. Monomers with functionality greater than two will introduce [branching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branching_%28polymer_chemistry%29) into a polymer, and the degree of polymerization will depend on the average functionality f<sub>av</sub> per monomer unit. For a system containing N<sub>0</sub> molecules initially and equivalent numbers of two functional groups A and B, the total number of functional groups is N<sub>0</sub>f<sub>av</sub>.

$$f_{av} = \frac{\sum N_i \cdot f_i}{\sum N_i}$$

And the **modified Carothers equation** is[^o8][^o9][^o10]

$x_{n} = \frac{2}{2-pf_{av}}$, where p equals to $\frac{2(N_0-N)}{N_0 \cdot f_{av}}$

## Related equations {#Related_equations}

Related to the Carothers equation are the following equations (for the simplest case of linear polymers formed from two monomers in equimolar quantities):

$$\begin{matrix} \bar{X}_w & = & \frac{1+p}{1-p} \\ \bar{M}_n & = & M_o\frac{1}{1-p} \\ \bar{M}_w & = & M_o\frac{1+p}{1-p}\\ PDI & = & \frac{\bar{M}_w}{\bar{M}_n}=1+p\\ \end{matrix}$$

where:

- *X*<sub>w</sub> is the *weight average degree of polymerization*,
- *M*<sub>n</sub> is the [number average molecular weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_average_molecular_weight),
- *M*<sub>w</sub> is the [weight average molecular weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight_average_molecular_weight),
- *M*<sub>o</sub> is the molecular weight of the repeating [monomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomer) unit,
- *Đ* is the [dispersity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersity) index. (formerly known as polydispersity index, symbol PDI)

The last equation shows that the maximum value of the *Đ* is 2, which occurs at a monomer conversion of 100% (or p = 1). This is true for step-growth polymerization of linear polymers. For [chain-growth polymerization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain-growth_polymerization) or for [branched](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Branching_%28polymer_chemistry%29) polymers, the Đ can be much higher.

In practice the average length of the polymer chain is limited by such things as the purity of the reactants, the presence of any [side reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Side_reaction) (which lower the yield), and the [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) of the medium.
