# Newman's conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Newman's conjecture"}

::: {#o2 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Newman's conjecture"}

**Newman's conjecture.**

:::

::: {#o3 .problem cue=unsolved}

Given arbitrary $m$, $r$, are there infinitely many values of $n$ such that the partition function at $n$ is congruent to $r$ mod $m$?

:::

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Newman's conjecture** is a [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) about the behavior of the [partition function](wikipedia://en/312250) [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) any integer. Specifically, it states that for any integers $m$ and $r$ such that $0 \le r \le m-1$, the value of the partition function $p(n)$ satisfies the congruence $p(n) \equiv r \pmod{m}$ for infinitely many non-negative integers $n$. It was formulated by mathematician Morris Newman in 1960.[^o4] It is unsolved as of .

:::

## History {#History}

Oddmund Kolberg was probably the first to [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) a related result, namely that the partition function takes both [even](wikipedia://en/143135) and [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) values infinitely often. The proof employed was of elementary nature and easily accessible, and was proposed as an exercise by Newman in the [American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly).[^o6][^o7][^o8]

1 year later, in 1960, Newman proposed the conjecture and proved the cases *m*=5 and 13 in his original paper,[^o4] and *m*=65 two years later.[^o9]

[Ken Ono](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ken_Ono), an American mathematician, made further advances by exhibiting sufficient conditions for the conjecture to hold for [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) $m$. He first showed that Newman's conjecture holds for prime $m$ if for each $r$ between 0 and $m-1$, there exists a nonnegative integer $n$ such that the following holds:

- $$24 \mid mn+1$$
- $$p \left(\frac{mn+1}{24} \right) \equiv r \pmod{m}$$

He used the result, together with a computer program, to prove the conjecture for all primes less than 1000, except 3.[^o10] Ahlgren expanded on his result to show that Ono's condition is, in fact, true for all [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) numbers [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556) to 6.[^o11]

Three years later, Ono showed that for every prime $m$ greater than 3, one of the following must hold:

- Newman's conjecture holds for $m$, or
- $m\mid p(mn+k)$ for all nonnegative integers $n$, and $1\le k < 24, 24k\equiv 1 \pmod{m}$.

Using a computer, he proved the theorem for all primes less than 200,000, except 3.[^o12]

Afterwards, Ahlgren and Boylan used Ono's criterion to extend Newman's conjecture to all primes except possibly 3.[^o13] Two years afterwards, they extended their result to all [prime powers](wikipedia://en/1630483) except powers of 2 or 3.[^o14]

## Partial progress and solved cases {#Partial_progress_and_solved_cases}

The weaker statement that $p(n)\equiv 0 \pmod{m}$ has at least 1 solution has been proved for all primes $m$. It was formerly known as the [Erdős–Ivić conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erdős–Ivić_conjecture), named after mathematicians [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) and [Aleksandar Ivić](wikipedia://en/76433068). It was settled by [A. Schinzel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._Schinzel).[^o10]
