# Scalar (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Scalar"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a **scalar** is an element of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) which is used to define a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) through the operation of *[scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373)*: a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28linear_algebra%29) (denoted $\mathbf{v}$) multiplied by a scalar (denoted $a$) produces another vector ($a\mathbf{v}$).[^o2][^o3][^o4] [Real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) and [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) may be used as scalars in real and [complex vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space), respectively. A [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) operation – not to be confused with scalar multiplication – may be defined on a vector space, allowing two vectors to be multiplied in the defined way to produce a scalar. A vector space equipped with a scalar product is called an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856).

:::

A scalar may also have other roles in terms of vector components, in normed vector spaces, in modules, and in transformations. A quantity described by multiple scalars, such as having both direction and magnitude, is called a *[vector](wikipedia://en/23218956)*.[^o5] The term scalar is also sometimes used informally to mean a vector, [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859), [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965), or other, usually, "compound" value that is actually reduced to a single component. Thus, for example, the product of a 1 × *n* matrix and an *n* × 1 matrix, which is formally a 1 × 1 matrix, is often said to be a **scalar**. The real component of a [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440) is also called its **scalar part**.

The term [scalar matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_matrix) is used to denote a matrix of the form *kI* where *k* is a scalar and *I* is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The word *scalar* derives from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language) word *scalaris*, an adjectival form of *scala* (Latin for "ladder"). The first recorded usage of the word "scalar" in mathematics occurs in [François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238)'s *Analytic Art* (*In artem analyticem isagoge*) (1591):[^o7][^o8]

Magnitudes that ascend or descend proportionally in keeping with their nature from one kind to another may be called scalar terms.

(Latin: *Magnitudines quae ex genere ad genus sua vi proportionaliter adscendunt vel descendunt, vocentur Scalares.*)

According to a citation in the *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)* the first recorded usage of the term "scalar" in English came with [W. R. Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in 1846, referring to the real part of a quaternion:

*The algebraically real part may receive, according to the question in which it occurs, all values contained on the one scale of progression of numbers from negative to positive infinity; we shall call it therefore the scalar part.*

## Definitions and properties {#Definitions_and_properties}

*Image: Vector components.svg.* Scalars are [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) used in linear algebra, as opposed to [vectors](wikipedia://en/23218956). This image shows a [Euclidean vector](wikipedia://en/32533). Its coordinates *x* and *y* are scalars, as is its length, but **v** is not a scalar.

### Scalars of vector spaces {#Scalars_of_vector_spaces}

A [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) is defined as a set of vectors (additive [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974)), a set of scalars ([field](wikipedia://en/10603)), and a scalar multiplication operation that takes a scalar *k* and a vector **v** to form another vector *k***v**. For example, in a [coordinate space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_space), the scalar multiplication $k(v_1, v_2, \dots, v_n)$ yields $(k v_1, k v_2, \dots, k v_n)$. In a (linear) [function space](wikipedia://en/340630), $kf$ is the function $x k(f(x))$.

The scalars can be taken from any field, including the [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024), [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158), real, and complex numbers, as well as [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615).

### Scalars as vector components {#Scalars_as_vector_components}

According to a fundamental theorem of linear algebra, every vector space has a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420). It follows that every vector space over a field *K* is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the corresponding [coordinate vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_vector_space) where each [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) consists of elements of *K* (E.g., coordinates (*a<sub>1</sub>*, *a<sub>2</sub>*, ..., *a<sub>n</sub>*) where *a<sub>i</sub>* ∈ *K* and *n* is the dimension of the vector space in consideration.). For example, every real vector space of [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) *n* is isomorphic to the *n*-dimensional real space **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.

### Scalars in normed vector spaces {#Scalars_in_normed_vector_spaces}

Alternatively, a vector space *V* can be equipped with a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) function that assigns to every vector **v** in *V* a scalar ||**v**||. By definition, multiplying **v** by a scalar *k* also multiplies its norm by |*k*|. If ||**v**|| is interpreted as the *length* of **v**, this operation can be described as **scaling** the length of **v** by *k*. A vector space equipped with a norm is called a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) (or *normed linear space*).

The norm is usually defined to be an element of *V*'s scalar field *K*, which restricts the latter to fields that support the notion of sign. Moreover, if *V* has dimension 2 or more, *K* must be closed under square root, as well as the four arithmetic operations; thus the rational numbers **Q** are excluded, but the [surd field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_surd) is acceptable. For this reason, not every scalar product space is a normed vector space.

### Scalars in modules {#Scalars_in_modules}

When the requirement that the set of scalars form a field is relaxed so that it need only form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) (so that, for example, the division of scalars need not be defined, or the scalars need not be [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative)), the resulting more general algebraic structure is called a [module](wikipedia://en/276410).

In this case the "scalars" may be complicated objects. For instance, if *R* is a ring, the vectors of the product space *R*<sup>*n*</sup> can be made into a module with the *n* × *n* matrices with entries from *R* as the scalars. Another example comes from [manifold theory](wikipedia://en/2073470), where the space of [sections](wikipedia://en/367621) of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) forms a module over the [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) of real functions on the manifold.

### Scaling transformation {#Scaling_transformation}

The scalar multiplication of vector spaces and modules is a special case of [scaling](wikipedia://en/651752), a kind of [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation).
