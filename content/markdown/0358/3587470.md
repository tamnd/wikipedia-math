# Effective stress

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Effective stress"}

*Image: Morocco Africa Flickr Rosino December 2005 84514010.jpg.* [Erg Chebbi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erg_Chebbi), Morocco

**Effective stress** is a fundamental concept in [soil mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_mechanics) and [geotechnical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geotechnical_engineering) that describes the portion of total [stress](wikipedia://en/228107) in a [soil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil) mass that is carried by the solid soil skeleton, rather than the [pore water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pore_water_pressure). This concept applies broadly to granular media like sand, silt, and clay, as well as to porous materials such as rock, concrete, metal powders and biological tissues. It is crucial for understanding the mechanical behaviour of porous media, as **effective stress** governs both their strength and volume change (deformation).[^o2]

:::

More formally, effective stress is defined as the stress that, for any given pore pressure $p$, produces the same strain or strength response in a porous material (such as soil or rock) as would be observed in a dry sample where $p = 0$. In other words, it is the stress that controls the mechanical behaviour of a porous body regardless of pore pressure present.

## History {#History}

[Karl von Terzaghi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_von_Terzaghi) first proposed the relationship for **effective stress** in 1925.[^o4][^o5][^o6] For him, the term "effective" meant the calculated [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28physics%29) that was effective in moving soil, or causing displacements. It has been often interpreted as the average stress carried by the [soil skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_skeleton).[^o7] Afterwards, different formulations have been proposed for the effective stress. [Maurice Biot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_Biot) fully developed the three-dimensional [soil consolidation theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_consolidation), extending the [one-dimensional model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_consolidation) previously developed by Terzaghi to more general hypotheses and introducing the set of basic equations of [Poroelasticity](wikipedia://en/3143054). [Alec Skempton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alec_Skempton) in his work in 1960,[^o7] has carried out an extensive review of available formulations and experimental data in literature about effective stress valid in soil, concrete and rock, in order to reject some of these expressions, as well as clarify what expression was appropriate according to several work hypotheses, such as stress–strain or strength behaviour, saturated or nonsaturated media, rock/concrete or soil behaviour, etc.

In 1962, work by [Jeremiah Jennings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremiah_Edmund_Bowden_Jennings) and [John Burland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Burland) examined the applicability of Terzaghi’s effective stress principle to [partly saturated soils](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soil_moisture).[^o8] Through a series of experiments undertaken at the [University of the Witwatersrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_the_Witwatersrand), including [oedometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oedometer_test) and compression tests on various soil types, they showed that behaviours such as volume changes and [shear strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_strength_%28soil%29) in partly saturated soils do not align with predictions based on effective stress changes alone. Their findings showed that the structural changes due to pressure deficiencies behave differently from changes due to applied stress.[^o9][^o10][^o11][^o8]

## Description {#Description}

The **effective stress** ($\sigma'$) acting on a soil is calculated from the total normal stress ($\sigma$) and [pore water pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pore_water_pressure) ($u$) according to:

$$\sigma' = \sigma - u\,$$

This equation is fundamental in understanding the strength of soils under drained conditions, which applies to coarse-grained soils (sand, silt) and fine-grained soils (clay) over the long-term. This is because soil strength is due primarily to interparticle friction,[^o13] which - similar to the concept of a block sliding on a table - is proportional to the normal stress. The pore water pressure reduces the normal stress and thus reduces the soil strength.

Much like the concept of stress itself, the formula is a construct, for the easier visualization of forces acting on a soil mass, especially simple analysis models for [slope stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope_stability), involving a slip plane.[^o14] With these models, it is important to know the total weight of the soil above (including water), and the pore water pressure within the slip plane, assuming it is acting as a confined layer.

However, the formula becomes confusing when considering the true behaviour of the soil particles under different measurable conditions, since none of the parameters are actually independent actors on the particles.

*Image: Effstress1.jpg.* Arrangement of Spheres showing contacts

Consider a grouping of round [quartz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartz) sand grains, piled loosely, in a classic "cannonball" arrangement. As can be seen, there is a contact stress where the spheres actually touch. Pile on more spheres and the contact stresses increase, to the point of causing frictional instability (dynamic [friction](wikipedia://en/11062)), and perhaps failure. The independent parameter affecting the contacts (both normal and shear) is the force of the spheres above. This can be calculated by using the overall average [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) of the spheres and the height of spheres above.

*Image: Effstress2.jpg.* Spheres immersed in water, reducing effective stress

If we then have these spheres in a [beaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beaker_%28glassware%29) and add some water, they will begin to float a little depending on their density ([buoyancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy)). With natural soil materials, the effect can be significant, as anyone who has lifted a large rock out of a lake can attest. The contact stress on the spheres decreases as the beaker is filled to the top of the spheres, but then nothing changes if more water is added. Although the water pressure between the spheres (pore water pressure) is increasing, the effective stress remains the same, because the concept of "total stress" includes the weight of all the water above. This is where the equation can become confusing, and the effective stress can be calculated using the buoyant density of the spheres (soil), and the height of the soil above.

*Image: Effstress3.jpg.* Spheres being injected with water, reducing effective stress

The concept of effective stress truly becomes interesting when dealing with non-[hydrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic) pore water pressure. Under the conditions of a pore pressure gradient, the ground water flows, according to the permeability equation ([Darcy's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darcy's_law)). Using our spheres as a model, this is the same as injecting (or withdrawing) water between the spheres. If water is being injected, the seepage force acts to separate the spheres and reduces the effective stress. Thus, the soil mass becomes weaker. If water is being withdrawn, the spheres are forced together and the effective stress increases.[^o15]

Two extremes of this effect are [quicksand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quicksand), where the groundwater gradient and seepage force act against [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity); and the "sandcastle effect",[^o16] where the water drainage and capillary action act to strengthen the sand. As well, effective stress plays an important role in [slope stability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slope_stability), and other [geotechnical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geotechnical_engineering) and [engineering geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering_geology) problems, such as [groundwater-related subsidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Groundwater-related_subsidence).
