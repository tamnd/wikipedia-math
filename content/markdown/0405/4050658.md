# Chakravala method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chakravala method"}

The ***chakravala* method** (चक्रवाल विधि) is a cyclic [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) to solve [indeterminate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_equation) [quadratic equations](wikipedia://en/25175), including [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877). It is commonly attributed to [Bhāskara II](wikipedia://en/480509), (c. 1114 – 1185 CE)[^o2][^o3] although some attribute it to [Jayadeva](wikipedia://en/1158578) (c. 950 ~ 1000 CE).[^o4] Jayadeva pointed out that [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206)'s approach to solving equations of this type could be generalized, and he then described this general method, which was later refined by Bhāskara II in his *[Bijaganita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijaganita)* treatise. He called it the Chakravala method: *chakra* meaning "wheel" in [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit), a reference to the cyclic nature of the algorithm.[^o5] C.-O. Selenius held that no European performances at the time of Bhāskara, nor much later, exceeded its marvellous height of mathematical complexity.[^o2][^o5]

:::

This method is also known as the **cyclic method** and contains traces of [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881).[^o6]

## History {#History}

*Chakra* in Sanskrit means cycle. As per popular legend, Chakravala indicates a mythical range of mountains which orbits around the Earth like a wall and not limited by light and darkness.[^o8]

[Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) in 628 CE studied indeterminate quadratic equations, including [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877)

$$\,x^2 = Ny^2 + 1,$$

for minimum integers *x* and *y*. Brahmagupta could solve it for several *N*, but not all.

Jayadeva and Bhaskara offered the first complete solution to the equation, using the *chakravala* method to find for $\,x^2 = 61y^2 + 1,$ the solution

$$\,x = 1 766 319 049, y = 226 153 980.$$

This case was notorious for its difficulty, and was first solved in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe) by [Brouncker](wikipedia://en/1426858) in 1657–58 in response to a challenge by [Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966), using continued fractions. A method for the general problem was first completely described rigorously by [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange) in 1766. Lagrange's method, however, requires the calculation of 10 iterations (not the 21 often claimed, since 10 iterations gives the solution $\,29718^2 = 61*3805^2 - 1,$ which can be squared to eliminate the remaining 11 iterations) for successive convergents of the [simple continued fraction](wikipedia://en/46802) for the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of 61, while the *chakravala* method is much simpler. Selenius, in his assessment of the *chakravala* method, states

"The method represents a best approximation algorithm of minimal length that, owing to several minimization properties, with minimal effort and avoiding large numbers automatically produces the best solutions to the equation. The *chakravala* method anticipated the European methods by more than a thousand years. But no European performances in the whole field of [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) at a time much later than Bhaskara's, nay nearly equal up to our times, equalled the marvellous complexity and ingenuity of *chakravala*."[^o2][^o5]

[Hermann Hankel](wikipedia://en/932515) calls the *chakravala* method

"the finest thing achieved in the theory of numbers before Lagrange."[^o10]

::: {#o12 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="The method"}

## The method {#The_method}

From [Brahmagupta's identity](wikipedia://en/5161105), we observe that for given *N*,

$$(x_1x_2 + Ny_1y_2)^2 - N(x_1y_2 + x_2y_1)^2 = (x_1^2 - Ny_1^2)(x_2^2 - Ny_2^2)$$

For the equation $x^2 - Ny^2 = k$, this allows the "composition" (*samāsa*) of two solution triples $(x_1, y_1, k_1)$ and $(x_2, y_2, k_2)$ into a new triple

$$(x_1x_2 + Ny_1y_2 \,,\, x_1y_2 + x_2y_1 \,,\, k_1k_2).$$

In the general method, the main idea is that any triple $(a,b,k)$ (that is, one which satisfies $a^2 - Nb^2 = k$) can be composed with the trivial triple $(m, 1, m^2 - N)$ to get the new triple $(am + Nb, a+bm, k(m^2-N))$ for any *m*. Assuming we started with a triple for which $\gcd(a,b)=1$, this can be scaled down by *k* (this is [Bhaskara's lemma](wikipedia://en/13486743)):

$$a^2 - Nb^2 = k \Rightarrow \left(\frac{am+Nb}{k}\right)^2 - N\left(\frac{a+bm}{k}\right)^2 = \frac{m^2-N}{k}$$

Since the signs inside the squares do not matter, the following substitutions are possible:

$$a\leftarrow\frac{am+Nb}{|k|}, b\leftarrow\frac{a+bm}{|k|}, k\leftarrow\frac{m^2-N}{k}$$

When a positive integer *m* is chosen so that (*a* + *bm*)/*k* is an integer, so are the other two numbers in the triple. Among such *m*, the method chooses one that minimizes the absolute value of *m*<sup>2</sup> − *N* and hence that of (*m*<sup>2</sup> − *N*)/*k*. Then the substitution relations are applied for *m* equal to the chosen value. This results in a new triple (*a*, *b*, *k*). The process is repeated until a triple with $k=1$ is found. This method always terminates with a solution, as proved by Lagrange in 1768.[^o13] Optionally, we can stop when *k* is ±1, ±2, or ±4, as Brahmagupta's approach gives a solution for those cases.

:::

## Brahmagupta's composition method {#Brahmagupta's_composition_method}

In AD 628, Brahmagupta discovered a general way to find $x$ and $y$ of $x^2 = Ny^2 + 1,$ when given $a^2 = Nb^2 + k$, when k is ±1, ±2, or ±4.[^o15]

### *k* = ±1 {#k_=_±1}

Using [Brahmagupta's identity](wikipedia://en/5161105) to compose the triple $(a,b,k)$ with itself:

$(a^2+Nb^2)^2-N(2ab)^2=k^2$ $\Rightarrow$ $(2a^2-k)^2-N(2ab)^2=k^2$

The new triple can be expressed as $(2a^2-k,2ab,k^2)$.

Substituting $k=-1$ gives a solution:

$$x=2a^2+1, y=2ab$$

For $k=1$, the original $(a,b)$ was already a solution. Substituting $k=1$ yields a second:

$$x=2a^2-1, y=2ab$$

### *k* = ±2 {#k_=_±2}

Again using the equation, $(2a^2-k)^2-N(2ab)^2=k^2$$\Rightarrow$$\left( \frac{2a^2-k}{k} \right)^2-N \left (\frac{2ab}{k} \right)^2=1$

Substituting $k=2$,

$$x=a^2-1, y=ab$$

Substituting $k=-2$,

$$x=a^2+1, y=ab$$

### *k* = 4 {#k_=_4}

Substituting $k=4$ into the equation $\left(\frac{2a^2-k}{k}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{2ab}{k}\right)^2=1$ creates the triple $\left(\frac{a^2-2}{2},\frac{ab}{2},1\right),$

which is a solution if $a$ is even:

$$x=\frac{a^2-2}{2}, y=\frac{ab}{2}.$$

If *a* is odd, start with the equations $\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2=1$ and $\left(\frac{2a^2-4}{4}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{2ab}{4} \right)^2=1$.

Leading to the triples $\left(\frac{a}{2},\frac{b}{2},1\right)$ and $\left(\frac{a^2-2}{2},\frac{ab}{2},1\right)$. Composing the triples gives $\left(\frac{a}{2}(a^2-3)\right)^2-N\left(\frac{b}{2}(a^2-1)\right)^2=1$

When $a$ is odd,

$$x=\frac{a}{2}(a^2-3), y=\frac{b}{2}(a^2-1)$$

### *k* = −4 {#k_=_−4}

When $k=-4$, then $\left(\frac{a}{2}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{b}{2}\right)^2=-1$. Composing with itself yields $\left(\frac{a^2+Nb^2}{4}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{ab}{2}\right)^2=1$$\Rightarrow$$\left(\frac{a^2+2}{2}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{ab}{2}\right)^2=1$.

Again composing itself yields $\left(\frac{(a^2+2)^2+Na^2b^2}{4}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{ab(a^2+2)}{2}\right)^2=1$$\Rightarrow$ $\left(\frac{a^4+4a^2+2}{2}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{ab(a^2+2)}{2}\right)^2=1$

Finally, from the earlier equations, compose the triples $\left(\frac{a^2+2}{2},\frac{ab}{2},1\right)$ and $\left(\frac{a^4+4a^2+2}{2},\frac{ab(a^2+2)}{2},1\right)$, to get

$\left(\frac{(a^2+2)(a^4+4a^2+2)+Na^2b^2 (a^2+2)}{4}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{ab(a^4+4a^2+3)}{2}\right)^2=1$$\Rightarrow$

$\left(\frac{(a^2+2)(a^4+4a^2+1)}{2}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{ab(a^2+3)(a^2+1)}{2}\right)^2=1$$\Rightarrow$$\left(\frac{(a^2+2)[(a^2+1)(a^2+3)-2)]}{2}\right)^2-N\left(\frac{ab(a^2+3)(a^2+1)}{2}\right)^2=1$.

This gives us the solutions

$x=\frac{(a^2+2)[(a^2+1)(a^2+3)-2]}{2}, \qquad y=\frac{ab(a^2+3)(a^2+1)}{2}$[^o20]

(Note, $k=-4$ is useful to find a solution to [Pell's Equation](wikipedia://en/24877), but it is not always the smallest integer pair. e.g. $36^2-52\cdot5^2=-4$. The equation will give you $x=1093435849, y=151632270$, which when put into Pell's Equation yields $1195601955878350801-1195601955878350800 = 1$, which works, but so does $x = 649,y=90$ for $N=52$.

::: {#o22 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### *n* = 61 {#n_=_61}

The *n* = 61 case (determining an integer solution satisfying $a^2 - 61b^2 = 1$), issued as a challenge by Fermat many centuries later, was given by Bhaskara as an example.[^o13]

We start with a solution $a^2 - 61b^2 = k$ for any *k* found by any means. In this case we can let *b* be 1, thus, since $8^2 - 61\cdot1^2 = 3$, we have the triple $(a,b,k) = (8, 1, 3)$. Composing it with $(m, 1, m^2-61)$ gives the triple $(8m+61, 8+m, 3(m^2-61))$, which is scaled down (or [Bhaskara's lemma](wikipedia://en/13486743) is directly used) to get:

$$\left( \frac{8m+61}{3}, \frac{8+m}{3}, \frac{m^2-61}{3} \right).$$

For 3 to divide $8+m$ and $|m^2-61|$ to be minimal, we choose $m=7$, so that we have the triple $(39, 5, -4)$. Now that *k* is −4, we can use Brahmagupta's idea: it can be scaled down to the rational solution $(39/2, 5/2, -1)\,$, which composed with itself three times, with $m={7,11,9}$ respectively, when k becomes square and scaling can be applied, this gives $(1523/2, 195/2, 1)\,$. Finally, such procedure can be repeated until the solution is found (requiring 9 additional self-compositions and 4 additional square-scalings): $(1766319049,\, 226153980,\, 1)$. This is the minimal integer solution.

### *n* = 67 {#n_=_67}

Suppose we are to solve $x^2 - 67y^2 = 1$ for *x* and *y*.[^o25]

We start with a solution $a^2 - 67b^2 = k$ for any *k* found by any means; in this case we can let *b* be 1, thus producing $8^2 - 67\cdot1^2 = -3$. At each step, we find an *m* > 0 such that *k* divides *a* + *bm*, and |*m*<sup>2</sup> − 67| is minimal. We then update *a*, *b*, and *k* to $\frac{am+Nb}{|k|}, \frac{a+bm}{|k|}$ and $\frac{m^2-N}{k}$ respectively.

**First iteration**

We have $(a,b,k) = (8,1,-3)$. We want a positive integer *m* such that *k* divides *a* + *bm*, i.e. 3 divides 8 + m, and |*m*<sup>2</sup> − 67| is minimal. The first condition implies that *m* is of the form 3*t* + 1 (i.e. 1, 4, 7, 10,… etc.), and among such *m*, the minimal value is attained for *m* = 7. Replacing (*a*, *b*, *k*) with $\left(\frac{am+Nb}{|k|}, \frac{a+bm}{|k|}, \frac{m^2-N}{k}\right)$, we get the new values $a = (8\cdot7+67\cdot1)/3 = 41, b = (8 + 1\cdot7)/3 = 5, k = (7^2-67)/(-3) = 6$. That is, we have the new solution:

$$41^2 - 67\cdot(5)^2 = 6.$$

At this point, one round of the cyclic algorithm is complete.

**Second iteration**

We now repeat the process. We have $(a,b,k) = (41,5,6)$. We want an *m* > 0 such that *k* divides *a* + *bm*, i.e. 6 divides 41 + 5*m*, and |*m*<sup>2</sup> − 67| is minimal. The first condition implies that *m* is of the form 6*t* + 5 (i.e. 5, 11, 17,… etc.), and among such *m*, |*m*<sup>2</sup> − 67| is minimal for *m* = 5. This leads to the new solution $\frac{41\cdot5 + 61\cdot5}{6}$

$$90^2 - 67 \cdot 11^2 = -7.$$

**Third iteration**

For 7 to divide 90 + 11*m*, we must have *m* = 2 + 7*t* (i.e. 2, 9, 16,… etc.) and among such *m*, we pick *m* = 9.

$$221^2 - 67\cdot 27^2 = -2.$$

**Final solution**

At this point, we could continue with the cyclic method (and it would end, after seven iterations), but since the right-hand side is among ±1, ±2, ±4, we can also use Brahmagupta's observation directly. Composing the triple (221, 27, −2) with itself, we get

$$\left(\frac{221^2 + 67\cdot27^2}{2}\right)^2 - 67\cdot(221\cdot27)^2 = 1,$$

that is, we have the integer solution:

$$48842^2 - 67 \cdot 5967^2 = 1.$$

This equation approximates $\sqrt{67}$ as $\frac{48842}{5967}$ to within a margin of about $2 \times 10^{-9}$.

:::
