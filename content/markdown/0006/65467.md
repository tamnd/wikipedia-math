# Persi Diaconis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Persi Diaconis"}

**Persi Warren Diaconis** (; born January 31, 1945) is an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) of [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greece) descent and former professional [magician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magic_%28illusion%29).[^o4] He is the [Mary V. Sunseri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_V._Sunseri) Professor of [Statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [Mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University).[^o6][^o7]

:::

He is particularly known for tackling mathematical problems involving [randomness](wikipedia://en/19196523) and [randomization](wikipedia://en/65462), such as [coin flipping](wikipedia://en/494410) and [shuffling playing cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffling_playing_cards).

## Biography {#Biography}

Diaconis left home at 14[^o9] to travel with magician [Dai Vernon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dai_Vernon), and was awarded a high school diploma based on grades given to him by his teachers after dropping out of [George Washington High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_Educational_Campus).[^o10] He returned to school at age 24 to learn math, motivated to read [William Feller](wikipedia://en/206644)'s famous two-volume treatise on probability theory, *An Introduction to Probability Theory and Its Applications*. He attended the [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York) for his undergraduate work, graduating in 1971, and then obtained a Ph.D. in mathematical statistics from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) in 1974 and became a mathematical probabilist.[^o11]

According to [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435), at school, Diaconis supported himself by playing [poker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poker) on ships between New York and [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America). Gardner recalls that Diaconis had "fantastic [second deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_dealing) and [bottom deal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bottom_dealing)".[^o12]

Diaconis is married to Stanford statistics professor [Susan Holmes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Susan_P._Holmes).[^o13]

## Career {#Career}

Diaconis received a [MacArthur Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_D._and_Catherine_T._MacArthur_Foundation) in 1982. In 1990, he published (with [Dave Bayer](wikipedia://en/1442686)) a paper entitled "Trailing the Dovetail Shuffle to Its Lair"[^o15] (a term coined by magician [Charles Jordan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jordan_%28magician%29) in the early 1900s) which established rigorous results on how many times a deck of playing cards must be [riffle shuffled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffle#Riffle) before it can be considered random according to the mathematical measure [total variation distance](wikipedia://en/683570). Diaconis is often cited for the simplified proposition that it takes seven shuffles to randomize a deck. More precisely, Diaconis showed that, in the [Gilbert–Shannon–Reeds model](wikipedia://en/39949312) of how likely it is that a riffle results in a particular [riffle shuffle permutation](wikipedia://en/39949298), it takes 5 riffles before the total variation distance of a 52-card deck begins to drop significantly from the maximum value of 1.0, and 7 riffles before it drops below 0.5 very quickly (a threshold phenomenon), after which it is reduced by a factor of 2 every shuffle. When [entropy](wikipedia://en/15445) is viewed as the probabilistic distance, riffle [shuffling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffling) seems to take less time to mix, and the threshold phenomenon goes away (because the entropy function is subadditive).[^o16]

Diaconis has coauthored several more recent papers expanding on his 1992 results and relating the problem of shuffling cards to other problems in mathematics. Among other things, they showed that the separation distance of an ordered [blackjack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackjack) deck (that is, aces on top, followed by 2's, followed by 3's, etc.) drops below .5 after 7 shuffles. Separation distance is an upper bound for variation distance.[^o17][^o18]

Diaconis has been hired by casino executives to search for subtle flaws in their automatic card shuffling machines. Diaconis soon found some and the horrified executives responded, "We are not pleased with your conclusions but we believe them and that's what we hired you for."[^o19]

He served on the Mathematical Sciences jury of the [Infosys Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infosys_Prize) in 2011 and 2012.

## Works {#Works}

The books written or coauthored by Diaconis include:

- *Group Representations In Probability And Statistics* (Institute of Mathematical Statistics, 1988)[^o30]
- *Magical Mathematics: The Mathematical Ideas That Animate Great Magic Tricks* (with [Ronald L. Graham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_L._Graham), Princeton University Press, 2012),[^o31] winner of the 2013 [Euler Book Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_Book_Prize)[^o32]
- *Ten Great Ideas about Chance* (with [Brian Skyrms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Skyrms), Princeton University Press, 2018)[^o33]

His other publications include:

- "Theories of data analysis: from magical thinking through classical statistics", in (1985). "Exploring Data Tables, Trends, and Shapes". Wiley. isbn:0-471-09776-4. <https://archive.org/details/exploringdatatab0000unse>
- Diaconis, P. (1978). "Statistical problems in ESP research". *Science*. 201 (4351). p. 131–136. doi:10.1126/science.663642
- Diaconis, P.; Holmes, S; Montgomery, R. (2007). "Dynamical bias in the coin toss". *SIAM Review*. 49 (2). p. 211–235. doi:10.1137/S0036144504446436
