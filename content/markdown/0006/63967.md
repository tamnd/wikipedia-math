# Double pendulum

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Double pendulum"}

*Image: Double-Pendulum.svg.* A double pendulum consists of two [pendulums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) attached end to end.

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the area of [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems), a **double pendulum**, also known as a **chaotic pendulum**, is a [pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) with another pendulum attached to its end, forming a complex [physical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_system) that exhibits rich [dynamic behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems) with a [strong sensitivity to initial conditions](wikipedia://en/4024).[^o2] The motion of a double pendulum is governed by a pair of coupled [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/32742753) and is [chaotic](wikipedia://en/6295).

:::

## Analysis and interpretation {#Analysis_and_interpretation}

Several variants of the double pendulum may be considered; the two limbs may be of equal or unequal lengths and masses, they may be [simple pendulums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_pendulum) or [compound pendulums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_pendulum) (also called complex pendulums) and the motion may be in three dimensions or restricted to one vertical plane. In the following analysis, the limbs are taken to be identical compound pendulums of length $\ell$ and mass $m$, and the motion is restricted to two dimensions.

*Image: Double-compound-pendulum-dimensioned.svg.* Double compound pendulum

*Image: Double pendulum simulation.gif.* Motion of the double compound pendulum (from [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089) of the equations of motion)

In a compound pendulum, the mass is distributed along its length. If the double pendulum mass is evenly distributed, then the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) of each limb is at its midpoint, and the limb has a [moment of inertia](wikipedia://en/157700) of $I = \tfrac{1}{12}m\ell^{2}$ about that point.

Although it is possible to derive the equations of a double pendulum with Newtonian mechanics, it is considered to be cumbersome to work with as it would require resolving vectors with respect to constraint forces. So it is more convenient to use the angles between each limb and the vertical as the [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514) defining the [configuration](wikipedia://en/473596) of the system. These angles are denoted $\theta_{1}$ and $\theta_{2}$. The position of the center of mass of each rod may be written in terms of these two coordinates. If the origin of the [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) is taken to be at the point of suspension of the first pendulum, then the center of mass of this pendulum is at:

$$\begin{aligned} x_1 &= \tfrac{1}{2} \ell \sin \theta_1 \\ y_1 &= -\tfrac{1}{2} \ell \cos \theta_1 \end{aligned}$$

and the center of mass of the second pendulum is at

$$\begin{aligned} x_2 &= \ell \left ( \sin \theta_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \sin \theta_2 \right ) \\ y_2 &= -\ell \left ( \cos \theta_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \cos \theta_2 \right ) \end{aligned}$$

This is enough information to write out the Lagrangian.

### Lagrangian {#Lagrangian}

The [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) is given by

$$\begin{aligned} L &= \text{kinetic energy} - \text{potential energy} \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2} m \left ( v_1^2 + v_2^2 \right ) + \tfrac{1}{2} I \left ( \dot\theta_1^2 + \dot\theta_2^2 \right ) - m g \left ( y_1 + y_2 \right ) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2} m \left ( \dot x_1^2 + \dot y_1^2 + \dot x_2^2 + \dot y_2^2 \right ) + \tfrac{1}{2} I \left ( \dot\theta_1^2 + \dot\theta_2^2 \right ) - m g \left ( y_1 + y_2 \right ) \end{aligned}$$

The first term is the *linear* [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327) of the [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961) of the bodies and the second term is the *rotational* kinetic energy around the center of mass of each rod. The last term is the [potential energy](wikipedia://en/23703) of the bodies in a uniform gravitational field. The [dot-notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_notation) indicates the [time derivative](wikipedia://en/1783069) of the variable in question.

Using the values of $x_1$ and $y_1$ defined above, we have

$$\begin{aligned} \dot x_1 &= \dot \theta_1 \left(\tfrac{1}{2}\ell \cos \theta_1 \right) \\[1ex] \dot y_1 &= \dot \theta_1 \left(\tfrac{1}{2} \ell \sin \theta_1 \right) \end{aligned}$$

which leads to

$$v_1^2 = \dot x_1^2 + \dot y_1^2 = \tfrac{1}{4} \dot \theta_1^2 \ell^2 \left(\cos^2 \theta_1 + \sin^2 \theta_1 \right) = \tfrac{1}{4} \ell^2 \dot \theta_1^2 .$$

Similarly, for $x_2$ and $y_2$ we have

$$\begin{aligned} \dot x_2 &= \ell \left(\dot \theta_1 \cos \theta_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \dot \theta_2 \cos \theta_2 \right) \\ \dot y_2 &= \ell \left(\dot \theta_1 \sin \theta_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \dot \theta_2 \sin \theta_2 \right) \end{aligned}$$

and therefore

$$\begin{aligned} v_2^2 &= \dot x_2^2 + \dot y_2^2 \\[1ex] &= \ell^2 \left( \dot \theta_1^2 \cos^2 \theta_1 + \dot \theta_1^2 \sin^2 \theta_1 + \tfrac{1}{4} \dot \theta_2^2 \cos^2 \theta_2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \dot \theta_2^2 \sin^2 \theta_2 + \dot \theta_1 \dot \theta_2 \cos \theta_1 \cos \theta_2 + \dot \theta_1 \dot \theta_2 \sin \theta_1 \sin \theta_2 \right) \\[1ex] &= \ell^2 \left( \dot \theta_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \dot \theta_2^2 + \dot \theta_1 \dot \theta_2 \cos \left(\theta_1 - \theta_2 \right) \right). \end{aligned}$$

Substituting the coordinates above into the definition of the Lagrangian, and rearranging the equation, gives

$$\begin{aligned} L &= \tfrac{1}{2} m \ell^2 \left( \dot \theta_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \dot \theta_1^2 + \tfrac{1}{4} \dot \theta_2^2 + \dot \theta_1 \dot \theta_2 \cos \left(\theta_1 - \theta_2 \right) \right) + \tfrac{1}{24} m \ell^2 \left( \dot \theta_1^2 + \dot \theta_2^2 \right) - m g \left(y_1 + y_2 \right) \\[1ex] &= \tfrac{1}{6} m \ell^2 \left ( \dot \theta_2^2 + 4 \dot \theta_1^2 + 3 {\dot \theta_1} {\dot \theta_2} \cos (\theta_1-\theta_2) \right) + \tfrac{1}{2} m g \ell \left ( 3 \cos \theta_1 + \cos \theta_2 \right ). \end{aligned}$$

The equations of motion can now be derived using the [Euler–Lagrange equations](wikipedia://en/294995), which are given by

$$\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_i} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_i} = 0, \quad i = 1,2.$$

We begin with the equation of motion for $\theta_1$. The derivatives of the Lagrangian are given by

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_1} = -\tfrac{1}{2} m \ell^2 \dot{\theta}_1 \dot{\theta}_2 \sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2) - \tfrac{3}{2} mg\ell \sin\theta_1$$

and

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_1} = \tfrac{4}{3} m\ell^2 \dot{\theta}_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} m\ell^2 \dot{\theta}_2 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2).$$

Thus

$$\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_1} = \tfrac{4}{3} m\ell^2 \ddot{\theta}_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} m\ell^2 \ddot{\theta}_2 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) - \tfrac{1}{2} m\ell^2 \dot{\theta}_2(\dot{\theta}_1 - \dot{\theta}_2) \sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2).$$

Combining these results and simplifying yields the first equation of motion,

$$\tfrac{4}{3} \ell \ddot{\theta}_1 + \tfrac{1}{2} \ell \ddot{\theta}_2 \cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2) + \tfrac{1}{2} \ell \dot{\theta}_2^2 \sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) + \tfrac{3}{2} g \sin\theta_1 = 0.$$

Similarly, the derivatives of the Lagrangian with respect to $\theta_2$ and $\dot{\theta}_2$ are given by

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \theta_2} = \tfrac{1}{2} m \ell^2 \dot{\theta}_1 \dot{\theta}_2 \sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2) - \tfrac{1}{2} mg\ell \sin\theta_2$$

and

$$\frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_2} = \tfrac{1}{3} m\ell^2 \dot{\theta}_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} m\ell^2 \dot{\theta}_1 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2).$$

Thus

$$\frac{d}{dt} \frac{\partial L}{\partial \dot{\theta}_2} = \tfrac{1}{3} m\ell^2 \ddot{\theta}_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} m\ell^2 \ddot{\theta}_1 \cos(\theta_1-\theta_2) - \tfrac{1}{2} m\ell^2 \dot{\theta}_1(\dot{\theta}_1 - \dot{\theta}_2) \sin(\theta_1 - \theta_2).$$

Plugging these results into the Euler-Lagrange equation and simplifying yields the second equation of motion,

$$\tfrac{1}{3} \ell \ddot{\theta}_2 + \tfrac{1}{2} \ell \ddot{\theta}_1 \cos(\theta_1 - \theta_2) - \tfrac{1}{2} \ell \dot{\theta}_1^2 \sin(\theta_1-\theta_2) + \tfrac{1}{2} g \sin\theta_2 = 0.$$

No [closed form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_form_expression) solutions for $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$ as functions of time are known, therefore the system can only be solved [numerically](wikipedia://en/170089), using the [Runge Kutta method](wikipedia://en/213671) or [similar techniques](wikipedia://en/272829).

## Chaotic motion {#Chaotic_motion}

*Image: Double-pendulum.png.* Parametric plot for the time evolution of the angles of a double pendulum. Note that the graph resembles [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436).

*Image: DPLE.jpg.* Long exposure of double pendulum exhibiting chaotic motion (tracked with an [LED](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LED))

*Image: 3-double-pendulums.gif.* Three double pendulums with nearly identical starting conditions diverge over time, demonstrating the chaotic nature of the system.

The double pendulum undergoes [chaotic motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaotic_motion), and clearly shows a sensitive dependence on [initial conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_conditions). The sensitivity of a dynamical system's trajectory at a particular initial condition can be quantified by the [Lyapunov exponent](wikipedia://en/166441) at that initial condition. The image to the left plots these values approximately as a function of starting angles, with lower values in blue and higher values in red.

The image to the right shows the amount of elapsed time before the pendulum flips over, as a function of initial position when released at rest. Here, the initial value of $\theta_{1}$ ranges along the $x$-direction from −3.14 to 3.14. The initial value $\theta_{2}$ ranges along the $y$-direction, from −3.14 to 3.14. The color of each pixel indicates whether either pendulum flips within:

- $\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}$ (black)
- $10\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}$ (red)
- $100\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}$ (green)
- $1000\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}$ (blue) or
- $10000\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}$ (purple).

Initial conditions that do not lead to a flip within $10000\sqrt{\frac{\ell}{g}}$ are plotted white.

The boundary of the central white region is defined in part by energy conservation with the following curve:

$$3 \cos \theta_1 + \cos \theta_2 = 2.$$

Within the region defined by this curve, that is if

$$3 \cos \theta_1 + \cos \theta_2 > 2,$$

then it is energetically impossible for either pendulum to flip. Outside this region, the pendulum can flip, but it is a complex question to determine when it will flip. Similar behavior is observed for a double pendulum composed of two [point masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_mass) rather than two rods with distributed mass.[^o6]

The lack of a natural excitation frequency has led to the use of [double pendulum systems in seismic resistance designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuned_mass_damper) in buildings, where the building itself is the primary inverted pendulum, and a secondary mass is connected to complete the double pendulum.[^o7]
