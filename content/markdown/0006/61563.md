# Spiral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spiral"}

*Image: NautilusCutawayLogarithmicSpiral.jpg.* Cutaway of a [nautilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautilus) shell showing the chambers arranged in an approximately [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373)

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **spiral** is a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) which emanates from a point, moving further away as it revolves around the point.[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5] It is a subtype of [whorled](wikipedia://en/1945043) patterns, a broad group that also includes [concentric objects](wikipedia://en/19049087).

:::

## Two-dimensional {#Two-dimensional}

*Image: Six types of spirals.png.* Spirals generated by six mathematical relationships between radius and angle

A [two-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional), or plane, spiral may be easily described using [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates), where the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) $r$ is a [monotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonic) [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) of angle $\varphi$:

- $$r=r(\varphi)\; .$$

The circle would be regarded as a [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_%28mathematics%29) case (the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) not being strictly monotonic, but rather [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132)).

In *$x$-$y$-coordinates* the curve has the parametric representation:

- $$x=r(\varphi)\cos\varphi \ ,\qquad y=r(\varphi)\sin\varphi\; .$$

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Some of the most important sorts of two-dimensional spirals include:

- The [Archimedean spiral](wikipedia://en/61559): $r=a \varphi$
- The [hyperbolic spiral](wikipedia://en/183045): $r = a/ \varphi$
- [Fermat's spiral](wikipedia://en/199584): $r= a\varphi^{1/2}$
- The [lituus](wikipedia://en/20144966): $r = a\varphi^{-1/2}$
- The [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373): $r=ae^{k\varphi}$
- The [Cornu spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornu_spiral) or *clothoid*
- The [Fibonacci spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_spiral) and [golden spiral](wikipedia://en/219370)
- The [Spiral of Theodorus](wikipedia://en/17206552): an approximation of the Archimedean spiral composed of contiguous right triangles
- The [involute](wikipedia://en/840704) of a circle

*Image: Schraublinie-hyp-spirale.svg.* Hyperbolic spiral as central projection of a helix

An *Archimedean spiral* is, for example, generated while coiling a carpet.[^o9]

A *hyperbolic spiral* appears as image of a helix with a special central projection (see diagram). A hyperbolic spiral is some times called *reciproke* spiral, because it is the image of an Archimedean spiral with a circle-inversion (see below).[^o10]

The name *logarithmic spiral* is due to the equation $\varphi=\tfrac{1}{k}\cdot \ln \tfrac{r}{a}$. Approximations of this are found in nature.

Spirals which do not fit into this scheme of the first 5 examples:

A *Cornu spiral* has two asymptotic points. The *spiral of Theodorus* is a polygon. The *Fibonacci Spiral* consists of a sequence of circle arcs. The *involute of a circle* looks like an Archimedean, but is not: see [Involute of a circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Involute_of_a_circle).

:::

### Geometric properties {#Geometric_properties}

The following considerations are dealing with spirals, which can be described by a polar equation $r=r(\varphi)$, especially for the cases $r(\varphi)=a\varphi^n$ (Archimedean, hyperbolic, Fermat's, lituus spirals) and the logarithmic spiral $r=ae^{k\varphi}$.

*Image: Sektor-steigung-pk-def.svg.* Definition of sector (light blue) and polar slope angle $\alpha$

**Polar slope angle**

The angle $\alpha$ between the spiral tangent and the corresponding polar circle (see diagram) is called *angle of the polar slope* and $\tan \alpha$ the *polar slope*.

From [vector calculus in polar coordinates](wikipedia://en/25120#Vector_calculus) one gets the formula

$$\tan\alpha=\frac{r'}{r}\ .$$

Hence the slope of the spiral $\;r=a\varphi^n \;$ is

- $$\tan\alpha=\frac{n}{\varphi}\ .$$

In case of an *Archimedean spiral* ($n=1$) the polar slope is $\tan\alpha=\tfrac{1}{\varphi}\ .$

In a *logarithmic spiral*, $\tan\alpha=k\$ is constant.

**Curvature**

The curvature $\kappa$ of a curve with polar equation $r=r(\varphi)$ is

$$\kappa = \frac{r^2 + 2(r')^2 - r\; r''}{(r^2+(r')^2)^{3/2}}\ .$$

For a spiral with $r=a\varphi^n$ one gets

- $$\kappa = \dotsb = \frac{1}{a\varphi^{n-1}}\frac{\varphi^2+n^2+n}{(\varphi^2+n^2)^{3/2}}\ .$$

In case of $n=1$ *(Archimedean spiral)* $\kappa=\tfrac{\varphi^2+2}{a(\varphi^2+1)^{3/2}}$. Only for $-1<n<0$ the spiral has an *inflection point*.

The curvature of a *logarithmic spiral* $\; r=a e^{k\varphi} \;$ is $\; \kappa=\tfrac{1}{r\sqrt{1+k^2}} \; .$

**Sector area**

The area of a sector of a curve (see diagram) with polar equation $r=r(\varphi)$ is

$$A=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\varphi_1}^{\varphi_2} r(\varphi)^2\; d\varphi\ .$$

For a spiral with equation $r=a\varphi^n\;$ one gets

- $$A=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\varphi_1}^{\varphi_2} a^2\varphi^{2n}\; d\varphi =\frac{a^2}{2(2n+1)}\big(\varphi_2^{2n+1}- \varphi_1^{2n+1}\big)\ , \quad \text{if}\quad n\ne-\frac{1}{2},$$

$$A=\frac{1}{2}\int_{\varphi_1}^{\varphi_2} \frac{a^2}{\varphi}\; d\varphi =\frac{a^2}{2}(\ln\varphi_2-\ln\varphi_1)\ ,\quad \text{if} \quad n=-\frac{1}{2}\ .$$

The formula for a *logarithmic spiral* $\; r=a e^{k\varphi} \;$ is $\ A=\tfrac{r(\varphi_2)^2-r(\varphi_1)^2)}{4k}\ .$

**Arc length**

The length of an arc of a curve with polar equation $r=r(\varphi)$ is

$$L=\int\limits_{\varphi_1}^{\varphi_2}\sqrt{\left(r^\prime(\varphi)\right)^2+r^2(\varphi)}\,\mathrm{d}\varphi \ .$$

For the spiral $r=a\varphi^n\;$ the length is

- $$L=\int_{\varphi_1}^{\varphi_2} \sqrt{\frac{n^2r^2}{\varphi^2} +r^2}\; d\varphi = a\int\limits_{\varphi_1}^{\varphi_2}\varphi^{n-1}\sqrt{n^2+\varphi^2}d\varphi \ .$$

Not all these integrals can be solved by a suitable table. In case of a Fermat's spiral, the integral can be expressed by [elliptic integrals](wikipedia://en/9960) only.

The arc length of a *logarithmic spiral* $\; r=a e^{k\varphi} \;$ is $\ L=\tfrac{\sqrt{k^2+1}}{k}\big(r(\varphi_2)-r(\varphi_1)\big) \ .$

**Circle inversion**

The [inversion at the unit circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_inversion) has in polar coordinates the simple description: $\ (r,\varphi) \mapsto (\tfrac{1}{r},\varphi)\$.

- The image of a spiral $\ r= a\varphi^n\$ under the inversion at the unit circle is the spiral with polar equation $\ r= \tfrac{1}{a}\varphi^{-n}\$. For example: The inverse of an Archimedean spiral is a hyperbolic spiral.

A logarithmic spiral $\; r=a e^{k\varphi} \;$ is mapped onto the logarithmic spiral $\; r=\tfrac{1}{a} e^{-k\varphi} \; .$

### Bounded spirals {#Bounded_spirals}

*Image: Spiral-arctan-1-2.svg.* Bounded spirals: $r=a \arctan(k\varphi)$ (left), $r=a (\arctan(k\varphi)+\pi/2)$ (right)

The function $r(\varphi)$ of a spiral is usually strictly monotonic, continuous and un[bounded](wikipedia://en/311509). For the standard spirals $r(\varphi)$ is either a power function or an exponential function. If one chooses for $r(\varphi)$ a *bounded* function, the spiral is bounded, too. A suitable bounded function is the [arctan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arctan) function:

::: {#o13 .example cue=inline-head name="Example 1"}

**Example 1**

Setting $\;r=a \arctan(k\varphi)\;$ and the choice $\;k=0.1, a=4, \;\varphi\ge 0\;$ gives a spiral, that starts at the origin (like an Archimedean spiral) and approaches the circle with radius $\;r=a\pi/2\;$ (diagram, left).

:::

::: {#o14 .example cue=inline-head name="Example 2"}

**Example 2**

For $\;r=a (\arctan(k\varphi)+\pi/2)\;$ and $\;k=0.2, a=2,\; -\infty<\varphi<\infty\;$ one gets a spiral, that approaches the origin (like a hyperbolic spiral) and approaches the circle with radius $\;r=a\pi\;$ (diagram, right).

:::

## Three-dimensional {#Three-dimensional}

### Helices {#Helices}

*Image: Schraube und archimedische Spirale.png.* An Archimedean spiral (black), a helix (green), and a conical spiral (red)

Two major definitions of "spiral" in the [American Heritage Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Heritage_Dictionary) are:[^o17]

1. a curve on a plane that winds around a fixed center point at a continuously increasing or decreasing distance from the point.
1. a three-dimensional curve that turns around an axis at a constant or continuously varying distance while moving parallel to the axis; a [helix](wikipedia://en/179924).

The first definition describes a [planar](wikipedia://en/19469852) curve, that extends in both of the perpendicular directions within its plane; the groove on one side of a [gramophone record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gramophone_record) closely approximates a plane spiral (and it is by the finite width and depth of the groove, but *not* by the wider spacing between than within tracks, that it falls short of being a perfect example); note that successive loops *differ* in diameter. In another example, the "center lines" of the arms of a [spiral galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_galaxy) trace [logarithmic spirals](wikipedia://en/42373).

The second definition includes two kinds of 3-dimensional relatives of spirals:

- A conical or [volute spring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volute_spring) (including the spring used to hold and make contact with the negative terminals of AA or AAA batteries in a [battery box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battery_%28electricity%29)), and the [vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex) that is created when water is draining in a sink is often described as a spiral, or as a [conical helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conical_helix).
- Quite explicitly, definition 2 also includes a cylindrical coil spring and a strand of [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), both of which are fairly helical, so that "helix" is a more *useful* description than "spiral" for each of them. In general, "spiral" is seldom applied if successive "loops" of a curve have the same diameter.[^o17]

In the side picture, the black curve at the bottom is an [Archimedean spiral](wikipedia://en/61559), while the green curve is a helix. The curve shown in red is a conical spiral.

Two well-known spiral [space curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve) are *conical spirals* and *spherical spirals*, defined below. Another instance of space spirals is the *toroidal spiral*.[^o18] A spiral wound around a helix,[^o19] also known as *double-twisted helix*,[^o20] represents objects such as [coiled coil filaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coiled_coil_filament).

### Conical spirals {#Conical_spirals}

*Image: Spiral-cone-arch-s.svg.* Conical spiral with Archimedean spiral as floor plan

If in the $x$-$y$-plane a spiral with parametric representation

$$x=r(\varphi)\cos\varphi \ ,\qquad y=r(\varphi)\sin\varphi$$

is given, then there can be added a third coordinate $z(\varphi)$, such that the now space curve lies on the [cone](wikipedia://en/782427) with equation $\;m(x^2+y^2)=(z-z_0)^2\ ,\ m>0\;$:

- $$x=r(\varphi)\cos\varphi \ ,\qquad y=r(\varphi)\sin\varphi\ , \qquad \color{red}{z=z_0 + mr(\varphi)} \ .$$

Spirals based on this procedure are called **conical spirals**.

::: {#o22 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**

Starting with an *archimedean spiral* $\;r(\varphi)=a\varphi\;$ one gets the conical spiral (see diagram)

$$x=a\varphi\cos\varphi \ ,\qquad y=a\varphi\sin\varphi\ , \qquad z=z_0 + ma\varphi \ ,\quad \varphi \ge 0 \ .$$

:::

### Spherical spirals {#Spherical_spirals}

*Image: Kugel-spirale-1-2.svg.* Clelia curve with $c=8$

Any [cylindrical map projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_map_projection) can be used as the basis for a **spherical spiral**: draw a straight line on the map and find its inverse projection on the sphere, a kind of [spherical curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_curve).

One of the most basic families of spherical spirals is the [Clelia curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clelia_curve), which project to straight lines on an [equirectangular projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equirectangular_projection). These are curves for which [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) and [colatitude](wikipedia://en/1828681) are in a linear relationship, analogous to Archimedean spirals in the plane; under the [azimuthal equidistant projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azimuthal_equidistant_projection) a Clelia curve projects to a planar Archimedean spiral.

If one represents a unit sphere by [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates)

$$x = \sin \theta \, \cos \varphi, \quad y = \sin \theta \, \sin \varphi, \quad z = \cos \theta,$$

then setting the linear dependency $\varphi=c\theta$ for the angle coordinates gives a [parametric curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_curve) in terms of parameter $\theta$,[^o25]

$$\bigl( \sin \theta\, \cos c\theta,\, \sin \theta\, \sin c\theta,\, \cos \theta \,\bigr).$$

Another family of spherical spirals is the [rhumb lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhumb_line) or loxodromes, that project to straight lines on the [Mercator projection](wikipedia://en/20771). These are the trajectories traced by a ship traveling with constant [bearing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bearing_%28navigation%29). Any loxodrome (except for the meridians and parallels) spirals infinitely around either pole, closer and closer each time, unlike a Clelia curve which maintains uniform spacing in colatitude. Under [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431), a loxodrome projects to a logarithmic spiral in the plane.

:::

## In nature {#In_nature}

The study of spirals in [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature) has a long history. [Christopher Wren](wikipedia://en/52946) observed that many [shells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exoskeleton) form a [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373); [Jan Swammerdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Swammerdam) observed the common mathematical characteristics of a wide range of shells from *[Helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helix_%28genus%29)* to *[Spirula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirula)*; and [Henry Nottidge Moseley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Nottidge_Moseley) described the mathematics of [univalve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univalve) shells. [D’Arcy Wentworth Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Arcy_Wentworth_Thompson)'s *[On Growth and Form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Growth_and_Form)* gives extensive treatment to these spirals. He describes how shells are formed by rotating a closed curve around a fixed axis: the [shape](wikipedia://en/169191) of the curve remains fixed, but its size grows in a [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670). In some shells, such as *[Nautilus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautilus)* and [ammonites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ammonite), the generating curve revolves in a plane perpendicular to the axis and the shell will form a planar discoid shape. In others it follows a skew path forming a [helico](wikipedia://en/179924)-spiral pattern. Thompson also studied spirals occurring in [horns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_%28anatomy%29), [teeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teeth), [claws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claw) and [plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plant).[^o28]

A model for the pattern of [florets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floret) in the head of a [sunflower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunflower)[^o29] was proposed by H. Vogel. This has the form

$$\theta = n \times 137.5^{\circ},\ r = c \sqrt{n}$$

where *n* is the index number of the floret and *c* is a constant scaling factor, and is a form of [Fermat's spiral](wikipedia://en/199584). The angle 137.5° is the [golden angle](wikipedia://en/315659) which is related to the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) and gives a close packing of florets.[^o30]

Spirals in plants and animals are frequently described as [whorls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whorl_%28botany%29). This is also the name given to spiral shaped [fingerprints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerprint).

:::

## As a symbol {#As_a_symbol}

The [Celtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celts) triple-spiral is in fact a pre-Celtic symbol.[^o32] It is carved into the rock of a stone lozenge near the main entrance of the prehistoric [Newgrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newgrange) monument in [County Meath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_Meath), Ireland. Newgrange was built around 3200 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BCE), predating the Celts; triple spirals were carved at least 2,500 years before the Celts reached Ireland, but have long since become part of Celtic culture.[^o33] The [triskelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskelion) symbol, consisting of three interlocked spirals or three bent human legs, appears in many early cultures: examples include [Mycenaean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mycenaean_Greece) vessels, coinage from [Lycia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycia), [staters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stater) of [Pamphylia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pamphylia) (at [Aspendos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aspendos), 370–333 BC) and [Pisidia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisidia), as well as the [heraldic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraldic) emblem on warriors' shields depicted on Greek pottery.[^o34]

Spirals occur commonly in pre-Columbian art in Latin and Central America. The more than 1,400 [petroglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroglyphs) (rock engravings) in [Las Plazuelas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Plazuelas), [Guanajuato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanajuato) [Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mexico), dating 750-1200 AD, predominantly depict spirals, dot figures and scale models.[^o35] In Colombia, monkeys, frog and lizard-like figures depicted in petroglyphs or as gold offering-figures frequently include spirals, for example on the palms of hands.[^o36] In Lower Central America, spirals along with circles, wavy lines, crosses and points are universal petroglyph characters.[^o37] Spirals also appear among the [Nazca Lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazca_Lines) in the coastal desert of Peru, dating from 200 BC to 500 AD. The [geoglyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoglyphs) number in the thousands and depict animals, plants and geometric motifs, including spirals.[^o38]

Spirals are also a symbol of [hypnosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypnosis), stemming from the [cliché](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cliché) of people and cartoon characters being hypnotized by staring into a spinning spiral. They are also used as a symbol of [dizziness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dizziness), where the eyes of a cartoon character, especially in [anime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anime) and [manga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manga), will turn into spirals to suggest that they are dizzy or dazed. The spiral is also found in structures as small as the [double helix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_helix) of [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA) and as large as a [galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_galaxy). Due to this frequent natural occurrence, the spiral is the official symbol of the [World Pantheist Movement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Pantheist_Movement).[^o39] The spiral is also a symbol of the [dialectic](wikipedia://en/52813) process and of [Dialectical monism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialectical_monism).

> The spiral is a frequent symbol for [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_experience) purification, both within [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) and beyond (one thinks of the spiral as the [neo-Platonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism) symbol for prayer and contemplation, circling around a subject and ascending at the same time, and as a [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) symbol for the gradual process on the Path to [Enlightenment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enlightenment_in_Buddhism)). \[...\] while a helix is repetitive, a spiral expands and thus epitomizes [growth](wikipedia://en/191933) – conceptually *ad infinitum*.[^o40]

## In art {#In_art}

The spiral has inspired artists throughout the ages. Among the most famous of spiral-inspired art is [Robert Smithson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Smithson)'s [earthwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthworks_%28art%29), "[Spiral Jetty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiral_Jetty)", at the [Great Salt Lake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Salt_Lake) in Utah.[^o42] The spiral theme is also present in David Wood's Spiral Resonance Field at the [Balloon Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anderson-Abruzzo_Albuquerque_International_Balloon_Museum) in Albuquerque, as well as in the critically acclaimed [Nine Inch Nails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nine_Inch_Nails) 1994 concept album *[The Downward Spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Downward_Spiral)*. The Spiral is also a prominent theme in the anime *[Gurren Lagann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurren_Lagann)*, where it represents a philosophy and way of life. It also central in Mario Merz and Andy Goldsworthy's work. The spiral is the central theme of the horror manga *[Uzumaki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzumaki)* by [Junji Ito](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Junji_Ito), where a small coastal town is afflicted by a curse involving spirals.
