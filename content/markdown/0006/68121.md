# Kolmogorov space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kolmogorov space"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and related branches of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* is a **T<sub>0</sub> space** or **Kolmogorov space** (named after [Andrey Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161)) if for every pair of distinct points of *X*, at least one of them has a [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) not containing the other.[^o2] In a T<sub>0</sub> space, all points are [topologically distinguishable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_distinguishable).

:::

This condition, called the **T<sub>0</sub> condition**, is the weakest of the [separation axioms](wikipedia://en/40117302). Nearly all topological spaces normally studied in mathematics are T<sub>0</sub> spaces. In particular, all [T<sub>1</sub> spaces](wikipedia://en/148721), i.e., all spaces in which for every pair of distinct points, each has a neighborhood not containing the other, are T<sub>0</sub> spaces. This includes all [T<sub>2</sub> (or Hausdorff) spaces](wikipedia://en/13637), i.e., all topological spaces in which distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods. In another direction, every [sober space](wikipedia://en/602490) (which may not be T<sub>1</sub>) is T<sub>0</sub>; this includes the underlying topological space of any [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754). Given any topological space one can construct a T<sub>0</sub> space by identifying topologically indistinguishable points.

T<sub>0</sub> spaces that are not T<sub>1</sub> spaces are exactly those spaces for which the [specialization preorder](wikipedia://en/542347) is a nontrivial [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order). Such spaces naturally occur in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), specifically in [denotational semantics](wikipedia://en/55275).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **T<sub>0</sub> space** is a topological space in which every pair of distinct points is [topologically distinguishable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_distinguishable). That is, for any two different points *x* and *y* there is an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) that contains one of these points and not the other. More precisely the topological space *X* is Kolmogorov or $\mathbf T_0$ if and only if:[^o2]

If $a,b\in X$ and $a\neq b$, there exists an open set *O* such that either $(a\in O) \wedge (b\notin O)$ or $(a\notin O) \wedge (b\in O)$.

Topologically distinguishable points are automatically distinct. On the other hand, if the [singleton sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set) {*x*} and {*y*} are [separated](wikipedia://en/148911) then the points *x* and *y* must be topologically distinguishable. That is,

*separated* ⇒ *topologically distinguishable* ⇒ *distinct*

The property of being topologically distinguishable is, in general, stronger than being distinct but weaker than being separated. In a T<sub>0</sub> space, the second arrow above also reverses; points are distinct [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they are distinguishable. This is how the T<sub>0</sub> axiom fits in with the rest of the [separation axioms](wikipedia://en/40117302).

:::

## Examples and counter examples {#Examples_and_counter_examples}

Nearly all topological spaces normally studied in mathematics are T<sub>0</sub>. In particular, all [Hausdorff (T<sub>2</sub>) spaces](wikipedia://en/13637), [T<sub>1</sub> spaces](wikipedia://en/148721) and [sober spaces](wikipedia://en/602490) are T<sub>0</sub>.

### Spaces that are not T<sub>0</sub> {#Spaces_that_are_not_T0}

- A set with more than one element, with the [trivial topology](wikipedia://en/382447). No points are distinguishable.
- The set **R**<sup>2</sup> where the open sets are the Cartesian product of an open set in **R** and **R** itself, i.e., the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) of **R** with the usual topology and **R** with the trivial topology; points (*a*,*b*) and (*a*,*c*) are not distinguishable.
- The space of all [measurable functions](wikipedia://en/44055) *f* from the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) **R** to the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) **C** such that the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288) $\left(\int_{\mathbb{R}} |f(x)|^2 \,dx\right)^{\frac{1}{2}} < \infty$. Two functions which are equal [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) are indistinguishable. See also below.

### Spaces that are T<sub>0</sub> but not T<sub>1</sub> {#Spaces_that_are_T0_but_not_T1}

- The [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) on Spec(*R*), the [prime spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_spectrum) of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R*, is always T<sub>0</sub> but generally not T<sub>1</sub>. The [non-closed points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-closed_point) correspond to [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) which are not [maximal](wikipedia://en/48164). They are important to the understanding of [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754).
- The [particular point topology](wikipedia://en/5336200) on any set with at least two elements is T<sub>0</sub> but not T<sub>1</sub> since the particular point is not closed (its closure is the whole space). An important special case is the [Sierpiński space](wikipedia://en/387651) which is the particular point topology on the set {0,1}.
- The [excluded point topology](wikipedia://en/5539197) on any set with at least two elements is T<sub>0</sub> but not T<sub>1</sub>. The only [closed point](wikipedia://en/33086408) is the excluded point.
- The [Alexandrov topology](wikipedia://en/542198) on a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) is T<sub>0</sub> but will not be T<sub>1</sub> unless the order is discrete (agrees with equality). Every finite T<sub>0</sub> space is of this type. This also includes the particular point and excluded point topologies as special cases.
- The [right order topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_order_topology) on a [totally ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_set) is a related example.
- The [overlapping interval topology](wikipedia://en/3197890) is similar to the particular point topology since every non-empty open set includes 0.
- Quite generally, a topological space *X* will be T<sub>0</sub> if and only if the [specialization preorder](wikipedia://en/542347) on *X* is a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order). However, *X* will be T<sub>1</sub> if and only if the order is discrete (i.e. agrees with equality). So a space will be T<sub>0</sub> but not T<sub>1</sub> if and only if the specialization preorder on *X* is a non-discrete partial order.

## Operating with T<sub>0</sub> spaces {#Operating_with_T0_spaces}

Commonly studied topological spaces are all T<sub>0</sub>. Indeed, when mathematicians in many fields, notably [analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analysis_%28mathematics%29), naturally run across non-T<sub>0</sub> spaces, they usually replace them with T<sub>0</sub> spaces, in a manner to be described below. To motivate the ideas involved, consider a well-known example. The space [L<sup>2</sup>(**R**)](wikipedia://en/45194) is meant to be the space of all [measurable functions](wikipedia://en/44055) *f* from the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) **R** to the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) **C** such that the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288) of |*f*(*x*)|<sup>2</sup> over the entire real line is [finite](wikipedia://en/11742). This space should become a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) by defining the norm ||*f*|| to be the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of that integral. The problem is that this is not really a norm, only a [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067), because there are functions other than the [zero function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_function) whose (semi)norms are [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29). The standard solution is to define L<sup>2</sup>(**R**) to be a set of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of functions instead of a set of functions directly. This constructs a [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) of the original seminormed vector space, and this quotient is a normed vector space. It inherits several convenient properties from the seminormed space; see below.

In general, when dealing with a fixed topology **T** on a set *X*, it is helpful if that topology is T<sub>0</sub>. On the other hand, when *X* is fixed but **T** is allowed to vary within certain boundaries, to force **T** to be T<sub>0</sub> may be inconvenient, since non-T<sub>0</sub> topologies are often important special cases. Thus, it can be important to understand both T<sub>0</sub> and non-T<sub>0</sub> versions of the various conditions that can be placed on a topological space.

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Kolmogorov quotient"}

## The Kolmogorov quotient {#The_Kolmogorov_quotient}

Topological indistinguishability of points is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259). No matter what topological space *X* might be to begin with, the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) under this equivalence relation is always T<sub>0</sub>. This quotient space is called the **Kolmogorov quotient** of *X*, which we will denote KQ(*X*). Of course, if *X* was T<sub>0</sub> to begin with, then KQ(*X*) and *X* are [naturally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_%28category_theory%29) [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic). Categorically, Kolmogorov spaces are a [reflective subcategory](wikipedia://en/3967424) of topological spaces, and the Kolmogorov quotient is the reflector.

Topological spaces *X* and *Y* are **Kolmogorov equivalent** when their Kolmogorov quotients are homeomorphic. Many properties of topological spaces are preserved by this equivalence; that is, if *X* and *Y* are Kolmogorov equivalent, then *X* has such a property if and only if *Y* does. On the other hand, most of the *other* properties of topological spaces *imply* T<sub>0</sub>-ness; that is, if *X* has such a property, then *X* must be T<sub>0</sub>. Only a few properties, such as being an [indiscrete space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_space), are exceptions to this rule of thumb. Even better, many [structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_%28mathematics%29) defined on topological spaces can be transferred between *X* and KQ(*X*). The result is that, if you have a non-T<sub>0</sub> topological space with a certain structure or property, then you can usually form a T<sub>0</sub> space with the same structures and properties by taking the Kolmogorov quotient.

The example of L<sup>2</sup>(**R**) displays these features. From the point of view of topology, the seminormed vector space that we started with has a lot of extra structure; for example, it is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), and it has a seminorm, and these define a [pseudometric](wikipedia://en/68503) and a [uniform structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_structure) that are compatible with the topology. Also, there are several properties of these structures; for example, the seminorm satisfies the [parallelogram identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelogram_identity) and the uniform structure is [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space). The space is not T<sub>0</sub> since any two functions in L<sup>2</sup>(**R**) that are equal [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) are indistinguishable with this topology. When we form the Kolmogorov quotient, the actual L<sup>2</sup>(**R**), these structures and properties are preserved. Thus, L<sup>2</sup>(**R**) is also a complete seminormed vector space satisfying the parallelogram identity. But we actually get a bit more, since the space is now T<sub>0</sub>. A seminorm is a norm if and only if the underlying topology is T<sub>0</sub>, so L<sup>2</sup>(**R**) is actually a complete normed vector space satisfying the parallelogram identity—otherwise known as a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932). And it is a Hilbert space that mathematicians (and [physicists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicists), in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202)) generally want to study. The notation L<sup>2</sup>(**R**) usually denotes the Kolmogorov quotient, the set of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of square integrable functions that differ on sets of measure zero, rather than simply the vector space of square integrable functions that the notation suggests.

:::

## Removing T<sub>0</sub> {#Removing_T0}

Although norms were historically defined first, people came up with the definition of seminorm as well, which is a sort of non-T<sub>0</sub> version of a norm. In general, it is possible to define non-T<sub>0</sub> versions of both properties and structures of topological spaces. First, consider a property of topological spaces, such as being [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637). One can then define another property of topological spaces by defining the space *X* to satisfy the property if and only if the Kolmogorov quotient KQ(*X*) is Hausdorff. This is a sensible, albeit less famous, property; in this case, such a space *X* is called *[preregular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preregular_space)*. (There even turns out to be a more direct definition of preregularity). Now consider a structure that can be placed on topological spaces, such as a [metric](wikipedia://en/20018). We can define a new structure on topological spaces by letting an example of the structure on *X* be simply a metric on KQ(*X*). This is a sensible structure on *X*; it is a [pseudometric](wikipedia://en/68503). (Again, there is a more direct definition of pseudometric.)

In this way, there is a natural way to remove T<sub>0</sub>-ness from the requirements for a property or structure. It is generally easier to study spaces that are T<sub>0</sub>, but it may also be easier to allow structures that aren't T<sub>0</sub> to get a fuller picture. The T<sub>0</sub> requirement can be added or removed arbitrarily using the concept of Kolmogorov quotient.
