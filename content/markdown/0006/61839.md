# Inner automorphism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inner automorphism"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), an **inner automorphism** is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), or [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) given by the [conjugation action](wikipedia://en/49176#Conjugacy_as_group_action) of a fixed element, called the *conjugating element*. They can be realized via operations from within the group itself, hence the adjective "inner". These inner automorphisms form a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the automorphism group, and the [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) of the automorphism group by this subgroup is defined as the [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If $G$ is a group and $g$ is an element of $G$ (alternatively, if $G$ is a ring, and $g$ is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666)), then the function

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi_g\colon G&\to G \\ \varphi_g(x)&:= g^{-1}xg \end{aligned}$$

is called **(right) conjugation by $g$** (see also [conjugacy class](wikipedia://en/49176)). This function is an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) of $G$: for all $x_1,x_2\in G,$

$$\varphi_g(x_1 x_2) = g^{-1} x_1 x_2g = g^{-1} x_1 \left(g g^{-1}\right) x_2 g = \left(g^{-1} x_1 g\right)\left(g^{-1} x_2 g\right) = \varphi_g(x_1)\varphi_g(x_2),$$

where the second equality is given by the insertion of the identity between $x_1$ and $x_2$. Furthermore, it has a left and right [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907), namely $\varphi_{g^{-1}}$. Thus, $\varphi_g$ is both a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) and [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539), and so an isomorphism of $G$ with itself, i.e. an automorphism. An **inner automorphism** is any automorphism that arises from conjugation.[^o4]

*Image: Venn Diagram of Homomorphisms.jpg.* General relationship between various group homomorphisms

When discussing right conjugation, the expression $g^{-1}xg$ is often denoted exponentially by $x^g$. This notation is used because composition of conjugations satisfies the identity: $\left(x^{g_1}\right)^{g_2} = x^{g_1g_2}$ for all $g_1, g_2 \in G$. This shows that right conjugation gives a right [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of $G$ on itself.

A common example is as follows:[^o5][^o6]

*Image: Diagram of Inn(G) Example.jpg.* Relationship of morphisms and elements

Describe a homomorphism $\Phi$ for which the image, $\text{Im} (\Phi)$, is a normal subgroup of inner automorphisms of a group $G$; alternatively, describe a [natural homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_homomorphism) of which the kernel of $\Phi$ is the center of $G$ (all $g \in G$ for which conjugating by them returns the trivial automorphism), in other words, $\text{Ker} (\Phi) = \text{Z}(G)$. There is always a natural homomorphism $\Phi : G \to \text{Aut}(G)$, which associates to every $g \in G$ an (inner) automorphism $\varphi_{g}$ in $\text{Aut}(G)$. Put identically, $\Phi : g \mapsto \varphi_{g}$.

Let $\varphi_{g}(x) := gxg^{-1}$ as defined above. This requires demonstrating that (1) $\varphi_{g}$ is a homomorphism, (2) $\varphi_{g}$ is also a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942), (3) $\Phi$ is a homomorphism.

1. $$\varphi_{g}(xx')=gxx'g^{-1} =gx(g^{-1}g)x'g^{-1} = (gxg^{-1})(gx'g^{-1}) = \varphi_{g}(x)\varphi_{g}(x')$$
1. The condition for bijectivity may be verified by simply presenting an inverse such that we can return to $x$ from $gxg^{-1}$. In this case it is conjugation by $g^{-1}$denoted as $\varphi_{g^{-1}}$.
1. $\Phi(gg')(x)=(gg')x(gg')^{-1}$ and $\Phi(g)\circ \Phi(g')(x)=\Phi(g) \circ (g'xg'^{-1}) = gg'xg'^{-1}g^{-1} = (gg')x(gg')^{-1}$

## Inner and outer automorphism groups {#Inner_and_outer_automorphism_groups}

The [composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_composition) of two inner automorphisms is again an inner automorphism, and with this operation, the collection of all inner automorphisms of $G$ is a group, the inner automorphism group of $G$ denoted $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$.

$\operatorname{Inn}(G)$ is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of the full [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) $\operatorname{Aut}(G)$ of $G$. The [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466), $\operatorname{Out}(G)$ is the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526)

$$\operatorname{Out}(G) = \operatorname{Aut}(G) / \operatorname{Inn}(G).$$

The outer automorphism group measures, in a sense, how many automorphisms of $G$ are not inner. Every non-inner automorphism yields a non-trivial element of $\operatorname{Out}(G)$, but different non-inner automorphisms may yield the same element of $\operatorname{Out}(G)$.

Saying that conjugation of $x$ by $a$ leaves $x$ unchanged is equivalent to saying that $a$ and $x$ commute:

$$a^{-1}xa = x \iff xa = ax$$

Therefore the existence and number of inner automorphisms that are not the [identity mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_mapping) is a kind of measure of the failure of the [commutative law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_law) in the group (or ring).

An automorphism of a group $G$ is inner if and only if it extends to every group containing $G$.[^o8]

By associating the element $a \in G$ with the inner automorphism $f(x) = x^{a}$ in $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$ as above, one obtains an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) between the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $G / Z(G)$ (where $Z(G)$ is the [center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_a_group) of $G$) and the inner automorphism group:

$$G\,/\,\mathrm{Z}(G) \cong \operatorname{Inn}(G)$$

This is a consequence of the [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_theorem), because $Z(G)$ is precisely the set of those elements of $G$ that give the identity mapping as corresponding inner automorphism (conjugation changes nothing).

### Non-inner automorphisms of finite $p$-groups {#Non-inner_automorphisms_of_finite_-groups}

A result of Wolfgang Gaschütz says that if $G$ is a finite non-abelian [$p$-group](wikipedia://en/24664), then $G$ has an automorphism of $p$-power order which is not inner.

It is an [open problem](wikipedia://en/1333335) whether every non-abelian $p$-group $G$ has an automorphism of order $p$. The latter question has positive answer whenever $G$ has one of the following conditions:

1. $G$ is nilpotent of class 2
1. $G$ is a [regular $p$-group](wikipedia://en/15442661)
1. $G / Z(G)$ is a [powerful $p$-group](wikipedia://en/1841291)
1. The [centralizer](wikipedia://en/145661) in $G$, $C_{G}$, of the center, $Z$, of the [Frattini subgroup](wikipedia://en/540707), $\Phi$, of $G$, $C_{G} \circ Z \circ \Phi(G)$, is not equal to $\Phi(G)$

### Types of groups {#Types_of_groups}

The inner automorphism group of a group $G$, $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$, is trivial (i.e., consists only of the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962)) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $G$ is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974).

The group $\operatorname{Inn}(G)$ is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) only when it is trivial.

At the opposite end of the spectrum, the inner automorphisms may exhaust the entire automorphism group; a group whose automorphisms are all inner and whose center is trivial is called [complete](wikipedia://en/876770). This is the case for all of the symmetric groups on $n$ elements when $n$ is not 2 or 6. When $n = 6$, the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) has a unique non-trivial class of non-inner automorphisms, and when $n = 2$, the symmetric group, despite having no non-inner automorphisms, is abelian, giving a non-trivial center, disqualifying it from being complete.

If the inner automorphism group of a [perfect group](wikipedia://en/585271) $G$ is simple, then $G$ is called [quasisimple](wikipedia://en/1642729).

## Lie algebra case {#Lie_algebra_case}

An automorphism of a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\text{𝔊}$ is called an inner automorphism if it is of the form $\operatorname{Ad}_{g}$, where $\operatorname{Ad}$ is the [adjoint map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_group) and $g$ is an element of a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) whose Lie algebra is $\text{𝔊}$. The notion of inner automorphism for Lie algebras is compatible with the notion for groups in the sense that an inner automorphism of a Lie group induces a unique inner automorphism of the corresponding Lie algebra.

## Extension {#Extension}

If $G$ is the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of a [ring](wikipedia://en/250424), $A$, then an inner automorphism on $G$ can be extended to a mapping on the [projective line over $A$](wikipedia://en/1271901) by the group of units of the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541), $M_{2}(A)$. In particular, the inner automorphisms of the [classical groups](wikipedia://en/10172878) can be extended in that way.
