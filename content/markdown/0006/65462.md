# Randomization

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Randomization"}

*Image: Shuffling cards.jpg.* Shuffling a deck of [playing cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Playing_card) will arrange them into a randomized sequence

**Randomization** is the process of making something [random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random). Randomization is not haphazard; instead, a [random process](wikipedia://en/47895) is a sequence of random variables describing a process whose outcomes do not follow a deterministic pattern, but follow an evolution described by probability distributions. For example, a random sample of individuals from a population refers to a sample where every individual has a known probability of being sampled. This would be contrasted with [nonprobability sampling](wikipedia://en/267625) where [arbitrary](wikipedia://en/3019808) individuals are selected.

:::

In various contexts, randomization may involve:

- generating a [random permutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_permutation) of a sequence (such as when [shuffling cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffle));
- selecting a [random sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sample) of a population (important in [statistical sampling](wikipedia://en/160361));
- allocating experimental units via [random assignment](wikipedia://en/2940730) to a treatment or control condition;
- generating random numbers ([random number generation](wikipedia://en/2736939)); or
- transforming a [data stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_stream) (such as when using a [scrambler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scrambler_%28randomizer%29) in [telecommunications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telecommunications)).

::: {#o3 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Randomization is used in statistics and in gambling.

### Statistics {#Statistics}

Randomization is a core principle in [statistical theory](wikipedia://en/27579), whose importance was emphasized by [Charles S. Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) in "[Illustrations of the Logic of Science](wikipedia://en/3967710#illus)" (1877–1878) and "[A Theory of Probable Inference](wikipedia://en/3967710#SIL)" (1883). Randomization-based inference is especially important in [experimental design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Experimental_design) and in [survey sampling](wikipedia://en/27578). The first use of "randomization" listed in the *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)* is its use by [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) in 1926.[^o5][^o6]

#### Randomized experiments {#Randomized_experiments}

In the statistical theory of [design of experiments](wikipedia://en/9541), randomization involves [randomly allocating](wikipedia://en/2940730) the experimental units across the [treatment groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatment_groups). For example, if an experiment compares a new drug against a standard drug, then the patients should be allocated to either the new drug or to the standard drug control using randomization. Randomization reduces confounding by equalising so-called factors ([independent variables](wikipedia://en/437701)) that have not been accounted for in the experimental design.

#### Survey sampling {#Survey_sampling}

[Survey sampling](wikipedia://en/27578) uses randomization, following the criticisms of previous "representative methods" by [Jerzy Neyman](wikipedia://en/2386106) in his 1922 report to the [International Statistical Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Statistical_Institute).

#### Resampling {#Resampling}

Some important methods of statistical inference use [resampling](wikipedia://en/3763850) from the observed data. Multiple alternative versions of the data-set that "might have been observed" are created by randomization of the original data-set, the only one observed. The variation of statistics calculated for these alternative data-sets is a guide to the uncertainty of statistics estimated from the original data.

### Gambling {#Gambling}

Randomization is used extensively in the field of [gambling](wikipedia://en/11921). Because poor randomization may allow a skilled gambler to take advantage, much research has been devoted to effective randomization. A classic example of randomizing is [shuffling playing cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffle).

:::

## Techniques {#Techniques}

Although historically "manual" randomization techniques (such as shuffling cards, drawing pieces of paper from a bag, spinning a [roulette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roulette) wheel) were common, nowadays automated techniques are mostly used. As both selecting [random samples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_sample) and [random permutations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_permutation) can be reduced to simply selecting random numbers, [random number generation](wikipedia://en/2736939) methods are now most commonly used, both [hardware random number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardware_random_number_generator) and [pseudo-random number generators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-random_number_generator).

### Optimization {#Optimization}

Randomization is used in optimization to alleviate the computational burden associated to robust control techniques: a sample of values of the uncertainty parameters is randomly drawn and robustness is enforced for these values only. This approach has gained popularity by the introduction of rigorous theories that permit one to have control on the probabilistic level of robustness, see [scenario optimization](wikipedia://en/24686102).

Non-algorithmic randomization methods include:

- Casting [yarrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yarrow) stalks (for the [I Ching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I_Ching))
- Throwing [dice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice)
- Flipping a coin
- Drawing straws
- [Shuffling cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffle)
- [Roulette](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roulette) wheels
- Drawing pieces of paper or balls from a bag
- "[Lottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lottery) machines"
- Observing [atomic decay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_decay) using a radiation counter
