# Topological ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Topological ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **topological ring** is a [ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29) $R$ that is also a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) such that both the addition and the multiplication are [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28topology%29) as maps[^n1] $R \times R \to R$ where $R \times R$ carries the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910). This means $R$ is an additive [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) and a multiplicative [topological semigroup](wikipedia://en/17359213).

:::

Topological rings are fundamentally related to [topological fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_field) and arise naturally while studying them, since for example the completion of a topological field may be a topological ring which is not a [field](wikipedia://en/10603).[^n2]

## General comments {#General_comments}

The [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) $R^\times$ of a topological ring $R$ is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) when endowed with the topology coming from the [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119#General_topology) of $R^\times$ into the product $R \times R$ as $\left(x, x^{-1}\right).$ However, if the unit group is endowed with the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) as a subspace of $R,$ it may not be a topological group, because inversion on $R^\times$ need not be continuous with respect to the subspace topology. An example of this situation is the [adele ring](wikipedia://en/247978) of a [global field](wikipedia://en/288358); its unit group, called the [idele group](wikipedia://en/1168614), is not a topological group in the subspace topology. If inversion on $R^\times$ is continuous in the subspace topology of $R$ then these two topologies on $R^\times$ are the same.

If one does not require a ring to have a unit, then one has to add the requirement of continuity of the additive inverse, or equivalently, to define the topological ring as a ring that is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) (with respect to addition) in which multiplication is continuous, too.

## Examples {#Examples}

Topological rings occur in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), for example as rings of continuous real-valued [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on some topological space (where the topology is given by pointwise convergence), or as rings of continuous [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) on some [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538); all [Banach algebras](wikipedia://en/4665) are topological rings. The [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024), [real](wikipedia://en/20646438), [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) and [$p$-adic](wikipedia://en/51423) numbers are also topological rings (even topological fields, see below) with their standard topologies. In the plane, [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) and [dual numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_numbers) form alternative topological rings. See [hypercomplex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercomplex_numbers) for other low-dimensional examples.

In [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), the following construction is common: given an [ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_%28ring%29) $I$ in a [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) ring $R,$ the [$I$-adic topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adic_topology) on $R$ is defined as follows: a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $U$ of $R$ is open [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) for every $x \in U$ there exists a natural number $n$ such that $x + I^n \subseteq U.$ This turns $R$ into a topological ring. The $I$-adic topology is [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) if and only if the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all powers of $I$ is the zero ideal $(0).$

The $p$-adic topology on the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) is an example of an $I$-adic topology (with $I = p\mathbb{Z}$).

## Completion {#Completion}

Every topological ring is a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) (with respect to addition) and hence a [uniform space](wikipedia://en/32339) in a natural manner. One can thus ask whether a given topological ring $R$ is [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_uniform_space). If it is not, then it can be *completed*: one can find an essentially unique complete topological ring $S$ that contains $R$ as a [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29) [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) such that the given topology on $R$ equals the [subspace topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) arising from $S.$ If the starting ring $R$ is metric, the ring $S$ can be constructed as a set of equivalence classes of [Cauchy sequences](wikipedia://en/6085) in $R,$ this equivalence relation makes the ring $S$ Hausdorff and using constant sequences (which are Cauchy) one realizes a (uniformly) continuous morphism (CM in the sequel) $c : R \to S$ such that, for all CM $f : R \to T$ where $T$ is Hausdorff and complete, there exists a unique CM $g : S \to T$ such that $f = g \circ c.$ If $R$ is not metric (as, for instance, the ring of all real-variable rational valued functions, that is, all functions $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{Q}$ endowed with the topology of pointwise convergence) the standard construction uses minimal Cauchy filters and satisfies the same universal property as above (see [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394), General Topology, III.6.5).

The rings of [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) and the [$p$-adic integers](wikipedia://en/51423) are most naturally defined as completions of certain topological rings carrying [$I$-adic topologies](wikipedia://en/60123).

:::

## Topological fields {#Topological_fields}

Some of the most important examples are [topological fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_field). A topological field is a topological ring that is also a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), and such that [inversion](wikipedia://en/229940) of non zero elements is a continuous function. The most common examples are the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) and all its [subfields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subfield_%28mathematics%29), and the [valued fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valued_field), which include the [$p$-adic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_field).
