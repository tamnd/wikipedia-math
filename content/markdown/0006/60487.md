# Abscissa and ordinate

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abscissa and ordinate"}

*Image: Cartesian-coordinate-system.svg.* Cartesian plane with marked points (signed ordered pairs of coordinates). For any point, the *abscissa* is the first value (x coordinate), and the *ordinate* is the second value (y coordinate).

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **abscissa** (; plural *abscissae* or *abscissas*) and the **ordinate** are respectively the first and second [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) of a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) in a [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706):[^o9][^o2]

:::

**abscissa** $\equiv x$-axis (horizontal) coordinate

**ordinate** $\equiv y$-axis (vertical) coordinate

Together they form an [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362) which defines the location of a point in two-dimensional [rectangular space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_coordinate_system).

More technically, the abscissa of a point is the signed measure of its projection on the primary axis. Its [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) is the distance between the projection and the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) of the axis, and its [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270) is given by the location on the projection relative to the origin (before: negative; after: positive). Similarly, the ordinate of a point is the signed measure of its projection on the secondary axis. [In three dimensions](wikipedia://en/7706), the third direction is sometimes referred to as the *applicate*.[^o3]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

Though the word "abscissa" (la) has been used at least since *De Practica Geometrie* (1220) by [Fibonacci](wikipedia://en/17949) (Leonardo of Pisa), its use in its modern sense may be due to Venetian mathematician [Stefano degli Angeli](wikipedia://en/46666300) in his work *Miscellaneum Hyperbolicum, et Parabolicum* (1659).[^o5] Historically, the term was used in the more general sense of a 'distance'.[^o6]

In his 1892 work *Vorlesungen über die Geschichte der Mathematik* ("*Lectures on history of mathematics*"), volume 2, German [historian of mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220) [Moritz Cantor](wikipedia://en/307975) writes:

> Gleichwohl ist durch \[Stefano degli Angeli\] vermuthlich ein Wort in den mathematischen Sprachschatz eingeführt worden, welches gerade in der analytischen Geometrie sich als zukunftsreich bewährt hat. \[…\] Wir kennen keine ältere Benutzung des Wortes Abscisse in lateinischen Originalschriften. Vielleicht kommt das Wort in Uebersetzungen der [Apollonischen Kegelschnitte](wikipedia://en/257242) vor, wo Buch I Satz 20 von ἀποτεμνομέναις die Rede ist, wofür es kaum ein entsprechenderes lateinisches Wort als abscissa geben möchte.[^o7] At the same time it was presumably by \[Stefano degli Angeli\] that a word was introduced into the mathematical vocabulary for which especially in analytic geometry the future proved to have much in store. \[…\] We know of no earlier use of the word *abscissa* in Latin original texts. Maybe the word appears in translations of the [Apollonian conics](wikipedia://en/257242), where \[in\] Book I, Chapter 20 there is mention of *ἀποτεμνομέναις,* for which there would hardly be a more appropriate Latin word than abscissa.

The use of the word *ordinate* is related to the Latin phrase *linea ordinata appliicata* 'line applied parallel'.

## In parametric equations {#In_parametric_equations}

In a somewhat obsolete variant usage, the abscissa of a point may also refer to any number that describes the point's location along some path, e.g. the parameter of a [parametric equation](wikipedia://en/576108).[^o9] Used in this way, the abscissa can be thought of as a coordinate-geometry analog to the [independent variable](wikipedia://en/437701) in a [mathematical model](wikipedia://en/20590) or experiment (with any ordinates filling a role analogous to [dependent variables](wikipedia://en/437701)).
