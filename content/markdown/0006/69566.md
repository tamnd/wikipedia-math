# Euler's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euler's theorem"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Euler's theorem** (also known as the **Fermat–Euler theorem** or **Euler's totient theorem**) states that, if $n$ and $a$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) positive integers, then $a^{\varphi(n)}$ is congruent to $1$ [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $n$, where $\varphi$ denotes [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452); that is

:::

$$a^{\varphi (n)} \equiv 1 \pmod{n}.$$

In 1736, [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) published a proof of [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453)[^o2] (stated by [Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) without proof), which is the restriction of Euler's theorem to the case where $n$ is a prime number. Subsequently, Euler presented other proofs of the theorem, culminating with his paper of 1763, in which he proved a generalization to the case where $n$ is not prime.[^o3]

The converse of Euler's theorem is also true: if the above congruence is true, then $a$ and $n$ must be coprime.

The theorem is further generalized by some of [Carmichael's theorems](wikipedia://en/1181756#Carmichael's_theorems).

The theorem may be used to easily reduce large powers modulo $n$. For example, consider finding the ones place decimal digit of $7^{222}$, i.e. $7^{222} \pmod{10}$. The integers 7 and 10 are coprime, and $\varphi(10) = 4$. So Euler's theorem yields $7^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{10}$, and we get $7^{222} \equiv 7^{4 \times 55 + 2} \equiv (7^4)^{55} \times 7^2 \equiv 1^{55} \times 7^2 \equiv 49 \equiv 9 \pmod{10}$.

In general, when reducing a power of $a$ modulo $n$ (where $a$ and $n$ are coprime), one needs to work modulo $\varphi(n)$ in the exponent of $a$:

if $x \equiv y \pmod{\varphi(n)}$, then $a^x \equiv a^y \pmod{n}$.

Euler's theorem underlies the [RSA cryptosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28cryptosystem%29), which is widely used in [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) communications. In this cryptosystem, Euler's theorem is used with $n$ being a product of two large [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), and the security of the system is based on the difficulty of [factoring](wikipedia://en/15491) such an integer.

## Proofs {#Proofs}

\1. Euler's theorem can be proven using concepts from the [theory of groups](wikipedia://en/19447):[^o6] The residue classes modulo $n$ that are coprime to $n$ form a group under multiplication (see the article [Multiplicative group of integers modulo *n*](wikipedia://en/1195234) for details). The [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of that group is $\varphi(n)$. [Lagrange's theorem](wikipedia://en/31150) states that the order of any subgroup of a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) divides the order of the entire group, in this case $\varphi(n)$. If $a$ is any number [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to $n$ then $a$ is in one of these residue classes, and its powers $a, a^{2}, ... , a^{k}$ modulo $n$ form a subgroup of the group of residue classes, with $a^{k} \equiv 1\pmod{n}$. Lagrange's theorem says $k$ must divide $\varphi(n)$, i.e. there is an integer $M$ such that $kM = \varphi(n)$. This then implies,

$$a^{\varphi(n)} = a^{kM} = (a^{k})^M \equiv 1^M =1 \pmod{n}.$$

\2. There is also a direct proof:[^o7][^o8] Let $R = x_{1}, x_{2}, ... , x_{\varphi(n)}$ be a [reduced residue system](wikipedia://en/10299080) ($\bmod n$) and let $a$ be any integer coprime to $n$. The proof hinges on the fundamental fact that multiplication by $a$ permutes the $x_{i}$: in other words if $ax_{j} \equiv ax_{k}\pmod{n}$ then $j = k$. (This law of cancellation is proved in the article [Multiplicative group of integers modulo *n*](wikipedia://en/1195234#Group_axioms).[^o9]) That is, the sets $R$ and $aR = ax_{1}, ax_{2}, ... , ax_{\varphi(n)}$, considered as sets of congruence classes ($\bmod n$), are identical (as sets—they may be listed in different orders), so the product of all the numbers in $R$ is congruent ($\bmod n$) to the product of all the numbers in $aR$:

$\prod_{i=1}^{\varphi(n)} x_i \equiv \prod_{i=1}^{\varphi(n)} ax_i = a^{\varphi(n)}\prod_{i=1}^{\varphi(n)} x_i \pmod{n},$ and using the cancellation law to cancel each $x_{i}$ gives Euler's theorem:

$$a^{\varphi(n)}\equiv 1 \pmod{n}.$$

:::
