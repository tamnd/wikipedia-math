::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ruler"}

A **ruler** is an instrument used to make [length measurements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_measurement), whereby a length is read from a series of markings called "rules" along an edge of the device.[^o2] Alternatively, it is called a **rule**, **scale**, **line gauge**, or **metre/meter stick**. Usually, the instrument is rigid and the edge itself is a [straightedge](wikipedia://en/147854) ("ruled straightedge"), which additionally allows one to draw straighter lines. Rulers are an important tool in geometry, geography and mathematics. They have been used since at least 2650 BC.

:::

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variants"}

## Variants {#Variants}

Rulers have long been made from different materials and in multiple sizes. Historically, they were mainly [wood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood) but [plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastics) have also been used. They can be created with length markings instead of being scribed. Metal is also used for more durable rulers for use in the workshop; sometimes a raised metal edge is embedded into a wooden desk ruler to preserve the edge when used for straight-line cutting and to prevent ink from feathering under the ruler when used to draw a straight line with a pen. Typically 12 in length, though some can go up to 100, it is useful for a ruler to be on a desk or workstation to help in drawing. Shorter rulers are convenient for keeping in a pocket.[^o5] Longer rulers (e.g. 18) are necessary in some cases, some examples being the [yardsticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yardstick) and [meter sticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meter_stick). Historically, long [measuring rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measuring_rod) were used for larger projects, now superseded by the [tape measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tape_measure), [the surveyor's wheel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveyor's_wheel) or laser [rangefinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rangefinding_telemeter).

:::

## Use in geometry {#Use_in_geometry}

In geometry, a straightedge (ruler without any rules on it) can be used to draw straight lines between points. A ruler is also used to draw accurate graphs and tables.

[Ruler and compass construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge) is a geometric construction method for producing lengths, [angles](wikipedia://en/1196), and other geometric figures using only a ruler and a [compass](wikipedia://en/492445). It is possible to bisect an angle into two equal parts with a ruler and compass. It is, however, impossible to do [angle trisection](wikipedia://en/91111), unless two marks are allowed on the ruler, allowing the problem to be solved via [neusis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neusis).

## History {#History}

*Image: Nippur-Elle (Top).jpg.* The Nippur cubit-rod, c. 2650 BC, in the [Archeological Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul_Archaeology_Museums) of [Istanbul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Istanbul), Turkey

*Image: MaryRose-carpentry tools1.jpg.* A wooden carpenter's rule and other tools found on board the 16th-century [carrack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrack) *[Mary Rose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Rose)*

In the [history of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_measurement) many distance units have been used which were based on the human body such as the [cubit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubit), [hand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_%28unit%29) and [foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_%28unit%29), and these units varied in length by era and location.[^o8] In the late 18th century, the [metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrication) came into use and has since been adopted to varying degrees in almost all countries around the world.

The oldest preserved measuring rod is a copper-alloy bar that dates from c. 2650 BC and was found by the German Assyriologist [Eckhard Unger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eckhard_Unger) while excavating at the [Sumerian city](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer) of [Nippur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nippur) (present-day [Iraq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iraq)).

Rulers made of [ivory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ivory) were in use by the [Indus Valley Civilization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indus_Valley_civilization) period prior to 1500 BC.[^o9] Excavations at [Lothal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lothal) (2400 BC) have yielded one such ruler calibrated to about 1/16 inch.[^o9] Ian Whitelaw holds that the [Mohenjo-Daro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohenjo-Daro) ruler is divided into units corresponding to \1.32 inch and these are marked out in decimal subdivisions with amazing accuracy, to within \0.005 in. Ancient bricks found throughout the region have dimensions that correspond to these units.[^o10]

[Anton Ullrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anton_Ullrich) invented the folding ruler in 1851. Frank Hunt later made the flexible ruler in 1902.[^o11]

## Curved and flexible rulers {#Curved_and_flexible_rulers}

The equivalent of a ruler for drawing or reproducing a smooth curve, where it takes the form of a rigid template, is known as a [French curve](wikipedia://en/387931). A flexible device that can be bent to the desired shape is known as a [flat spline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_spline), or (in its more modern incarnation) a *flexible curve*. Historically, a flexible [lead](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lead) rule used by [masons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonry) that could be bent to the curves of a [molding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molding_%28decorative%29) was known as a [lesbian rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesbian_rule).[^o13]

## Philosophy {#Philosophy}

[Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741) famously used rulers as an example in his discussion of [language games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language-game) in the *[Philosophical Investigations](wikipedia://en/23674)* (1953). He pointed out that the [standard meter bar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre#International_prototype_metre_bar) in Paris was the criterion against which all other rulers were determined to be one meter long. However, there was no analytical way to demonstrate that the standard meter bar itself was one meter long. It could only be asserted as one meter as part of a language game.
