# Genus (mathematics)

*Image: Double torus illustration.png.* A genus-2 surface

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Genus"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **genus** (: **genera**) has a few different, but closely related, meanings. Intuitively, the genus is the number of "holes" of a [surface](wikipedia://en/27865).[^n1] A [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) has genus 0, while a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) has genus 1.

:::

## Topology {#Topology}

### Orientable surfaces {#Orientable_surfaces}

*Image: Mug and Torus morph.gif.* The coffee cup and donut shown in this animation both have genus one.

The **genus** of a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233), [orientable surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable_surface) is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) representing the maximum number of cuttings along non-intersecting [simple closed curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_closed_curve) without rendering the resultant [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) disconnected.[^n2] It is equal to the number of [handles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Handle_%28mathematics%29) on it. Alternatively, it can be defined in terms of the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) $\chi$, via the relationship $\chi=2-2g$ for [closed surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_surface), where $g$ is the genus. For surfaces with $b$ [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) components, the equation reads $\chi=2-2g-b$.

In layman's terms, the genus is the number of "holes" an object has ("holes" interpreted in the sense of doughnut holes; a hollow sphere would be considered as having zero holes in this sense).[^o6] A [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) has 1 such hole, while a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) has 0. The green surface pictured above has 2 holes of the relevant sort.

For instance:

- The [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) $S^2$ and a [disc](wikipedia://en/329542) both have genus zero.
- A [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) has genus one, as does the surface of a coffee mug with a handle.

Explicit construction of [surfaces of the genus *g*](wikipedia://en/60481511) is given in the article on the [fundamental polygon](wikipedia://en/1668732).

::: {#o12 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Non-orientable genus"}

### Non-orientable surfaces {#Non-orientable_surfaces}

The **[non-orientable](wikipedia://en/187446) genus**, **demigenus**, or **Euler genus** of a connected, non-orientable closed surface is a positive integer representing the number of [cross-caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-cap) attached to a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). Alternatively, it can be defined for a closed surface in terms of the Euler characteristic χ, via the relationship χ = 2 − *k*, where *k* is the non-orientable genus.

For instance:

- A [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325) has a non-orientable genus 1.
- A [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412) has non-orientable genus 2.

:::

### Knot {#Knot}

The **[genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus_of_a_knot)** of a [knot](wikipedia://en/366808) *K* is defined as the minimal genus of all [Seifert surfaces](wikipedia://en/1343951) for *K*.[^o14] A Seifert surface of a knot is however a [manifold with boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_with_boundary), the boundary being the knot, i.e. [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071). The genus of such a surface is defined to be the genus of the two-manifold, which is obtained by gluing the unit disk along the boundary.

### Handlebody {#Handlebody}

The **genus** of a 3-dimensional [handlebody](wikipedia://en/864472) is an integer representing the maximum number of cuttings along embedded [disks](wikipedia://en/329542) without rendering the resultant manifold disconnected. It is equal to the number of handles on it.

For instance:

- A [ball](wikipedia://en/160556) has genus 0.
- A solid torus *D*<sup>2</sup> × *S*<sup>1</sup> has genus 1.

### Graph theory {#Graph_theory}

The **genus** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is the minimal integer *n* such that the graph can be drawn without crossing itself on a sphere with *n* handles (i.e. an oriented surface of the genus *n*). Thus, a planar graph has genus 0, because it can be drawn on a sphere without self-crossing.

The **non-orientable genus** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is the minimal integer *n* such that the graph can be drawn without crossing itself on a sphere with *n* cross-caps (i.e. a non-orientable surface of (non-orientable) genus *n*). (This number is also called the **demigenus**.)

The **Euler genus** is the minimal integer *n* such that the graph can be drawn without crossing itself on a sphere with *n* cross-caps or on a sphere with *n/2* handles.[^o17]

In [topological graph theory](wikipedia://en/1631772) there are several definitions of the genus of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). Arthur T. White introduced the following concept. The genus of a group *G* is the minimum genus of a (connected, undirected) [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) for *G*.

The [graph genus problem](wikipedia://en/8149170#Computational_complexity) is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete).[^o18]

## Algebraic geometry {#Algebraic_geometry}

There are two related definitions of **genus** of any [projective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_scheme) algebraic [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) $X$: the [arithmetic genus](wikipedia://en/1950390) and the [geometric genus](wikipedia://en/3115807).[^o21] When $X$ is an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) with [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of definition the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), and if $X$ has no [singular points](wikipedia://en/30807), then these definitions agree and coincide with the topological definition applied to the [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) of $X$ (its [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) of complex points). For example, the definition of [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225) from [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) is *connected non-singular projective curve of genus 1 with a given [rational point](wikipedia://en/2960200) on it*.

By the [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261#Applications), an irreducible plane curve of degree $d$ given by the vanishing locus of a section $s \in \Gamma(\mathbb{P}^2, \mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{P}^2}(d))$ of the [twisting sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twisting_sheaf_of_Serre) has geometric genus

$$g=\frac{(d-1)(d-2)}{2}-s,$$

where $s$ is the number of singularities when properly counted.

:::

## Differential geometry {#Differential_geometry}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a genus of an [oriented manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_manifold) $M$ may be defined as a complex number $\Phi(M)$ subject to the conditions

- $$\Phi(M_{1}\amalg M_{2})=\Phi(M_{1})+\Phi(M_{2})$$
- $$\Phi(M_{1}\times M_{2})=\Phi(M_{1})\cdot \Phi(M_{2})$$
- $\Phi(M_{1})=\Phi(M_{2})$ if $M_{1}$ and $M_{2}$ are [cobordant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cobordant).

In other words, $\Phi$ is a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) $R\to\mathbb{C}$, where $R$ is Thom's [oriented cobordism ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_cobordism_ring).[^o23]

The genus $\Phi$ is multiplicative for all bundles on [spinor](wikipedia://en/29276) manifolds with a connected compact structure if $\log_{\Phi}$ is an [elliptic integral](wikipedia://en/9960) such as $\log_{\Phi}(x)=\int^{x}_{0}(1-2\delta t^{2}+\varepsilon t^{4})^{-1/2}dt$ for some $\delta,\varepsilon\in\mathbb{C}.$ This genus is called an elliptic genus.

The Euler characteristic $\chi(M)$ is not a genus in this sense since it is not invariant concerning cobordisms.

## Biology {#Biology}

Genus can be also calculated for the graph spanned by the net of chemical interactions in [nucleic acids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleic_acid) or [proteins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein). In particular, one may study the growth of the genus along the chain. Such a function (called the genus trace) shows the topological complexity and domain structure of biomolecules.[^o25]
