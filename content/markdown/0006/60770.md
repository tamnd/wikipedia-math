# Curvature

*Image: Cell-Shape-Dynamics-From-Waves-to-Migration-pcbi.1002392.s007.ogv.* A migrating wild-type *[Dictyostelium discoideum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictyostelium_discoideum)* cell whose boundary is colored by curvature. Scale bar: 5 μm.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Curvature"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **curvature** is any of several strongly related concepts in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) that intuitively measure the amount by which a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) deviates from being a [straight line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Straight_line) or by which a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) deviates from being a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29). If a curve or surface is contained in a larger space, curvature can be defined *extrinsically* relative to the ambient space. [Curvature of Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/651361) of dimension at least two can be defined *intrinsically* without reference to a larger space.

:::

For curves, curvature describes how sharply the curve bends. The canonical examples are [circles](wikipedia://en/6220): smaller circles bend more sharply and hence have higher curvature. For a point on a general curve, the direction of the curve is described by its [tangent line](wikipedia://en/31482). How sharply the curve is bending at that point can be measured by how much that tangent line changes direction per unit distance along the curve.

Curvature measures the [angular](wikipedia://en/1196) rate of change of the direction of the tangent line, or the [unit tangent vector](wikipedia://en/666987#Definitions), of the curve per unit distance along the curve. Curvature is expressed in units of [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) per unit distance. For a circle, that rate of change is the same at all points on the circle and is equal to the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of the circle's [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815). Straight lines don't change direction and have zero curvature. The curvature at a point on a twice [differentiable curve](wikipedia://en/493403) is the [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301#Euclidean_vector_space) of its [curvature vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvature_vector) at that point and is also the curvature of its [osculating circle](wikipedia://en/1896705), which is the circle that best approximates the curve near that point.

For surfaces (and, more generally for higher-dimensional [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470)), that are [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the concept of curvature is more complex, as it depends on the choice of a direction on the surface or manifold. This leads to the concepts of *maximal curvature*, *[minimal curvature](wikipedia://en/276734)*, and *[mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210)*.

## History {#History}

The history of curvature began with the [ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece)' basic distinction between straight and circular lines, with the concept later developed by figures like [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) and [Apollonius](wikipedia://en/661433). The development of calculus in the 17th century, particularly by Newton and Leibniz, provided tools to systematically calculate curvature for curves. Euler then extended the study to surfaces, followed by Gauss's crucial insight of "intrinsic" curvature, which is independent of how a surface is embedded in space, and Riemann's generalization to higher dimensions.[^o3]

In *Tractatus de configurationibus qualitatum et motuum,*[^o4] the 14th-century philosopher and mathematician [Nicole Oresme](wikipedia://en/593659) introduces the concept of curvature as a measure of departure from straightness; for circles he has the curvature as being inversely proportional to the radius; and he attempts to extend this idea to other curves as a continuously varying magnitude.[^o5]

The curvature of a [differentiable curve](wikipedia://en/493403) was originally defined through [osculating circles](wikipedia://en/1896705). In this setting, [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) showed that the center of curvature is the intersection point of two infinitely close [normal lines](wikipedia://en/173224) to the curve.[^o6]

## Curves {#Curves}

Intuitively, curvature describes for any part of a curve how much the curve direction changes over a small distance along the curve. The direction of the curve at any point $\boldsymbol{P}$ is described by a unit tangent vector, $\boldsymbol{T}$. A section of a curve is also called an arc, and length along the curve is [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401), $s$. So the curvature for a small section of the curve is the angle of the change of the direction of the tangent vector divided by the arc length $\Delta s$. For a general curve which might have a varying curvature along its length, the curvature at a point $\boldsymbol{P}$ on the curve is the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602) of the curvature of sections containing $\boldsymbol{P}$ as the length of the sections approaches zero. For a twice differentiable curve, that limit is the magnitude of the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the unit tangent vector with respect to arc length. Using the lowercase Greek letter [kappa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kappa) to denote curvature:

$$\kappa = \left \| \frac {\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{T}} {\mathrm{d}s} \right \| .$$

Curvature is a [differential-geometric property](wikipedia://en/493403#Re-parametrization_and_equivalence_relation) of the curve; it does not depend on the parametrization of the curve. In particular, it does not depend on the orientation of the parametrized curve, i.e. which direction along the curve is associated with increasing parameter values.

### Arc-length parametrization {#Arc-length_parametrization}

A curve that is [parametrized by arc length](wikipedia://en/493403#Length_and_natural_parametrization) is a [vector-valued function](wikipedia://en/3675281) that is denoted by the Greek letter [gamma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma) with an overbar, $lh=40\%$, that describes the position of a point on the curve, $P$, in terms of its arc-length distance, $s$ along the curve from some other reference point on the curve. Thus for some interval $I = [a, b]$ in $\mathbb{R}$, $\operatorname{lh}=40\%: I \to \mathbb{R}^{n}$ with

$$\boldsymbol{P}(s) = \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}(s).$$

If $lh=40\%$ is a differentiable curve, then the first derivative of $lh=40\%$, $\operatorname{lh}=40\%'(s)$ is a unit tangent vector, $\boldsymbol{T}(s)$, and

$$\left\| \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}'(s) \right\| = 1$$

$$\boldsymbol{T}(s) = \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}'(s).$$

If $lh=40\%$ is twice differentiable, the second derivative of $lh=40\%$ is $\boldsymbol{T}'(s)$, which is also the curvature vector, $\boldsymbol{K}(s)$.

$$\boldsymbol{K}(s) = \boldsymbol{T}'(s) = \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}''(s)$$

Curvature is the magnitude of the second derivative of $lh=40\%$.

$$\kappa(s) = \left\| \boldsymbol{K}(s) \right\| = \left\| \boldsymbol{T}'(s) \right\| = \left\| \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}''(s) \right\|$$

The parameter $s$ can also be interpreted as a time parameter. Then $\operatorname{lh}=40\%(s)$ describes the path of a particle that moves along the curve at a constant unit speed. Curvature can then be understood as a measure of how fast the direction of the particle rotates.[^o9]

### General parametrization {#General_parametrization}

A twice differentiable curve, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}: [a, b] \to \mathbb{R}^{n}$, that is not parametrized by arc length can be re-parametrized by arc length provided that $\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)$ is everywhere not zero, so that $1/\lVert \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)\rVert$ is always a finite positive number.

The arc-length parameter, $s$, is defined by

$$s(t) ~{=}~ \int_a^t \left\| \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(x) \right\| \, \mathrm{d}{x},$$

which has an inverse function $t(s)$. The arc-length parametrization is the function $lh=40\%$ which is defined as

$$\boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}(s) ~{=}~ \boldsymbol{\gamma}(t(s)).$$

Both $\mathbf{\gamma}$ and $lh=40\%$ trace the same path in $\mathbb{R}^n$ and so have the same curvature vector and curvature at each point $\boldsymbol{P}$ on the curve. For a given $s$ and its corresponding $t = t(s)$, point $\boldsymbol{P}$ and its unit tangent vector, $\boldsymbol{T}$, curvature vector, $\boldsymbol{K}$, and curvature, $\kappa$, are:

$$\boldsymbol{P} = \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}(s) = \boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)$$

$$\boldsymbol{T} = \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}'(s) = \frac {\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)} {\left\| \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \right\| }$$

$$\boldsymbol{K} = \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}''(s) = \frac {\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)} {\left\| \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \right\|^2} - \boldsymbol{T} \left ( \boldsymbol{T} \cdot \frac {\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)} {\left\| \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \right\|^2} \right)$$

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa = \left\| \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}''(s) \right\| & = \frac {\sqrt{ \bigl\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)\bigr\|\vphantom{'}^2 \bigl\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)\bigr\|\vphantom{'}^2 - \bigl( \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t) \bigr) \vphantom{'}^2 } } {\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)\bigr\|\vphantom{'}^3} \\ \\ & = \frac {\left\| \boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t) \right\|} {\left\| \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \right\|^2} \sqrt{ 1- \left ( {\boldsymbol{T}} \cdot \frac {\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)} {\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)\bigr\|} \right ) ^2~ } ~~.\\ \end{aligned}$$

The curvature vector, $\boldsymbol{K}$, is the [perpendicular component](wikipedia://en/1508507) of $\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t) / \lVert \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)\rVert^{2}$ relative to the tangent vector $\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)$. This is also reflected in the second expression for the curvature: the expression inside the parentheses is $\cos \theta$, where $\theta$ is the angle between the vectors $\boldsymbol{T}$ and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)$, so that the square root produces $\sin \theta$.

If $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ is twice continuously differentiable, then so is $s(t)$ and $lh=40\%$, while $\boldsymbol{T}(t)$ is continuously differentiable, and $\boldsymbol{K}(t)$ and $\kappa(t)$ are continuous.

Often it is difficult or impossible to express the arc-length parametrization, $lh=40\%$, in [closed form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_form_expression) even when $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ is given in closed form. This is typically the case when it is difficult or impossible to express $s(t)$ or its inverse $t(s)$ in closed form. However curvature can be expressed only in terms of the first and second derivatives of $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, without direct reference to $lh=40\%$.

::: {#o12 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Curvature vector"}

### Curvature vector {#Curvature_vector}

The curvature vector, denoted with an upper-case $\boldsymbol{K}$, is the derivative of the unit tangent vector, $\boldsymbol{T}$, with respect to arc length, $s$:

$$\boldsymbol{K} = \frac {\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{T}} {\mathrm{d}s}.$$

The curvature vector represents both the direction towards which the curve is turning as well as how sharply it turns.

The curvature vector has the following properties:

- The magnitude of the curvature vector is the curvature: $$\kappa = \left\| \boldsymbol{K} \right\|.$$
- The curvature vector is perpendicular to the unit tangent vector $\boldsymbol{T}$, or in terms of the dot product: $$\boldsymbol{K} \cdot \boldsymbol{T} = 0~.$$
- The curvature vector is the second derivative of an arc-length parametrization $lh=40\%$: $$\boldsymbol{K}(s) = \boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}''(s).$$
- The curvature vector of a general parametrization, $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, is the [perpendicular component](wikipedia://en/1508507) of $\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t) / \lVert \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)\rVert^{2}$relative to the tangent vector $\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)$: $$\boldsymbol{K}(t) = \frac {\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)} {\left\| \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \right\|^2} - \boldsymbol{T} \left ( \boldsymbol{T} \cdot \frac {\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)} {\left\| \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \right\|^2} \right).$$ If the curve is in $\mathbb{R}^3$, then the curvature vector can also be expressed as: $$\boldsymbol{K}(t) = \boldsymbol{T} \times \frac {\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t)} {\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t)\right\|^2} \times \boldsymbol{T}$$ where × denotes the [vector cross product](wikipedia://en/157092).
- If the curvature vector is not zero:
  - The curvature vector points from the point on the curve, $\boldsymbol{P}$, in the direction of the center of the osculating circle.
  - The curvature vector and the tangent vector are perpendicular vectors that span the osculating plane, the plane containing the osculating circle.
  - The curvature vector scaled to unit length is the unit normal vector, $\boldsymbol{N}$: $$\boldsymbol{N} = \frac {K} {\left\| K \right\|}.$$
- The curvature vector is a [differential-geometric property](wikipedia://en/493403#Re-parametrization_and_equivalence_relation) of the curve at $\boldsymbol{P}$; it does not depend on how the curve is parametrized.

:::

### Osculating circle {#Osculating_circle}

*Image: Osculating.svg.*

Historically, the curvature of a differentiable curve was defined through the [osculating circle](wikipedia://en/1896705), which is the circle that best approximates the curve at a point. More precisely, given a point $\boldsymbol{P}$ on a curve, every other point $\boldsymbol{Q}$ of the curve defines a circle (or sometimes a line) passing through $\boldsymbol{Q}$ and [tangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_%28geometry%29) to the curve at $\boldsymbol{P}$. The osculating circle is the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602), if it exists, of this circle when $\boldsymbol{Q}$ tends to $\boldsymbol{P}$. Then the *center of curvature* and the *radius of curvature* of the curve at $\boldsymbol{P}$ are the center and the radius of the osculating circle.

The radius of curvature, $R$, is the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of the curvature,[^o14] provided that the curvature is not zero:

$$R = \frac{1}{\kappa}~.$$

For a curve $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, since a non-zero curvature vector, $\boldsymbol{K}(t)$, points from the point $\boldsymbol{P} = \boldsymbol{\gamma}(t)$ towards the center of curvature, but the magnitude of $\boldsymbol{K}(t)$ is the curvature, $\kappa(t)$, the center of curvature, $\boldsymbol{C}(t)$ is

$$\boldsymbol{C}(t) = \boldsymbol{\gamma}(t) + \frac {\boldsymbol{K}(t)} {\kappa(t)^2}~.$$

When the curvature is zero, for example on a straight line or at a point of inflection, the radius of curvature is infinite and the center of curvature is indeterminate or "at infinity".

### Curvature from arc and chord length {#Curvature_from_arc_and_chord_length}

Given two points $\boldsymbol{P}$ and $\boldsymbol{Q}$ on a curve $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$, let $s(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})$ be the arc length of the portion of the curve between $\boldsymbol{P}$ and $\boldsymbol{Q}$ and let $d(\mathbf{P},\mathbf{Q})$ denote the length of the line segment from $\boldsymbol{P}$ to $\boldsymbol{Q}$. The curvature of $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ at $\boldsymbol{P}$ is given by the limit

$$\kappa(\boldsymbol{P}) = \lim_{\boldsymbol{Q}\to \boldsymbol{P}}\sqrt\frac{24\bigl(s(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{Q})-d(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{Q})\bigr)}{s(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{Q})\vphantom{Q}^3}~,$$

where the limit is taken as the point $\boldsymbol{Q}$ approaches $\boldsymbol{P}$ on $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$. The denominator can equally well be taken to be $d(\boldsymbol{P},\boldsymbol{Q})^{3}$. The formula is valid in any dimension. The formula follows by verifying it for the osculating circle.

### Exceptional cases {#Exceptional_cases}

There may be some situations where the preconditions for the above formulas do not apply, but where it is still appropriate to apply the concept of curvature.

It can be useful to apply the concept of curvature to a curve $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ at a point $\boldsymbol{P} = \boldsymbol{\gamma}(t_{0})$ if the [one-sided derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-differentiable) for $\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t_{0})$ exist but are different values, or likewise for $\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t_{0})$. In such a case, it could be useful to describe the curve with curvature at each side. Such might be the case of a curve that is [constructed piecewise](wikipedia://en/404130).

Another situation occurs when the limit of a ratio results in an indeterminate $0 / 0$ value for the curvature, for example when both derivatives exist but are both zero. In such a case, it might be possible to evaluate the underlying limit using [l'Hôpital's rule](wikipedia://en/18531).

### Examples {#Examples}

The following are examples of curves with application of the relevant concepts and formulas.

#### Circle {#Circle}

*Image: Circle-tangent-angle-over-arc-length.svg.* For an arc on a circle of radius R, the ratio of the size of the angle between the arc endpoint tangents, measured in radians, divided by the arc length L is (L/R)/L = 1/R.

A geometric explanation for why the curvature of a circle of radius $R$ at any point $\boldsymbol{P}$ is $1/R$ is partially illustrated by the diagram to the right.

The length of the red arc is $L$ and the measure in radians of the arc's central angle, angle ACB, is $L/R$. The angle between the arc endpoint tangents is angle BDE, which is the same size as the central angle, because both angles are supplementary to angle BDA.

The ratio of the angle between the arc endpoint tangents, measured in radians, divided by the arc length $L$ is $(L/R)/L = 1/R$.

Since the ratio is $1/R$ for any arc of the circle that is less than a half circle, for arcs containing any given point $\boldsymbol{P}$ on the circle, the limit of the ratio as arc length approaches zero is also $1/R$. Hence the curvature of the circle at any point $\boldsymbol{P}$ is $1/R$.

A common parametrization of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) of radius $r$ is $\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t) = (r \cos t, r \sin t)$. Then

$$\begin{array}{lll} \boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) = (-r~\mathrm{sin}~t, r~\mathrm{cos}~t) & \qquad & \left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \right\| = r \\ \boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t) = (-r~\mathrm{cos}~t, -r~\mathrm{sin}~t) & \qquad & \left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t) \right\| = r \\\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t) \cdot \boldsymbol{\gamma}''(t) = 0 ~.\\ \end{array}$$

The general formula for curvature gives

$$\kappa(t)= \frac{\sqrt{r^2\, r^2 - 0^2\,}} {r^3} = \frac 1r~.$$

and the formula for a plane curve gives

$$\kappa(t)= \frac{r^2\sin^2 t + r^2\cos^2 t}{\bigl(r^2\cos^2 t+r^2\sin^2 t\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}} = \frac 1r.$$

It follows, as expected, that the radius of curvature is the radius of the circle, and that the center of curvature is the center of the circle.

The circle is a rare case where the arc-length parametrization is easy to compute, as it is

$$\boldsymbol\bar\gamma(s)= \left(r\cos \frac sr,\, r\sin \frac sr\right).$$

It is an arc-length parametrization, since the norm of

$$\boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}'(s) = \left(-\sin \frac sr,\, \cos \frac sr\right)$$

is equal to one. Then

$$\kappa(s) = \left\|\boldsymbol{\bar\gamma}''(s)\right\| = \left\|\left(-\frac 1r\cos \frac sr,\, -\frac 1r \sin \frac sr\right)\right\| = \frac 1r$$

gives the same value for the curvature.

The same circle can also be defined by the implicit equation $F(x, y) = 0$ with $F(x, y) = x^{2} + y^{2} - r^{2}$. Then, the formula for the curvature in this case gives

$$\begin{aligned} \kappa &= \frac{\left|F_y^2F_{xx}-2F_xF_yF_{xy}+F_x^2F_{yy}\right|}{\bigl(F_x^2+F_y^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}}\\ &=\frac{8y^2 + 8x^2}{\bigl(4x^2+4y^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}}\\ &=\frac {8r^2}{\bigl(4r^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}} =\frac1r.\end{aligned}$$

#### Parabola {#Parabola}

*Image: Parabola-curvature-comb.svg.* Parabola y = x<sup>2</sup>, its curvature, its comb (see below) with scaling factor = 1, and the osculating circle at x = 0.

Consider the [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) $y = ax^{2} + bx + c$.

It is the graph of a function, with derivative $2ax + b$, and second derivative $2a$. So, the signed curvature is

$$k(x)=\frac{2a}{ \bigl(1+\left(2ax+b\right)^2\bigr)\vphantom{)}^{3/2}}~.$$

It has the sign of $a$ for all values of $x$. This means that, if $a > 0$, the concavity is upward directed everywhere; if $a < 0$, the concavity is downward directed; for $a = 0$, the curvature is zero everywhere, confirming that the parabola degenerates into a line in this case.

The (unsigned) curvature is maximal for $x = -\tfrac{b}{2a}$, that is at the [stationary point](wikipedia://en/885651) (zero derivative) of the function, which is the [vertex](wikipedia://en/6865890) of the parabola.

Consider the parametrization $\mathbf{\gamma}(t) = (t, at^{2} + bt + c) = (x, y)$. The first derivative of $x$ is $1$, and the second derivative is zero. Substituting into the formula for general parametrizations gives exactly the same result as above, with $x$ replaced by $t$ and with primes referring to derivatives with respect to the parameter $t$.

The same parabola can also be defined by the implicit equation $F(x, y) = 0$ with $F(x, y) = ax^{2} + bx + c - y$. As $F_{y} = -1$, and $F_{yy} = F_{xy} = 0$, one obtains exactly the same value for the (unsigned) curvature. However, the signed curvature is not defined for an implicit equation since the signed curvature depends on an orientation of the curve that is not provided by the implicit equation.

:::

### Plane curves {#Plane_curves}

Let $\boldsymbol{\gamma}(t) = (x(t), y(t))$ be a proper [parametric representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_representation) of a twice differentiable plane curve. Here *proper* means that on the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of definition of the parametrization, the derivative $\tfrac{d\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{dt}$ exists and is nowhere equal to the zero vector.

The curvature $\kappa$ of a plane curve can be expressed in ways that are specific to two dimensions, such as

$$\kappa = \frac{\left|x'y''-y'x''\right|}{\bigl({x'}^2+{y'}^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}},$$

where primes refer to derivatives with respect to $t$.

This can be expressed in a [coordinate-free](wikipedia://en/18858579) way as

$$\kappa = \frac{\left|\det\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}',\boldsymbol{\gamma}''\right)\right|}{\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}'\|^3},$$

where the numerator is the absolute value of the determinant of the 2-by-2 matrix with $\boldsymbol{\gamma}'$ and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}''$ as the columns.

These formulas can be understood as an application of the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) formula for curvature in three dimensions. Since the operands have zeros in the third dimension, the cross product result will have zero values for the first two dimensions, so only the value in the third dimension is relevant to calculating the magnitude of the cross product. The formula for the value of the third dimension thus appears in the numerator of the above formulas.

#### Signed curvature {#Signed_curvature}

For plane curves, it can be useful to express the curvature as a single scalar that can be positive or negative, called the **signed curvature** or **oriented curvature** and denoted with a lowercase k. The signed curvature formulas are similar to those for $\kappa$ except that they omit taking the absolute value of the numerator:

$$k = \frac{x'y''-y'x''}{\bigl({x'}^2+{y'}^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}} = \frac{\det\left(\boldsymbol{\gamma}',\boldsymbol{\gamma}''\right)}{\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}'\|^3}~.$$

Then $k = \pm \kappa$. Whether $k$ is positive or negative depends on the orientation of the curve. Whether a positive $k$ corresponds to [clockwise or counterclockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clockwise) turning depends on the orientation of the curve and the [orientation of the coordinate axes](wikipedia://en/7706#Orientation_and_handedness). With a [standard orientation of the coordinate axes](wikipedia://en/7706#In_two_dimensions), when moving along the curve in the direction of increasing $t$, $k$ is positive if the curve turns to the left, counterclockwise, and it is negative if the curve turns to the right, clockwise. This is consistent with the convention of treating counterclockwise rotations as rotations through a [positive angle](wikipedia://en/1196#Signed_angles). However, since the sign of $k$ is dependent on the orientation of the parametrization, $k$ is not [differential-geometric property](wikipedia://en/493403#Re-parametrization_and_equivalence_relation) property of the curve.

Except for orientation issues, the signed curvature for a plane curve captures similar information as the curvature vector, which for a plane curve is constrained to just one dimension, the line that is perpendicular to the unit tangent vector.

Using a standard orientation of the coordinate axes, let $lh=70\%$ be the [unit normal vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_normal_vector) obtained from the unit tangent vector, $\boldsymbol{T}$, by a counterclockwise rotation of $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$. Then $lh=70\%$ is dependent on the orientation of the curve and points to the left when moving along the curve in the direction of increasing $t$. However the curvature vector, $\boldsymbol{K}$ is equal to the product of the signed curvature, $k$ and $lh=70\%$, because their orientation dependencies cancel:

$$\boldsymbol{K} = k\,\boldsymbol{\bar N}.$$

Similarly, the center of curvature can be expressed using the signed curvature and $lh=70\%$:

$$\boldsymbol{C}(s)= \boldsymbol{\gamma}(s) + \frac{\mathbf{\bar N}(s)}{k(s)}.$$

#### Graph of a function {#Graph_of_a_function}

The [graph of a function](wikipedia://en/87352) $y = f(x)$, is a special case of a parametrized curve, of the form

$$\begin{aligned} x&=t\\ y&=f(t). \end{aligned}$$

As the first and second derivatives of $x$ are 1 and 0, previous formulas simplify to

$$\kappa = \frac{\left|y''\right|}{\bigl(1+{y'}^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}}$$

for the curvature and to

$$k = \frac{y''}{\bigl(1+{y'}^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}}$$

for the signed curvature.

In the general case of a curve, the sign of the signed curvature is somewhat arbitrary, as it depends on the orientation of the curve. In the case of the graph of a function, there is a natural orientation by increasing values of $x$. This gives additional significance to the sign of the signed curvature.

The sign of the signed curvature is the same as the sign of the second derivative of $f$. If it is positive then the graph has an upward concavity, and, if it is negative the graph has a downward concavity. If it is zero, then one has an [inflection point](wikipedia://en/379845) or an [undulation point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undulation_point).

When the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368) of the graph (that is the derivative of the function) is small, the signed curvature is well approximated by the second derivative. More precisely, using [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578), one has

$$k(x)=y'' \Bigl(1 + O\bigl({\textstyle y'}^2\bigr) \Bigr).$$

It is common in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) to approximate the curvature with the second derivative, for example, in [beam theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_theory) or for deriving the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) of a string under tension, and other applications where small slopes are involved. This often allows systems that are otherwise [nonlinear](wikipedia://en/146103) to be treated approximately as linear.

#### Implicit curve {#Implicit_curve}

For a curve defined by an [implicit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation) $F(x, y) = 0$ with [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives) denoted $F_{x}$ , $F_{y}$ , $F_{xx}$ , $F_{xy}$ , $F_{yy}$ , the curvature is given by[^o25]

$$\kappa = \frac{\left|F_y^2F_{xx}-2F_xF_yF_{xy}+F_x^2F_{yy}\right|}{\bigl(F_x^2+F_y^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}}.$$

The signed curvature is not defined, as it depends on an orientation of the curve that is not provided by the implicit equation. Note that changing $F$ into $-F$ would not change the curve defined by $F(x, y) = 0$, but it would change the sign of the numerator if the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) were omitted in the preceding formula.

A point of the curve where $F_{x} = F_{y} = 0$ is a [singular point](wikipedia://en/4230456), which means that the curve is not differentiable at this point, and thus that the curvature is not defined (most often, the point is either a crossing point or a [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758)).

The above formula for the curvature can be derived from the expression of the curvature of the graph of a function by using the [implicit function theorem](wikipedia://en/581005) and the fact that, on such a curve, one has

$$\frac {dy}{dx}=-\frac{F_x}{F_y}.$$

#### Polar coordinates {#Polar_coordinates}

If a curve is defined in [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) by the radius expressed as a function of the polar angle, that is $r$ is a function of $\theta$, then its curvature is

$$\kappa(\theta) = \frac{\left|r^2 + 2{r'}^2 - r\, r''\right|}{\bigl(r^2+{r'}^2 \bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}},$$

where the prime refers to differentiation with respect to $\theta$.

This results from the formula for general parametrizations, by considering the parametrization

$$\begin{aligned} x&=r\cos \theta\\ y&=r\sin \theta. \end{aligned}$$

#### Curvature comb {#Curvature_comb}

*Image: Curvature comb.png.* Curvature comb

A *curvature comb*[^o28] can be used to represent graphically the curvature of every point on a curve. If $\boldsymbol{\gamma}$ is a curve parametrized $t$, its comb is defined as the parametrized curve defined by

$$\mathrm{Comb}(t) = \boldsymbol{\gamma}(t) - s\,\kappa(t)\boldsymbol{N}(t),$$

where $\kappa$ is the curvature, $\boldsymbol{N}$ is the unit normal vector that points toward the center of curvature, and $s$ is a scaling factor that is chosen to enhance the graphical representation.

Curvature combs are useful when combining two different curves in CAD environments. They provide a visual representation of the continuity between the curves. The continuity can be defined as being in one of four levels.

G0 : The 2 curvature combs are at an angle at the junction.

G1 : The teeth of the 2 combs are parallel at the junction but are of different length.

G2 : The teeth are parallel and of the same length. However the tangents of the 2 combs are not the same.

G3 : The teeth are parallel and of the same length and the tangents of the 2 combs are the same.

The above image shows a G2 continuity at the 2 junctions.

::: {#o30 .statement cue=named-heading name="Frenet–Serret formulas for plane curves"}

#### Frenet–Serret formulas for plane curves {#Frenet–Serret_formulas_for_plane_curves}

*Image: FrenetTN.svg.* The vectors $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{N}$ at two points on a plane curve, a translated version of the second frame (dotted), and $\delta\mathbf{T}$ the change in $\mathbf{T}$. Here $\delta s$ is the distance between the points. In the limit $\tfrac{d\mathbf{T}}{ds}$ will be in the direction $\mathbf{N}$. The curvature describes the rate of rotation of the frame.

The [first Frenet–Serret formula](wikipedia://en/666987) relates the unit tangent vector, curvature, and the normal vector of an [arc-length parametrization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc-length_parametrization)

$$\mathbf T'(s) = \kappa(s) \mathbf N(s),$$

where the primes refer to the derivatives with respect to the arc length $s$, and $\mathbf{N}(s)$ is the normal unit vector in the direction of $\mathbf{T}(s)$.

As planar curves have zero [torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_of_curves), the second Frenet–Serret formula provides the relation

$$\begin{aligned} \frac {d\mathbf{N}}{ds} &= -\kappa\mathbf{T},\\ &= -\kappa\frac{d\boldsymbol{\gamma}}{ds}. \end{aligned}$$

For a general parametrization by a parameter $t$, one needs expressions involving derivatives with respect to $t$. As these are obtained by multiplying by $\tfrac{ds}{dt}$ the derivatives with respect to $s$, one has, for any proper parametrization

$$\mathbf{N}'(t) = -\kappa(t)\boldsymbol{\gamma}'(t).$$

:::

### Curves in three dimensions {#Curves_in_three_dimensions}

*Image: Torus-Knot uebereinander animated.gif.* Animation of the curvature and the acceleration vector $'T'(s)$

For a parametrically defined curve in three dimensions given in Cartesian coordinates by $\mathbf{\gamma}(t) = (x(t), y(t), z(t))$, the curvature is

$$\kappa=\frac{\sqrt{\bigl(z''y'-y''z'\bigr)\vphantom{'}^2+\bigl(x''z'-z''x'\bigr)\vphantom{'}^2+\bigl(y''x'-x''y'\bigr)\vphantom{'}^2}} {\bigl({x'}^2+{y'}^2+{z'}^2\bigr)\vphantom{'}^{3/2}},$$

where the prime denotes differentiation with respect to the parameter $t$. Both the curvature[^o32] and the curvature vector can be expressed using the [vector cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) and the unit tangent vector $\boldsymbol{T}$:

$$\boldsymbol{K} = \boldsymbol{T} \times \frac {\boldsymbol{\gamma}''} {\left\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}'\right\|^2} \times \boldsymbol{T}$$

$$\kappa = \frac{\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}' \times \boldsymbol{\gamma}''\bigr\|}{\bigl\|\boldsymbol{\gamma}'\bigr\|\vphantom{'}^3}~.$$

These formulas are related to the general formulas for curvature and the curvature vector, except that they use the vector cross product instead of the scalar dot product to express the perpendicular component of $\boldsymbol{\gamma}'' / \lVert \boldsymbol{\gamma}'\rVert\hat{}2$ relative to $\boldsymbol{\gamma}'$.

## Surfaces {#Surfaces}

Differential geometry of surfaces The curvature of curves drawn on a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) is the main tool for the defining and studying the curvature of the surface.

::: {#o35 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Curvature of curves on surfaces"}

### Curves on surfaces {#Curves_on_surfaces}

For a curve drawn on a surface (embedded in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)), several curvatures are defined, which relates the direction of curvature to the surface's unit [normal vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector), including the:

- [normal curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_curvature)
- [geodesic curvature](wikipedia://en/483526)
- [geodesic torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_torsion)

Any non-singular curve on a smooth surface has its tangent vector $\mathbf{T}$ contained in the [tangent plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_plane) of the surface. The **normal curvature**, $k_{n}$, is the curvature of the curve projected onto the plane containing the curve's tangent $\mathbf{T}$ and the surface normal $\mathbf{u}$; the **geodesic curvature**, $k_{g}$, is the curvature of the curve projected onto the surface's tangent plane; and the **geodesic torsion** (or **relative torsion**), $\tau_{r}$, measures the rate of change of the surface normal around the curve's tangent.

Let the curve be [arc-length parametrized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc-length_parametrization), and let $\mathbf{t} = \mathbf{u} \times \mathbf{T}$ so that $\mathbf{T}, \mathbf{t}, \mathbf{u}$ form an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063), called the **[Darboux frame](wikipedia://en/11067444)**. The above quantities are related by:

$$\begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{T}'\\ \mathbf{t}'\\ \mathbf{u}' \end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix} 0&\kappa_\mathrm{g}&\kappa_\mathrm{n}\\ -\kappa_\mathrm{g}&0&\tau_\mathrm{r}\\ -\kappa_\mathrm{n}&-\tau_\mathrm{r}&0 \end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix} \mathbf{T}\\ \mathbf{t}\\ \mathbf{u} \end{pmatrix}$$

#### Principal curvature {#Principal_curvature}

*Image: Minimal surface curvature planes-en.svg.* [Saddle surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddle_surface) with normal planes in directions of principal curvatures

All curves on the surface with the same tangent vector at a given point will have the same normal curvature, which is the same as the curvature of the curve obtained by intersecting the surface with the plane containing $\mathbf{T}$ and $\mathbf{u}$. Taking all possible tangent vectors, the maximum and minimum values of the normal curvature at a point are called the **principal curvatures**, $k_{1}$ and $k_{2}$, and the directions of the corresponding tangent vectors are called **principal normal directions**.

:::

### Normal sections {#Normal_sections}

Curvature can be evaluated along surface [normal sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_section), similar to above (see for example the [Earth radius of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius_of_curvature)).

### Developable surfaces {#Developable_surfaces}

Some curved surfaces, such as those made from a smooth sheet of paper, can be flattened down into the plane without distorting their intrinsic features in any way. Such [developable surfaces](wikipedia://en/2878111) have zero Gaussian curvature (see below).[^o39]

### Gaussian curvature {#Gaussian_curvature}

In contrast to curves, which do not have intrinsic curvature, but do have extrinsic curvature (they only have a curvature given an embedding), surfaces can have intrinsic curvature, independent of an embedding. The [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623), named after [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125), is equal to the product of the principal curvatures, $k_{1}k_{2}$. It has a dimension of length<sup>−2</sup> and is positive for [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859), negative for one-sheet [hyperboloids](wikipedia://en/145844) and zero for planes and [cylinders](wikipedia://en/859283#Cylindrical_surfaces). It determines whether a surface is [locally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally) convex (when it is positive) or locally saddle-shaped (when it is negative).

Gaussian curvature is an *intrinsic* property of the surface, meaning it does not depend on the particular [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) of the surface; intuitively, this means that ants living on the surface could determine the Gaussian curvature. For example, an ant living on a sphere could measure the sum of the interior angles of a triangle and determine that it was greater than 180 degrees, implying that the space it inhabited had positive curvature. On the other hand, an ant living on a cylinder would not detect any such departure from [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417); in particular the ant could not detect that the two surfaces have different mean curvatures (see below), which is a purely extrinsic type of curvature.

Formally, Gaussian curvature only depends on the [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) of the surface. This is [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125)'s celebrated [Theorema Egregium](wikipedia://en/259906), which he found while concerned with geographic surveys and mapmaking.

An intrinsic definition of the Gaussian curvature at a point $P$ is the following: imagine an ant which is tied to $P$ with a short thread of length $r$. It runs around $P$ while the thread is completely stretched and measures the length $C(r)$ of one complete trip around $P$. If the surface were flat, the ant would find $C(r) = 2\pi r$. On curved surfaces, the formula for $C(r)$ will be different, and the Gaussian curvature $K$ at the point $P$ can be computed by the [Bertrand–Diguet–Puiseux theorem](wikipedia://en/18766220) as

$$K = \lim_{r\to 0^+} 3\left(\frac{2\pi r-C(r)}{\pi r^3}\right).$$

The [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the Gaussian curvature over the whole surface is closely related to the surface's [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420); see the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229).

The discrete analog of curvature, corresponding to curvature being concentrated at a point and particularly useful for [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra), is the [(angular) defect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defect_%28geometry%29); the analog for the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229) is [Descartes' theorem on total angular defect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defect_%28geometry%29#Descartes.27_theorem).

Because (Gaussian) curvature can be defined without reference to an embedding space, it is not necessary that a surface be embedded in a higher-dimensional space in order to be curved. Such an intrinsically curved two-dimensional surface is a simple example of a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652).

### Mean curvature {#Mean_curvature}

The mean curvature is an *extrinsic* measure of curvature equal to half the sum of the [principal curvatures](wikipedia://en/2072472), $\tfrac{k_{1} + k_{2}}{2}$. It was introduced by [Sophie Germain](wikipedia://en/27791) in her work on elasticity theory. It has a dimension of length<sup>−1</sup>. Mean curvature is closely related to the first variation of [surface area](wikipedia://en/27725). In particular, a [minimal surface](wikipedia://en/276734) such as a [soap film](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_film) has mean curvature zero and a [soap bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_bubble) has constant mean curvature. Unlike Gauss curvature, the mean curvature is extrinsic and depends on the embedding, for instance, a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) and a plane are locally [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) but the mean curvature of a plane is zero while that of a cylinder is nonzero.

### Second fundamental form {#Second_fundamental_form}

The intrinsic and extrinsic curvature of a surface can be combined in the second fundamental form. This is a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) in the tangent plane to the surface at a point whose value at a particular tangent vector $\mathbf{X}$ to the surface is the normal component of the acceleration of a curve along the surface tangent to $\mathbf{X}$; that is, it is the normal curvature to a curve tangent to $\mathbf{X}$ (see above). Symbolically,

$$\operatorname{I\!I}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}) = \mathbf{N}\cdot (\nabla_\mathbf{X} \mathbf{X})$$

where $\mathbf{N}$ is the unit normal to the surface. For unit tangent vectors $\mathbf{X}$, the second fundamental form assumes the maximum value $k_{1}$ and minimum value $k_{2}$, which occur in the principal directions $\mathbf{u}_{1}$ and $\mathbf{u}_{2}$, respectively. Thus, by the [principal axis theorem](wikipedia://en/11376126), the second fundamental form is

$$\operatorname{I\!I}(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{X}) = k_1\left(\mathbf{X}\cdot \mathbf{u}_1\right)^2 + k_2\left(\mathbf{X}\cdot \mathbf{u}_2\right)^2.$$

Thus the second fundamental form encodes both the intrinsic and extrinsic curvatures.

### Shape operator {#Shape_operator}

An encapsulation of surface curvature can be found in the shape operator, $S$, which is a [self-adjoint](wikipedia://en/187408) [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) from the tangent plane to itself (specifically, the differential of the [Gauss map](wikipedia://en/378881)).

For a surface with tangent vectors $\mathbf{X}$ and normal $\mathbf{N}$, the shape operator can be expressed compactly in [index summation notation](wikipedia://en/195407) as

$$\partial_a \mathbf{N} = -S_{ba} \mathbf{X}_{b} .$$

(Compare the alternative expression of curvature for a plane curve.)

The [Weingarten equations](wikipedia://en/19149724) give the value of $S$ in terms of the coefficients of the [first](wikipedia://en/510355) and [second fundamental forms](wikipedia://en/652078) as

$$S= \left(EG-F^2\right)^{-1}\begin{pmatrix} eG-fF& fG-gF \\ fE-eF & gE- fF\end{pmatrix}.$$

The principal curvatures are the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of the shape operator, the principal curvature directions are its [eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429), the Gauss curvature is its [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), and the mean curvature is half its [trace](wikipedia://en/43270).

## Curvature of space {#Curvature_of_space}

By extension of the former argument, a space of three or more dimensions can be intrinsically curved. The curvature is *intrinsic* in the sense that it is a property defined at every point in the space, rather than a property defined with respect to a larger space that contains it. In general, a curved space may or may not be conceived as being embedded in a higher-dimensional [ambient space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_space); if not then its curvature can only be defined intrinsically.

After the discovery of the intrinsic definition of curvature, which is closely connected with [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610), many mathematicians and scientists questioned whether ordinary physical space might be curved, although the success of Euclidean geometry up to that time meant that the radius of curvature must be astronomically large. In the theory of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), which describes [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) and [cosmology](wikipedia://en/5378), the idea is slightly generalised to the "curvature of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758)"; in relativity theory spacetime is a [pseudo-Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/287555). Once a time coordinate is defined, the three-dimensional space corresponding to a particular time is generally a curved Riemannian manifold; but since the time coordinate choice is largely arbitrary, it is the underlying spacetime curvature that is physically significant.

Although an arbitrarily curved space is very complex to describe, the curvature of a space which is locally [isotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotropic) and [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/363325) is described by a single Gaussian curvature, as for a surface; mathematically these are strong conditions, but they correspond to reasonable physical assumptions (all points and all directions are indistinguishable). A positive curvature corresponds to the inverse square radius of curvature; an example is a sphere or [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere). An example of negatively curved space is [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) (see also: [non-positive curvature](wikipedia://en/12989803)). A space or space-time with zero curvature is called ***flat***. For example, [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is an example of a flat space, and [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space) is an example of a flat spacetime. There are other examples of flat geometries in both settings, though. A [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) or a [cylinder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29) can both be given flat metrics, but differ in their [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). Other topologies are also possible for curved space (see also: [Shape of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_of_the_universe)).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

*Image: Parallel Transport.svg.* Moving a vector along a curve from A → N → B → A produces another vector. The inability to return to the initial vector is measured by the holonomy of the surface. In a space with no curvature, the angle α is 0 degrees, and in a space with curvature, the angle α is greater than 0 degrees. The more space is curved, the greater the magnitude of the angle α.

The mathematical notion of *curvature* is also defined in much more general contexts.[^o47] Many of these generalizations emphasize different aspects of the curvature as it is understood in lower dimensions.

One such generalization is kinematic. The curvature of a curve can naturally be considered as a kinematic quantity, representing the force felt by a certain observer moving along the curve; analogously, curvature in higher dimensions can be regarded as a kind of [tidal force](wikipedia://en/30719) (this is one way of thinking of the [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618)). This generalization of curvature depends on how nearby test particles diverge or converge when they are allowed to move freely in the space; see [Jacobi field](wikipedia://en/875509).

Another broad generalization of curvature comes from the study of [parallel transport](wikipedia://en/285595) on a surface. For instance, if a vector is moved around a loop on the surface of a sphere keeping parallel throughout the motion, then the final position of the vector may not be the same as the initial position of the vector. This phenomenon is known as [holonomy](wikipedia://en/644921).[^o48] Various generalizations capture in an abstract form this idea of curvature as a measure of holonomy; see [curvature form](wikipedia://en/489440). A closely related notion of curvature comes from [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/23912155) in physics, where the curvature represents a field and a [vector potential](wikipedia://en/422936) for the field is a quantity that is in general path-dependent: it may change if an observer moves around a loop.

Two more generalizations of curvature are the [scalar curvature](wikipedia://en/285622) and [Ricci curvature](wikipedia://en/276582). In a curved surface such as the sphere, the area of a disc on the surface differs from the area of a disc of the same radius in flat space. This difference (in a suitable limit) is measured by the scalar curvature. The difference in area of a sector of the disc is measured by the Ricci curvature. Each of the scalar curvature and Ricci curvature are defined in analogous ways in three and higher dimensions. They are particularly important in relativity theory, where they both appear on the side of [Einstein's field equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein's_field_equations) that represents the geometry of spacetime (the other side of which represents the presence of matter and energy). These generalizations of curvature underlie, for instance, the notion that curvature can be a property of a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873); see [curvature of a measure](wikipedia://en/11766887).

Another generalization of curvature relies on the ability to [compare](wikipedia://en/6626644) a curved space with another space that has *constant* curvature. Often this is done with triangles in the spaces. The notion of a triangle makes senses in [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018), and this gives rise to [$\operatorname{CAT}(k)$ spaces](wikipedia://en/12941912).
