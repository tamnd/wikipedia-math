# Pseudosphere

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudosphere"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **pseudosphere** is a surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$. It is the most famous example of a **pseudospherical surface**. A pseudospherical surface is a surface piecewise smoothly immersed in $\mathbb{R}^3$ with constant negative [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623). A "pseudospherical surface of radius $R$" is a surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$ having [curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) −1/*R*<sup>2</sup> at each point. Its name comes from the analogy with the sphere of radius $R$, which is a surface of curvature 1/*R*<sup>2</sup>. Examples include the tractroid, [Dini's surfaces](wikipedia://en/25057351), [breather surfaces](wikipedia://en/13160226), and the [Kuen surface](wikipedia://en/79573381).

:::

The term "pseudosphere" was introduced by [Eugenio Beltrami](wikipedia://en/543760) in his 1868 paper on models of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).[^o2]

## Tractroid {#Tractroid}

*Image: Pseudosphere.png.* Tractroid

By "the pseudosphere", people usually mean the tractroid. The tractroid is obtained by [revolving](wikipedia://en/329549) a [tractrix](wikipedia://en/841685) about its [asymptote](wikipedia://en/3107). As an example, the (half) pseudosphere (with radius 1) is the surface of revolution of the tractrix parametrized by[^o4]

$$t \mapsto \left( t - \tanh t, \operatorname{sech}\,t \right), \quad \quad 0 \le t < \infty.$$

It is a [singular space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) (the equator is a singularity), but away from the singularities, it has constant negative [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) and therefore is locally [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) to a [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/318742).

The name "pseudosphere" comes about because it has a [two-dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) of constant negative Gaussian curvature, just as a sphere has a surface with constant positive Gaussian curvature. Just as the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) has at every point a [positively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) curved geometry of a [dome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome) the whole pseudosphere has at every point the [negatively](wikipedia://en/154616) curved geometry of a [saddle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saddle_surface).

As early as 1693 [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127) found that the volume and the surface area of the pseudosphere are finite,[^o5] despite the infinite extent of the shape along the axis of rotation. For a given edge [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) $R$, the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) is $4\pi R^{2}$ just as it is for the sphere, while the [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) is $\tfrac{2}{3}\pi R^{3}$ and therefore half that of a sphere of that radius.[^o6]

The pseudosphere is an important geometric precursor to mathematical [fabric arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabric_arts) and [pedagogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedagogy).[^o8]

## Line congruence {#Line_congruence}

A **line congruence** is a 2-parameter families of lines in $\mathbb{R}^3$. It can be written as

$$X(u, v, t)=x(u, v)+t p(u, v)$$

where each pick of $u, v \in \mathbb{R}$ picks a specific line in the family.

A **focal surface** of the line congruence is a surface that is tangent to the line congruence. At each point on the surface,

$$\det\left(\partial_u X, \partial_v X, p\right)=0$$

The above equation expands to a quadratic equation in $t$:

$$\det (\partial_u x(u, v)+ t\partial_u p(u, v), \partial_v x(u, v)+ t\partial_v p(u, v), p(u, v)) = 0$$

Thus, for each $(u, v) \in \mathbb{R}^2$, there in general exists two choices of $t_1(u, v), t_2(u, v)$. Thus a generic line congruence has exactly two focal surfaces parameterized by $t_1(u, v), t_2(u, v)$.

For a bundle of lines normal to a smooth surface, the two focal surfaces correspond to its evolutes: the loci of centers of principal curvature.

In 1879, Bianchi proved that if a line congruence is such that the corresponding points on the two focal surfaces are at a constant distance 1, that is, $|t_1(u, v) - t_2(u, v)| = 1$, then both of the focal surfaces have constant curvature -1.

In 1880, Lie proved a partial converse. Let $X$ be a pseudospherical surface. Then there exists a second pseudospherical surface $\hat{X}$ and a line congruence $\mathcal{L}$ such that $X$ and $\hat{X}$ are the focal surfaces of $\mathcal{L}$. Furthermore, $\hat{X}$ and $\mathcal{L}$ may be constructed from $X$ by integrating a sequence of [ODEs](wikipedia://en/32742753).

## Universal covering space {#Universal_covering_space}

*Image: Geodesics on the pseudosphere and three other models of hyperbolic geometry.png.* The pseudosphere and its relation to three other models of hyperbolic geometry

The half pseudosphere of curvature −1 is [covered](wikipedia://en/250323) by the interior of a [horocycle](wikipedia://en/11084460). In the [Poincaré half-plane model](wikipedia://en/461942) one convenient choice is the portion of the half-plane with $y \ge 1$.[^o11] Then the covering map is periodic in the $x$ direction of period 2$\pi$, and takes the horocycles $y = c$ to the meridians of the pseudosphere and the vertical geodesics $x = c$ to the tractrices that generate the pseudosphere. This mapping is a local isometry, and thus exhibits the portion $y \ge 1$ of the upper half-plane as the [universal covering space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_covering_space) of the pseudosphere. The precise mapping is

$$(x,y)\mapsto \big(v(\operatorname{arcosh} y)\cos x, v(\operatorname{arcosh} y) \sin x, u(\operatorname{arcosh} y)\big) ,$$

where

$$t\mapsto \big(u(t) = t - \operatorname{tanh} t,v(t) = \operatorname{sech} t\big)$$

is the parametrization of the tractrix above.

## Hyperboloid {#Hyperboloid}

*Image: Deforming a pseudosphere to Dini's surface.gif.* Deforming the pseudosphere to a portion of [Dini's surface](wikipedia://en/25057351). In differential geometry, this is a [Lie transformation](wikipedia://en/306645#New_solutions_from_old). In the corresponding solutions to the [sine-Gordon equation](wikipedia://en/306645), this deformation corresponds to a [Lorentz Boost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_Boost) of the static 1-[soliton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soliton_%28topological%29) solution.

In some sources that use the [hyperboloid model](wikipedia://en/4771189) of the hyperbolic plane, the hyperboloid is referred to as a **pseudosphere**.[^o13] This usage of the word is because the hyperboloid can be [thought of as a sphere](wikipedia://en/145844#Relation_to_the_sphere) of imaginary radius, embedded in a [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space).

## Relation to solutions to the sine-Gordon equation {#Relation_to_solutions_to_the_sine-Gordon_equation}

Pseudospherical surfaces can be constructed from solutions to the [sine-Gordon equation](wikipedia://en/306645).[^o15] A sketch proof starts with reparametrizing the tractroid with coordinates in which the [Gauss–Codazzi equations](wikipedia://en/9633335) can be rewritten as the sine-Gordon equation.

On a surface, at each point, draw a cross, pointing at the two directions of [principal curvature](wikipedia://en/2072472). These crosses can be integrated into two families of curves, making up a coordinate system on the surface. Let the coordinate system be written as $(x, y)$.

At each point on a pseudospherical surface there in general exists two asymptotic directions. Along them, the curvature is zero. Let the angle between the asymptotic directions be $\theta$.

A theorem states that

$$\partial_{xx} \theta - \partial_{yy} \theta = \sin\theta$$

In particular, for the tractroid the Gauss–Codazzi equations are the sine-Gordon equation applied to the static soliton solution, so the Gauss–Codazzi equations are satisfied. In these coordinates the [first](wikipedia://en/510355) and [second fundamental forms](wikipedia://en/652078) are written in a way that makes clear the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) is −1 for any solution of the sine-Gordon equations.

Then any solution to the sine-Gordon equation can be used to specify a first and second fundamental form which satisfy the Gauss–Codazzi equations. There is then a theorem that any such set of initial data can be used to at least locally specify an immersed surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

This connection between sine-Gordon equations and pseudospherical surfaces mean that one can identify solutions to the equation with surfaces. Then, any way to generate new sine-Gordon solutions from old automatically generates new pseudospherical surfaces from old, and vice versa.

A few examples of sine-Gordon solutions and their corresponding surface are given as follows:

- Static 1-soliton: pseudosphere
- Moving 1-soliton: [Dini's surface](wikipedia://en/25057351)
- Breather solution: [Breather surface](wikipedia://en/13160226)
- 2-soliton: [Kuen surface](wikipedia://en/79573381)
