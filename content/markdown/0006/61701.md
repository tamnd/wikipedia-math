# Venn diagram

*Image: Venn diagram gr la ru.svg.* Venn diagram showing the uppercase [glyphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glyph) shared by the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) (upper left), [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_alphabets) (upper right), and [Russian Cyrillic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_alphabet) (bottom) alphabets

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Venn diagram"}

A **Venn diagram** is a widely used [diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagram) style that shows the logical relation between [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), popularized by [John Venn](wikipedia://en/251482) (1834–1923) in the 1880s. The diagrams are used to teach elementary [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), and to illustrate simple set relationships in [probability](wikipedia://en/22934), [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science). A Venn diagram uses simple closed curves on a plane to represent sets. The curves are often circles or ellipses.

:::

Very similar ideas had been proposed before Venn such as by [Christian Weise](wikipedia://en/34885135) in 1712 (*Nucleus Logicoe Wiesianoe*) and [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in 1768 (*[Letters to a German Princess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_to_a_German_Princess)*). The idea was popularised by Venn in *Symbolic Logic*, Chapter V "Diagrammatic Representation", published in 1881.

## Details {#Details}

A Venn diagram, also called a *set diagram* or *logic diagram*, shows *all* possible logical relations between a finite collection of different sets. These diagrams depict [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29) as points in the plane, and sets as regions inside closed curves. A Venn diagram consists of multiple overlapping closed curves, usually circles, each representing a set. The points inside a curve labelled *S* represent elements of the set *S*, while points outside the boundary represent elements not in the set *S*. This lends itself to intuitive visualizations; for example, the set of all elements that are members of both sets *S* and *T*, denoted *S* ∩ *T* and read "the intersection of *S* and *T*", is represented visually by the area of overlap of the regions *S* and *T*.[^o46]

In Venn diagrams, the curves are overlapped in every possible way, showing all possible relations between the sets. They are thus a special case of [Euler diagrams](wikipedia://en/2427526), which do not necessarily show all relations. Venn diagrams were conceived around 1880 by John Venn. They are used to teach elementary set theory, as well as illustrate simple set relationships in probability, logic, statistics, linguistics, and computer science.

A Venn diagram in which the area of each shape is proportional to the number of elements it contains is called an **area-proportional** (or **scaled**) **Venn diagram**.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

*Image: Venn diagram of legs and flying.svg.* Sets of creatures with two legs, and creatures that fly

This example involves two sets of creatures, represented as overlapping circles: one circle that represents all types of creatures that have two legs, and another representing creatures that can fly. Each separate type of creature can be imagined as a point somewhere in the diagram. Living creatures that have two legs *and* can fly—for example, parrots—are then in both sets, so they correspond to points in the region where the two circles overlap. This overlapping region would only contain those elements (in this example, creatures) that are members of both the set of two-legged creatures and set of flying creatures.

Humans and penguins are bipedal, and so are in the "has two legs" circle, but since they cannot fly, they appear in the part of the that circle that does not overlap with the "can fly" circle. Mosquitoes can fly, but have six, not two, legs, so the point for mosquitoes is in the part of the "can fly" circle that does not overlap with the "has two legs" circle. Creatures that are neither two-legged nor able to fly (for example, whales and spiders) would all be represented by points outside both circles.

The combined region of the two sets is called their *[union](wikipedia://en/47949)*, denoted by A ∪ B, where A is the "has two legs" circle and B the "can fly" circle. The union in this case contains all living creatures that either are two-legged or can fly (or both). The region included in both A and B, where the two sets overlap, is called the *[intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429)* of A and B, denoted by A ∩ B.

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Venn-stainedglass-gonville-caius.jpg.* [Stained-glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stained-glass) window with Venn diagram in [Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College,_Cambridge)

Venn diagrams were introduced in 1880 by [John Venn](wikipedia://en/251482) in a paper entitled "On the Diagrammatic and Mechanical Representation of Propositions and Reasonings"[^o47] in the *Philosophical Magazine and Journal of Science*,[^o48] about the different ways to represent [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094) by diagrams.[^o33][^o34][^o30] The use of these types of diagrams in [formal logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_logic), according to [Frank Ruskey](wikipedia://en/33272681) and Mark Weston, predates Venn but are "rightly associated" with him as he "comprehensively surveyed and formalized their usage, and was the first to generalize them".[^o31]

Diagrams of overlapping circles representing unions and intersections, such as [Borromean rings](wikipedia://en/873118), were already in frequent use in the Middle Ages. However, the extent to which these types of diagrams can be considered precursors to Venn diagrams is disputed.[^o35][^o6] [Euler diagrams](wikipedia://en/2427526), which are similar to Venn diagrams but do not necessarily contain all possible unions and intersections,[^o7][^o8] were named after the mathematician [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in the 18th century.[^o27][^o38][^o32] However, these diagrams, which are considered the precursors of Venn diagrams,[^o9] can also be clearly traced back to the 16th century. Pioneers in this tradition of Euler diagrams included [Erhard Weigel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erhard_Weigel) (1625–1699) and his students [Johann Christoph Sturm](wikipedia://en/2173920) (1635-1703) and [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) (1646–1716). [Christian Weise](wikipedia://en/34885135) (1642–1708) is also worth mentioning, whose student Johann Christian Lange worked intensively on these diagrams.[^o44][^o35] Euler further developed these diagrams, and [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant) (1724–1804) and his students popularized them in the 19th century.[^o10]

Venn did not use the term "Venn diagram" and referred to the concept as "Eulerian Circles".[^o30] He became acquainted with Euler diagrams in 1862 and wrote that Venn diagrams did not occur to him "till much later", while attempting to adapt Euler diagrams to [Boolean logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_logic).[^o11] In the opening sentence of his 1880 article Venn wrote that Euler diagrams were the only diagrammatic representation of logic to gain "any general acceptance".[^o33][^o34]

Venn viewed his diagrams as a pedagogical tool, analogous to verification of physical concepts through experiment. As an example of their applications, he noted that a three-set diagram could show the [syllogism](wikipedia://en/48046): 'All *A* is some *B*. No *B* is any *C*. Hence, no *A* is any *C*.'[^o11]

[Charles L. Dodgson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_L._Dodgson) (Lewis Carroll) includes "Venn's Method of Diagrams" as well as "Euler's Method of Diagrams" in an "Appendix, Addressed to Teachers" of his book *Symbolic Logic* (4th edition published in 1896). The term "Venn diagram" was later used by [Clarence Irving Lewis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clarence_Irving_Lewis) in 1918, in his book *A Survey of Symbolic Logic*.[^o31][^o29]

In the 20th century, Venn diagrams were further developed. [David Wilson Henderson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Wilson_Henderson) showed, in 1963, that the existence of an *n*-Venn diagram with *n*-fold [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) implied that *n* was a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666).[^o36] He also showed that such symmetric Venn diagrams exist when *n* is five or seven. In 2002, Peter Hamburger found symmetric Venn diagrams for *n* = 11 and in 2003, Griggs, Killian, and Savage showed that symmetric Venn diagrams exist for all other primes. These combined results show that rotationally symmetric Venn diagrams exist, if and only if *n* is a prime number.[^o37]

Venn diagrams and Euler diagrams were incorporated as part of instruction in set theory, as part of the [new math](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_math) movement in the 1960s. Since then, they have also been adopted in the curriculum of other fields such as reading.[^o43] With the work of [Sun-Joo Shin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun-Joo_Shin), Venn diagrams have been recognized as a logical system equivalent to [symbolic logic](wikipedia://en/2498388).[^o12] Similar methods were then adopted in mathematics and subsequently in computer science.[^o13][^o14]

## Popular culture {#Popular_culture}

Venn diagrams have been commonly used as a format for internet humor. One of the earliest recurring examples appeared in 2006 on *Indexed*, a blog by writer and illustrator Jessica Hagy in which she used hand-drawn Venn diagrams on index cards to offer jokes and social commentary. *Time* magazine named *Indexed* one of its 25 best blogs in 2007, describing it as using Venn diagrams to "graphically and (often hilariously) highlight life's profundities and absurdities."[^o16] In August 2018, a Venn diagram comparing phrases used by preachers, DJs, and bank robbers spread across Twitter, Facebook, and Reddit, inspiring numerous user-created variations and being documented as a recurring meme format.[^o17] At least one politician has been mocked for misusing Venn diagrams.[^o18]

## Overview {#Overview}

A Venn diagram is constructed with a collection of simple closed curves drawn in a plane. According to Lewis,[^o29] the "principle of these diagrams is that classes \[or *sets*\] be represented by regions in such relation to one another that all the possible logical relations of these classes can be indicated in the same diagram. That is, the diagram initially leaves room for any possible relation of the classes, and the actual or given relation, can then be specified by indicating that some particular region is null or is not-null".[^o29]

Venn diagrams normally comprise overlapping [circles](wikipedia://en/6220). The interior of the circle symbolically represents the elements of the set, while the exterior represents elements that are not members of the set. For instance, in a two-set Venn diagram, one circle may represent the group of all [wooden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wood) objects, while the other circle may represent the set of all tables. The overlapping region, or *intersection*, would then represent the set of all wooden tables. Shapes other than circles can be employed as shown below by Venn's own higher set diagrams. Venn diagrams do not generally contain information on the relative or absolute sizes ([cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174)) of sets. That is, they are [schematic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schematic) diagrams generally not drawn to scale.

Venn diagrams are similar to Euler diagrams. However, a Venn diagram for *n* component sets must contain all 2<sup>*n*</sup> hypothetically possible zones, that correspond to some combination of inclusion or exclusion in each of the component sets.[^o49] Euler diagrams contain only the actually possible zones in a given context. In Venn diagrams, a shaded zone may represent an empty zone, whereas in an Euler diagram, the corresponding zone is missing from the diagram. For example, if one set represents *dairy products* and another *cheeses*, the Venn diagram contains a zone for cheeses that are not dairy products. Assuming that in the context *cheese* means some type of dairy product, the Euler diagram has the cheese zone entirely contained within the dairy-product zone—there is no zone for (non-existent) non-dairy cheese. This means that as the number of contours increases, Euler diagrams are typically less visually complex than the equivalent Venn diagram, particularly if the number of non-empty intersections is small.[^o42]

## Extensions to higher numbers of sets {#Extensions_to_higher_numbers_of_sets}

For higher numbers of sets, some loss of symmetry in the diagrams is unavoidable. Venn was keen to find "symmetrical figures ... elegant in themselves,"[^o38] that represented higher numbers of sets, and he devised an *elegant* four-set diagram using [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277) (see below). He also gave a construction for Venn diagrams for *any* number of sets, where each successive curve that delimits a set interleaves with previous curves, starting with the three-circle diagram.

### Edwards–Venn diagrams {#Edwards–Venn_diagrams}

[Anthony William Fairbank Edwards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthony_William_Fairbank_Edwards) constructed a series of Venn diagrams for higher numbers of sets by segmenting the surface of a sphere, which became known as Edwards–Venn diagrams.[^o39] For example, three sets can be easily represented by taking three hemispheres of the sphere at right angles (*x* = 0, *y* = 0 and *z* = 0). A fourth set can be added to the representation, by taking a curve similar to the seam on a tennis ball, which winds up and down around the equator, and so on. The resulting sets can then be projected back to a plane, to give *cogwheel* diagrams with increasing numbers of teeth—as shown here. These diagrams were devised while designing a [stained-glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stained-glass) window in memory of Venn.[^o39]

### Other diagrams {#Other_diagrams}

*Image: The\_11\_logical\_relations\_between\_two\_sets.svg.* A set of 11 [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) diagrams illustrating the 11 exhaustive logical relations between two sets. Unlike Venn diagrams, which show all possible intersections, this representation focuses on the topological configurations possible between two regions.

Edwards–Venn diagrams are [topologically equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_equivalence) to diagrams devised by [Branko Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976), which were based around intersecting [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) with increasing numbers of sides. They are also two-dimensional representations of [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783).

[Henry John Stephen Smith](wikipedia://en/47123) devised similar *n*-set diagrams using [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) curves[^o39] with the series of equations

$$y_i = \frac{\sin\left(2^i x\right)}{2^i} \text{ where } 0 \leq i \leq n-1 \text{ and } i \in \mathbb{N}.$$

[Charles Lutwidge Dodgson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Lutwidge_Dodgson) (also known as Lewis Carroll) devised a five-set diagram known as [Carroll's square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carroll's_square_%28diagram%29). Joaquin and Boyles, on the other hand, proposed supplemental rules for the standard Venn diagram, in order to account for certain problem cases. For instance, regarding the issue of representing singular statements, they suggest to consider the Venn diagram circle as a representation of a set of things, and use [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) and set theory to treat categorical statements as statements about sets. Additionally, they propose to treat singular statements as statements about [set membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_membership). So, for example, to represent the statement "a is F" in this retooled Venn diagram, a small letter "a" may be placed inside the circle that represents the set F.[^o45]

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

*Image: Venn3tab.svg.* Venn diagram as a truth table demonstrating [logical disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962)

Venn diagrams correspond to [truth tables](wikipedia://en/25512250) for the propositions $x\in A$, $x\in B$, etc., in the sense that each region of Venn diagram corresponds to one row of the truth table.[^o40][^o41] This type is also known as Johnston diagram. Another way of representing sets is with John F. Randolph's [R-diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-diagram).
