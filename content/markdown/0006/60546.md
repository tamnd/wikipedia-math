# Unique factorization domain

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unique factorization domain"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **unique factorization domain** (**UFD**) (also sometimes called a **factorial ring** following the terminology of [Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394)) is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) in which a statement analogous to the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556) holds. Specifically, a UFD is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) (a [nontrivial](wikipedia://en/1872874) [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) in which the product of any two non-zero elements is non-zero) in which every non-zero non-[unit](wikipedia://en/289666) element can be written as a product of [irreducible elements](wikipedia://en/60693), uniquely up to order and units.

:::

Important examples of UFDs are the integers and [polynomial rings](wikipedia://en/373065) in one or more variables with coefficients coming from the integers or from a [field](wikipedia://en/10603).

## Definition {#Definition}

Formally, a unique factorization domain is defined to be an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) *R* in which every non-zero element *x* of *R* which is not a unit can be written as a finite product of [irreducible elements](wikipedia://en/60693) *p*<sub>*i*</sub> of *R*:

*x* = *p*<sub>1</sub> *p*<sub>2</sub> ⋅⋅⋅ *p*<sub>*n*</sub> with *n* ≥ 1

and this representation is unique in the following sense: If *q*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *q*<sub>*m*</sub> are irreducible elements of *R* such that

*x* = *q*<sub>1</sub> *q*<sub>2</sub> ⋅⋅⋅ *q*<sub>*m*</sub> with *m* ≥ 1,

then *m* = *n*, and there exists a [bijective map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) *φ* : $\{1, ..., n\}$ → $\{1, ..., m\}$ such that *p*<sub>*i*</sub> is [associated](wikipedia://en/289666#Associatedness) to *q*<sub>*φ*(*i*)</sub> for *i* ∈ $\{1, ..., n\}$.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Most rings familiar from elementary mathematics are UFDs:

- All [principal ideal domains](wikipedia://en/25039), hence all [Euclidean domains](wikipedia://en/10376), are UFDs. In particular, the [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) (also see *[Fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556)*), the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) and the [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035) are UFDs.
- If *R* is a UFD, then so is *R*\[*X*\], the [ring of polynomials](wikipedia://en/373065) with coefficients in *R*. Unless *R* is a field, *R*\[*X*\] is not a principal ideal domain. By induction, a polynomial ring in any number of variables over any UFD (and in particular over a field or over the integers) is a UFD.
- The [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) ring *K**X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> over a field *K* (or more generally over a [regular](wikipedia://en/506959#Regular_ring) UFD such as a PID) is a UFD. On the other hand, the formal power series ring over a UFD need not be a UFD, even if the UFD is [local](wikipedia://en/153391). For example, if *R* is the localization of *k*\[*x*, *y*, *z*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>3</sup> + *z*<sup>7</sup>) at the [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) (*x*, *y*, *z*) then *R* is a local ring that is a UFD, but the formal power series ring *R**X* over *R* is not a UFD.
- The [Auslander–Buchsbaum theorem](wikipedia://en/30774055) states that every [regular local ring](wikipedia://en/506959) is a UFD.
- $\textstyle \mathbb{Z}\bigl[e^{2 \pi i/n}\bigr]$ is a UFD for all integers 1 ≤ *n* ≤ 22, but not for *n* = 23. This is the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of the [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462) $\mathbb{Q}(e^{2 \pi i/n})$. For rings of integers of other [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field), see *[List of number fields with class number one](wikipedia://en/22779719)*.
- Mori showed that if the completion of a [Zariski ring](wikipedia://en/27901866), such as a [Noetherian local ring](wikipedia://en/56129), is a UFD, then the ring is a UFD.[^n1] The converse of this is not true: there are Noetherian local rings that are UFDs but whose completions are not. The question of when this happens is rather subtle: for example, for the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of *k*\[*x*, *y*, *z*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>3</sup> + *z*<sup>5</sup>) at the prime ideal (*x*, *y*, *z*), both the local ring and its completion are UFDs[^n2][^n3], but in the apparently similar example of the localization of *k*\[*x*, *y*, *z*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>3</sup> + *z*<sup>7</sup>) at the prime ideal (*x*, *y*, *z*) the local ring is a UFD[^o9] but its completion is not.
- Let $R$ be a field of any [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) other than 2. Klein and Nagata showed that the ring *R*\[*X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub>\]/*Q* is a UFD whenever *Q* is a nonsingular quadratic form in the *X*s and *n* is at least 5. When *n* = 4, the ring need not be a UFD. For example, *R*\[*X*, *Y*, *Z*, *W*\]/(*XY* − *ZW*) is not a UFD, because the element *XY* equals the element *ZW* so that *XY* and *ZW* are two different factorizations of the same element into irreducibles.
- The ring *Q*\[*x*, *y*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup> + 2*y*<sup>2</sup> + 1) is a UFD, but the ring *Q*(*i*)\[*x*, *y*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup> + 2*y*<sup>2</sup> + 1) is not. On the other hand, The ring *Q*\[*x*, *y*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> − 1) is not a UFD, but the ring *Q*(*i*)\[*x*, *y*\]/(*x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> − 1) is.[^n4] Similarly the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) **R**\[*X*, *Y*, *Z*\]/(*X*<sup>2</sup> + *Y*<sup>2</sup> + *Z*<sup>2</sup> − 1) of the 2-dimensional [real sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) is a UFD, but the coordinate ring **C**\[*X*, *Y*, *Z*\]/(*X*<sup>2</sup> + *Y*<sup>2</sup> + *Z*<sup>2</sup> − 1) of the complex sphere is not.
- Suppose that the variables *X*<sub>*i*</sub> are given weights *w*<sub>*i*</sub>, and *F*(*X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub>) is a [homogeneous polynomial](wikipedia://en/1698977) of weight *w*. Then if *c* is coprime to *w* and *R* is a UFD and either every [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) [projective module](wikipedia://en/364488) over *R* is [free](wikipedia://en/326454) or *c* is 1 mod *w*, the ring *R*\[*X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub>, *Z*\]/(*Z*<sup>*c*</sup> − *F*(*X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub>)) is a UFD.[^n5]

::: {#o13 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

- The [quadratic integer ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_integer_ring) $\mathbb Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ of all [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) of the form $a+b\sqrt{-5}$, where *a* and *b* are integers, is not a UFD because 6 factors as both 2×3 and as $\left(1+\sqrt{-5}\right)\left(1-\sqrt{-5}\right)$. These truly are different factorizations, because the only units in this ring are 1 and −1; thus, none of 2, 3, $1+\sqrt{-5}$, and $1-\sqrt{-5}$ are [associate](wikipedia://en/289666#Associatedness). It is not hard to show that all four factors are irreducible as well, though this may not be obvious.[^n6] See also *[Algebraic integer](wikipedia://en/98981)*.
- For a [square-free positive integer](wikipedia://en/29525) *d*, the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of $\mathbb Q[\sqrt{-d}]$ will fail to be a UFD unless *d* is one of the nine [Heegner numbers](wikipedia://en/522147).
- The ring of formal power series over the complex numbers is a UFD, but the [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of those that converge everywhere, in other words the ring of [entire functions](wikipedia://en/10372) in a single complex variable, is not a UFD, since there exist entire functions with an infinity of zeros, and thus an infinity of irreducible factors, while a UFD factorization must be finite, e.g.:

$$\sin \pi z = \pi z \prod_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(1-{{z^2}\over{n^2}}\right).$$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Some concepts defined for integers can be generalized to UFDs:

- In UFDs, every [irreducible element](wikipedia://en/60693) is [prime](wikipedia://en/60692). (In any integral domain, every prime element is irreducible, but the converse does not always hold. For instance, the element *z* ∈ *K*\[*x*, *y*, *z*\]/(*z*<sup>2</sup> − *xy*) is irreducible, but not prime.) Note that this has a partial converse: a domain satisfying the [ACCP](wikipedia://en/22243102) is a UFD if and only if every irreducible element is prime.
- Any two elements of a UFD have a [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) and a [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961). Here, a greatest common divisor of *a* and *b* is an element *d* that [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) both *a* and *b*, and such that every other common divisor of *a* and *b* divides *d*. All greatest common divisors of *a* and *b* are [associated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_element).
- Any UFD is [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209). In other words, if *R* is a UFD with [quotient field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_field) *K*, and if an element *k* in *K* is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_root) of a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) with [coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficients) in *R*, then *k* is an element of *R*.
- Let *S* be a [multiplicatively closed subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicatively_closed_subset) of a UFD A not containing 0. Then the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) *S*<sup>−1</sup>*A* is a UFD. A partial converse to this also holds; see below.

:::

## Equivalent conditions for a ring to be a UFD {#Equivalent_conditions_for_a_ring_to_be_a_UFD}

A [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) integral domain is a UFD if and only if every [height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Height_%28ring_theory%29) 1 [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) is principal (a proof is given at the end). Also, a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) is a UFD if and only if its [ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907) is trivial. In this case, it is in fact a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039).

In general, for an integral domain *A*, the following conditions are equivalent:

1. *A* is a UFD.
1. Every nonzero [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) of *A* contains a [prime element](wikipedia://en/60692).
1. *A* satisfies [ascending chain condition on principal ideals](wikipedia://en/22243102) (ACCP), and the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) *S*<sup>−1</sup>*A* is a UFD, where *S* is a [multiplicatively closed subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicatively_closed_subset) of *A* generated by prime elements. (Nagata criterion)
1. *A* satisfies [ACCP](wikipedia://en/22243102) and every [irreducible](wikipedia://en/60693) is [prime](wikipedia://en/60692).
1. *A* is [atomic](wikipedia://en/9599592) and every [irreducible](wikipedia://en/60693) is [prime](wikipedia://en/60692).
1. *A* is a [GCD domain](wikipedia://en/2932361) satisfying [ACCP](wikipedia://en/22243102).
1. *A* is a [Schreier domain](wikipedia://en/22284043),[^o18] and [atomic](wikipedia://en/9599592).
1. *A* is a [pre-Schreier domain](wikipedia://en/22284043) and [atomic](wikipedia://en/9599592).
1. *A* has a [divisor theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisor_theory) in which every divisor is principal.
1. *A* is a [Krull domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Krull_domain) in which every [divisorial ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisorial_ideal) is principal (in fact, this is the definition of UFD in Bourbaki.)
1. *A* is a Krull domain and every prime ideal of height 1 is principal.[^n7]

In practice, (2) and (3) are the most useful conditions to check. For example, it follows immediately from (2) that a PID is a UFD, since every prime ideal is generated by a prime element in a PID.

For another example, consider a Noetherian integral domain in which every height one prime ideal is principal. Since every prime ideal has finite height, it contains a height one prime ideal (induction on height) that is principal. By (2), the ring is a UFD.
