# Impossible trident

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Impossible trident"}

*Image: Poiuyt--opaque (rotated).svg.* An impossible trident with backgrounds, to enhance the illusion

*Image: RogerHaywardUndecidable Monument.jpg.* [Roger Hayward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Hayward)'s *Undecidable Monument*

An **impossible trident**,[^o2] also known as an **impossible fork**, **blivet**,[^o4] **poiuyt**,[^o7] or **devil's tuning fork**,[^o5] is a drawing of an [impossible object](wikipedia://en/56110) (undecipherable figure), a kind of an [optical illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_illusion). It appears to have three cylindrical prongs at one end which then mysteriously transform into two rectangular prongs at the other end.

:::

In 1964, D. H. Schuster reported that he noticed an ambiguous figure of a new kind in the advertising section of an aviation journal. He dubbed it a "three-stick [clevis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clevis)". He described the novelty as follows: "Unlike other ambiguous drawings, an actual shift in visual fixation is involved in its perception and resolution."[^o6] The word "poiuyt" appeared on the March 1965 cover[^o7] of *[Mad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mad_%28magazine%29)* magazine bearing the four-eyed [Alfred E. Neuman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_E._Neuman) balancing the impossible fork on his finger with caption "Introducing 'The Mad Poiuyt (the last six letters on the top row of [QWERTY](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QWERTY) typewriters, right to left). An anonymously contributed version described as a "hole location gauge" was printed in the June 1964 issue of *[Analog Science Fiction and Fact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_Science_Fiction_and_Fact)*, with the comment that "this outrageous piece of draftsmanship evidently escaped from the Finagle & Diddle Engineering Works".[^o8] Subsequently, a correspondent revealed that he had encountered the type of figure about twenty years previously, and had used it as a business logo since 1952.[^o9]

The term "blivet" for the impossible fork was popularized by *[Worm Runner's Digest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worm_Runner's_Digest)* magazine. In 1967, Harold Baldwin published there an article, "Building better blivets", in which he described the rules for the construction of drawings based on the impossible fork.[^o10][^o5] In December 1968, American optical designer and artist [Roger Hayward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Hayward) wrote a humorous submission "Blivets—Research and Development" for *The Worm Runner's Digest* in which he presented various drawings based on the blivet,[^o11][^o12] with a short sequel the following year.[^o13] The article was reprinted later in an anthology; in an introduction to the article, [James V. McConnell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_V._McConnell) "explained" the term as follows: "The blivet was first discovered in 1892 in Pfulingen, Germany, by a cross-eyed dwarf named Erasmus Wolfgang Blivet."[^o14] Hayward also published another sequel,*Blivets—the Makin's*.[^o15]
