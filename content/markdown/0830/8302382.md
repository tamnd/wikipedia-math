# Contraharmonic mean

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Contraharmonic mean"}

In mathematics, a **contraharmonic mean** (or **antiharmonic mean**[^o18]) is a function complementary to the [harmonic mean](wikipedia://en/14463). The contraharmonic [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) is a [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010) of the [Lehmer mean](wikipedia://en/7422265), $L_p$, where *p* = 2.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The contraharmonic mean of a set of positive [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)[^o4] is defined as the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of the squares of the numbers divided by the arithmetic mean of the numbers:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{C}\left(x_1, x_2, \dots, x_n\right) &= {{1 \over n} \left(x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2\right) \over {1 \over n}\left(x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n\right)}, \\[3pt] &= {{x_1^2 + x_2^2 + \cdots + x_n^2} \over {x_1 + x_2 + \cdots + x_n}}. \end{aligned}$$

### Two-variable formulae {#Two-variable_formulae}

From the formulas for the arithmetic mean and harmonic mean of two variables we have:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{A}(a, b) &= {{a + b} \over 2} \\ \operatorname{H}(a, b) &= {1 \over {{1 \over 2} \cdot {\left({1 \over a} + {1 \over b}\right)}}} = {{2ab} \over {a + b}} \\ \operatorname{C}(a, b) &= 2 \cdot A(a ,b) - H(a, b) \\ &= a + b - {{2ab} \over {a + b}} = {{(a + b)^2 - 2ab} \over {a + b}} \\ &= {{a^2 + b^2} \over {a + b}} \end{aligned}$$

Notice that for two variables the average of the harmonic and contraharmonic means is exactly equal to the arithmetic mean:

As *a* gets closer to 0 then **H**(*a*, *b*) also gets closer to 0. The harmonic mean is very sensitive to low values. On the other hand, the contraharmonic mean is sensitive to larger values, so as *a* approaches 0 then **C**(*a*, *b*) approaches *b* (so their average remains **A**(*a*, *b*)).

There are two other notable relationships between 2-variable means. First, the geometric mean of the arithmetic and harmonic means is equal to the geometric mean of the two values:

$$\operatorname{G}(\operatorname{A}(a, b), \operatorname{H}(a, b)) = \operatorname{G}\left({{a + b} \over 2}, {{2ab} \over {a + b}}\right) = \sqrt{{{a + b}\over 2} \cdot {{2ab} \over {a + b}}} = \sqrt{ab} = \operatorname{G}(a, b)$$

The second relationship is that the geometric mean of the arithmetic and contraharmonic means is the root mean square:

$$\begin{aligned} &\operatorname{G}\left(\operatorname{A}(a, b), \operatorname{C}(a, b)\right) ={} \operatorname{G}\left({{a + b} \over 2}, {{a^2 + b^2} \over {a + b}}\right) \\ ={} &\sqrt{{{a + b} \over 2} \cdot {{a^2 + b^2} \over {a + b}}} ={} \sqrt{{{a^2 + b^2} \over 2}} \\[2pt] ={} &\operatorname{R}(a, b) \end{aligned}$$

The contraharmonic mean of two variables can be constructed geometrically using a trapezoid.[^o6]

### Additional constructions {#Additional_constructions}

The contraharmonic mean can be constructed on a circle similar to the way the [Pythagorean means](wikipedia://en/3314539) of two variables are constructed.[^o8] The contraharmonic mean is the remainder of the diameter on which the harmonic mean lies.[^o9]

:::

## History {#History}

The contraharmonic mean was discovered by the Greek mathematician [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388) in the 4th century BCE.[^o11]

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The contraharmonic mean satisfies characteristic properties of a [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of some list of positive values $\mathbf{x}$:

- $$\min\left(\mathbf{x}\right) \le \operatorname{C}\left(\mathbf{x}\right) \le \max\left(\mathbf{x}\right)$$
- $$\operatorname{C}\left(t \cdot \mathbf{x}_1, t \cdot \mathbf{x}_2,\, \dots,\, t \cdot \mathbf{x}_n\right) = t \cdot C\left(\mathbf{x}_1, \mathbf{x}_2,\, \dots,\, \mathbf{x}_n\right)\text{ for }t > 0$$

The first property implies the *fixed point property*, that for all *k* > 0,

It is not [monotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonic) − increasing a value of $\mathbf{x}$ can *decrease* the value of the contraharmonic mean. For instance $C(1, 4) > C(2, 4)$.

The contraharmonic mean is higher in value than the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) and also higher than the [root mean square](wikipedia://en/66819):

$$\min(\mathbf{x}) \leq \operatorname{H}(\mathbf{x}) \leq \operatorname{G}(\mathbf{x}) \leq \operatorname{L}(\mathbf{x}) \leq \operatorname{A}(\mathbf{x}) \leq \operatorname{R}(\mathbf{x}) \leq \operatorname{C}(\mathbf{x}) \leq \max(\mathbf{x})$$

where **x** is a list of values, **H** is the harmonic mean, **G** is [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046), **L** is the [logarithmic mean](wikipedia://en/4490764), **A** is the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612), **R** is the [root mean square](wikipedia://en/66819) and **C** is the contraharmonic mean. Unless all values of **x** are the same, the ≤ signs above can be replaced by \<.

The name *contraharmonic* may be due to the fact that when taking the mean of only two variables, the contraharmonic mean is as high above the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) as the arithmetic mean is above the harmonic mean (i.e., the arithmetic mean of the two variables is equal to the arithmetic mean of their harmonic and contraharmonic means).

### Relationship to arithmetic mean and variance {#Relationship_to_arithmetic_mean_and_variance}

The contraharmonic mean of a random variable is equal to the sum of the arithmetic mean and the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) divided by the arithmetic mean.[^o15]

$$\operatorname{C}(\mathbf{x}) = \operatorname{A}(\mathbf{x}) + \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\mathbf{x})}{\operatorname{A}(\mathbf{x})}$$

The [ratio of the variance and the arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/16693606) was proposed as a test statistic by Clapham.[^o16]

Since the variance is always ≥0 the contraharmonic mean is always greater than or equal to the arithmetic mean.

### Other relationships {#Other_relationships}

Any integer contraharmonic mean of two different positive integers is the hypotenuse of a [Pythagorean triple](wikipedia://en/24172), while any hypotenuse of a Pythagorean triple is a contraharmonic mean of two different positive integers.[^o18]

It is also related to Katz's statistic[^o19]

$$J_n = \sqrt { \frac { n } { 2 } } \frac { s^2 - m } { m }$$

where *m* is the mean, *s*<sup>2</sup> the variance and *n* is the sample size.

*J*<sub>n</sub> is asymptotically normally distributed with a mean of zero and variance of 1.

:::

## Uses in statistics {#Uses_in_statistics}

The problem of a size biased sample was discussed by Cox in 1969 on a problem of sampling fibres. The [expectation](wikipedia://en/9653) of size biased sample is equal to its contraharmonic mean,[^o21] and the contraharmonic mean is also used to estimate bias fields in *multiplicative* models, rather than the arithmetic mean as used in *additive* models.[^o22]

The contraharmonic mean can be used to average the intensity value of neighbouring [pixels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel) in graphing, so as to reduce noise in images and make them clearer to the eye.[^o23]

The probability of a fibre being sampled is proportional to its length. Because of this the usual sample mean (arithmetic mean) is a biased estimator of the true mean. To see this consider

$$g(x) = \frac{x f(x)}{m}$$

where *f*(*x*) is the true population distribution, *g*(*x*) is the length weighted distribution and *m* is the sample mean. Taking the usual expectation of the mean here gives the contraharmonic mean rather than the usual (arithmetic) mean of the sample.[^o24] This problem can be overcome by taking instead the expectation of the harmonic mean (1/*x*). The expectation and variance of 1/*x* are

$$\operatorname{E}\left[ \frac{1}{x} \right] = \frac{1}{m}$$

and has variance

$$\operatorname{Var} \left( \frac{1}{x} \right) = \frac{m E\left[\frac{1}{x} - 1\right]}{nm^2}$$

where $E$ is the expectation operator. Asymptotically $E[1/x]$ is distributed normally.

The asymptotic efficiency of length biased sampling depends compared to random sampling on the underlying distribution. if *f*(*x*) is [log normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Log_normal) the efficiency is 1 while if the population is [gamma distributed](wikipedia://en/207079) with index *b*, the efficiency is $b/(b - 1)$. This distribution has been used in modelling consumer behaviour[^o25] as well as quality sampling.

It has been used longside the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) in transport planning in the form of its inverse.[^o26]
