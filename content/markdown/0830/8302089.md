# Alchian–Allen effect

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alchian–Allen effect"}

The **Alchian–Allen effect** was described in 1964 by [Armen Alchian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armen_Alchian) and [William R Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_R._Allen_%28economist%29) in the book *University Economics* (now called *Exchange and Production*[^o2]). It states that when the prices of two [substitute goods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Substitute_good), such as high and low grades of the same product, are both increased by a fixed per-unit amount such as a transportation cost or a lump-sum tax, consumption will shift toward the higher-grade product. This is because the added per-unit amount decreases the relative price of the higher-grade product.

:::

Suppose, for example, that high-grade coffee beans are \$3/pound and low-grade beans \$1.50/pound; in this example, high-grade beans cost twice as much as low-grade beans. If a per-pound international shipping cost of \$1 is added, the effective prices are now \$4 and \$2.50: High-grade beans now cost only 1.6 times as much as low-grade beans. This reduced ratio of difference will induce distant coffee-buyers to now choose a higher ratio of high-to-low grade beans than local coffee-buyers.

Another example is that the effect would predict that Australians drink higher-quality Californian wine than Californians, and vice versa, because it is only worth the transportation costs for the most expensive wine.[^o3]

The effect has been studied as it applies to illegal drugs and it has been shown that the potency of marijuana increased in response to higher enforcement budgets,[^o4] and there was a similar effect for alcohol in the U.S. during [Prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prohibition).[^o5] This effect is called [iron law of prohibition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron_law_of_prohibition).

Colloquially, the Alchian–Allen theorem is also known as the “shipping the good apples out” theorem ([Thomas Borcherding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Borcherding)),[^o6] or as the “third law of demand.”[^o7]
