# Mathematica: A World of Numbers... and Beyond

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematica: A World of Numbers... and Beyond"}

*Image: Mathematica exhibit - mobius strip.jpg.* Large [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817) with traveling arrow

***Mathematica: A World of Numbers... and Beyond*** is a [kinetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kinetic_art) and static exhibition of mathematical concepts designed by [Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Eames) and [Ray Eames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Eames), originally debuted at the [California Museum of Science and Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Museum_of_Science_and_Industry) in 1961. Duplicates have since been made, and they, as well as the original, have been moved to other institutions.[^o2]

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Mathematica exhibit - mathematical relationships.jpg.* Explanatory panel about mathematical relationships and their graphic counterparts

In March, 1961 a new science wing at the [California Museum of Science and Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California_Museum_of_Science_and_Industry)[^o4] in Los Angeles opened. The [IBM Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) had been asked by the museum to make a contribution; IBM in turn asked the famous California designer team of [Charles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Eames) and [Ray Eames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_Eames) to come up with a good proposal. The result was that the Eames Office was commissioned by IBM to design an interactive exhibition called *Mathematica: A World of Numbers... and Beyond*.[^o5][^o2] This was the first of many exhibitions designed by the Eames Office.[^o6]

The 3000 sqft exhibition stayed at the museum until January 1998, making it the longest running of any corporate sponsored museum exhibition.[^o7][^o2] Furthermore, it is the only one of the dozens of exhibitions designed by the Office of [Charles and Ray Eames](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_and_Ray_Eames) that is still extant.[^o6] This original *Mathematica* exhibition was reassembled for display at the Alyce de Roulet Williamson Gallery at [Art Center College of Design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Center_College_of_Design) in [Pasadena, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pasadena,_California), July 30 through October 1, 2000. From October 6, 2001 to May 5, 2002, it was exhibited at the [Exploratorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exploratorium) in San Francisco.[^o8]

After 2004, it is now owned by and on display at the [New York Hall of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_Hall_of_Science) in [Queens, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queens,_New_York), though it currently lacks the overhead plaques with quotations from mathematicians that were part of the original installation.[^o9]

### Duplicates {#Duplicates}

In November, 1961 an exact duplicate was made for Chicago's [Museum of Science and Industry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Science_and_Industry_%28Chicago%29), where it was shown until late 1980. From there it was sold and relocated to the [Museum of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Science_%28Boston%29) in [Boston, Massachusetts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boston,_Massachusetts), where it is permanently on display in the Theater of Electricity.[^o11] The Boston installation bears the closest resemblance to the original Eames design, including numerous overhead plaques featuring historic quotations from famous mathematicians. As part of a refurbishment, a graphic panel was added to supplement the original History Wall timeline, to recognize the contributions of both men and women mathematicians of the late 20th and early 21st centuries.[^o11]

A third copy was made for the IBM Pavilion at the [1964/1965 New York World's Fair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1964_New_York_World's_Fair).[^o12] Subsequently, it was briefly on display in [Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan), and was then installed in the [Pacific Science Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Science_Center) in [Seattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle) where it stayed until 1980. It was briefly re-installed in New York City at the [590 Madison Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/590_Madison_Avenue) IBM Headquarters Building, before being moved to [SciTrek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SciTrek) in [Atlanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlanta) in 1998, but that organization was shut down in 2004 due to funding cuts. The exhibit was then shipped to [Petaluma, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petaluma,_California) to Lucia Eames, the daughter of the original designers. In 2015, the exhibit was acquired by the [Henry Ford Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Ford_Museum) in [Dearborn, Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dearborn,_Michigan),[^o13] where it is now on display.[^o14]

## Exhibits {#Exhibits}

*Image: Multiplication Machine.jpg.* Interactive multiplication machine

*Image: The Pseudosphere.jpg.* [Pseudosphere](wikipedia://en/60766) model

Some of the displays are minimally interactive, in that they start to operate at the push of a button. Other displays are motorized and run continuously, or operate automatically on a fixed cycle as long as electrical power is supplied. The moving display elements combine with noise made by balls falling through the probability machine to fill the exhibit space with an atmosphere of continuous activity.

- Large-scale [bean machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bean_machine) showing the [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876) as an approximation of the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542)
- [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817) with a motorized red arrow that can trace a complete circuit of the one-sided surface
- A curved funnel-shaped surface modeling a [gravitational well](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_well) using [ball bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_bearing) orbiting in ellipses
- A three-dimensional cube illustrating the concept of [multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_%28mathematics%29)
- [Soap bubbles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_bubble) and films, as examples of [minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734)
- [Mechanical linkages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_linkage), such as the [Sarrus linkage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sarrus_linkage)
- [Topological surfaces](wikipedia://en/29954), such as the [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412)
- Models showing [perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspective_%28visual%29) and geometric [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097)
- An arrangement of strings and lights demonstrating [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673)
- An automated dice game demonstrating [random walk](wikipedia://en/235451)
- "Image Wall" of beautiful [mathematics and art](wikipedia://en/22468661) images, such as the [Fibonacci series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_series) and the [Golden Spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Spiral)[^o11]
- "History Wall" [timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline) of mathematical thought and discoveries, and contributions from different world cultures[^o11]

In addition, large placards hang from the ceiling, carrying interesting quotations from famous mathematicians. Some installations have omitted this feature, although it was an integral part of the original exhibition.

Five "Mathematical Peepshow" animated movies were created for the IBM Pavilion at the 1964/1965 New York World's Fair. They covered [Eratosthenes](wikipedia://en/46117), [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741), [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), and [exponential growth](wikipedia://en/9678).[^o16] The movies are now viewable on YouTube and other websites.

## *Men of Modern Mathematics* poster {#Men_of_Modern_Mathematics_poster}

In 1966, five years after the opening of the Mathematica Exhibit, IBM published a 2 x [timeline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline) poster, titled *Men of Modern Mathematics*. It was based on the items displayed on the exhibit's History Wall, and free copies were distributed to schools. The timeline covered the period from 1000 AD to approximately 1950 AD, and the poster featured biographical and historical items, along with numerous pictures showing progress in various areas of science, including architecture. The mathematical items in this chart were prepared by Professor [Raymond Redheffer](wikipedia://en/22117165) of [UCLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California_Los_Angeles).[^o18] Long after the chart was distributed, mathematics departments around the world have proudly displayed this chart on their walls.[^o18][^o20]

In 2012, IBM Corporation released a free [iPad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPad) application, *Minds of Modern Mathematics*, based on the poster but updated to the present, including expanded coverage of [women mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_women_in_mathematics). The app was developed by IBM with the assistance of the Eames Office.[^o19][^o20]
