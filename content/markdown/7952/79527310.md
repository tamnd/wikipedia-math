# Bitensor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bitensor"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), a **bitensor** (or **bi-tensor**[^o2]) is a [tensorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensorial) object that depends on two points in a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), as opposed to ordinary [tensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensors) which depend on a single point.[^o3] Bitensors provide a framework for describing relationships between different points in [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) and are used in the study of various phenomena in [curved spacetime](wikipedia://en/1016422).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A *bitensor* is a tensorial object that depends on two points in a manifold, rather than on a single point as ordinary tensors do.[^o6] A *bitensor field* $B$ can be formally defined as a [map](wikipedia://en/516931) from the [product manifold](wikipedia://en/24104095) to an appropriate [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $B: M \times M \to V$, where $M$ is a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) and $V$ is the vector space corresponding to the [tensor space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_space) being considered.[^o6][^o3]

In the language of [fiber bundles](wikipedia://en/276526), a bitensor of type $(r,s,r',s')$ is defined as a [section](wikipedia://en/367621) of the [exterior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_product) [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) bundle $T^r_s M \boxtimes T^{r'}_{s'} M$, where $T^r_s M$ denotes the tensor bundle of [rank](wikipedia://en/29965#definition) $(r,s)$ and $\boxtimes$ represents the exterior tensor product $B \in \Gamma(T^r_s M \boxtimes T^{r'}_{s'} M)$, where $\Gamma$ denotes the space of sections.[^o6]

The exterior tensor product bundle is constructed as $\mathcal{V}_1 \boxtimes \mathcal{V}_2 = \mathrm{pr}_1^* \mathcal{V}_1 \otimes \mathrm{pr}_2^* \mathcal{V}_2$ where $\mathrm{pr}_i$ are [projection operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_operator) that project onto the respective factors of the product manifold $M \times M$, and $\mathrm{pr}_i^*$ denotes the [pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508) of the respective bundles.[^o6]

In coordinate notation, a bitensor $T$ with components $T^{\mu\nu'\ldots}_{\alpha\beta'\ldots}(x,y)$ has indices associated with two different points $x$ and $y$ in the manifold. By convention, unprimed indices (such as $\mu$, $\alpha$) refer to the first point, while primed indices (such as $\nu'$, $\beta'$) refer to the second point. The simplest example of a bitensor is a *biscalar field*, which is a scalar function of two points. Applications include [parallel transport](wikipedia://en/285595), [heat kernels](wikipedia://en/3904336), and various [Green's functions](wikipedia://en/311001) employed in quantum field theory in curved spacetime.[^o6][^o3]

:::

## History {#History}

The concept of bitensors was first formally developed by mathematician [Harold Stanley Ruse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Stanley_Ruse) in his 1931 paper *An Absolute Partial Differential Calculus*, published in the [Quarterly Journal of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarterly_Journal_of_Mathematics). Ruse introduced bitensors as a generalization of [tensor calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_calculus) to functions of two sets of variables, drawing an analogy with [partial differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differentiation) in [elementary calculus](wikipedia://en/5176). He developed the formalism for bitensor transformations, [covariant derivatives](wikipedia://en/431848), and scalar connections, establishing the foundation for what he termed an "absolute partial differential calculus."[^o8][^o9]
