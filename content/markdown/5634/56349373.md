# Markov theorem

*Image: Alexander closure of a braid.svg.* Braid closure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Markov theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) the **Markov theorem** gives necessary and sufficient conditions for two [braids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_%28mathematics%29) to have closures that are equivalent [knots](wikipedia://en/366808) or [links](wikipedia://en/1161784). The conditions are stated in terms of the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) structures on braids.

:::

Braids are algebraic objects described by diagrams; the relation to topology is given by [Alexander's theorem](wikipedia://en/45513201) which states that every [knot](wikipedia://en/366808) or [link](wikipedia://en/1161784) in three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is the closure of a [braid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_%28mathematics%29). The Markov theorem, proved by Russian mathematician [Andrei Andreevich Markov Jr.](wikipedia://en/14457637)[^o2] describes the elementary moves generating the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on braids given by the equivalence of their closures.

More precisely Markov's theorem can be stated as follows:[^o3][^o4] given two braids represented by elements $\beta_n, \beta_m'$ in the braid groups $B_n, B_m$, their closures are equivalent links if and only if $\beta_m'$ can be obtained from applying to $\beta_n$ a sequence of the following operations:

1. [conjugating](wikipedia://en/49176) $\beta_n$ in $B_n$;
1. replacing $\beta_n$ by $\beta_n\sigma_{n}^{\pm 1} \in B_{n+1}$ (here $\sigma_i$ are the standard generators of the braid groups; geometrically this amounts to adding a strand to the right of the braid diagram and twisting it once with the (previously) last strand);
1. the inverse of the previous operation (if $\beta_n = \beta_{n-1}\sigma_{n-1}^{\pm 1}$ with $\beta_{n-1} \in B_{n-1}$ replace with $\beta_{n-1}$).

In 1974 American mathematician [Joan Birman](wikipedia://en/8625257) published a [monograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph), *[Braids, Links, and Mapping Class Groups](wikipedia://en/66274280)*, based on a graduate course she taught as a visiting professor at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in 1971–72; this book contains the first complete proof of the Markov theorem.[^o5]
