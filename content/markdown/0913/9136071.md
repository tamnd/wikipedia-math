# Axis–angle representation

3D rotation group

*Image: Angle axis vector.svg.* The angle $\theta$ and axis unit vector $\mathbf{e}$ define a rotation, concisely represented by the rotation vector $\theta\mathbf{e}$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Axis–angle representation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **axis–angle representation** parameterizes a [rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_%28geometry%29) in a [three-dimensional](wikipedia://en/3054853) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) by two quantities: a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) $\mathbf{e}$ indicating the [direction](wikipedia://en/54516626) of an [axis of rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_of_rotation), and an [angle of rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_rotation) $\theta$ describing the magnitude and sense (e.g., [clockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clockwise)) of the [rotation about the axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_around_a_fixed_axis). Only two numbers, not three, are needed to define the direction of a unit vector $\mathbf{e}$ rooted at the origin because the magnitude of $\mathbf{e}$ is constrained. For example, the [elevation and azimuth](wikipedia://en/48386) angles of $\mathbf{e}$ suffice to locate it in any particular Cartesian coordinate frame.

:::

By [Rodrigues' rotation formula](wikipedia://en/1525933), the angle and axis determine a transformation that rotates three-dimensional vectors. The rotation occurs in the sense prescribed by the [right-hand rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule).

The rotation axis is sometimes called the *[Euler axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_axis)*. The axis–angle representation is predicated on [Euler's rotation theorem](wikipedia://en/865138), which dictates that any rotation or sequence of rotations of a rigid body in a three-dimensional space is equivalent to a pure rotation about a single fixed axis.

It is one of many [rotation formalisms in three dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_formalisms_in_three_dimensions).

## Rotation vector {#Rotation_vector}

The axis–angle representation is equivalent to the more concise **rotation vector**, also called the **Euler vector** (not to be confused with a vector of [Euler angles](wikipedia://en/411492)). In this case, both the rotation axis and the angle are represented by a vector codirectional with the rotation axis whose length is the rotation angle $\theta$,

$$\boldsymbol{\theta} = \theta \mathbf{e} \,.$$

It is used for the [exponential](wikipedia://en/672731) and [logarithm](wikipedia://en/2931687) maps involving this representation.

Many rotation vectors correspond to the same rotation. In particular, a rotation vector of length $\theta + 2\pi M$, for any integer $M$, encodes exactly the same rotation as a rotation vector of length $\theta$. Thus, there are at least a countable infinity of rotation vectors corresponding to any rotation. Furthermore, all rotations by $2\pi M$ are the same as no rotation at all, so, for a given integer $M$, all rotation vectors of length $2\pi M$, in all directions, constitute a two-parameter uncountable infinity of rotation vectors encoding the same rotation as the zero vector. These facts must be taken into account when inverting the exponential map, that is, when finding a rotation vector that corresponds to a given rotation matrix. The exponential map is *onto* but not *one-to-one*.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Say you are standing on the ground and you pick the direction of gravity to be the negative $z$ direction. Then if you turn to your left, you will rotate $\tfrac{-\pi}{2}$ radians (or [\-90°](wikipedia://en/76956)) about the $-z$ axis. Viewing the axis-angle representation as an [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362), this would be

$$( \mathrm{axis}, \mathrm{angle} ) = \left( \begin{bmatrix} e_x \\ e_y \\ e_z \end{bmatrix},\theta \right) = \left( \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ -1 \end{bmatrix},\frac{-\pi}{2}\right).$$

The above example can be represented as a rotation vector with a magnitude of $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ pointing in the $z$ direction,

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ 0 \\ \frac{\pi}{2} \end{bmatrix}.$$

## Uses {#Uses}

The axis–angle representation is convenient when dealing with [rigid-body dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigid-body_dynamics). It is useful to both characterize [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789), and also for converting between different representations of rigid body [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_%28physics%29), such as homogeneous transformations and twists.

When a [rigid body](wikipedia://en/455769) rotates [around a fixed axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_around_a_fixed_axis), its axis–angle data are a [constant](wikipedia://en/320861) rotation axis and the rotation angle [continuously dependent](wikipedia://en/6122) on [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time).

Plugging the three eigenvalues 1 and $e^{\pm i\theta}$ and their associated three orthogonal axes in a Cartesian representation into [Mercer's theorem](wikipedia://en/303990) is a convenient construction of the Cartesian representation of the Rotation Matrix in three dimensions.

:::

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Application"}

## Application {#Application}

[Rodrigues' rotation formula](wikipedia://en/1525933), named after [Olinde Rodrigues](wikipedia://en/1003283), is an efficient algorithm for rotating a [Euclidean vector](wikipedia://en/32533), given a rotation axis and an angle of rotation. In other words, Rodrigues' formula provides an algorithm to compute the exponential map from $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ to $\operatorname{SO}(3)$ without computing the full matrix exponential.

If $\mathbf{v}$ is a vector in $\mathbf{R}^{3}$ and $\mathbf{e}$ is a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) rooted at the origin describing an axis of rotation about which $\mathbf{v}$ is rotated by an angle $\theta$, Rodrigues' rotation formula to obtain the rotated vector is

$$\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{rot} = \mathbf{v} + (\sin\theta) (\mathbf{e} \times \mathbf{v}) + (1 - \cos\theta) (\mathbf{e} \times (\mathbf{e} \times \mathbf{v})) \,.$$

For the rotation of a single vector it may be more efficient than converting $\mathbf{e}$ and $\theta$ into a rotation matrix to rotate the vector.

:::

## Relationship to other representations {#Relationship_to_other_representations}

There are several ways to represent a rotation. It is useful to understand how different representations relate to one another, and how to convert between them. Here the unit vector is denoted $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ instead of $\mathbf{e}$.

### Exponential map from 𝔰𝔬(3) to SO(3) {#Exponential_map_from_𝔰𝔬(3)_to_SO(3)}

The [exponential map](wikipedia://en/44612290) effects a transformation from the axis-angle representation of rotations to [rotation matrices](wikipedia://en/856005),

$$\exp\colon \mathfrak{so}(3) \to \mathrm{SO}(3) \,.$$

Essentially, by using a [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) one derives a closed-form relation between these two representations. Given a unit vector $\boldsymbol\omega \in \mathfrak{so}(3) = \mathbb{R}^3$ representing the unit rotation axis, and an angle, $\theta \in \mathbf{R}$, an equivalent rotation matrix $R$ is given as follows, where $\mathbf{K}$ is the [cross product matrix](wikipedia://en/157092#Conversion_to_matrix_multiplication) of $\boldsymbol{\omega}$, that is, $\mathbf{Kv} = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}$ for all vectors $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbf{R}^{3}$,

$$R = \exp(\theta \mathbf{K}) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty\frac{(\theta \mathbf{K})^k}{k!} = I + \theta \mathbf{K} + \frac{1}{2!}(\theta \mathbf{K})^2 + \frac{1}{3!}(\theta \mathbf{K})^3 + \cdots$$

Because $\mathbf{K}$ is skew-symmetric, and the sum of the squares of its above-diagonal entries is 1, the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) $P(t)$ of $\mathbf{K}$ is $P(t) = \det(\mathbf{K} - t\mathbf{I}) = -(t^{3} + t)$. Since, by the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547), $P(\mathbf{K})$ = 0, this implies that

$$\mathbf{K}^3 = -\mathbf{K} \,.$$

As a result, $\mathbf{K}^{4} = -\mathbf{K}^{2}$, $\mathbf{K}^{5} = \mathbf{K}$, $\mathbf{K}^{6} = \mathbf{K}^{2}$, $\mathbf{K}^{7} = -\mathbf{K}$.

This cyclic pattern continues indefinitely, and so all higher powers of $\mathbf{K}$ can be expressed in terms of $\mathbf{K}$ and $\mathbf{K}^{2}$. Thus, from the above equation, it follows that

$$R = I + \left(\theta - \frac{\theta^3}{3!} + \frac{\theta^5}{5!} - \cdots\right) \mathbf{K} + \left(\frac{\theta^2}{2!} - \frac{\theta^4}{4!} + \frac{\theta^6}{6!} - \cdots\right) \mathbf{K}^2 \,,$$

that is,

$$R = I + (\sin\theta) \mathbf{K} + (1-\cos\theta) \mathbf{K}^2\, ,$$

by the [Taylor series formula for trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30448#Trigonometric_functions).

This is a Lie-algebraic derivation, in contrast to the geometric one in the article [Rodrigues' rotation formula](wikipedia://en/1525933).[^o11]

Due to the existence of the above-mentioned exponential map, the unit vector $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ representing the rotation axis, and the angle $\theta$ are sometimes called the *exponential coordinates* of the rotation matrix $R$.

### Log map from SO(3) to 𝔰𝔬(3) {#Log_map_from_SO(3)_to_𝔰𝔬(3)}

Let $\mathbf{K}$ continue to denote the 3 × 3 matrix that effects the cross product with the rotation axis $\boldsymbol{\omega}$: $\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{v}) = \boldsymbol{\omega} \times \mathbf{v}$ for all vectors $\mathbf{v}$ in what follows.

To retrieve the axis–angle representation of a [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005), calculate the angle of rotation from the [trace of the rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005#Determining_the_angle):

$$\theta = \arccos\left( \frac{\operatorname{Tr}(R) - 1}{2} \right)$$

and then use that to find the normalized axis,

$$\boldsymbol{\omega} = \frac{1}{2 \sin \theta} \begin{bmatrix} R_{32}-R_{23} \\ R_{13}-R_{31} \\ R_{21}-R_{12} \end{bmatrix} ~,$$

where $R_{ij}$ is the component of the rotation matrix, $R$, in the $i$-th row and $j$-th column.

This formula **does not work** for $\omega$ if $R$ is symmetric. Because, this is possible only when $\theta = k \pi$ for some integer $k$, so sin($\theta$) = 0, causing a division by 0 in the formula. However, the *limit* of the formula for $\omega$, as $\theta \to k \pi$, gives the correct value for $\omega$. For the general case the $\omega$ may also be found using null space of $R-I$, see [rotation matrix#Determining the axis](wikipedia://en/856005#Determining_the_axis).

The axis-angle representation is not unique since a rotation of $-\theta$ about $-\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is the same as a rotation of $\theta$ about $\boldsymbol{\omega}$. Of course, adding any integer multiple of 2π to $\theta$ will also result in the identical rotation; a better method is to constrain $\theta$ to the interval \[0, 2π) or (-π, π\].

If $\theta$ is sought for a known $(R,\omega)$ pair, then it **must be consistent** with the orientation of the $\omega$ axis (in other words, it must lie in the appropriate quadrant of the unit circle defined by $\sin \theta$ and $\cos \theta$). Considering that the cross-product matrix $\mathbf{K}$ is also available for a given $\omega$, this consistency can be ensured by generalizing the above trace formula as follows:[^o13]

$$\theta = {\rm atan2}(-{\rm Tr}(\mathbf{K} R), {\rm Tr}(R) - 1),$$

where [atan2](wikipedia://en/3856703) is the two-argument arctangent function, which provides the correct sign for $\theta$ in the $(-\pi,\pi]$ interval. This method resolves the sign ambiguity that exists in the off-axis terms of $R$.

The [matrix logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_logarithm) of the rotation matrix $R$ is

$$\log R = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } \theta = 0 \\ \dfrac{\theta}{2 \sin \theta } \left(R - R^\mathsf{T}\right) & \text{if } \theta \ne 0 \text{ and } \theta \in (-\pi, \pi) \end{cases}$$

An exception occurs when $R$ has [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) equal to −1. In this case, the log is not unique. However, even in the case where $\theta = \pi$ the [Frobenius norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_norm) of the log is

$$\| \log(R) \|_\mathrm{F} = \sqrt{2} |\theta |\,.$$

Given rotation matrices $A$ and $B$,

$$d_g(A,B) := \left\| \log\left(A^\mathsf{T} B\right)\right\|_\mathrm{F}$$

is the geodesic distance on the 3D manifold of rotation matrices.

For small rotations, the above computation of $\theta$ may be numerically imprecise as the derivative of arccos goes to infinity as $\theta \to 0$. In that case, the off-axis terms will actually provide better information about $\theta$ since, for small angles, $R \approx I + \theta\mathbf{K}$. (This is because these are the first two terms of the Taylor series for $\exp(\theta\mathbf{K})$.)

This formulation also has numerical problems at $\theta = \pi$, where the off-axis terms do not give information about the rotation axis (which is still defined up to a sign ambiguity). In that case, we must reconsider the above formula.

$$R = I + \mathbf{K} \sin\theta + \mathbf{K}^2 (1-\cos\theta)$$

At $\theta = \pi$, we have

$$R = I + 2 \mathbf{K}^2 = I + 2(\boldsymbol{\omega} \otimes \boldsymbol{\omega} - I) = 2 \boldsymbol{\omega} \otimes \boldsymbol{\omega} - I$$

and so let

$$B := \boldsymbol{\omega} \otimes \boldsymbol{\omega} = \frac{1}{2}(R+I) \,,$$

so the diagonal terms of $B$ are the squares of the elements of $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ and the signs (up to sign ambiguity) can be determined from the signs of the off-axis terms of $B$.

### Unit quaternions {#Unit_quaternions}

The following expression transforms axis–angle coordinates to [versors](wikipedia://en/2644621) (unit [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440)):

$$\mathbf q = \left(\cos\tfrac{\theta}{2}, \boldsymbol{\omega} \sin\tfrac{\theta}{2}\right)$$

Given a versor $\mathbf{q} = r + \mathbf{v}$ represented with its [scalar](wikipedia://en/51440#Scalar_and_vector_parts) $r$ and vector $\mathbf{v}$, the axis–angle coordinates can be extracted using the following:

$$\begin{aligned} \theta &= 2\arccos r \\[8px] \boldsymbol{\omega} &= \begin{cases} \dfrac{\mathbf{v}}{ \sin \tfrac{\theta}{2} }, & \text{if } \theta \neq 0 \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases} \end{aligned}$$

A more numerically stable expression of the rotation angle uses the [atan2](wikipedia://en/3856703) function:

$$\theta = 2 \operatorname{atan2}(|\mathbf{v}|,r)\,,$$

where $\lvert \mathbf{v}\rvert$ is the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm) of the 3-vector $\mathbf{v}$.
