# Universal embedding theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Universal embedding theorem"}

The **universal embedding theorem**, or **Krasner–Kaloujnine universal embedding theorem**, is a theorem from the mathematical discipline of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) first published in 1951 by [Marc Krasner](wikipedia://en/37126879) and [Lev Kaluznin](wikipedia://en/59686732).[^o2] The theorem states that any [group extension](wikipedia://en/345807) of a group $H$ by a group $A$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the regular [wreath product](wikipedia://en/146625) $A \operatorname{Wr} H.$ The theorem is named for the fact that the group $A \operatorname{Wr} H$ is said to be *[universal](wikipedia://en/32245)* with respect to all extensions of $H$ by $A.$

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $H$ and $A$ be groups, let $K = A^{H}$ be the set of all functions from $H$ to $A,$ and consider the [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of $H$ on itself by multiplication. This action extends naturally to an action of $H$ on $K$, defined as $(h\cdot \phi)(g)=\phi(h^{-1}g),$ where $\phi\in K,$ and $g$ and $h$ are both in $H.$ This is an automorphism of $K,$ so we can construct the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) $K \rtimes H$, which is termed the *regular [wreath product](wikipedia://en/146625)*, and denoted $A \operatorname{Wr} H$ or $A\wr H.$ The group $K = A^{H}$ (which is isomorphic to $\{(\phi,1)\in A\wr H:\phi\in K\}$) is called the *base group* of the wreath product.

The **Krasner–Kaloujnine universal embedding theorem** states that if $G$ has a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) $A$ and $H = G/A,$ then there is an [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) of groups $\theta:G\to A\wr H$ such that $A$ maps [surjectively](wikipedia://en/27873) onto $\text{im}(\theta)\cap K.$[^o5] This is equivalent to the wreath product $A \operatorname{Wr} H$ having a subgroup isomorphic to $G,$ where $G$ is any extension of $H$ by $A.$

:::

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

This proof comes from Dixon–Mortimer.[^o8]

Define a homomorphism $\psi:G\to H$ whose kernel is $A.$ Choose a set $T=\{t_u:u\in H\}$ of (right) coset representatives of $A$ in $G,$ where $\psi(t_u)=u.$ Then for all $x$ in $G,$ $t_u^{-1} x t_{\psi(x)^{-1}u}\in\ker \psi=A.$ For each $x$ in $G,$ we define a function $f_x:H\to A$ such that $f_x(u)=t_u^{-1} x t_{\psi(x)^{-1}u}.$ Then the embedding $\theta$ is given by $\theta(x)=(f_x,\psi(x))\in A\wr H.$

We now prove that this is a homomorphism. If $x$ and $y$ are in $G,$ then $\theta(x)\theta(y)=(f_x(\psi(x).f_y),\psi(xy)).$ Now $\psi(x).f_y(u)=f_y(\psi(x)^{-1}u),$ so for all $u$ in $H,$

$$f_x(u)(\psi(x).f_y(u)) = t_u^{-1} x t_{\psi(x)^{-1}u} t_{\psi(x)^{-1}u}^{-1} y t_{\psi(y)^{-1}\psi(x)^{-1}u}=t_u xy t^{-1}_{\psi(xy)^{-1}u},$$

so $f_{x} f_{y} = f_{xy}.$ Hence $\theta$ is a homomorphism as required.

The homomorphism is injective. If $\theta(x)=\theta(y),$ then both $f_{x}(u) = f_{y}(u)$ (for all *u*) and $\psi(x)=\psi(y).$ Then $t_u^{-1} x t_{\psi(x)^{-1}u}=t_u^{-1} y t_{\psi(y)^{-1}u},$ but we can cancel $t^{-1}_{u}$ and $t_{\psi(x)^{-1}u}=t_{\psi(y)^{-1}u}$ from both sides, so $x = y,$ hence $\theta$ is injective. Finally, $\theta(x)\in K$ precisely when $\psi(x)=1,$ in other words when $x\in A$ (as $A=\ker\psi$).

:::

## Generalizations and related results {#Generalizations_and_related_results}

- The [Krohn–Rhodes theorem](wikipedia://en/513506#Definitions_and_description_of_the_Krohn–Rhodes_theorem) is a statement similar to the universal embedding theorem, but for [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799). A semigroup $S$ is a *divisor* of a semigroup $T$ if it is the [image](wikipedia://en/71925) of a [subsemigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsemigroup) of $T$ under a homomorphism. The theorem states that every finite semigroup $S$ is a divisor of a finite alternating wreath product of finite [simple groups](wikipedia://en/47600) (each of which is a divisor of $S$) and finite [aperiodic semigroups](wikipedia://en/669994).
- An alternate version of the theorem exists which requires only a group $G$ and a subgroup $A$ (not necessarily normal).[^o10] In this case, $G$ is isomorphic to a subgroup of the regular wreath product $A \operatorname{Wr} (G/\operatorname{Core}(A)).$
