::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Regular complex polygon"}

*Image: Complex 1-topes as k-edges.svg.* Complex 1-polytopes represented in the [Argand plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argand_plane) as regular polygons for *p* = 2, 3, 4, 5, and 6, with black vertices. The centroid of the *p* vertices is shown seen in red. The sides of the polygons represent one application of the symmetry generator, mapping each vertex to the next counterclockwise copy. These polygonal sides are not edge elements of the polytope, as a complex 1-polytope can have no edges (it often *is* a complex edge) and only contains vertex elements.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **regular complex polygon** is a generalization of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) in [real space](wikipedia://en/1482083) to an analogous structure in a [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), where each real dimension is accompanied by an [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) one. A regular polygon exists in 2 real dimensions, $\mathbb{R}^2$, while a complex polygon exists in two complex dimensions, $\mathbb{C}^2$, which can be given real representations in 4 dimensions, $\mathbb{R}^4$, which then must be projected down to 2 or 3 real dimensions to be visualized. A *complex polygon* is generalized as a [complex polytope](wikipedia://en/8581934) in $\mathbb{C}^n$.

:::

A complex polygon may be understood as a collection of complex points, lines, planes, and so on, where every point is the junction of multiple lines, every line of multiple planes, and so on.

The *regular complex polygons* have been completely characterized, and can be described using a symbolic notation developed by [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter).

A *regular complex polygon* with all 2-edges can be represented by a [graph](wikipedia://en/12401), while forms with *k*-edges can only be related by [hypergraphs](wikipedia://en/232535). A *k*-edge can be seen as a set of vertices, with no order implied. They may be drawn with pairwise 2-edges, but this is not structurally accurate.

## Regular complex polygons {#Regular_complex_polygons}

While 1-polytopes can have unlimited *p*, finite regular complex polygons, excluding the double prism polygons <sub>*p*</sub>{4}<sub>2</sub>, are limited to 5-edge (pentagonal edges) elements, and infinite regular apeirogons also include 6-edge (hexagonal edges) elements.

### Notations {#Notations}

#### Shephard's modified Schläfli notation {#Shephard's_modified_Schläfli_notation}

[Shephard](wikipedia://en/33354532) originally devised a modified form of [Schläfli's notation](wikipedia://en/489394) for regular polytopes. For a polygon bounded by *p*<sub>1</sub>-edges, with a *p*<sub>2</sub>-set as vertex figure and overall symmetry group of order *g*, we denote the polygon as *p*<sub>1</sub>(*g*)*p*<sub>2</sub>.

The number of vertices *V* is then *g*/*p*<sub>2</sub> and the number of edges *E* is *g*/*p*<sub>1</sub>.

The complex polygon illustrated above has eight square edges (*p*<sub>1</sub>=4) and sixteen vertices (*p*<sub>2</sub>=2). From this we can work out that *g* = 32, giving the modified Schläfli symbol 4(32)2.

#### Coxeter's revised modified Schläfli notation {#Coxeter's_revised_modified_Schläfli_notation}

A more modern notation <sub>*p*<sub>1</sub></sub>{*q*}<sub>*p*<sub>2</sub></sub> is due to [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter),[^n2] and is based on [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890). As a symmetry group, its symbol is <sub>*p*<sub>1</sub></sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*p*<sub>2</sub></sub>.

The symmetry group <sub>*p*<sub>1</sub></sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*p*<sub>2</sub></sub> is represented by 2 generators R<sub>1</sub>, R<sub>2</sub>, where: R<sub>1</sub><sup>*p*<sub>1</sub></sup> = R<sub>2</sub><sup>*p*<sub>2</sub></sup> = I. If *q* is even, (R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>*q*/2</sup> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>*q*/2</sup>. If *q* is odd, (R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>(*q*−1)/2</sup>R<sub>2</sub> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>(*q*−1)/2</sup>R<sub>1</sub>. When *q* is odd, *p*<sub>1</sub>=*p*<sub>2</sub>.

For <sub>4</sub>\[4\]<sub>2</sub> has R<sub>1</sub><sup>4</sup> = R<sub>2</sub><sup>2</sup> = I, (R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>2</sup> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>2</sup>.

For <sub>3</sub>\[5\]<sub>3</sub> has R<sub>1</sub><sup>3</sup> = R<sub>2</sub><sup>3</sup> = I, (R<sub>2</sub>R<sub>1</sub>)<sup>2</sup>R<sub>2</sub> = (R<sub>1</sub>R<sub>2</sub>)<sup>2</sup>R<sub>1</sub>.

Coxeter enumerated this list of regular complex polygons in $\mathbb{C}^2$. A regular complex polygon, <sub>*p*</sub>{*q*}<sub>*r*</sub> or , has *p*-edges, and *r*-gonal [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719). <sub>*p*</sub>{*q*}<sub>*r*</sub> is a finite polytope if (*p* + *r*)*q* > *pr*(*q* − 2).

Its symmetry is written as <sub>*p*</sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*r*</sub>, called a *[Shephard group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shephard_group)*, analogous to a [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004), while also allowing [unitary reflections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_reflection).

For nonstarry groups, the order of the group <sub>*p*</sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*r*</sub> can be computed as $g = 8/q \cdot (1/p+2/q+1/r-1)^{-2}$.[^o15]

The [Coxeter number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_number) for <sub>*p*</sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*r*</sub> is $h = 2/(1/p+2/q+1/r-1)$, so the group order can also be computed as $g = 2h^2/q$. A regular complex polygon can be drawn in orthogonal projection with *h*-gonal symmetry.

The [dual polygon](wikipedia://en/19865549) of <sub>*p*</sub>{*q*}<sub>*r*</sub> is <sub>*r*</sub>{*q*}<sub>*p*</sub>. A polygon of the form <sub>*p*</sub>{*q*}<sub>*p*</sub> is self-dual. Groups of the form <sub>*p*</sub>\[2*q*\]<sub>2</sub> have a half symmetry <sub>*p*</sub>\[*q*\]<sub>*p*</sub>, so a regular polygon is the same as quasiregular . As well, regular polygon with the same node orders, , have an [alternated](wikipedia://en/4349117) construction , allowing adjacent edges to be two different colors.[^n4]

The group order, *g*, is used to compute the total number of vertices and edges. It will have *g*/*r* vertices, and *g*/*p* edges. When *p*=*r*, the number of vertices and edges are equal. This condition is required when *q* is odd.

### Visualizations of regular complex polygons {#Visualizations_of_regular_complex_polygons}

#### 2D graphs {#2D_graphs}

Polygons of the form <sub>*p*</sub>{2*r*}<sub>*q*</sub> can be visualized by *q* color sets of *p*-edge. Each *p*-edge is seen as a regular polygon, while there are no faces.

**Complex polygons <sub>2</sub>{*r*}<sub>*q*</sub>**

Polygons of the form <sub>2</sub>{4}<sub>*q*</sub> are called generalized [orthoplexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthoplex). They share vertices with the 4D *q*-*q* [duopyramids](wikipedia://en/36040636), vertices connected by 2-edges.

**Complex polygons <sub>*p*</sub>{4}<sub>2</sub>**

Polygons of the form <sub>*p*</sub>{4}<sub>2</sub> are called generalized [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783) (squares for polygons). They share vertices with the 4D *p*-*p* [duoprisms](wikipedia://en/2114155), vertices connected by p-edges. Vertices are drawn in green, and *p*-edges are drawn in alternate colors, red and blue. The perspective is distorted slightly for odd dimensions to move overlapping vertices from the center.

**Complex polygons <sub>*p*</sub>{*r*}<sub>2</sub>**

**Complex polygons <sub>*p*</sub>{*r*}<sub>*p*</sub>**

Polygons of the form <sub>*p*</sub>{*r*}<sub>*p*</sub> have equal number of vertices and edges. They are also self-dual.

#### 3D perspective {#3D_perspective}

3D [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405) projections of complex polygons <sub>*p*</sub>{4}<sub>2</sub> can show the point-edge structure of a complex polygon, while scale is not preserved.

The duals <sub>2</sub>{4}<sub>*p*</sub>: are seen by adding vertices inside the edges, and adding edges in place of vertices.

## Quasiregular polygons {#Quasiregular_polygons}
