# Plato's beard

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Plato's beard"}

In [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), **Plato's beard** is a [paradoxical](wikipedia://en/24390) argument dubbed by [Willard Van Orman Quine](wikipedia://en/33833) in his 1948 paper "On What There Is". The phrase came to be identified as the philosophy of understanding something based on what does not exist.[^o2]

:::

## Doctrine {#Doctrine}

Quine defined [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s beard – and his reason for naming it so – in the following words:

> This is the old [Platonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_idealism) riddle of nonbeing. Nonbeing must in some sense be, otherwise what is it that there is not? This tangled [doctrine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctrine) might be nicknamed Plato's beard; historically it has proved tough, frequently dulling the edge of [Occam's razor](wikipedia://en/36797).[^o4]

The argument has been favored by prominent philosophers including [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163), [A. J. Ayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A._J._Ayer) and [C. J. F. Williams](wikipedia://en/31437132).[^o5] Declaring that not *p* (¬*p*) cannot exist, one may be forced to abandon [truisms](wikipedia://en/391239) such as [negation](wikipedia://en/161019) and *[modus tollens](wikipedia://en/18901)*. There are also variations to Quine's original, which included its application both to singular and general terms.[^o6] Quine initially applied the doctrine to singular terms only before expanding it so that it covers general terms as well.[^o6]

[Karl Popper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Popper) stated the inverse. "Only if Plato's beard is sufficiently tough, and tangled by many entities, can it be worth our while to use Ockham's razor."[^o7] Russell's theory of "singular descriptions", which clearly show "how we might meaningfully use seeming names without supposing that there be the entities allegedly named", is supposed to "detangle" Plato's beard.[^o8][^o9]
