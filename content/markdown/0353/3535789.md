# Binet–Cauchy identity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binet–Cauchy identity"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the **Binet–Cauchy identity**, named after [Jacques Philippe Marie Binet](wikipedia://en/2252908) and [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842), states that[^o2]

$$\left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i c_i\right) \left(\sum_{j=1}^n b_j d_j\right) = \left(\sum_{i=1}^n a_i d_i\right) \left(\sum_{j=1}^n b_j c_j\right) + \sum_{1\le i < j \le n} (a_i b_j - a_j b_i ) (c_i d_j - c_j d_i )$$

for every choice of [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) (or more generally, elements of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346)). Setting $a_{i} = c_{i}$ and $b_{j} = d_{j}$, it gives [Lagrange's identity](wikipedia://en/2557590), which is a stronger version of the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128) for the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$. The Binet-Cauchy identity is a special case of the [Cauchy–Binet formula](wikipedia://en/357354) for matrix determinants.

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="The Binet–Cauchy identity and exterior algebra"}

## The Binet–Cauchy identity and exterior algebra {#The_Binet–Cauchy_identity_and_exterior_algebra}

When $n = 3$, the first and second terms on the right hand side become the squared magnitudes of [dot](wikipedia://en/157093) and [cross products](wikipedia://en/157092) respectively; in $n$ dimensions these become the magnitudes of the dot and [wedge products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product). We may write it

$$(a \cdot c)(b \cdot d) = (a \cdot d)(b \cdot c) + (a \wedge b) \cdot (c \wedge d)$$

where $\mathbf{a}$, $\mathbf{b}$, $\mathbf{c}$, and $\mathbf{d}$ are vectors. It may also be written as a formula giving the dot product of two wedge products, as

$$(a \wedge b) \cdot (c \wedge d) = (a \cdot c)(b \cdot d) - (a \cdot d)(b \cdot c)\,,$$

which can be written as

$$(a \times b) \cdot (c \times d) = (a \cdot c)(b \cdot d) - (a \cdot d)(b \cdot c)$$

in the $n = 3$ case.

In the special case $\mathbf{a} = \mathbf{c}$ and $\mathbf{b} = \mathbf{d}$, the formula yields

$$|a \wedge b|^2 = |a|^2|b|^2 - |a \cdot b|^2.$$

When both $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ are unit vectors, we obtain the usual relation

$$\sin^2 \phi = 1 - \cos^2 \phi$$

where $\varphi$ is the angle between the vectors.

This is a special case of the [Inner product](wikipedia://en/221537#Inner_product) on the exterior algebra of a vector space, which is defined on wedge-decomposable elements as the [Gram determinant](wikipedia://en/987959#Gram_determinant) of their components.

:::

## Einstein notation {#Einstein_notation}

A relationship between the [Levi–Cevita symbols](wikipedia://en/195982) and the generalized [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890) is

$$\frac{1}{k!}\varepsilon^{\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_k\mu_{k+1}\cdots\mu_{n}} \varepsilon_{\lambda_1\cdots\lambda_k\nu_{k+1}\cdots\nu_{n}} = \delta^{\mu_{k+1}\cdots\mu_{n}}_{\nu_{k+1}\cdots\nu_{n}}\,.$$

The $(a \wedge b) \cdot (c \wedge d) = (a \cdot c)(b \cdot d) - (a \cdot d)(b \cdot c)$ form of the Binet–Cauchy identity can be written as

$$\frac{1}{(n-2)!}\left(\varepsilon^{\mu_1\cdots\mu_{n-2}\alpha\beta} ~ a_{\alpha} ~ b_{\beta} \right)\left( \varepsilon_{\mu_1\cdots\mu_{n-2}\gamma\delta} ~ c^{\gamma} ~ d^{\delta}\right) = \delta^{\alpha\beta}_{\gamma\delta} ~ a_{\alpha} ~ b_{\beta} ~ c^{\gamma} ~ d^{\delta}\,.$$

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Expanding the last term,

$$\begin{aligned} &\sum_{1\le i < j \le n} (a_i b_j - a_j b_i ) (c_i d_j - c_j d_i ) \\ ={}&{} \sum_{1\le i < j \le n} (a_i c_i b_j d_j + a_j c_j b_i d_i) + \sum_{i=1}^n a_i c_i b_i d_i - \sum_{1\le i < j \le n} (a_i d_i b_j c_j + a_j d_j b_i c_i) - \sum_{i=1}^n a_i d_i b_i c_i \end{aligned}$$

where the second and fourth terms are the same and artificially added to complete the sums as follows:

$$= \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n a_i c_i b_j d_j - \sum_{i=1}^n \sum_{j=1}^n a_i d_i b_j c_j.$$

This completes the proof after factoring out the terms indexed by *i*.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

A general form, also known as the [Cauchy–Binet formula](wikipedia://en/357354), states the following: Suppose *A* is an *m*×*n* [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) and *B* is an *n*×*m* matrix. If *S* is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of {1, ..., *n*} with *m* elements, we write *A<sub>S</sub>* for the *m*×*m* matrix whose columns are those columns of *A* that have indices from *S*. Similarly, we write *B<sub>S</sub>* for the *m*×*m* matrix whose *rows* are those rows of *B* that have indices from *S*. Then the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the [matrix product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_product) of *A* and *B* satisfies the identity

$$\det(AB) = \sum_{ S\subset\{1,\ldots,n\} \atop |S| = m} \det(A_S)\det(B_S),$$

where the sum extends over all possible subsets *S* of {1, ..., *n*} with *m* elements.

We get the original identity as special case by setting

$$A = \begin{pmatrix}a_1&\dots&a_n\\b_1&\dots& b_n\end{pmatrix},\quad B = \begin{pmatrix}c_1&d_1\\\vdots&\vdots\\c_n&d_n\end{pmatrix}.$$

:::
