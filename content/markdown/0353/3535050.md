# Hjelmslev transformation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hjelmslev transformation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hjelmslev transformation** is an effective method for [mapping](wikipedia://en/516931) an entire [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/318742) into a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) with a finite [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815). The transformation was invented by Danish mathematician [Johannes Hjelmslev](wikipedia://en/30961758). It utilizes [Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Ivanovich_Lobachevsky)'s 23rd theorem[^o2] from his work [Geometrical Investigations on the Theory of Parallels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrical_Investigations_on_the_Theory_of_Parallels).

:::

*Image: Hjemslev-mapping-example.png.* The Hjelmslev transformation is a method of mapping an infinite line into a finite one in [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).

Lobachevsky observes, using a combination of his 16th and 23rd theorems, that it is a fundamental characteristic of [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) that there must exist a distinct [angle of parallelism](wikipedia://en/1341579) for any given line length.[^o3] Let us say for the length AE, its angle of parallelism is angle BAF. This being the case, line AH and EJ will be [hyperparallel](wikipedia://en/241291), and therefore will never meet. Consequently, any line drawn perpendicular to base AE between A and E must necessarily cross line AH at some finite distance. [Johannes Hjelmslev](wikipedia://en/30961758) discovered from this a method of compressing an entire hyperbolic plane into a finite circle.

## Construction {#Construction}

### Formalisation {#Formalisation}

The Hjelmslev transformation is a function designated as $H(P)$ which operates upon all points $P_n$ in hyperbolic (Lobachevskian) space. Given an angle $\alpha$ such that $0 < \alpha < \frac{\pi}{2}$, and an origin $O$, this mapping yields images $P'_n$ where the following properties are preserved:

1. The image of a circle with a center at $O$ is a circle with a center at $O$.
1. The image of a rectilinear angle is a rectilinear angle.
1. Any angle with vertex $O$ is mapped onto itself, i.e. any angle with vertex $O$ is preserved.
1. The image of a right angle with one side passing through $O$ is a right angle with one side passing through $O$.
1. The image of any straight line will be a finite straight line segment.
1. Finally, the point order is maintained throughout a transformation, i.e. if B is between A and C, the image of B will be between the image of A and the image of C.

This function is useful in the studies of hyperbolic (Lobachevskian) space because it produces characteristic figures of parallel lines. Given a set of two parallel lines $\overline{AB}$, $\overline{CD}$ such that $\overline{AB} \parallel \overline{CD}$, the resulting images $\overline{A'B'}$, $\overline{C'D'}$ will form a triangle $\triangle A'B'IC'D'$ with an imaginary vertex $I$ in their direction of parallelism.

### Transformation of a single point $P$ into image $P'$ {#Transformation_of_a_single_point_P_into_image_P'}

Given $\alpha$, $O$, $P$, in order to find the $P'$ (image) of $P$. First draw the line segment $\overline{OP}$, connecting the point $P$ to the origin $O$. Next, construct an auxiliary line $\overline{OQ}$ such that $\angle POQ = \alpha$. Point $Q$ is only necessary to define the line $\overline{OQ}$.

*Image: Line OQ at angle -alpha from OP.png.* Line $\overline{OQ}$ at angle $\alpha$ from $\overline{OP}$.

Now construct the perpendicular line $\overline{PP''}$ passing through the point $P$, perpendicular to $\overline{OQ}$. This will form the right angle $\angle OP''P$ at point $P''$:

*Image: Perpendicular of line PP-double-prime onto line OQ..png.* Perpendicular $\overline{PP''}$ onto $\overline{OQ}$.

Using line segment $\overline{OP''}$ as a radius, construct a circle with center $O$ such that the circumference of said circle intersects $\overline{OP}$ at a point $\color{red} P'$. Thus, we obtain the point $\color{red} P'$ on line segment $OP$, which is the Hjelmslev transformation $H(P)$ of the given point $P$. $H(P) = \color{red} P'$:

*Image: Point P-prime from radius OP-double-prime.png.* Point $P'$ from radius $\overline{OP''}$.

### The Hjelmslev Disk {#The_Hjelmslev_Disk}

Let $\overline{P_1P_2}$ be parallel to $\overline{P_3P_4}$, where $\angle P_3 P_1 P_2$ is the angle of parallelism. By performing the transformation for every point on the two parallel lines, we yield the Hjelmslev circle:

*Image: Transformation of Parallel Lines P1 P2 and P3 P4.png.*

The circumference of the circle created does not have a corresponding location within the plane, and therefore, the product of a Hjelmslev transformation is more aptly called a **Hjelmslev Disk**. Likewise, when this transformation is extended in all three dimensions, it is referred to as a **Hjelmslev Ball**.

## The Hjelmslev transformation and the Klein model {#The_Hjelmslev_transformation_and_the_Klein_model}

If we represent hyperbolic space by means of the [Klein model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein_model), and take the center of the Hjelmslev transformation to be the center point of the Klein model, then the Hjelmslev transformation maps points in the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) to points in a disk centered at the origin with a radius less than one. Given a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) k, the Hjelmslev transformation, if we ignore rotations, is in effect what we obtain by mapping a vector u representing a point in the Klein model to ku, with 0\<k\<1. It is therefore in terms of the model a [uniform scaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_scaling) which sends lines to lines and so forth. To beings living in a hyperbolic space it might be a suitable way of making a map.
