# Dedekind-finite ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dedekind-finite ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is said to be a **Dedekind-finite ring** (also called **directly finite rings**[^o2][^o14][^o6] and **Von Neumann finite rings**[^o3][^o14][^o6]) if *ab* = 1 implies *ba* = 1 for any two ring elements *a* and *b*. In other words, all one-sided inverses in the ring are two-sided. Numerous examples of Dedekind-finite rings include [commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_rings), [finite rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_rings), and [Noetherian rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_rings).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A ring $R$ is Dedekind-finite if any of the following equivalent conditions hold:[^o6]

- All one sided inverses are two sided: $xy = 1$ implies $yx = 1$.
- Each element that has a right inverse has a left inverse: For $x \in R$, if there is a $y \in R$ where $xy = 1$, then there is a $z \in R$ such that $zx = 1$.
- Capacity condition: $xy=1$, $xz=0$ implies $z=0$.
- Each element has at most one right inverse.
- Each element that has a left inverse has a right inverse.
- Dual of the capacity condition: $yx=1$, $zx=0$ implies $z=0$.
- Each element has at most one left inverse.
- Each element that has a right inverse also has a two sided inverse.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Any [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) is Dedekind-finite.[^o6]
- Any [finite ring](wikipedia://en/23706953) is Dedekind-finite.[^o3]
- Any [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) $M_n(F)$ over a commutative ring $F$ is Dedekind-finite.[^o6]
- Any [domain](wikipedia://en/487627) is Dedekind-finite.[^o3]
- Any left/right [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) is Dedekind-finite.[^o3][^o6]
- Given a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$, the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) $kG$ is Dedekind-finite.[^o6]
- A Dedekind-finite ring with an [idempotent](wikipedia://en/361940) $e^2=e$ implies that the [corner ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corner_ring) $eRe$ is also Dedekind-finite.[^o14]
- A ring with finitely many [nilpotents](wikipedia://en/252235) is Dedekind-finite.[^o6]
- A [unit-regular ring](wikipedia://en/907524#Generalizations_and_specializations) is Dedekind-finite.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o10 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

## Non-examples {#Non-examples}

A counter-example can be constructed by considering the [free algebra](wikipedia://en/494669) $R\langle x,y\rangle$ (a "[polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065)" in two non-commuting indeterminates, that is, $xy \neq yx$), where the ring $R$ has no [zero divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_divisors), being divided by the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) $I=(xy-1)$. Then $x+I \in R\langle x,y\rangle/I$ has a right inverse but is not invertible. This illustrates that Dedekind-finite rings need not be closed under [homomorpic images](wikipedia://en/26411).[^o14]

Another non-example is the [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623) $\operatorname{End}(V)$ of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) (or [free module](wikipedia://en/326454)) $V$ with a countably infinite basis $e_1,e_2,\dots$. Let $L\in\operatorname{End}(V)$ be the left [shift](wikipedia://en/467384#Sequences) defined by $L(e_1)=0$ and $L(e_i)=e_{i-1}$ for $i\geq2$, and let $R\in\operatorname{End}(V)$ be the right shift $R(e_i)=e_{i+1}$. Then $LR=1$, but $RL(e_1)=0$.[^o3]

A matrix ring over a Dedekind-finite ring may also fail to be Dedekind-finite. For this, one can consider $R=k\langle s,t,u,v,w,x,y,z\rangle$, where $k$ is a field, let $A=\begin{pmatrix}s&t\\u&v\end{pmatrix}$, $B=\begin{pmatrix}w&x\\y&z\end{pmatrix}$, and $J$ the two-sided ideal of $R$ generated by the entries of $AB-I$. Then $R/J$ is a domain, but $AB=I\ne BA$ in $M_2(R/J)$.[^o11]

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Dedekind-finite rings are closed under [subrings](wikipedia://en/24873145)[^o2][^o14], [direct products](wikipedia://en/8887),[^o6][^o14] and finite [direct sums](wikipedia://en/2084687).[^o14] This makes the class of Dedekind-finite rings a [quasivariety](wikipedia://en/12747933), which can also be seen from the fact that its axioms are [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations) and the [Horn sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_sentence) $ab=1 \implies ba=1$.[^o14]

A ring is Dedekind-finite if and only if so is its [opposite ring](wikipedia://en/3129323).[^o14] If either a ring $R$, its [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R[X]$ with indeterminates $X$, the [free word algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_word_algebra) $R[\tilde{X}]$ over $X$ with coefficients in $R$, or the [power series ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_series_ring) $R[[X]]$ are Dedekind-finite, then they all are Dedekind-finite.[^o14] Letting $\text{Rad}(R)$ denote the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) of the ring $R$, the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $R/\text{Rad}(R)$ is Dedekind-finite if and only if so is $R$, and this implies that [local rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_rings) and [semilocal rings](wikipedia://en/977725) are also Dedekind-finite.[^o14] This extends to the fact that, given a ring $R$ and a [nilpotent ideal](wikipedia://en/6389159) $I$, the ring $R$ is Dedekind-finite [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) so is the quotient ring $R/I$,[^o14] and as a consequence, a ring is also Dedekind-finite if and only if the [upper triangular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular_matrices) with coeffecients in the ring also form a Dedekind-finite ring.[^o14]
