# Kirchhoff's diffraction formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kirchhoff's diffraction formula"}

**[Kirchhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gustav_Kirchhoff)'s diffraction formula**[^o2][^o3] (also called **Fresnel–Kirchhoff diffraction formula**) approximates [light](wikipedia://en/9426) intensity and phase in optical [diffraction](wikipedia://en/8603): light fields in the boundary regions of shadows. The approximation can be used to model light [propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) in a wide range of configurations, either [analytically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_expression) or using [numerical modelling](wikipedia://en/21506). It gives an expression for the wave disturbance when a [monochromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochromatic) [spherical wave](wikipedia://en/33691#Spherical_waves) is the incoming wave of a situation under consideration. This formula is derived by applying the [Kirchhoff integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff_integral_theorem), which uses the [Green's second identity](wikipedia://en/805700) to derive the solution to the homogeneous [scalar wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691), to a spherical wave with some approximations.

:::

The [Huygens–Fresnel principle](wikipedia://en/14359) can be derived by the Fresnel–Kirchhoff diffraction formula.

::: {#o5 .statement cue=named-heading name="Derivation of Kirchhoff's diffraction formula"}

## Derivation of Kirchhoff's diffraction formula {#Derivation_of_Kirchhoff's_diffraction_formula}

[Kirchhoff's integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff_integral_theorem), sometimes referred to as the Fresnel–Kirchhoff integral theorem,[^o6] uses [Green's second identity](wikipedia://en/805700) to derive the solution of the homogeneous [monochromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochromatic) [scalar wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) at an arbitrary spatial position **P** in terms of the solution of the wave equation and its first order derivative at all points on an arbitrary closed surface $S$ as the boundary of some volume including **P**.

The solution provided by the integral theorem for a [monochromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochromatic) source is[^o2]

$$U(\textbf{P}) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{S} \left[ U \frac{\partial}{\partial \textbf{n}} \left( \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \right) - \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \frac{\partial U}{\partial \textbf{n}} \right]dS,$$

where $U$ is the spatial part of the solution of the homogeneous [scalar wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) (i.e., $V(\mathbf{r},t) = U(\mathbf{r}) e^{-i\omega t}$ as the homogeneous scalar wave equation solution), *k* is the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber), and *s* is the distance from **P** to an (infinitesimally small) integral surface element, and $\frac{\partial f}{\partial \textbf{n}}$ is a [normal derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_derivative), that is a derivative of the function $f$ along the [unit normal vector](wikipedia://en/173224) $\textbf{n}$ of an integral surface element $dS$. The surface normal unit vector $\textbf{n}$ is toward the inside of the enclosed volume in this integral.

In deriving the Kirchoff's diffraction formula, following assumptions are made.

- The distance between a [point source](wikipedia://en/7622892) **P<sub>0</sub>** of a spherical wave and an integral area, and the distance between the integral area and an observation point **P**, are much greater than the wave wavelength $\lambda$.
- A wave on the aperture is same to the wave that would be present if there was no wave obstacle.[^o7]
- $U$ and $\frac{\partial U}{\partial \textbf{n}} = \nabla U \cdot \textbf{n}$ are zero at the obstacle.

The 2nd and 3rd conditions are called *Kirchhoff's boundary conditions*, and they together lead to a physically incorrectly condition that the calculated wave is discontinuous at the aperture boundaries.

### Point source {#Point_source}

*Image: A diagram used to derive the Kirchhoff diffraction formula - 0, 2026-September-11.png.* A geometrical arrangement to derive the Kirchhoff's diffraction formula. A screen is consisted of an aperture A<sub>1</sub> (opening) and opaque areas A<sub>2</sub>. A<sub>3</sub> is a part of a sphere of radius R centered at C. The [Kirchhoff's integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff_integral_theorem) is applied to the closed surface consisted of these 3 areas.

Consider a monochromatic spherical wave point source at **P**<sub>0</sub>, which illuminates an aperture A<sub>1</sub> in a screen as shown in the right figure. The [intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensity_%28physics%29) of the wave emitted by a point source falls off as the inverse square of the distance traveled, so the amplitude falls off as the inverse of the distance. The complex amplitude of the disturbance at a distance $r$ is given by

$$U(r) = \frac{a e^{ikr}}{r},$$

where $a$ represents the [magnitude](wikipedia://en/577301#Complex_numbers) of the disturbance at the point source.

The disturbance at a spatial position **P** can be found by applying the [Kirchhoff's integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff_integral_theorem) to the closed surface formed by the screen, consisted of A<sub>1</sub> and A<sub>2</sub>, and a unblocked part of a sphere of radius **R** centered at **C**, A<sub>3</sub>. The integration is performed over the areas *A*<sub>1</sub>, *A*<sub>2</sub> and *A*<sub>3</sub>, giving

$$U(P) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \left[\int_{A_1} + \int_{A_2} + \int_{A_3} \right]\left( U \frac{\partial}{\partial n} \left( \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \right) - \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \frac{\partial U}{\partial n} \right) dS.$$

To solve the equation, it is assumed that the values of $U$ and $\frac{\partial U}{\partial n}$ in the aperture area *A*<sub>1</sub> are the same as when the screen is not present (*1st assumption of the Kirchhoff's diffraction formula, one of two Kirchhoff's boundary conditions*), so at the position **Q** on the aperture,

$$U_{A_1} = \frac{a e^{ikr}}{r},$$

$$\frac{\partial U_{A_1}}{\partial n} = \nabla U_{A_1} \cdot n = \frac{ae^{ikr}}{r} \left[ik - \frac{1}{r} \right] \cos(n,r),$$

[^o9]where $r$ is the length of the straight line **P<sub>0</sub>Q**, and $(n,r)$ is the angle between a straightly extended version of **P**<sub>0</sub>**Q** (the extension is directed from **P<sub>0</sub>** to **Q**) and the (inward) [unit normal vector](wikipedia://en/173224) $n$ to the aperture. Note that $0 < (n,r) < \frac{\pi}{2}$ so $\cos(n,r)$ is a positive real number on *A*<sub>1</sub>.

At **Q**, we also have

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial n} \left( \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \right) = \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \left[ik - \frac{1}{s} \right] \cos(n,s),$$

where $s$ is the length of the straight line **PQ**, and $(n,s)$ is the angle between a straightly extended version of **PQ** (the extension is directed from **P** to **Q**) and the (inward) normal $n$ to the aperture. Note that $\frac{\pi}{2} < (n,s) < \frac{3 \pi}{2}$ so $\cos(n,s)$ is a negative real number on *A*<sub>1</sub>.

Two more following assumptions are made.

- (*2nd assumption of the Kirchhoff's diffraction formula*) In the above normal derivatives, the terms $\frac{1}{r}$ and $\frac{1}{s}$ in both square brackets are assumed to be negligible compared with the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber) $k=\frac{2\pi}{\lambda}$, means $r$ and $s$ are much greater than the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) $\lambda$. Thus, $$\begin{array}{lcr} \frac{\partial U_{A_1}}{\partial n} & \approx & ik \frac{ae^{ikr}}{r} \cos(n,r), \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial n} \left( \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \right) & \approx & ik \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \cos(n,s). \end{array}$$
- (*3*r*d assumption of the Kirchhoff's diffraction formula, one of the two Kirchhoff's boundary conditions*) Kirchhoff assumes that the values of $U$ and $\frac{\partial U}{\partial n}$ on the opaque areas marked by *A*<sub>2</sub> are zero. This implies that $U$ and $\frac{\partial U}{\partial n}$ are discontinuous at the edge of the aperture *A*<sub>1</sub>. Of course this is generally not the case.[^o10][^o11]

The contribution from *A*<sub>3</sub> to the integral is expected to be zero, and it can be justified by one of the following reasons. (The 2nd one is preferred as the 1st one has a physical flaw.)

1. Make the assumption that the source starts to radiate at a particular time, and make *R* large enough, so that when the disturbance at **P** is being considered, no contributions from *A*<sub>3</sub> will have arrived there.[^o2] Such a wave is no longer [monochromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochromatic), since a monochromatic wave must exist at all times, while the monochromaticity is assumed in the Kirchoff's diffraction formula. This physically flawed assumption is not necessary, and a more formal argument avoiding it has been derived.[^o12]
1. A wave emanated from the aperture *A*<sub>1</sub> is expected to evolve toward a spherical wave as it propagates.[^o13] So, if *R* is large enough, then the wave on *A*<sub>3</sub> is $U_{A_3}(r') \approx \frac{b e^{ikr'}}{r'}$ with $r'$ being the distance from the center of the aperture *A*<sub>1</sub>, thus the integral on *A*<sub>3</sub> becomes $$\begin{array}{lcl} U(P) & = & \frac{1}{4\pi} \int \left[ U \frac{\partial}{\partial n} \left( \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \right) - \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \frac{\partial U}{\partial n} \right] dA_3\\ & \sim & \frac{1}{4\pi} \int \left[ \frac{b e^{ikr'}}{r'} ik \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \cos(n,s) - \frac{e^{iks}}{s} ik \frac{be^{ikr'}}{r'} \cos(n,r') \right] dA_3 \\ & = & \frac{ik}{4\pi} \frac{b e^{ik(r'+s)}}{r's} \int \left[ \cos(n,s) - \cos(n,r') \right] dA_3 \\ & \sim & \frac{ib}{2\lambda} \frac{e^{ik(r'+s)}}{r'^2} \int \left[ \cos(n,r') - \cos(n,r') \right] dA_3 \\ & = & 0 \end{array}$$ where $r'$ is the distance from the center of the aperture *A*<sub>1</sub> to an integral surface element.

As a result, finally, the integral above, which represents the complex amplitude at **P**, becomes

$$U(P) = -\frac{ia}{2\lambda} \int_{A_1} \frac{e^{ik(r+s)}}{rs} [\cos(n,r) - \cos(n,s)] d{A_1}.$$

This is the *Kirchhoff* or *Fresnel–Kirchhoff diffraction formula*.

This formula can also be derived by using a different geometry in which non-physical (no so wave blocking) arbitrary closed surface, that is apart from the point wave source **P<sub>0</sub>** and surrounds the observation point **P**, is the integral surface to which the [Kirchhoff's integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff_integral_theorem) is applied.[^o16] In this geometry, among three assumptions made above, only the 2nd assumption - $r$ (The distance between **P<sub>0</sub>** and an element of the integral surface) and $s$ (The distance between the integral surface element and **P**) are much greater than the wavelength $\lambda$ - is required. The result is the same integral form of the Fresnel–Kirchhoff diffraction formula above, except that the integral surface is the chosen closed surface.

::: {#o15 .statement cue=named-heading name="Equivalence to Huygens–Fresnel principle"}

#### Equivalence to Huygens–Fresnel principle {#Equivalence_to_Huygens–Fresnel_principle}

*Image: A diagram used to derive the Huygens–Fresnel principle from the Kirchhoff diffraction formula - 0, 2026-September-11.png.* A geometric arrangement used to express the Kirchhoff's diffraction formula in a form similar to the Huygens–Fresnel principle.

The [Huygens–Fresnel principle](wikipedia://en/14359) can be derived by integrating over a different closed surface (the boundary of some volume having an observation point *P*). In the right diagram, the aperture area *A*<sub>1</sub> in the diagram of [the above section](wikipedia://en/31559837#Point_source) is replaced by (1) a part of a wavefront (emitted from a *P*<sub>0</sub>) at *r*<sub>0</sub>, which is the closest to the aperture, and (2) a portion of a cone with a vertex at *P*<sub>0</sub>, labelled *A*<sub>4</sub>, that is straight lines perpendicular to the wavefront and between the wavefront and the edges of the aperture. If the wavefront is positioned such that the wavefront is very close to the edges of the aperture, then the contribution from *A*<sub>4</sub> can be neglected (*an additional assumption on the top of three assumptions used to derive the Kirchhoff's diffraction formula for a spherical wave point source [above](wikipedia://en/31559837#Point_source)*). On this new *A<sub>1</sub>*, the inward (toward the volume enclosed by the closed integral surface, so toward the right side in the diagram) normal $n$ to *A<sub>1</sub>* is along the radial direction from *P*<sub>0</sub>, i.e., the direction perpendicular to the wavefront. As a result, the angle $(n,r)=0$ and the angle $(n,s)$ is related with the angle $\chi$ (the angle as defined in [Huygens–Fresnel principle](wikipedia://en/14359)) as

$$(n,s) = \pi - \chi.$$

The complex amplitude of the wavefront at *r*<sub>0</sub> is given by

$$U(r_0) = \frac{ae^{ikr_0}}{r_0}.$$

So, the diffraction formula in [the above section](wikipedia://en/31559837#Point_source) becomes

$$U(P) = -\frac{i}{2\lambda} \frac{ae^{ikr_0}}{r_0} \int_{S} \frac{e^{iks}}{s} (1 + \cos\chi) dS,$$

where the integral is done over the part of the wavefront at *r*<sub>0</sub> which is the closest to the aperture in the diagram. This integral leads to the [Huygens–Fresnel](wikipedia://en/14359) principle (with the obliquity factor $\frac {1 + \cos\chi}{2}$).

In the derivation of this integral, instead of the geometry depicted in the diagram of this section, two closed surfaces (no physical surfaces so they do not block waves), one is a sphere of radius *r*<sub>0</sub> centered at the point wave source *P*<sub>0</sub>, and the other is an arbitrary closed surface that contains this inner sphere and is infinitely far from it (e.g., another sphere of infinite radius), can be used.[^o16] In this geometry, the observation point *P* is located in the volume enclosed by these closed surfaces, so the Fresnel-Kirchhoff diffraction formula is applied on the two spheres. (The surface normal on these integral surfaces are, say again, toward the enclosed volume.) In the formula application to this geometry, the additional assumption of neglecting the integral on A4, used in the above diagram of this section, is not required. The integral on the outer surface is zero as long as it is infinitely far from *P*, so as a result, the [Huygens–Fresnel](wikipedia://en/14359) principle in the same integral from above is derived, except that the integral surface is the inner sphere.

:::

### Extended source {#Extended_source}

Assume that an aperture is illuminated by waves from an extended monochromatic wave source.[^o18] The complex amplitude at the aperture is given by $U_0(r)$.[^o19]

It is assumed, as [before](wikipedia://en/31559837#Point_source), that the values of $U$ and $\frac{\partial U}{\partial n}$ in the area *A*<sub>1</sub> are the same as when a screen having the aperture is not present, that the values of $U$ and $\frac{\partial U}{\partial n}$ in *A*<sub>2</sub> are zero (Kirchhoff's boundary conditions), and that 1/*s* is negligible compared with a wavenumber *k* (*s* is much greater than the wavelength $\lambda$)[^o20]. By the same logic as [before](wikipedia://en/31559837#Point_source), the integral over *A*<sub>3</sub> is zero. By applying the [Kirchhoff's integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirchhoff_integral_theorem) to *A*<sub>1</sub>, we then have

$$U(P) = \frac{1}{4\pi} \int_{A_1} \frac{e^{iks}}{s} \left[ ik U_0(r) \cos(n,s) - \frac{\partial U_0(r)}{\partial n} \right]\, dS.$$

This is the most general form of the Kirchhoff diffraction formula.

To solve this equation for an extended wave source, an additional integration would be required to sum contributions from individual points in the source. If, however, we assume that the distance between the source and the aperture is significantly greater than the wavelength[^o21], then we may approximately write a wave in the aperture like a spherical wave (*It may be an additional assumption on the top of three assumptions used to derive the Kirchhoff's diffraction formula for a point wave source [above](wikipedia://en/31559837#Point_source).*)

$$U_0(r) \approx a(r) e^{ikr},$$

where $a(r)$ is the magnitude of the disturbance at the point $r$ in the aperture. We then have

$$\frac{\partial {U_0(r)}}{\partial n} = \nabla U_0(r) \cdot n = \left( e^{ikr} \frac{da(r)}{dr} + ika(r)e^{ikr} \right)\hat{r} \cdot n \approx ik a(r)e^{ikr} \cos(n,r)$$

where a slowly varying wave magnitude ($\left| \frac{da(r)}{dr} \right| << \left | ika(r) \right |$[^o22]), as expected from the assumption of the source far from the aperture, is used, and $\hat{r}$ is a unit radial vector. Thus,

$$U(P) = -\frac{i}{2\lambda} \int_S a(r) \frac{e^{ik(s+r)}}{s} [ \cos\chi + \cos(n,r)]\, dS$$

where $(n,s) = \pi - \chi$ ($\chi$ is an angle between the surface normal $n$ and the line **PQ** at **Q** in the diagram [here](wikipedia://en/31559837#Point_source).) is used.

:::

## Fraunhofer and Fresnel diffraction equations {#Fraunhofer_and_Fresnel_diffraction_equations}

In spite of the various approximations that were made in arriving at the Kirchoff's diffraction formula,[^o24] the formula is adequate to describe the majority of problems in instrumental optics. This is mainly because the wavelength of light is much smaller than the dimensions of any obstacles encountered by the light in many problems under consideration. Analytical solutions are not possible for most configurations, but the [Fresnel diffraction](wikipedia://en/2649192) equation and [Fraunhofer diffraction](wikipedia://en/799986) equation, which are approximations of the Kirchhoff's formula for the [near field](wikipedia://en/271708#Near_field) and [far field](wikipedia://en/271708#Far_field), respectively, can be applied to a very wide range of optical systems.

One of the important assumptions made in arriving at the Kirchhoff diffraction formula is that *r* (the distance between a point wave source **P**<sub>0</sub> and a point on an aperture **Q**) and *s* (the distance between **Q** and an observation point **P**) are significantly greater than the wavelength λ. Here, other approximations can be made, further simplifying the diffraction formula:

- One of the below geometries is satisfied:
  - The triangle **P<sub>0</sub>QP** is stretched along the edge **P**<sub>0</sub>**P** such that the edge is approximately parallel to other edges **P<sub>0</sub>Q** and **QP**.
  - The triangle **P<sub>0</sub>QP** is about symmetric to the screen (having the aperture); **P<sub>0</sub>** and **P** are approximately mirrored with respect to the screen.
- The aperture is much smaller than the lengths of **P<sub>0</sub>Q** (that is *r*), and **QP** (that is *s*).

Then, the following formula simplification can be made:

- cos(*n, r*) − cos(*n, s*) is approximately replaced with 2cos *β*, where *β* is the angle between **P**<sub>0</sub>**P** and the normal to the aperture.[^o25] The factor 1/*rs* is approximately replaced with 1/*r*<sub>c</sub>*s*<sub>c</sub>, where *r*<sub>c</sub> and *s*<sub>c</sub> are the distances of **P**<sub>0</sub> and **P** to the origin **O** located on the aperture. Then, given the aperture (denoted by $S$ from now on), **P**<sub>0</sub>, and **P**, the Kirchhoff's diffraction formula becomes: $$U(P) = -\frac{ia}{2\lambda} \int_{S} \frac{e^{ik(r+s)}}{rs} [\cos(n,r) - \cos(n,s)] d{S} \approx -\frac{ia \cos\beta}{\lambda r_\mathbf{c} s_\mathbf{c}} \int_{S} e^{ik(r+s)}\,dS.$$

Let the aperture lies in the *xy* plane, and the coordinates of **P**<sub>0</sub>, **P** and **Q** (a general point in the aperture)[^o26] are (*x*<sub>0</sub>, *y*<sub>0</sub>, *z*<sub>0</sub>), (*x*, *y*, *z*) and (*x*', *y*', 0) respectively. We then have (*r* and *s* being the distances of **P**<sub>0</sub> and **P** to **Q** respectively):

$$~r^2 = (x_0 - x')^2 + (y_0 - y')^2 + z_0^2,$$

$$~s^2 = (x - x')^2 + (y - y')^2 + z^2,$$

$$~r_\mathbf{c}^2 = x_0^2 + y_0^2 + z_0^2,$$

$$~s_\mathbf{c}^2 = x^2 + y^2 + z^2.$$

We can express *r* and *s* as follows:

$$r = r_\mathbf{c}\left[1 - \frac{2(x_0x' + y_0y')}{r_\mathbf{c}^2} + \frac{x'^2 + y'^2}{r_\mathbf{c}^2}\right]^{1/2},$$

$$s = s_\mathbf{c}\left[1 - \frac{2(xx' + yy')}{s_\mathbf{c}^2} + \frac{x'^2 + y'^2}{s_\mathbf{c}^2}\right]^{1/2}.$$

These can be expanded as [power series](wikipedia://en/30448#Binomial_series):

$$r = r_\mathbf{c}\left[1 - \frac{1}{2r_\mathbf{c}^2}\left[2(x_0x' + y_0y') - (x'^2 + y'^2)\right] + \frac{1}{8r_\mathbf{c}^4}\left[2(x_0x' + y_0y') - (x'^2 + y'^2)\right]^2 + \cdots\right],$$

$$s = s_\mathbf{c}\left[1 - \frac{1}{2s_\mathbf{c}^2}\left[2(xx' + yy') - (x'^2 + y'^2)\right] + \frac{1}{8s_\mathbf{c}^4}\left[2(xx' + yy') - (x'^2 + y'^2)\right]^2 + \cdots\right].$$

The complex amplitude at **P** can now be expressed as

$$U(P) \approx -\frac{ia \cos\beta}{\lambda r_\mathbf{c}s_\mathbf{c}} \int_{S} e^{ik(r+s)}\,dS = -\frac{i\cos\beta}{\lambda} \frac{ae^{ik(r_\mathbf{c} + s_\mathbf{c})}}{r_\mathbf{c}s_\mathbf{c}} \int_S e^{ikf(x',y')}\, dx' dy',$$

where *f*(*x*', *y*') includes all the terms in the expressions above for *s* and *r* apart from the first term in each expression and can be written in the form

$$f(x',y') = c_1 x' + c_2 y' + c_3 x'^2 + c_4 y'^2 + c_5x'y' \cdots,$$

where the *c*<sub>i</sub> are constants.

### Fraunhofer diffraction {#Fraunhofer_diffraction}

If all the terms in *f*(*x*', *y*') can be neglected except for the 1st order terms in *x*' and *y*', we have the [Fraunhofer diffraction](wikipedia://en/799986) equation. If the [direction cosines](wikipedia://en/2472593) of **P**<sub>0</sub>**Q** and **PQ** are

$$\begin{aligned} l_0 &= -x_0/r_\mathbf{c}, \\ m_0 &= -y_0/r_\mathbf{c}, \\ l &= x/s_\mathbf{c}, \\ m &= y/s_\mathbf{c}. \end{aligned}$$

The [Fraunhofer diffraction equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraunhofer_diffraction_equation) is then

$$U(P) = C \int_S e^{ik[(l_0 - l)x' + (m_0 - m)y']}\, dx' dy',$$

where *C* is a constant. This can also be written in the form

$$U(P) = C\int_S e^{i(\mathbf k_0 - \mathbf k) \cdot \mathbf r'}\, dx'dy',$$

where **k**<sub>0</sub> and **k** are the [wave vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_vector) of the waves traveling from **P**<sub>0</sub> to the aperture and from the aperture to **P** respectively, and **r**' is a point in the aperture.

If the spherical wave point source is replaced by an extended source whose complex amplitude at the aperture is given by *U*<sub>0</sub>(**r'**), then the [Fraunhofer diffraction](wikipedia://en/799986) equation is:

$$U(P) \propto \int_S a_0 (\mathbf r') e^{i(\mathbf k_0 - \mathbf k) \cdot \mathbf r'}\, dx'dy',$$

where *a*<sub>0</sub>(**r'**) is, as [before](wikipedia://en/31559837#Extended_source), the magnitude of the disturbance at the aperture.

In deriving the Fraunhofer diffraction equation, in addition to the approximations made in deriving the Kirchhoff's diffraction equation, it is assumed that

- the triangle **P<sub>0</sub>QP** is stretched along the edge **P<sub>0</sub>P** (so that this edge is approximately parallel to other edges **P<sub>0</sub>Q** and **QP**), *or* the triangle **P<sub>0</sub>QP** is about symmetric to the screen,
- *r* (the length of **P<sub>0</sub>Q**) and *s* (the length of **QP**) are significantly greater than the size of the aperture,
- second- and higher-order terms in the expression *f*(*x*', *y*') can be neglected.

### Fresnel diffraction {#Fresnel_diffraction}

When the quadratic terms cannot be neglected but all higher order terms can, the equation becomes the [Fresnel diffraction](wikipedia://en/2649192) equation.

In deriving the Fresnel diffraction equation, in addition to the approximations made in deriving the Kirchhoff's diffraction equation, it is assumed that:

- the triangle **P<sub>0</sub>QP** is stretched along the edge **P<sub>0</sub>P** (so that this edge is approximately parallel to other edges **P<sub>0</sub>Q** and **QP**), *or* the triangle **P<sub>0</sub>QP** is about symmetric to the screen,
- *r* (the length of **P<sub>0</sub>Q**) and *s* (the length of **QP**) are significantly greater than the size of the aperture,
- third- and higher-order terms in the expression *f*(*x*', *y*') can be neglected.
