# Quasi-Frobenius ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasi-Frobenius ring"}

In mathematics, especially [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), the class of **Frobenius rings** and their generalizations are the extension of work done on [Frobenius algebras](wikipedia://en/1814652). Perhaps the most important generalization is that of **quasi-Frobenius rings** (QF rings), which are in turn generalized by right **pseudo-Frobenius rings** (PF rings) and right **finitely pseudo-Frobenius rings** (FPF rings). Other diverse generalizations of quasi-Frobenius rings include **QF-1**, **QF-2** and **QF-3** rings.

:::

These types of rings can be viewed as descendants of algebras examined by [Georg Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Frobenius). A partial list of pioneers in quasi-Frobenius rings includes [R. Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134), [K. Morita](wikipedia://en/12034472), [T. Nakayama](wikipedia://en/17772141), [C. J. Nesbitt](wikipedia://en/14373679), and [R. M. Thrall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_M._Thrall).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A ring *R* is **quasi-Frobenius** if and only if *R* satisfies any of the following equivalent conditions:

1. *R* is [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) on one side and [self-injective](wikipedia://en/473775#Self-injective_rings) on one side.
1. *R* is [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) on a side and self-injective on a side.
1. All right (or all left) *R* modules which are [projective](wikipedia://en/364488) are also [injective](wikipedia://en/473775).
1. All right (or all left) *R* modules which are injective are also projective.

A **Frobenius ring** *R* is one satisfying any of the following equivalent conditions. Let *J*=J(*R*) be the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) of *R*.

1. *R* is quasi-Frobenius and the [socle](wikipedia://en/3571350) $\mathrm{soc}(R_R)\cong R/J$ as right *R* modules.
1. *R* is quasi-Frobenius and $\mathrm{soc}(_R R)\cong R/J$ as left *R* modules.
1. As right *R* modules $\mathrm{soc}(R_R)\cong R/J$, and as left *R* modules $\mathrm{soc}(_R R)\cong R/J$.

For a commutative ring *R*, the following are equivalent:

1. *R* is Frobenius
1. *R* is quasi-Frobenius
1. *R* is a finite direct sum of [local](wikipedia://en/153391) artinian rings which have unique [minimal ideals](wikipedia://en/34233472). (Such rings are examples of "zero-dimensional [Gorenstein local rings](wikipedia://en/515613)".)

A ring *R* is **right pseudo-Frobenius** if any of the following equivalent conditions are met:

1. Every [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_module) right *R* module is a [generator](wikipedia://en/9550415) for the category of right *R* modules.
1. *R* is right self-injective and is a [cogenerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_cogenerator) of Mod-*R*.
1. *R* is right self-injective and is [finitely cogenerated](wikipedia://en/326430) as a right *R* module.
1. *R* is right self-injective and a right [Kasch ring](wikipedia://en/34212423).
1. *R* is right self-injective, [semilocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semilocal_ring) and the socle soc(*R*<sub>*R*</sub>) is an [essential submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_submodule) of *R*.
1. *R* is a cogenerator of Mod-*R* and is a left Kasch ring.

A ring *R* is **right finitely pseudo-Frobenius** if and only if every [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) faithful right *R* module is a generator of Mod-*R*.

:::

## Thrall's QF-1,2,3 generalizations {#Thrall's_QF-1,2,3_generalizations}

In the seminal article (Thrall 1948), R. M. Thrall focused on three specific properties of (finite-dimensional) QF algebras and studied them in isolation. With additional assumptions, these definitions can also be used to generalize QF rings. A few other mathematicians pioneering these generalizations included [K. Morita](wikipedia://en/12034472) and H. Tachikawa.

Following (Anderson Fuller 1992), let *R* be a left or right Artinian ring:

- *R* is QF-1 if all faithful left modules and faithful right modules are [balanced modules](wikipedia://en/34302118).
- *R* is QF-2 if each indecomposable projective right module and each indecomposable projective left module has a unique minimal submodule. (I.e. they have simple socles.)
- *R* is QF-3 if the [injective hulls](wikipedia://en/665821) E(*R*<sub>*R*</sub>) and E(<sub>*R*</sub>*R*) are both projective modules.

The numbering scheme does not necessarily outline a hierarchy. Under more lax conditions, these three classes of rings may not contain each other. Under the assumption that *R* is left or right Artinian however, QF-2 rings are QF-3. There is even an example of a QF-1 and QF-3 ring which is not QF-2.

## Nakayama permutations {#Nakayama_permutations}

T. Nakayama defined (Nakayama 41) [quasi-Frobenius rings](wikipedia://en/31557888) as **Artinian rings with a Nakayama permutation**.

For an [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273), or a [semiperfect ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiperfect_ring) $R$ in general, we can define a **Nakayama permutation** as follows:

Given a complete orthogonal set $e_1, \dots, e_m$ of local [idempotents](wikipedia://en/361940), we can assume ordering of this set in such a way that for some $n \le m$ the right modules $e_1 R, \dots, e_n R$ form a complete set of finitely generated [projective modules](wikipedia://en/364488). Then $\pi \in S_n$ is a Nakayama permutation of a semiperfect ring $R$ if and only if for each $k \le n$:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{soc}(e_k R) &\cong \operatorname{top}(e_{\pi(k)} R) && \text{(R)} \\ \operatorname{soc}(R e_{\pi(k)}) &\cong \operatorname{top}(R e_k). && \text{(L)} \end{aligned}$$

In particular, a ring with a Nakayama permutation is a QF-2 [Kasch ring](wikipedia://en/34212423). A ring is (left and right) [pseudo-Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Frobenius_ring) if and only if it is a [self-injective ring](wikipedia://en/473775) with a Nakayama permutation.

A quasi-Frobenius ring is **Frobenius** if its Nakayama permutation preserves multiplicities. That is, given an indecomposable decomposition of a right module $R_R$, the number of indecomposable summands isomorphic to $e_kR$ equals the number of indecomposable summands isomorphic $e_{\pi(k)} R$ for any $k \le n$. In particular, a [basic](wikipedia://en/22658615#Basic_ring) quasi-Frobenius ring is a Frobenius ring.

## Examples {#Examples}

- Every Frobenius *k* algebra is a Frobenius ring.
- Every [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring) is quasi-Frobenius, since all modules are projective and injective. Even more is true however: semisimple rings are all Frobenius. This is easily verified by the definition, since for semisimple rings $\mathrm{soc}(R_R)=\mathrm{soc}(_R R)=R$ and *J* = rad(*R*) = 0.
- The [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $\mathbb{Z} / n \mathbb{Z}$ is QF for any positive integer *n*>1.
- Commutative Artinian [serial rings](wikipedia://en/31157948) are all Frobenius, and in fact have the additional property that every quotient ring *R*/*I* is also Frobenius. It turns out that among commutative Artinian rings, the serial rings are exactly the rings whose (nonzero) quotients are all Frobenius.
- Many exotic PF and FPF rings can be found as examples in (Faith Page 1984)

:::

## Notes {#Notes}

The definitions for QF, PF and FPF are easily seen to be categorical properties, and so they are preserved by [Morita equivalence](wikipedia://en/3296134), however being a Frobenius ring *is not* preserved.

For one-sided Noetherian rings the conditions of left or right PF both coincide with QF, but FPF rings are still distinct.

A finite-dimensional algebra *R* over a field *k* is a Frobenius *k*-algebra if and only if *R* is a Frobenius ring.

QF rings have the property that all of their modules can be embedded in a [free](wikipedia://en/326454) *R* module. This can be seen in the following way. A module *M* embeds into its [injective hull](wikipedia://en/665821) *E*(*M*), which is now also projective. As a projective module, *E*(*M*) is a summand of a free module *F*, and so *E*(*M*) embeds in *F* with the inclusion map. By composing these two maps, *M* is embedded in *F*.
