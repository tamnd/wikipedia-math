# Pseudo-Euclidean space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudo-Euclidean space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), a **pseudo-Euclidean space** of signature $(k, n - k)$ is a finite-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28mathematics%29) [real $n$-space](wikipedia://en/1482083) together with a non-[degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_form) [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) $q$. Such a quadratic form can, given a suitable choice of [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) $(e_{1}, ..., e_{n})$, be applied to a vector $x = x_{1}e_{1} + \cdots + x_{n}e_{n}$, giving

$$q(x) = \left(x_1^2 + \dots + x_k^2\right) - \left( x_{k+1}^2 + \dots + x_n^2\right) ,$$

which is called the *scalar square* of the vector $x$.[^o2]

:::

For [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697), $k = n$, implying that the quadratic form is [positive-definite](wikipedia://en/20437320).[^o3] When $0 < k < n$, $q$ is an [isotropic quadratic form](wikipedia://en/10917170). If $1 \le i \le k < j \le n$, then $q(e_{i} + e_{j}) = 0$, so that $e_{i} + e_{j}$ is a [null vector](wikipedia://en/1453977). In a pseudo-Euclidean space with $k < n$, unlike in a Euclidean space, there exist vectors with [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) scalar square.

As with the term *Euclidean space*, the term *pseudo-Euclidean space* may be used to refer to an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) or a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) depending on the author, with the latter alternatively being referred to as a **pseudo-Euclidean vector space**[^o4] (see *[Point–vector distinction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point–vector_distinction)*).

## Geometry {#Geometry}

The geometry of a pseudo-Euclidean space is consistent despite some properties of Euclidean space not applying, most notably that it is not a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) as explained below. The [affine structure](wikipedia://en/243890) is unchanged, and thus also the concepts [line](wikipedia://en/946975), [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) and, generally, of an [affine subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_subspace) ([flat](wikipedia://en/13005617)), as well as [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860).

### Positive, zero, and negative scalar squares {#Positive,_zero,_and_negative_scalar_squares}

*Image: DoubleCone.png.* $n = 3$, $k$ is either 1 or 2 depending on the choice of [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270) of $q$

A [null vector](wikipedia://en/1453977) is a vector for which the quadratic form is zero. Unlike in a Euclidean space, such a vector can be non-zero, in which case it is self-orthogonal. If the quadratic form is indefinite, a pseudo-Euclidean space has a [linear cone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_cone) of null vectors given by $\{\,x\mid q(x) = 0\,\}$. When the pseudo-Euclidean space provides a model for [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) (see below), the null cone is called the [light cone](wikipedia://en/310474) of the origin.

The null cone separates two [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358),[^o7] respectively for which $q(x) > 0$ and $q(x) < 0$. If $k \ge 2$, then the set of vectors for which $q(x) > 0$ is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233). If $k = 1$, then it consists of two disjoint parts, one with $x_{1} > 0$ and another with $x_{1} < 0$. Similarly, if $n - k \ge 2$, then the set of vectors for which $q(x) < 0$ is connected. If $n - k = 1$, then it consists of two disjoint parts, one with $x_{n} > 0$ and another with $x_{n} < 0$.

### Interval {#Interval}

The quadratic form $q$ corresponds to the square of a vector in the Euclidean case. To define the [vector norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_norm) (and distance) in an [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) manner, one has to get [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208) of scalar squares, which leads to possibly [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) distances; see *[Square root of negative numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_root_of_negative_numbers)*. But even for a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) with positive scalar squares of all three sides (whose square roots are real and positive), the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) does not hold in general.

Hence terms *norm* and *distance* are avoided in pseudo-Euclidean geometry, which may be replaced with *scalar square* and *spacetime interval* respectively.

Though, for a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) whose [tangent vectors](wikipedia://en/191999) all have scalar squares of the same sign, the [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) is defined. It has important applications: see *[Proper time](wikipedia://en/494418)*, for example.

### Rotations and spheres {#Rotations_and_spheres}

*Image: Hyperboloid1.png.*

The [rotations](wikipedia://en/410009) [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of such space is the [indefinite orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/293609) $O(q)$, also denoted as $O(k,\,n - k)$ without a reference to particular quadratic form.[^o10] Such "rotations" preserve the form $q$ and, hence, the scalar square of each vector including whether it is positive, zero, or negative.

Whereas Euclidean space has a [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090), pseudo-Euclidean space has the [hypersurfaces](wikipedia://en/525101) $\{\,x | q(x) = 1\,\}$ and $\{\,x | q(x) = -1\,\}$. Such a hypersurface, called a [quasi-sphere](wikipedia://en/51406535), is preserved by the appropriate indefinite orthogonal group.

### Symmetric bilinear form {#Symmetric_bilinear_form}

The quadratic form $q$ gives rise to a [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) defined as follows:

$$\langle x, y\rangle = \tfrac12[q(x + y) - q(x) - q(y)] = \left(x_1 y_1 + \ldots + x_k y_k\right) - \left(x_{k+1}y_{k+1} + \ldots + x_n y_n\right).$$

The quadratic form can be expressed in terms of the bilinear form: $q(x) = \langle x, x\rangle$.

When $\langle x, y\rangle = 0$, $x$ and $y$ are [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221) vectors of the pseudo-Euclidean space.

This bilinear form is often referred to as the [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product), and sometimes as "inner product" or "dot product", but it does not define an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) and it does not have the properties of the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of Euclidean vectors.

If $x$ and $y$ are orthogonal and $q(x)q(y) < 0$, then $x$ is [hyperbolic-orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic-orthogonal) to $y$.

The [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) of the real $n$-space is [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/755400). There are no ortho*normal* bases in a pseudo-Euclidean space for which the bilinear form is indefinite, because it cannot be used to define a [vector norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_norm).

### Subspaces and orthogonality {#Subspaces_and_orthogonality}

For a (positive-dimensional) subspace[^o13] $U$ of a pseudo-Euclidean space, when the quadratic form $q$ is [restricted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_restriction) to $U$, following three cases are possible:

1. $q|_{U}$ is either [positive or negative definite](wikipedia://en/20437320). Then, $U$ is essentially [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9697) (up to the sign of $q$).
1. $q|_{U}$ is indefinite, but non-degenerate. Then, $U$ is itself pseudo-Euclidean. It is possible only if $\dim\,U \ge 2$; if $\dim\,U = 2$, which means than $U$ is a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), then it is called a [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane_%28quadratic_forms%29).
1. $q|_{U}$ is degenerate.

One of the most jarring properties (for a Euclidean intuition) of pseudo-Euclidean vectors and flats is their [orthogonality](wikipedia://en/102221). When two non-zero [Euclidean vectors](wikipedia://en/32533) are orthogonal, they are not [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear). The intersections of any Euclidean [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) with its [orthogonal complement](wikipedia://en/1058833) is the [$\{0\}$ subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_vector_space). But the definition from the previous subsection immediately implies that any vector $\mathbf{\nu}$ of zero scalar square is orthogonal to itself. Hence, the [isotropic line](wikipedia://en/8088814) $N = \langle\mathbf{\nu}\rangle$ generated by a [null vector](wikipedia://en/1453977) $\mathbf{\nu}$ is a subset of its orthogonal complement $N^{\perp}$.

The formal definition of the orthogonal complement of a vector subspace in a pseudo-Euclidean space gives a perfectly well-defined result, which satisfies the equality $\dim\,U + \dim\,U^{\perp} = n$ due to the quadratic form's non-degeneracy. It is just the condition

$U \cap U^{\perp} = \{0\}$ or, equivalently, $U + U^{\perp} =$ all space,

which can be broken if the subspace $U$ contains a null direction.[^o14] While subspaces [form a lattice](wikipedia://en/56357#Lattice_of_subspaces), as in any vector space, this $\perp$ operation is not an [orthocomplementation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthocomplementation), in contrast to [inner product spaces](wikipedia://en/14856).

For a subspace $N$ composed *entirely* of null vectors (which means that the scalar square $q$, restricted to $N$, equals to $0$), always holds:

$N \subset N^{\perp}$ or, equivalently, $N \cap N^{\perp} = N$.

Such a subspace can have up to $\min(k, n - k)$ [dimensions](wikipedia://en/38267).[^o15]

For a (positive) Euclidean $k$-subspace its orthogonal complement is a $(n - k)$-dimensional negative "Euclidean" subspace, and vice versa. Generally, for a $(d_{+} + d_{-} + d_{0})$-dimensional subspace $U$ consisting of $d_{+}$ positive and $d_{-}$ negative dimensions (see *[Sylvester's law of inertia](wikipedia://en/1127460)* for clarification), its orthogonal "complement" $U^{\perp}$ has $(k - d_{+} - d_{0})$ positive and $(n - k - d_{-} - d_{0})$ negative dimensions, while the rest $d_{0}$ ones are degenerate and form the $U \cap U^{\perp}$ intersection.

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Parallelogram law and Pythagorean theorem"}

### Parallelogram law and Pythagorean theorem {#Parallelogram_law_and_Pythagorean_theorem}

The [parallelogram law](wikipedia://en/362953) takes the form

$$q(x) + q(y) = \tfrac12(q(x + y) + q(x - y)).$$

Using the [square of the sum](wikipedia://en/4677#Examples) identity, for an arbitrary triangle one can express the scalar square of the third side from scalar squares of two sides and their bilinear form product:

$$q(x + y) = q(x) + q(y) + 2\langle x, y \rangle.$$

This demonstrates that, for orthogonal vectors, a pseudo-Euclidean analog of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) holds:

$$\langle x, y \rangle = 0 \Rightarrow q(x) + q(y) = q(x + y).$$

:::

## Algebra and tensor calculus {#Algebra_and_tensor_calculus}

Like Euclidean spaces, every pseudo-Euclidean vector space generates a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305). Unlike properties above, where replacement of $q$ to $-q$ changed numbers but not [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the sign reversal of the quadratic form results in a distinct Clifford algebra, so for example $\operatorname{Cl}_{1,2}(\mathbf{R})$ and $\operatorname{Cl}_{2,1}(\mathbf{R})$ are not isomorphic.

Just like over any vector space, there are pseudo-Euclidean [tensors](wikipedia://en/29965). Like with a Euclidean structure, there are [raising and lowering indices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raising_and_lowering_indices) operators but, unlike the case with [Euclidean tensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_tensor), there is no bases where these operations do not change values of components. If there is a vector $v^{\beta}$, the corresponding [covariant vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariant_vector) is:

$$v_\alpha = q_{\alpha\beta} v^\beta\,,$$

and with the standard-form

$$q_{\alpha\beta} = \begin{pmatrix} I_{k\times k} & 0 \\ 0 & -I_{(n-k)\times(n-k)} \end{pmatrix}$$

the first $k$ components of $v_{\alpha}$ are numerically the same as ones of $v^{\beta}$, but the remaining $n - k$ have the [opposite sign](wikipedia://en/228312).

The correspondence between contravariant and covariant tensors makes a [tensor calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_calculus) on [pseudo-Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/287555) a generalization of one on Riemannian manifolds.

## Application {#Application}

*Image: Conjugate-unit-hyperbolas.svg.* [Conjugate hyperbolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola) in pseudo-Euclidean space with $n = 2$, $k = 1$

Suppose a pseudo-Euclidean space has two vectors, with non-zero quadratic forms, spanning a plane in the space. If the vectors have the same sign for their quadratic forms, the restriction of the spatial-form to the plane will be a [definite quadratic form](wikipedia://en/20437320). In the other case the restriction yields an [isotropic quadratic form](wikipedia://en/10917170). By rescaling axes, if necessary, the form becomes $x^{2} - y^{2}$ and the plane is known as the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane_%28quadratic_forms%29) in the theory of quadratic forms. When the form is set to plus and minus one, the solution sets are a pair of [conjugate hyperbolas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_hyperbola). This pair of figures, described long ago by [Apollonius of Perga](wikipedia://en/257242), was used in 1908 to indicate time and distance units by [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537).

The two planar cases give two theories of angle: the definite case corresponds to [circular angle](wikipedia://en/1196) while the isotropic case yields [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981). Just as a rotation by a circular angle can be represented by [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) of the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), so [hyperbolic rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_rotation) results from group action of the [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) group on the plane of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559). In the [theory of special relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_special_relativity) the idea of speed is changed to [rapidity](wikipedia://en/4520673), which is a hyperbolic angle. In the jargon of physics, a hyperbolic rotation is a [Lorentz boost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_boost).

The hyperbolic rotation does not conserve Euclidean distances, so it is *not* a [Euclidean motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_motion). Nevertheless, [area](wikipedia://en/1209) is conserved, which is a *Euclidean notion*, so the geometry is pseudo-Euclidean but not Euclidean.

:::
