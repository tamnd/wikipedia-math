# Robert Murphy (mathematician)

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Robert Murphy"}

**Robert Murphy** [FRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Society) (1806 – 12 March 1843) was an Irish mathematician and physicist who made contributions to algebra.

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Robert Murphy was born in 1806 in [Mallow, County Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mallow,_County_Cork), Ireland and was baptized in the [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) on 8 March 1807, the third son (of seven children) of John Murphy, a shoemaker, and Margaret Murphy. When he was 11, Murphy was run over by a cart in an accident that resulted in a fractured thighbone. This incident left him bedridden for one year. During this time, Murphy read the works of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) and studied algebra.[^o4]

Anonymous solutions to mathematical problems posed in a local newspaper brought Murphy attention from a mathematical tutor in Cork, named Mulcahy. Money was found for him to attend Mr Hopley's school in Mallow. Murphy then had sponsorship to take him to [Trinity College, Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Dublin), in 1823; but was not admitted. With support from [Robert Woodhouse](wikipedia://en/564987), he was brought to Cambridge on the strength of a pamphlet on the [duplication of the cube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duplication_of_the_cube). He was admitted as a pensioner to [Gonville and Caius College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gonville_and_Caius_College,_Cambridge), on 7 July 1825.[^o5]

Murphy won the 1st Mathematics Prize in 1826 and went on to graduate with a first class degree, B.A. in 1829, as 3rd [wrangler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wrangler_%28University_of_Cambridge%29). It led to Murphy being awarded a [Perse Fellowship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Perse). At the same time, to help his financial position, he was appointed as Librarian.

## Later life {#Later_life}

Murphy gave six Hebrew lectures in 1830 (receiving £10 as payment), and was appointed as a junior dean in charge of discipline and chapel services in October 1831, a position he held until 1833. He was ordained a deacon on 4 June 1831 and gave Greek lectures in 1832. But he never obtained a senior fellowship at Caius.[^o5]

While living in London in difficult circumstances, Murphy wrote a paper on what are now called [non-commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_ring). He was elected to a Stokes Fellowship by Caius College, Cambridge in 1838. He was appointed as an examiner of mathematics and natural philosophy at the University of London in October 1838.

## Death {#Death}

Murphy's years of alcohol abuse took a toll on his health. In 1843, he contracted [tuberculosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tuberculosis) of the lungs. He died soon after, on March 12, 1843. Murphy was buried in [Kensal Green Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kensal_Green_Cemetery), London, where "\[t\]he grave has no headstone nor landing stone nor surround. It is totally unmarked".

## Publications {#Publications}

In 1830 Murphy was commissioned to write a book on the mathematical theory of electricity, for the use of students at Cambridge. *Elementary Principles of Electricity, Heat, and Molecular Actions, part i. On Electricity* (Cambridge) was published in 1833. His other book was *Theory of Algebraical Equations*, in "Library of Useful Knowledge", London, 1839, reprinted 1847.[^o9]

### Papers and articles {#Papers_and_articles}

*Refutation of a Pamphlet Written by the Rev. John Mackey Entitled "A Method of Making a Cube a Double of a Cube, Founded on the Principles of Elementary Geometry", wherein His Principles Are Proved Erroneous and the Required Solution Not Yet Obtained* (1824) was the work that launched Murphy's career. Another notable paper was (1833). "On the inverse method of definite integrals". *Transactions of the Cambridge Philosophical Society*. 4. p. 353–408 It included the first citation of the work of [George Green](wikipedia://en/243343). Murphy recognized its value, in particular Green's "1828 essay".[^o5]

Murphy contributed other mathematical papers to the *Cambridge Philosophical Transactions* (1831–1836), [Philosophical Magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Magazine) (1833–1842), and the *[Philosophical Transactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_Transactions)* (1837). Encouraged by [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289), Murphy wrote articles for the [Society for the Diffusion of Useful Knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Society_for_the_Diffusion_of_Useful_Knowledge) and for the *[Penny Cyclopaedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penny_Cyclopaedia)*. His final works were *Remark on Primitive Radices* (1841), *Calculations of Logarithms by Means of Algebraic Fractions* (1841), and *On Atmospheric Refraction* (1842). De Morgan claimed "He had a true genius for mathematical invention".
