# Doignon's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Doignon's theorem"}

**Doignon's theorem** in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) is an analogue of [Helly's theorem](wikipedia://en/1409506) for the [integer lattice](wikipedia://en/3333402). It states that, if a family of [convex sets](wikipedia://en/6292) in $d$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) have the property that the intersection of every $2^d$ contains an integer point, then the intersection of all of the sets contains an integer point. Therefore, $d$-dimensional [integer linear programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_linear_program) form an [LP-type problem](wikipedia://en/34676009) of combinatorial dimension $2^d$, and can be solved by certain generalizations of [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730) algorithms in an amount of time that is linear in the number of constraints of the problem and [fixed-parameter tractable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-parameter_tractable) in its dimension. The same theorem applies more generally to any [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246), not just the integer lattice.

:::

The theorem can be classified as belonging to [convex geometry](wikipedia://en/4165882), [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468), and the [geometry of numbers](wikipedia://en/250153). It is named after Belgian mathematician and [mathematical psychologist](wikipedia://en/2081516) Jean-Paul Doignon, who published it in 1973. Doignon credits [Francis Buekenhout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_Buekenhout) with posing the question answered by this theorem. It is also called the **Doignon–Bell–Scarf theorem**, crediting [mathematical economists](wikipedia://en/18967255) David E. Bell and [Herbert Scarf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Scarf), who both rediscovered it in 1977 and pointed out its applications to integer programming.

The result is tight: there exist systems of [half-spaces](wikipedia://en/647297) for which every $2^d-1$ have an integer point in their intersection, but for which the whole system has no integer intersection. Such a system can be obtained, for instance, by choosing halfspaces that contain all but one vertex of the [unit cube](wikipedia://en/407763). Another way of phrasing the result is that the Helly number of convex subsets of the integers is exactly $2^d$. More generally, the Helly number of any discrete set of Euclidean points equals the maximum number of points that can be chosen to form the vertices of a [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885) that contains no other point from the set. Generalizing both Helly's theorem and Doignon's theorem, the Helly number of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $\mathbb{Z}^d\times\mathbb{R}^k$ is $2^d(k+1)$.
