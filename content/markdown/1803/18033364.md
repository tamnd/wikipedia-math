# Trivially perfect graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trivially perfect graph"}

*Image: Trivially perfect graph.svg.* Construction of a trivially perfect graph from nested intervals and from the reachability relationship in a tree

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **trivially perfect graph** is a graph with the property that in each of its [induced subgraphs](wikipedia://en/1655188) the size of the [maximum independent set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_independent_set) equals the number of [maximal cliques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_clique).[^o2] Trivially perfect graphs were first studied by () but were named by (Golumbic 1978); Golumbic writes that "the name was chosen since it is trivial to show that such a graph is [perfect](wikipedia://en/670531)." Trivially perfect graphs are also known as **comparability graphs of trees**,[^o5] **arborescent comparability graphs**,[^o6] and **quasi-threshold graphs**.[^o7]

:::

## Equivalent characterizations {#Equivalent_characterizations}

Trivially perfect graphs have several other equivalent characterizations:

- They are the [comparability graphs](wikipedia://en/6978672) of [order-theoretic trees](wikipedia://en/704160). That is, let $T$ be a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) such that for each $t \in T$, the set $\{s \in T : s < t\}$ is [well-ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordered) by the [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) $<$, and also $T$ possesses a minimum element $r$. Then the comparability graph of $T$ is trivially perfect, and every trivially perfect graph can be formed in this way.[^o9]
- They are the graphs that do not have a $P_{4}$ [path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190) or a $C_{4}$ [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) as [induced subgraphs](wikipedia://en/1655188).[^o10]
- They are the graphs in which every connected [induced subgraph](wikipedia://en/1655188) contains a [universal vertex](wikipedia://en/51352780).[^n1]
- They are the graphs that can be represented as the [interval graphs](wikipedia://en/363423) for a set of nested [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172). A set of intervals is nested if, for every two intervals in the set, either the two are disjoint or one contains the other.[^o12]
- They are the graphs that are both [chordal](wikipedia://en/744165) and [cographs](wikipedia://en/1175666).[^o13] This follows from the characterization of chordal graphs as the graphs without induced cycles of length greater than three, and of cographs as the graphs without [induced paths](wikipedia://en/7714430) on four vertices ($P_{4}$).
- They are the graphs that are both cographs and interval graphs.[^o13]
- They are the graphs that can be formed, starting from one-vertex graphs, by two operations: disjoint union of two smaller trivially perfect graphs, and the addition of a new vertex adjacent to all the vertices of a smaller trivially perfect graph.[^o14] These operations correspond, in the underlying forest, to forming a new forest by the disjoint union of two smaller forests and forming a tree by connecting a new root node to the roots of all the trees in a forest.
- They are the graphs in which, for every edge $uv$, the [neighborhoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28graph_theory%29) of $u$ and $v$ (including $u$ and $v$ themselves) are nested: one neighborhood must be a subset of the other.[^o15]
- They are the [permutation graphs](wikipedia://en/17625375) defined from [stack-sortable permutations](wikipedia://en/39287529).[^o16]
- They are the graphs with the property that in each of its induced subgraphs the [clique cover number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clique_cover_number) equals the number of [maximal cliques](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_clique).[^n2]
- They are the graphs with the property that in each of its induced subgraphs the [clique number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clique_number) equals the [pseudo-Grundy number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Grundy_number).[^n2]
- They are the graphs with the property that in each of its induced subgraphs the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) equals the [pseudo-Grundy number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Grundy_number).[^n2]

## Related classes of graphs {#Related_classes_of_graphs}

It follows from the equivalent characterizations of trivially perfect graphs that every trivially perfect graph is also a [cograph](wikipedia://en/1175666), a [chordal graph](wikipedia://en/744165), a [Ptolemaic graph](wikipedia://en/50730814), an [interval graph](wikipedia://en/363423), and a [perfect graph](wikipedia://en/670531).

The [threshold graphs](wikipedia://en/11503485) are exactly the graphs that are both themselves trivially perfect and the complements of trivially perfect graphs (co-trivially perfect graphs).[^o21]

[Windmill graphs](wikipedia://en/24373456) are trivially perfect.

## Recognition {#Recognition}

(Chu 2008) describes a simple [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) algorithm for recognizing trivially perfect graphs, based on [lexicographic breadth-first search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicographic_breadth-first_search). Whenever the LexBFS algorithm removes a vertex $v$ from the first set on its queue, the algorithm checks that all remaining neighbors of $v$ belong to the same set; if not, one of the forbidden induced subgraphs can be constructed from $v$. If this check succeeds for every $v$, then the graph is trivially perfect. The algorithm can also be modified to test whether a graph is the [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) of a trivially perfect graph, in linear time.

Determining if a general graph is $k$ edge deletions away from a trivially perfect graph is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete),[^n3] fixed-parameter tractable[^n4] and can be solved in $O(2.45^{k}(m + n))$ time.[^n5]
