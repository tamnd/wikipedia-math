# Defining equation (physical chemistry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Defining equation"}

In **physical chemistry**, there are numerous quantities associated with [chemical compounds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compounds) and [reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction); notably in terms of *amounts* of substance, *activity* or *concentration* of a substance, and the *rate* of reaction. This article uses [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_units).

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

[Theoretical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_chemistry) requires [quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity) from core [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), such as [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time), [volume](wikipedia://en/32498), [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050), and [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure). But the highly quantitative nature of physical chemistry, in a more specialized way than core physics, uses [molar amounts of substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amount_of_substance) rather than simply counting numbers; this leads to the specialized definitions in this article. Core physics itself rarely uses the mole, except in areas overlapping [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) and [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry).

## Notes on nomenclature {#Notes_on_nomenclature}

*Entity* refers to the type of particle/s in question, such as [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms), [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecules), [complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_complex), [radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_%28chemistry%29), [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ions), [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrons) etc.

Conventionally for [concentrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentrations) and [activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_%28chemistry%29), square brackets \[ \] are used around the [chemical molecular formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_formula). For an arbitrary atom, generic letters in upright non-bold typeface such as A, B, R, X or Y etc. are often used.

No standard symbols are used for the following quantities, as specifically applied to a [substance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter):

- the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of a substance *m*,
- the number of moles of the substance *n*,
- [partial pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_pressure) of a gas in a gaseous mixture *p* (or *P*),
- some form of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) of a substance (for chemistry [enthalpy](wikipedia://en/10274) *H* is common),
- [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) of a substance *S*
- the [electronegativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronegativity) of an atom or [chemical bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_bond) *χ*.

Usually the symbol for the quantity with a subscript of some reference to the quantity is used, or the quantity is written with the reference to the chemical in round brackets. For example, the mass of [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) might be written in subscripts as *m*<sub>H<sub>2</sub>O</sub>, *m*<sub>water</sub>, *m*<sub>aq</sub>, *m*<sub>w</sub> (if clear from context) etc., or simply as *m*(H<sub>2</sub>O). Another example could be the electronegativity of the [fluorine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluorine)-fluorine [covalent bond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covalent_bond), which might be written with subscripts *χ*<sub>F-F</sub>, *χ*<sub>FF</sub> or *χ*<sub>F-F</sub> etc., or brackets *χ*(F-F), *χ*(FF) etc.

Neither is standard. For the purpose of this article, the nomenclature is as follows, closely (but not exactly) matching standard use.

For general equations with no specific reference to an entity, quantities are written as their symbols with an index to label the component of the mixture - i.e. *q<sub>i</sub>*. The labeling is arbitrary in initial choice, but once chosen fixed for the calculation.

If any reference to an actual entity (say hydrogen ions H<sup>\+</sup>) or any entity at all (say X) is made, the quantity symbol *q* is followed by curved ( ) brackets enclosing the molecular formula of X, i.e. *q*(X), or for a component *i* of a mixture *q*(X<sub>*i*</sub>). No confusion should arise with the notation for a [mathematical function](wikipedia://en/185427).

## Kinetics and equilibria {#Kinetics_and_equilibria}

The defining formulae for the [equilibrium constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constants) *K<sub>c</sub>* (all reactions) and *K<sub>p</sub>* (gaseous reactions) apply to the general chemical reaction:

$$\ce{{\nu_1 X1} + {\nu_2 X2} + \cdots + \nu_\mathit{r} X_\mathit{r} <=> {\eta_1 Y1} + {\eta_2 Y2} + \cdots + \eta_\mathit{p} {Y}_\mathit{p}}$$

and the defining equation for the [rate constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rate_constant) *k* applies to the simpler synthesis reaction (one [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_%28chemistry%29) only):

$$\ce{{\nu_1 X1} + {\nu_2 X2} + \cdots + \nu_\mathit{r} X_\mathit{r} -> \eta {Y}}$$

where:

- *i* = dummy index labelling component *i* of [reactant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reagent) mixture,
- *j* = dummy index labelling component *i* of [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_%28chemistry%29) mixture,
- X<sub>*i*</sub> = component *i* of the reactant mixture,
- Y<sub>*j*</sub> = reactant component *j* of the product mixture,
- *r* (as an index) = number of reactant components,
- *p* (as an index) = number of product components,
- *ν<sub>i</sub>* = [stoichiometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometry) number for component *i* in product mixture,
- *η<sub>j</sub>* = stoichiometry number for component *j* in product mixture,
- *σ<sub>i</sub>* = [order of reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_reaction) for component *i* in reactant mixture.

The dummy indices on the substances *X* and *Y* *label* the components (arbitrary but fixed for calculation); they are not the *numbers* of each component molecules as in usual [chemistry notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_formula).

The units for the chemical constants are unusual since they can vary depending on the stoichiometry of the reaction, and the number of reactant and product components. The general units for equilibrium constants can be determined by usual methods of [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267). For the generality of the kinetics and equilibria units below, let the indices for the units be;

$$S_1 = \sum_{j=1}^p \eta_j - \sum_{i=1}^r \nu_i \,,\quad\, S_2 = 1-\sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i\,.$$

For the constant *K<sub>c</sub>*;

Substitute the concentration units into the equation and simplify:,

$$\begin{aligned} K_c & = \frac{\prod_{j=1}^p \left [ \ce Y_j \right ]^{y_j}}{\prod_{i=1}^r \left [ \ce X_i \right ]^{x_i} } \\ \left[K_c\right] & = \frac{\prod_{j=1}^p [\ce M]^{y_j}}{\prod_{i=1}^r [\ce M]^{x_i} } \\ & = \frac{[\ce M]^{y_1} [\ce M]^{y_2} \cdots [\ce M]^{y_p}}{[\ce M]^{x_1} [\ce M]^{x_2} \cdots [\ce M]^{x_r} } \\ & = \frac{[\ce M]^{\sum_{j=1}^p y_j}}{[\ce M]^{\sum_{i=1}^r x_i} } \\ & = [\ce M]^{\sum_{j=1}^p y_j - \sum_{i=1}^r x_i} \end{aligned} \ (\ce{M = mol \; dm^{-3}})$$

The procedure is exactly identical for *K<sub>p</sub>*.

For the constant *k*

$$\begin{aligned} k & = \frac{\frac{\mathrm{d}[\ce Y]}{\mathrm{d}t}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}\left[ \ce X_i \right]^{\sigma_i}} \\ \left[k\right] & = \frac{[\ce M]s^{-1}}{\prod_{i=1}^{r}[\ce M]^{\sigma_i}} \\ & = \frac{[\ce M]s^{-1}}{[\ce M]^{\sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i}} \\ & = [\ce M]^{1-\sum_{i=1}^{r} \sigma_i} s^{-1} \end{aligned}$$

## Electrochemistry {#Electrochemistry}

Notation for [half-reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-reaction) [standard electrode potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_electrode_potential) is as follows. The [redox reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox)

$$\ce{A + BX <=> B + AX}$$

split into:

- a [reduction reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox): $\ce{B+ + e^- <=> B}$
- and an [oxidation reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox): $\ce{A+ + e^- <=> A}$

(written this way by convention) the electrode potential for the half reactions are written as $E^\ominus\left(\ce{A+} \vert \ce{A} \right)$ and $E^\ominus\left( \ce{B+} \vert \ce{B} \right)$ respectively.

For the case of a metal-metal half electrode, letting M represent the [metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metal) and *z* be its [valency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_%28chemistry%29), the half reaction takes the form of a reduction reaction:

$$\ce{{M^{+\mathit{z}}} + \mathit{z} e^- <=> M}$$
