# Horvitz–Thompson estimator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Horvitz–Thompson estimator"}

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **Horvitz–Thompson estimator**, named after [Daniel G. Horvitz](wikipedia://en/71233125) and Donovan J. Thompson,[^o2] is a method for estimating the total[^o3] and mean of a [pseudo-population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-population) in a [stratified sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratified_sample) by applying [inverse probability weighting](wikipedia://en/33350282) to account for the difference in the sampling distribution between the collected data and the target population. The Horvitz–Thompson estimator is frequently applied in [survey analyses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_survey) and can be used to account for [missing data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Missing_values), as well as many [sources of unequal selection probabilities](wikipedia://en/26286630#Sources_for_unequal_selection_probabilities).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="The method"}

## The method {#The_method}

Formally, let $Y_i, i = 1, 2, \ldots, n$ be an [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) sample from $n$ of $N \ge n$ distinct [strata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratum_%28statistics%29) with an overall mean $\mu$. Suppose further that $\pi_i$ is the [inclusion probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_probability) that a randomly sampled individual in a superpopulation belongs to the $i$th stratum. The Horvitz–Thompson estimator of the total is given by:[^o6]

$$\hat{Y}_\mathrm{HT} = \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{Y_i}{\pi_i},$$

and the Horvitz–Thompson estimate of the mean is given by:

$$\hat{\mu}_\mathrm{HT} = \frac1N\hat{Y}_{HT} = \frac1N\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{Y_i}{\pi_i}.$$

In a [Bayesian](wikipedia://en/4890) probabilistic framework $\pi_i$ is considered the proportion of individuals in a target population belonging to the $i$th stratum. Hence, $Y_i/\pi_i$ could be thought of as an estimate of the complete sample of persons within the $i$th stratum. The Horvitz–Thompson estimator can also be expressed as the limit of a weighted [bootstrap](wikipedia://en/6885770) [resampling](wikipedia://en/3763850) estimate of the mean. It can also be viewed as a special case of multiple [imputation](wikipedia://en/1309220) approaches.[^o7]

For [post-stratified](wikipedia://en/12106854) study designs, estimation of $\pi$ and $\mu$ are done in distinct steps. In such cases, computating the variance of $\hat{\mu}_{HT}$ is not straightforward. Resampling techniques such as the bootstrap or the jackknife can be applied to gain consistent estimates of the variance of the Horvitz–Thompson estimator.[^o8] The "survey" package for [R](wikipedia://en/376707) conducts analyses for post-stratified data using the Horvitz–Thompson estimator.[^o9]

:::

::: {#o11 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of Horvitz–Thompson unbiased estimation of the mean"}

## Proof of Horvitz–Thompson unbiased estimation of the mean {#Proof_of_Horvitz–Thompson_unbiased_estimation_of_the_mean}

For this proof it will be useful to represent the sample as a random subset $S\subseteq\{1,\ldots,N\}$ of size $n$. We can then define indicator random variables $I_j = \mathbf{1}[j \in S]$ representing whether for each $j$ in $\{1,\ldots,N\}$ whether it is present in the sample. Note that for any observation in the sample, the expectation is the definition of the inclusion probability: $\pi_i = \operatorname{\mathbb E}\left(I_i\right) = \Pr(i\in S)$.

Taking the expectation of the estimator we can prove it is unbiased as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{\mathbb E}\left(\hat{\mu}_\mathrm{HT}\right) &= \operatorname{\mathbb E}\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i\in S} \frac{Y_i}{\pi_i}\right)\\[6pt] &=\operatorname{\mathbb E}\left(\frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \frac{Y_j}{\pi_j}I_j\right) \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{j=1}^N \frac{Y_j}{\pi_j}\operatorname{\mathbb E}\left(I_j \right)\\&= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N \frac{Y_j}{\pi_j}\pi_j \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{N}\sum_{j=1}^N Y_j \end{aligned}$$

The Hansen–Hurwitz (1943) is known to be inferior to the Horvitz–Thompson (1952) strategy, associated with a number of Inclusion Probabilities Proportional to Size (IPPS) sampling procedures.[^o12]

:::
