# Complete topological vector space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complete topological vector space"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **complete topological vector space** is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) with the property that whenever points get progressively closer to each other, then there exists some point $x$ towards which they all get closer. The notion of "points that get progressively closer" is made rigorous by [Cauchy nets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_net) or [Cauchy filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_filter), which are generalizations of [Cauchy sequences](wikipedia://en/6085), while "point $x$ towards which they all get closer" means that this Cauchy [net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_net) or filter [converges to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_filter) $x.$ The notion of completeness for TVSs uses the theory of [uniform spaces](wikipedia://en/32339) as a framework to generalize the notion of [completeness for metric spaces](wikipedia://en/7832). But unlike metric-completeness, TVS-completeness does not depend on any metric and is defined for all TVSs, including those that are not [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738) or [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637).

:::

Completeness is an extremely important property for a topological vector space to possess. The notions of completeness for [normed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) and [metrizable TVSs](wikipedia://en/64278078), which are commonly defined in terms of [completeness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_metric) of a particular norm or metric, can both be reduced down to this notion of TVS-completeness – a notion that is independent of any particular norm or metric. A [metrizable topological vector space](wikipedia://en/64278078) $X$ with a [translation invariant metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_invariant_metric)[^o2] $d$ is complete as a TVS if and only if $(X, d)$ is a [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832), which by definition means that every $d$-[Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) converges to some point in $X.$ Prominent examples of complete TVSs that are also [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) include all [F-spaces](wikipedia://en/200440) and consequently also all [Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/233801), [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989), and [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932). Prominent examples of complete TVS that are (typically) not metrizable include strict [LF-spaces](wikipedia://en/2314852) such as the [space of test functions](wikipedia://en/67789038) $C_c^\infty(U)$ with it canonical LF-topology, the [strong dual space](wikipedia://en/63951372) of any non-normable [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801), as well as many other [polar topologies](wikipedia://en/1789978) on [continuous dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) or other [topologies on spaces of linear maps](wikipedia://en/41677409).

Explicitly, a [topological vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_vector_spaces) (TVS) is **complete** if every [net](wikipedia://en/22170), or equivalently, every [filter](wikipedia://en/47516955), that is [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_net) with respect to the space's canonical [uniformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformity_%28topology%29) necessarily converges to some point. Said differently, a TVS is complete if its canonical uniformity is a [complete uniformity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_uniformity). The **canonical uniformity** on a TVS $(X, \tau)$ is the unique[^o3] translation-invariant [uniformity](wikipedia://en/32339) that induces on $X$ the topology $\tau.$ This notion of "TVS-completeness" depends only on vector subtraction and the topology of the TVS; consequently, it can be applied to all TVSs, including those whose topologies can not be defined in terms [metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) or [pseudometrics](wikipedia://en/68503). A [first-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-countable) TVS is complete [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) every Cauchy sequence (or equivalently, every [elementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_filter) Cauchy filter) converges to some point.

Every topological vector space $X,$ even if it is not [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738) or not [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), has a [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28uniform_space%29), which by definition is a complete TVS $C$ into which $X$ can be [TVS-embedded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVS-embedding) as a [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [vector subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subspace). Moreover, every Hausdorff TVS has a Hausdorff completion, which is necessarily unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [TVS-isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVS-isomorphism). However, as discussed below, all TVSs have infinitely many non-Hausdorff completions that are not TVS-isomorphic to one another.

## Definitions {#Definitions}

This section summarizes the definition of a complete [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/63987005) (TVS) in terms of both [nets](wikipedia://en/22170) and [prefilters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefilter). Information about convergence of nets and filters, such as definitions and properties, can be found in the article about [filters in topology](wikipedia://en/47516955).

Every topological vector space (TVS) is a commutative [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) with identity under addition and the canonical uniformity of a TVS is defined entirely in terms of subtraction (and thus addition); [scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373) is not involved and no additional structure is needed.

### Canonical uniformity {#Canonical_uniformity}

The **diagonal** of $X$ is the set[^n1]

$$\Delta_X ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{(x, x) : x \in X\}$$

and for any $N \subseteq X,$ the **canonical entourage**/**canonical vicinity around $N$** is the set

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} \Delta_X(N) ~&~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{(x, y) \in X \times X ~:~ x - y \in N\} \\ &= \bigcup_{y \in X} [(y + N) \times \{y\}] \\ &= \Delta_X + (N \times \{0\}) \end{alignedat}$$

where if $0 \in N$ then $\Delta_X(N)$ contains the diagonal $\Delta_X(\{0\}) = \Delta_X.$

If $N$ is a [symmetric set](wikipedia://en/22260858) (that is, if $- N = N$), then $\Delta_X(N)$ is **symmetric**, which by definition means that $\Delta_X(N) = \left(\Delta_X(N)\right)^{\operatorname{op}}$ holds where $\left(\Delta_X(N)\right)^{\operatorname{op}} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left\{(y, x) : (x, y) \in \Delta_X(N)\right\},$ and in addition, this symmetric set's composition with itself is:

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} \Delta_X(N) \circ \Delta_X(N) ~&~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left\{(x, z) \in X \times X ~:~ \text{ there exists } y \in X \text{ such that } x, z \in y + N\right\}\\ & = \bigcup_{y \in X} [(y + N) \times (y + N)] \\ &= \Delta_X + (N \times N). \end{alignedat}$$

If $\mathcal{L}$ is any neighborhood basis at the origin in $(X, \tau)$ then the [family of subsets](wikipedia://en/723043) of $X \times X:$ $\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left\{\Delta_X(N) : N \in \mathcal{L}\right\}$ is a [prefilter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefilter) on $X \times X.$ If $\mathcal{N}_{\tau}(0)$ is the [neighborhood filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_filter) at the origin in $(X, \tau)$ then $\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}(0)}$ forms a [base of entourages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_of_entourages) for a [uniform structure](wikipedia://en/32339) on $X$ that is considered canonical.[^n2] Explicitly, by definition, the **canonical uniformity** on $X$ induced by $(X, \tau)$[^n2] is the [filter](wikipedia://en/12646549) $\mathcal{U}_{\tau}$ on $X \times X$ [generated by the above prefilter](wikipedia://en/47516955):

$$\mathcal{U}_{\tau} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}(0)}^{\uparrow} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left\{S \subseteq X \times X ~:~ \text{there exists } N \in \mathcal{N}_{\tau}(0) \text{ such that } \Delta_X(N) \subseteq S\right\}$$

where $\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}(0)}^{\uparrow}$ denotes the [upward closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upward_closure) of $\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{N}_{\tau}(0)}$ in $X \times X.$ The same canonical uniformity would result by using a neighborhood basis of the origin rather the filter of all neighborhoods of the origin. If $\mathcal{L}$ is any neighborhood basis at the origin in $(X, \tau)$ then the filter on $X \times X$ generated by the prefilter $\mathcal{B}_{\mathcal{L}}$ is equal to the canonical uniformity $\mathcal{U}_{\tau}$ induced by $(X, \tau).$

### Cauchy net {#Cauchy_net}

The general theory of [uniform spaces](wikipedia://en/32339) has its own definition of a "Cauchy prefilter" and "Cauchy net". For the canonical uniformity on $X,$ these definitions reduce down to those given below.

Suppose $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I}$ is a net in $X$ and $y_{\bullet} = \left(y_j\right)_{j \in J}$ is a net in $Y.$ The product $I \times J$ becomes a [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165) by declaring $(i, j) \leq \left(i_2, j_2\right)$ if and only if $i \leq i_2$ and $j \leq j_2.$ Then

$$x_{\bullet} \times y_{\bullet} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left(x_i, y_j\right)_{(i, j) \in I \times J}$$

denotes the (**Cartesian**) **product net**, where in particular $x_{\bullet} \times x_{\bullet} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left(x_i, x_j\right)_{(i, j) \in I \times I}.$ If $X = Y$ then the image of this net under the vector addition map $X \times X \to X$ denotes the **sum** of these two nets:[^n3]

$$x_{\bullet} + y_{\bullet} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left(x_i + y_j\right)_{(i, j) \in I \times J}$$

and similarly their **difference** is defined to be the image of the product net under the vector subtraction map $(x, y) \mapsto x - y$:

$$x_{\bullet} - y_{\bullet} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left(x_i - y_j\right)_{(i, j) \in I \times J}.$$

In particular, the notation $x_{\bullet} - x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I} - \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I}$ denotes the $I^2$-indexed net $\left(x_i - x_j\right)_{(i, j) \in I \times I}$ and not the $I$-indexed net $\left(x_i - x_i\right)_{i \in I} = (0)_{i \in I}$ since using the latter as the definition would make the notation useless.

A [net](wikipedia://en/22170) $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I}$ in a TVS $X$ is called a **Cauchy net**[^n4] if

$$x_{\bullet} - x_{\bullet} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left(x_i - x_j\right)_{(i, j) \in I \times I} \to 0 \quad \text{ in } X.$$

Explicitly, this means that for every neighborhood $N$ of $0$ in $X,$ there exists some index $i_0 \in I$ such that $x_i - x_j \in N$ for all indices $i, j \in I$ that satisfy $i \geq i_0$ and $j \geq i_0.$ It suffices to check any of these defining conditions for any given [neighborhood basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_basis) of $0$ in $X.$ A **[Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085)** is a sequence that is also a Cauchy net.

If $x_{\bullet} \to x$ then $x_{\bullet} \times x_{\bullet} \to (x, x)$ in $X \times X$ and so the continuity of the vector subtraction map $S : X \times X \to X,$ which is defined by $S(x, y) ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ x - y,$ guarantees that $S\left(x_{\bullet} \times x_{\bullet}\right) \to S(x, x)$ in $X,$ where $S\left(x_{\bullet} \times x_{\bullet}\right) = \left(x_i - x_j\right)_{(i, j) \in I \times I} = x_{\bullet} - x_{\bullet}$ and $S(x, x) = x - x = 0.$ This proves that every convergent net is a Cauchy net. By definition, a space is called complete if the converse is also always true. That is, $X$ is complete if and only if the following holds:

whenever $x_{\bullet}$ is a net in $X,$ then $x_{\bullet}$ converges (to some point) in $X$ if and only if $x_{\bullet} - x_{\bullet} \to 0$ in $X.$

A similar characterization of completeness holds if filters and prefilters are used instead of nets.

A series $\sum_{i=1}^{\infty} x_i$ is called a **Cauchy series** (respectively, a **convergent series**) if the sequence of [partial sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum) $\left(\sum_{i=1}^n x_i\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ is a [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) (respectively, a [convergent sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_sequence)).[^n5] Every convergent series is necessarily a Cauchy series. In a complete TVS, every Cauchy series is necessarily a convergent series.

### Cauchy filter and Cauchy prefilter {#Cauchy_filter_and_Cauchy_prefilter}

A [prefilter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefilter) $\mathcal{B}$ on a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $X$ is called a **Cauchy prefilter**[^n6] if it satisfies any of the following equivalent conditions:

$\mathcal{B} - \mathcal{B} \to 0$ in $X.$

The family $\mathcal{B} - \mathcal{B} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{B - C : B, C \in \mathcal{B}\}$ is a prefilter.

Explicitly, $\mathcal{B} - \mathcal{B} \to 0$ means that for every neighborhood $N$ of the origin in $X,$ there exist $B, C \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $B - C \subseteq N.$ $\{B - B : B \in \mathcal{B}\} \to 0$ in $X.$

The family $\{B - B : B \in \mathcal{B}\}$ is a prefilter equivalent to $\mathcal{B} - \mathcal{B}$ (*equivalence* means these prefilters generate the same filter on $X$).

Explicitly, $\{B - B : B \in \mathcal{B}\} \to 0$ means that for every neighborhood $N$ of the origin in $X,$ there exists some $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $B - B \subseteq N.$ For every neighborhood $N$ of the origin in $X,$ $\mathcal{B}$ contains some $N$-small set (that is, there exists some $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $B - B \subseteq N$).[^n7]

A subset $B \subseteq X$ is called **$N$-small** or **small of order $N$**[^n6] if $B - B \subseteq N.$ For every neighborhood $N$ of the origin in $X,$ there exists some $x \in X$ and some $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $B \subseteq x + N.$[^n6]

This statement remains true if "$B \subseteq x + N$" is replaced with "$x + B \subseteq N.$" Every neighborhood of the origin in $X$ contains some subset of the form $x + B$ where $x \in X$ and $B \in \mathcal{B}.$

It suffices to check any of the above conditions for any given [neighborhood basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_basis) of $0$ in $X.$ A **[Cauchy filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_filter)** is a Cauchy prefilter that is also a [filter](wikipedia://en/12646549) on $X.$

If $\mathcal{B}$ is a prefilter on a topological vector space $X$ and if $x \in X,$ then $\mathcal{B} \to x$ in $X$ if and only if $x \in \operatorname{cl} \mathcal{B}$ and $\mathcal{B}$ is Cauchy.[^n3]

### Complete subset {#Complete_subset}

For any $S \subseteq X,$ a prefilter $\mathcal{C}$ **on $S$** is necessarily a subset of $\wp(S)$; that is, $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \wp(S).$

A subset $S$ of a TVS $(X, \tau)$ is called a **complete subset** if it satisfies any of the following equivalent conditions:

Every Cauchy prefilter $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \wp(S)$ on $S$ [converges to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_filter) at least one point of $S.$

If $X$ is Hausdorff then every prefilter on $S$ will converge to at most one point of $X.$ But if $X$ is not Hausdorff then a prefilter may converge to multiple points in $X.$ The same is true for nets. Every Cauchy net in $S$ [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_net) to at least one point of $S.$ $S$ is a [complete uniform space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_uniform_space) (under the point-set topology definition of "[complete uniform space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_uniform_space)") when $S$ is endowed with the uniformity induced on it by the canonical uniformity of $X.$

The subset $S$ is called a **sequentially complete subset** if every Cauchy sequence in $S$ (or equivalently, every elementary Cauchy filter/prefilter on $S$) converges to at least one point of $S.$

Importantly, convergence to points outside of $S$ does not prevent a set from being complete: If $X$ is not Hausdorff and if every Cauchy prefilter on $S$ converges to some point of $S,$ then $S$ will be complete even if some or all Cauchy prefilters on $S$ also converge to points(s) in $X \setminus S.$ In short, there is no requirement that these Cauchy prefilters on $S$ converge only to points in $S.$ The same can be said of the convergence of Cauchy nets in $S.$

As a consequence, if a TVS $X$ is not Hausdorff then every subset of the closure of $\{0\}$ in $X$ is complete because it is compact and every compact set is necessarily complete. In particular, if $\varnothing \neq S \subseteq \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ is a proper subset, such as $S = \{0\}$ for example, then $S$ would be complete even though every Cauchy net in $S$ (and also every Cauchy prefilter on $S$) converges to every point in $\operatorname{cl}_X \{0\},$ including those points in $\operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ that do not belong to $S.$ This example also shows that complete subsets (and indeed, even compact subsets) of a non-Hausdorff TVS may fail to be closed. For example, if $\varnothing \neq S \subseteq \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ then $S = \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ if and only if $S$ is closed in $X.$

### Complete topological vector space {#Complete_topological_vector_space}

A [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $X$ is called a **complete topological vector space** if any of the following equivalent conditions are satisfied:

$X$ is a [complete uniform space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_uniform_space) when it is endowed with its canonical uniformity.

In the general theory of [uniform spaces](wikipedia://en/32339), a uniform space is called a [complete uniform space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_uniform_space) if each [Cauchy filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_filter) on $X$ converges to some point of $X$ in the topology induced by the uniformity. When $X$ is a TVS, the topology induced by the canonical uniformity is equal to $X$'s given topology (so convergence in this induced topology is just the usual convergence in $X$). $X$ is a complete subset of itself. There exists a neighborhood of the origin in $X$ that is also a complete subset of $X.$[^n6]

This implies that every [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632) TVS is complete (even if the TVS is not Hausdorff). Every Cauchy prefilter $\mathcal{C} \subseteq \wp(X)$ on $X$ [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_prefilter) in $X$ to at least one point of $X.$

If $X$ is Hausdorff then every prefilter on $X$ will converge to at most one point of $X.$ But if $X$ is not Hausdorff then a prefilter may converge to multiple points in $X.$ The same is true for nets. Every Cauchy [filter](wikipedia://en/12646549) on $X$ converges in $X$ to at least one point of $X.$ Every Cauchy net in $X$ [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_net) in $X$ to at least one point of $X.$

where if in addition $X$ is [pseudometrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) or metrizable (for example, a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space)) then this list can be extended to include:

$X$ is sequentially complete.

A topological vector space $X$ is **sequentially complete** if any of the following equivalent conditions are satisfied:

$X$ is a sequentially complete subset of itself. Every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges in $X$ to at least one point of $X.$ Every elementary Cauchy prefilter on $X$ converges in $X$ to at least one point of $X.$ Every elementary Cauchy filter on $X$ converges in $X$ to at least one point of $X.$

:::

## Uniqueness of the canonical uniformity {#Uniqueness_of_the_canonical_uniformity}

The existence of the canonical uniformity was demonstrated above by defining it. The theorem below establishes that the canonical uniformity of any TVS $(X, \tau)$ is the only uniformity on $X$ that is both (1) translation invariant, and (2) generates on $X$ the topology $\tau.$

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Existence and uniqueness of the canonical uniformity)"}

**Theorem[^n8] (Existence and uniqueness of the canonical uniformity).**

The topology of any TVS can be derived from a unique translation-invariant uniformity. If $\mathcal{N}(0)$ is any [neighborhood base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_base) of the origin, then the family $\left\{\Delta(N) : N \in \mathcal{N}(0)\right\}$ is a base for this uniformity.

:::

This section is dedicated to explaining the precise meanings of the terms involved in this uniqueness statement.

### Uniform spaces and translation-invariant uniformities {#Uniform_spaces_and_translation-invariant_uniformities}

For any subsets $\Phi, \Psi \subseteq X \times X,$ let[^n1]

$$\Phi^{\operatorname{op}} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{(y, x) ~:~ (x, y) \in \Phi\}$$

and let

$$\begin{alignedat}{4} \Phi \circ \Psi ~&~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left\{(x, z) : \text{ there exists } y \in X \text{ such that } (x, y) \in \Psi \text{ and } (y, z) \in \Phi\right\} \\ &=~ \bigcup_{y \in X} \{(x, z) ~:~ (x, y) \in \Psi \text{ and } (y, z) \in \Phi\} \end{alignedat}$$

A non-empty family $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \wp(X \times X)$ is called a **base of entourages** or a **fundamental system of entourages** if $\mathcal{B}$ is a [prefilter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefilter) on $X \times X$ satisfying all of the following conditions:

Every set in $\mathcal{B}$ contains the diagonal of $X$ as a subset; that is, $\Delta_X ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{(x, x) : x \in X\} \subseteq \Phi$ for every $\Phi \in \mathcal{B}.$ Said differently, the prefilter $\mathcal{B}$ is fixed on $\Delta_X.$ For every $\Omega \in \mathcal{B}$ there exists some $\Phi \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $\Phi \circ \Phi \subseteq \Omega.$ For every $\Omega \in \mathcal{B}$ there exists some $\Phi \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $\Phi \subseteq \Omega^{\operatorname{op}} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{(y, x) : (x, y) \in \Omega\}.$

A **uniformity** or **uniform structure** on $X$ is a [filter](wikipedia://en/12646549) $\mathcal{U}$ on $X \times X$ that is generated by some base of entourages $\mathcal{B},$ in which case we say that $\mathcal{B}$ is a **base of entourages for $\mathcal{U}.$**

For a commutative additive group $X,$ a **translation-invariant fundamental system of entourages**[^n8] is a fundamental system of entourages $\mathcal{B}$ such that for every $\Phi \in \mathcal{B},$ $(x, y) \in \Phi$ if and only if $(x + z, y + z) \in \Phi$ for all $x, y, z \in X.$ A uniformity $\mathcal{B}$ is called a **translation-invariant uniformity**[^n8] if it has a base of entourages that is translation-invariant. The canonical uniformity on any TVS is translation-invariant.[^n8]

The binary operator $\;\circ\;$ satisfies all of the following:

$(\Phi \circ \Psi)^{\operatorname{op}} = \Psi^{\operatorname{op}} \circ \Phi^{\operatorname{op}}.$ If $\Phi \subseteq \Phi_2$ and $\Psi \subseteq \Psi_2$ then $\Phi \circ \Psi \subseteq \Phi_2 \circ \Psi_2.$ Associativity: $\Phi \circ (\Psi \circ \Omega) = (\Phi \circ \Psi) \circ \Omega.$ Identity: $\Phi \circ \Delta_X = \Phi = \Delta_X \circ \Phi.$ Zero: $\Phi \circ \varnothing = \varnothing = \varnothing \circ \Phi$

**Symmetric entourages**

Call a subset $\Phi \subseteq X \times X$ **symmetric** if $\Phi = \Phi^{\operatorname{op}},$ which is equivalent to $\Phi^{\operatorname{op}} \subseteq \Phi.$ This equivalence follows from the identity $\left(\Phi^{\operatorname{op}}\right)^{\operatorname{op}} = \Phi$ and the fact that if $\Psi \subseteq X \times X,$ then $\Phi \subseteq \Psi$ if and only if $\Phi^{\operatorname{op}} \subseteq \Psi^{\operatorname{op}}.$ For example, the set $\Phi^{\operatorname{op}} \cap \Phi$ is always symmetric for every $\Phi \subseteq X \times X.$ And because $(\Phi \cap \Psi)^{\operatorname{op}} = \Phi^{\operatorname{op}} \cap \Psi^{\operatorname{op}},$ if $\Phi$ and $\Psi$ are symmetric then so is $\Phi \cap \Psi.$

### Topology generated by a uniformity {#Topology_generated_by_a_uniformity}

**Relatives**

Let $\Phi \subseteq X \times X$ be arbitrary and let $\operatorname{Pr}_1, \operatorname{Pr}_2 : X \times X \to X$ be the canonical projections onto the first and second coordinates, respectively.

For any $S \subseteq X,$ define

$$S \cdot \Phi ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{y \in X : \Phi \cap (S \times \{x\}) \neq \varnothing\} ~=~ \operatorname{Pr}_2 (\Phi \cap (S \times X))$$

$$\Phi \cdot S ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{x \in X : \Phi \cap (\{x\} \times S) \neq \varnothing\} ~=~ \operatorname{Pr}_1 (\Phi \cap (X \times S)) = S \cdot \left(\Phi^{\operatorname{op}}\right)$$

where $\Phi \cdot S$ (respectively, $S \cdot \Phi$) is called the set of **left** (respectively, **right**) **$\Phi$-relatives** of (points in) $S.$ Denote the special case where $S = \{p\}$ is a singleton set for some $p \in X$ by:

$$p \cdot \Phi ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{p\} \cdot \Phi ~=~ \{y \in X : (p, y) \in \Phi\}$$

$$\Phi \cdot p ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \Phi \cdot \{p\} ~=~ \{x \in X : (x, p) \in \Phi\} ~=~ p \cdot \left(\Phi^{\operatorname{op}}\right)$$

If $\Phi, \Psi \subseteq X \times X$ then $(\Phi \circ \Psi) \cdot S = \Phi \cdot (\Psi \cdot S).$ Moreover, $\,\cdot\,$ [right distributes over](wikipedia://en/103118) both unions and intersections, meaning that if $R, S \subseteq X$ then $(R \cup S) \cdot \Phi ~=~ (R \cdot \Phi) \cup (S \cdot \Phi)$ and $(R \cap S) \cdot \Phi ~\subseteq~ (R \cdot \Phi) \cap (S \cdot \Phi).$

**Neighborhoods and open sets**

Two points $x$ and $y$ are **$\Phi$-close** if $(x, y) \in \Phi$ and a subset $S \subseteq X$ is called **$\Phi$-small** if $S \times S \subseteq \Phi.$

Let $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \wp(X \times X)$ be a base of entourages on $X.$ The **neighborhood prefilter** at a point $p \in X$ and, respectively, on a subset $S \subseteq X$ are the [families of sets](wikipedia://en/723043):

$$\mathcal{B} \cdot p ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \mathcal{B} \cdot \{p\} = \{\Phi \cdot p : \Phi \in \mathcal{B}\} \qquad \text{ and } \qquad \mathcal{B} \cdot S ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{\Phi \cdot S : \Phi \in \mathcal{B}\}$$

and the filters on $X$ that each generates is known as the **neighborhood filter** of $p$ (respectively, of $S$). Assign to every $x \in X$ the neighborhood prefilter

$$\mathcal{B} \cdot x ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{\Phi \cdot x : \Phi \in \mathcal{B}\}$$

and use the [neighborhood definition of "open set"](wikipedia://en/30450#Neighborhood_definition) to obtain a [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_%28structure%29) on $X$ called the **topology induced by $\mathcal{B}$** or the **induced topology**. Explicitly, a subset $U \subseteq X$ is open in this topology if and only if for every $u \in U$ there exists some $N \in \mathcal{B} \cdot u$ such that $N \subseteq U;$ that is, $U$ is open if and only if for every $u \in U$ there exists some $\Phi \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $\Phi \cdot u ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \{x \in X : (x, u) \in \Phi\} \subseteq U.$

The closure of a subset $S \subseteq X$ in this topology is:

$$\operatorname{cl}_X S = \bigcap_{\Phi \in \mathcal{B}} (\Phi \cdot S) = \bigcap_{\Phi \in \mathcal{B}} (S \cdot \Phi).$$

**Cauchy prefilters and complete uniformities**

A prefilter $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \wp(X)$ on a uniform space $X$ with uniformity $\mathcal{U}$ is called a **Cauchy prefilter** if for every entourage $N \in \mathcal{U},$ there exists some $F \in \mathcal{F}$ such that $F \times F \subseteq N.$

A uniform space $(X, \mathcal{U})$ is called a **complete uniform space** (respectively, a **sequentially complete uniform space**) if every Cauchy prefilter (respectively, every elementary Cauchy prefilter) on $X$ converges to at least one point of $X$ when $X$ is endowed with the topology induced by $\mathcal{U}.$

**Case of a topological vector space**

If $(X, \tau)$ is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) then for any $S \subseteq X$ and $x \in X,$

$$\Delta_X(N) \cdot S = S + N \qquad \text{ and } \qquad \Delta_X(N) \cdot x = x + N,$$

and the topology induced on $X$ by the canonical uniformity is the same as the topology that $X$ started with (that is, it is $\tau$).

## Uniform continuity {#Uniform_continuity}

Let $X$ and $Y$ be TVSs, $D \subseteq X,$ and $f : D \to Y$ be a map. Then $f : D \to Y$ is **[uniformly continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_continuous)** if for every neighborhood $U$ of the origin in $X,$ there exists a neighborhood $V$ of the origin in $Y$ such that for all $x, y \in D,$ if $y - x \in U$ then $f(y) - f(x) \in V.$

Suppose that $f : D \to Y$ is uniformly continuous. If $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I}$ is a Cauchy net in $D$ then $f \circ x_{\bullet} = \left(f\left(x_i\right)\right)_{i \in I}$ is a Cauchy net in $Y.$ If $\mathcal{B}$ is a Cauchy prefilter in $D$ (meaning that $\mathcal{B}$ is a family of subsets of $D$ that is Cauchy in $X$) then $f\left(\mathcal{B}\right)$ is a Cauchy prefilter in $Y.$ However, if $\mathcal{B}$ is a Cauchy filter on $D$ then although $f\left(\mathcal{B}\right)$ will be a Cauchy prefilter, it will be a Cauchy filter in $Y$ if and only if $f : D \to Y$ is surjective.

## TVS completeness vs completeness of (pseudo)metrics {#TVS_completeness_vs_completeness_of_(pseudo)metrics}

### Preliminaries: Complete pseudometric spaces {#Preliminaries:_Complete_pseudometric_spaces}

We review the basic notions related to the general theory of complete pseudometric spaces. Recall that every [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) is a [pseudometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudometric_%28topology%29) and that a pseudometric $p$ is a metric if and only if $p(x, y) = 0$ implies $x = y.$ Thus every [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is a [pseudometric space](wikipedia://en/68503) and a pseudometric space $(X, p)$ is a metric space if and only if $p$ is a metric.

If $S$ is a subset of a [pseudometric space](wikipedia://en/68503) $(X, d)$ then the **[diameter](wikipedia://en/8007)** of $S$ is defined to be

$$\operatorname{diam}(S) ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \sup_{} \{d(s, t) : s, t \in S\}.$$

A prefilter $\mathcal{B}$ on a pseudometric space $(X, d)$ is called a **$d$-Cauchy prefilter** or simply a **Cauchy prefilter** if for each [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) $r > 0,$ there is some $B \in \mathcal{B}$ such that the diameter of $B$ is less than $r.$

Suppose $(X, d)$ is a pseudometric space. A [net](wikipedia://en/22170) $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I}$ in $X$ is called a **$d$-Cauchy net** or simply a **Cauchy net** if $\operatorname{Tails}\left(x_{\bullet}\right)$ is a Cauchy prefilter, which happens if and only if

for every $r > 0$ there is some $i \in I$ such that if $j, k \in I$ with $j \geq i$ and $k \geq i$ then $d\left(x_j, x_k\right) < r$

or equivalently, if and only if $\left(d\left(x_j, x_k\right)\right)_{(i,j) \in I \times I} \to 0$ in $\mathbb{R}.$ This is analogous to the following characterization of the converge of $x_{\bullet}$ to a point: if $x \in X,$ then $x_{\bullet} \to x$ in $(X, d)$ if and only if $\left(x_i, x\right)_{i \in I} \to 0$ in $\mathbb{R}.$

A **[Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085)** is a sequence that is also a Cauchy net.[^o35]

Every pseudometric $p$ on a set $X$ induces the usual canonical topology on $X,$ which we'll denote by $\tau_p$; it also induces a canonical [uniformity](wikipedia://en/32339) on $X,$ which we'll denote by $\mathcal{U}_p.$ The topology on $X$ induced by the uniformity $\mathcal{U}_p$ is equal to $\tau_p.$ A net $x_{\bullet} = \left(x_i\right)_{i \in I}$ in $X$ is Cauchy with respect to $p$ if and only if it is Cauchy with respect to the uniformity $\mathcal{U}_p.$ The pseudometric space $(X, p)$ is a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_pseudometric_space) (resp. a sequentially complete) pseudometric space if and only if $\left(X, \mathcal{U}_p\right)$ is a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_uniform_space) (resp. a sequentially complete) uniform space. Moreover, the pseudometric space $(X, p)$ (resp. the uniform space $\left(X, \mathcal{U}_p\right)$) is complete if and only if it is sequentially complete.

A pseudometric space $(X, d)$ (for example, a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018)) is called **complete** and $d$ is called a **complete pseudometric** if any of the following equivalent conditions hold:

Every Cauchy prefilter on $X$ converges to at least one point of $X.$ The above statement but with the word "prefilter" replaced by "filter." Every Cauchy net in $X$ converges to at least one point of $X.$

If $d$ is a metric on $X$ then any limit point is necessarily unique and the same is true for limits of Cauchy prefilters on $X.$ Every Cauchy sequence in $X$ converges to at least one point of $X.$

Thus to prove that $(X, d)$ is complete, it suffices to only consider Cauchy sequences in $X$ (and it is not necessary to consider the more general Cauchy nets). The canonical uniformity on $X$ induced by the pseudometric $d$ is a complete uniformity.

And if addition $d$ is a metric then we may add to this list:

Every decreasing sequence of closed balls whose diameters shrink to $0$ has non-empty intersection.[^n9]

### Complete pseudometrics and complete TVSs {#Complete_pseudometrics_and_complete_TVSs}

Every [F-space](wikipedia://en/200440), and thus also every [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801), [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), and [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) is a complete TVS. Note that every *F*-space is a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550) but there are normed spaces that are Baire but not Banach.[^n10]

A pseudometric $d$ on a vector space $X$ is said to be a **translation invariant pseudometric** if $d(x, y) = d(x + z, y + z)$ for all vectors $x, y, z \in X.$

Suppose $(X, \tau)$ is [pseudometrizable TVS](wikipedia://en/64278078) (for example, a metrizable TVS) and that $p$ is any pseudometric on $X$ such that the topology on $X$ induced by $p$ is equal to $\tau.$ If $p$ is translation-invariant, then $(X, \tau)$ is a complete TVS if and only if $(X, p)$ is a complete pseudometric space.[^n11] If $p$ is not translation-invariant, then may be possible for $(X, \tau)$ to be a complete TVS but $(X, p)$ to not be a complete pseudometric space[^n11] (see this footnote[^o41] for an example).[^n11]

::: {#o45 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Klee)"}

**Theorem[^n12][^o44] (Klee).**

Let $d$ be any[^o46] metric on a vector space $X$ such that the topology $\tau$ induced by $d$ on $X$ makes $(X, \tau)$ into a topological vector space. If $(X, d)$ is a complete metric space then $(X, \tau)$ is a complete-TVS.

:::

### Complete norms and equivalent norms {#Complete_norms_and_equivalent_norms}

Two norms on a vector space are called **[equivalent](wikipedia://en/990534#Definition)** if and only if they induce the same topology.[^o48] If $p$ and $q$ are two equivalent norms on a vector space $X$ then the [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) $(X, p)$ is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) if and only if $(X, q)$ is a Banach space. See this footnote for an example of a continuous norm on a Banach space that is not equivalent to that Banach space's given norm.[^o49][^o48] All norms on a finite-dimensional vector space are equivalent and every finite-dimensional normed space is a Banach space.[^o50] Every Banach space is a complete TVS. A normed space is a Banach space (that is, its canonical norm-induced metric is complete) if and only if it is complete as a topological vector space.

## Completions {#Completions}

A **completion**[^n13] of a TVS $X$ is a complete TVS that contains a dense vector subspace that is TVS-isomorphic to $X.$ In other words, it is a complete TVS $C$ into which $X$ can be [TVS-embedded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVS-embedding) as a [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [vector subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subspace). Every TVS-embedding is a [uniform embedding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_embedding).

Every topological vector space has a completion. Moreover, every Hausdorff TVS has a Hausdorff completion, which is necessarily unique [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [TVS-isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TVS-isomorphism). However, all TVSs, even those that are Hausdorff, (already) complete, and/or metrizable have infinitely many non-Hausdorff completions that are not TVS-isomorphic to one another.

### Examples of completions {#Examples_of_completions}

For example, the vector space consisting of scalar-valued [simple functions](wikipedia://en/1890594) $f$ for which $|f|_p < \infty$ (where this seminorm is defined in the usual way in terms of [Lebesgue integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration)) becomes a [seminormed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminormed_space) when endowed with this seminorm, which in turn makes it into both a [pseudometric space](wikipedia://en/68503) and a non-Hausdorff non-complete TVS; any completion of this space is a non-Hausdorff complete seminormed space that when [quotiented](wikipedia://en/898784) by the closure of its origin (so as to [obtain a Hausdorff TVS](wikipedia://en/237213)) results in (a space [linearly](wikipedia://en/18102) [isometrically-isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometric_isomorphism) to) the usual complete Hausdorff [$L^p$-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lp-space) (endowed with the usual complete $\| \cdot \|_p$ [norm](wikipedia://en/990534)).

As another example demonstrating the usefulness of completions, the completions of [topological tensor products](wikipedia://en/2702982), such as [projective tensor products](wikipedia://en/63566617) or [injective tensor products](wikipedia://en/63566619), of the Banach space $\ell^1(S)$ with a complete Hausdorff locally convex TVS $Y$ results in a complete TVS that is TVS-isomorphic to a "generalized" $\ell^1(S; Y)$-space consisting $Y$-valued functions on $S$ (where this "generalized" TVS is defined analogously to original space $\ell^1(S)$ of scalar-valued functions on $S$). Similarly, the completion of the injective tensor product of the [space of scalar-valued $C^k$-test functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_of_smooth_test_functions) with such a TVS $Y$ is TVS-isomorphic to the analogously defined TVS of [$Y$-valued $C^k$](wikipedia://en/63944266) test functions.

### Non-uniqueness of all completions {#Non-uniqueness_of_all_completions}

As the example below shows, regardless of whether or not a space is Hausdorff or already complete, every [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) has infinitely many non-isomorphic completions.[^n14]

However, every Hausdorff TVS has a Hausdorff completion that is unique up to TVS-isomorphism.[^n14] But nevertheless, every Hausdorff TVS still has infinitely many non-isomorphic non-Hausdorff completions.

::: {#o57 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example** (**Non-uniqueness of completions**):[^n13] Let $C$ denote any complete TVS and let $I$ denote any TVS endowed with the [indiscrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_topology), which recall makes $I$ into a complete TVS. Since both $I$ and $C$ are complete TVSs, so is their product $I \times C.$ If $U$ and $V$ are non-empty open subsets of $I$ and $C,$ respectively, then $U = I$ and $(U \times V) \cap (\{0\} \times C) = \{0\} \times V \neq \varnothing,$ which shows that $\{0\} \times C$ is a dense subspace of $I \times C.$ Thus by definition of "completion," $I \times C$ is a completion of $\{0\} \times C$ (it doesn't matter that $\{0\} \times C$ is already complete). So by identifying $\{0\} \times C$ with $C,$ if $X \subseteq C$ is a dense vector subspace of $C,$ then $X$ has both $C$ and $I \times C$ as completions.

:::

### Hausdorff completions {#Hausdorff_completions}

Every Hausdorff TVS has a Hausdorff completion that is unique up to TVS-isomorphism.[^n14] But nevertheless, as shown above, every Hausdorff TVS still has infinitely many non-isomorphic non-Hausdorff completions.

::: {#o62 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Properties of Hausdorff completions"}

**Properties of Hausdorff completions[^n15].**

Suppose that $X$ and $C$ are Hausdorff TVSs with $C$ complete. Suppose that $E : X \to C$ is a TVS-embedding onto a dense vector subspace of $C.$ Then

**Universal property**: for every continuous linear map $f : X \to Z$ into a complete Hausdorff TVS $Z,$ there exists a unique continuous linear map $F : C \to Z$ such that $f = F \circ E.$

If $E_2 : X \to C_2$ is a TVS embedding onto a dense vector subspace of a complete Hausdorff TVS $C_2$ having the above universal property, then there exists a unique (bijective) TVS-isomorphism $I : C \to C_2$ such that $E_2 = I \circ E.$

:::

::: {#o64 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=math-theorem name="Corollary"}

**Corollary[^n15].**

Suppose $C$ is a complete Hausdorff TVS and $X$ is a dense vector subspace of $C.$ Then every continuous linear map $f : X \to Z$ into a complete Hausdorff TVS $Z$ has a unique continuous linear extension to a map $C \to Z.$

:::

**Existence of Hausdorff completions**

A Cauchy filter $\mathcal{B}$ on a TVS $X$ is called a **minimal Cauchy filter**[^n15] if there does not exist a Cauchy filter on $X$ that is strictly coarser than $\mathcal{B}$ (that is, "strictly coarser than $\mathcal{B}$" means contained as a proper subset of $\mathcal{B}$).

If $\mathcal{B}$ is a Cauchy filter on $X$ then the filter generated by the following prefilter:

$$\left\{B + N ~:~ B \in \mathcal{B} \text{ and } N \text{ is a neighborhood of } 0 \text{ in } X\right\}$$

is the unique minimal Cauchy filter on $X$ that is contained as a subset of $\mathcal{B}.$[^n15] In particular, for any $x \in X,$ the neighborhood filter at $x$ is a minimal Cauchy filter.

Let $\mathbb{M}$ be the set of all minimal Cauchy filters on $X$ and let $E : X \rightarrow \mathbb{M}$ be the map defined by sending $x \in X$ to the neighborhood filter of $x$ in $X.$ Endow $\mathbb{M}$ with the following vector space structure: Given $\mathcal{B}, \mathcal{C} \in \mathbb{M}$ and a scalar $s,$ let $\mathcal{B} + \mathcal{C}$ (resp. $s \mathcal{B}$) denote the unique minimal Cauchy filter contained in the filter generated by $\left\{B + C : B \in \mathcal{B}, C \in \mathcal{C}\right\}$ (resp. $\{s B : B \in \mathcal{B}\}$).

For every [balanced](wikipedia://en/1734238) neighborhood $N$ of the origin in $X,$ let

$$\mathbb{U}(N) ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left\{\mathcal{B} \in \mathbb{M} ~:~ \text{ there exist } B \in \mathcal{B} \text{ and a neighborhood } V \text{ of the origin in } X \text{ such that } B + V \subseteq N\right\}$$

If $X$ is Hausdorff then the collection of all sets $\mathbb{U}(N),$ as $N$ ranges over all balanced neighborhoods of the origin in $X,$ forms a vector topology on $\mathbb{M}$ making $\mathbb{M}$ into a complete Hausdorff TVS. Moreover, the map $E : X \rightarrow \mathbb{M}$ is a TVS-embedding onto a dense vector subspace of $\mathbb{M}.$[^n15]

If $X$ is a [metrizable TVS](wikipedia://en/64278078) then a Hausdorff completion of $X$ can be constructed using equivalence classes of Cauchy sequences instead of minimal Cauchy filters.

### Non-Hausdorff completions {#Non-Hausdorff_completions}

This subsection details how every non-Hausdorff TVS $X$ can be TVS-embedded onto a dense vector subspace of a complete TVS. The proof that every Hausdorff TVS has a Hausdorff completion is widely available and so this fact will be used (without proof) to show that every non-Hausdorff TVS also has a completion. These details are sometimes useful for extending results from Hausdorff TVSs to non-Hausdorff TVSs.

Let $I = \operatorname{cl} \{0\}$ denote the closure of the origin in $X,$ where $I$ is endowed with its subspace topology induced by $X$ (so that $I$ has the [indiscrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_topology)). Since $I$ has the trivial topology, it is easily shown that every vector subspace of $X$ that is an algebraic complement of $I$ in $X$ is necessarily a [topological complement](wikipedia://en/63033543) of $I$ in $X.$[^n16][^n17] Let $H$ denote any topological complement of $I$ in $X,$ which is necessarily a Hausdorff TVS (since it is TVS-isomorphic to the quotient TVS $X / I$[^o71]). Since $X$ is the [topological direct sum](wikipedia://en/63033543) of $I$ and $H$ (which means that $X = I \oplus H$ in the category of TVSs), the canonical map

$$I \times H \to I \oplus H = X \quad \text{ given by } \quad (x, y) \mapsto x + y$$

is a TVS-isomorphism.[^n17] Let $A ~:~ X = I \oplus H ~\to~ I \times H$ denote the inverse of this canonical map. (As a side note, it follows that every open and every closed subset $U$ of $X$ satisfies $U = I + U.$[^o73])

The Hausdorff TVS $H$ can be TVS-embedded, say via the map $\operatorname{In}_H : H \to C,$ onto a dense vector subspace of its completion $C.$ Since $I$ and $C$ are complete, so is their product $I \times C.$ Let $\operatorname{Id}_I : I \to I$ denote the identity map and observe that the product map $\operatorname{Id}_I \times \operatorname{In}_H : I \times H \to I \times C$ is a TVS-embedding whose image is dense in $I \times C.$ Define the map[^o74]

$$B : X = I \oplus H \to I \times C \quad \text{ by } \quad B ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left(\operatorname{Id}_I \times \operatorname{In}_H\right) \circ A$$

which is a TVS-embedding of $X = I \oplus H$ onto a dense vector subspace of the complete TVS $I \times C.$ Moreover, observe that the closure of the origin in $I \times C$ is equal to $I \times \{0\},$ and that $I \times \{0\}$ and $\{0\} \times C$ are topological complements in $I \times C.$

To summarize,[^n17] given any algebraic (and thus topological) complement $H$ of $I ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \operatorname{cl} \{0\}$ in $X$ and given any completion $C$ of the Hausdorff TVS $H$ such that $H \subseteq C,$ then the natural inclusion[^o76]

$$\operatorname{In}_H : X = I \oplus H \to I \oplus C$$

is a well-defined TVS-embedding of $X$ onto a dense vector subspace of the complete TVS $I \oplus C$ where moreover,

$$X = I \oplus H \subseteq I \oplus C \cong I \times C.$$

### Topology of a completion {#Topology_of_a_completion}

::: {#o80 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Topology of a completion)"}

**Theorem[^n8][^n18] (Topology of a completion).**

Let $C$ be a complete TVS and let $X$ be a dense vector subspace of $X.$ If $\mathcal{N}_X(0)$ is any [neighborhood base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_base) of the origin in $X$ then the set

$$\mathcal{N}_X(0) ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left\{\operatorname{cl}_C N ~:~ N \in \mathcal{N}_X (0)\right\}$$

is a neighborhood of the origin in the completion $C$ of $X.$

If $X$ is locally convex and $\mathcal{P}$ is a family of continuous seminorms on $X$ that generate the topology of $X,$ then the family of all continuous extensions to $C$ of all members of $\mathcal{P}$ is a generating family of seminorms for $C.$

:::

Said differently, if $C$ is a completion of a TVS $X$ with $X \subseteq C$ and if $\mathcal{N}$ is a [neighborhood base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_base) of the origin in $X,$ then the family of sets

$$\left\{\operatorname{cl}_C N ~:~ N \in \mathcal{N}\right\}$$

is a neighborhood basis at the origin in $C.$[^n3]

::: {#o83 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Completions of quotients)"}

**Theorem[^n19] (Completions of quotients).**

Let $M$ be a [metrizable topological vector space](wikipedia://en/64278078) and let $N$ be a closed vector subspace of $M.$ Suppose that $C$ is a completion of $M.$ Then the completion of $M / N$ is TVS-isomorphic to $C / \operatorname{cl}_C N.$ If in addition $M$ is a normed space, then this TVS-isomorphism is also an isometry.

:::

**Grothendieck's Completeness Theorem**

Let $\mathcal{E}$ denote the equicontinuous compactology on the continuous dual space $X^\prime,$ which by definition consists of all [equicontinuous](wikipedia://en/460642) [weak-\* closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology) and weak-\* [bounded](wikipedia://en/1789812) [absolutely convex subsets](wikipedia://en/1734292) of $X^\prime$[^n20] (which are necessarily weak-\* compact subsets of $X^\prime$). Assume that every $E^\prime \in \mathcal{E}$ is endowed with the [weak-\* topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak-*_topology). A [filter](wikipedia://en/12646549) $\mathcal{B}$ on $X^\prime$ is said to converge continuously to $x^\prime \in X^\prime$ if there exists some $E^\prime \in \mathcal{E} \cap \mathcal{B}$ containing $x^\prime$ (that is, $x^\prime \in E^\prime$) such that the trace of $\mathcal{B}$ on $E^\prime,$ which is the family $\mathcal{B}\big\vert_{E^\prime} ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ \left\{B \cap E^\prime : B \in \mathcal{B}\right\},$ [converges to](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_filter) $x^\prime$ in $E^\prime$ (that is, if $\mathcal{B}\big\vert_{E^\prime} \to x^\prime$ in the given weak-\* topology).[^n21] The filter $\mathcal{B}$ converges continuously to $x^\prime$ if and only if $\mathcal{B} - x^\prime$ converges continuously to the origin, which happens if and only if for every $x \in X,$ the filter $\langle \mathcal{B}, x + \mathcal{N} \rangle \to \langle x^\prime, x\rangle$ in the scalar field (which is $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$) where $\mathcal{N}$ denotes any neighborhood basis at the origin in $X,$ $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ denotes the [duality pairing](wikipedia://en/63735167), and $\langle \mathcal{B}, x + \mathcal{N} \rangle$ denotes the filter generated by $\{\langle B, x + N \rangle ~:~ B \in \mathcal{B}, N \in \mathcal{N}\}.$[^n21] A map $f : X^\prime \to T$ into a topological space (such as $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$) is said to be γ-continuous if whenever a filter $\mathcal{B}$ on $X^\prime$ converges continuously to $x^\prime \in X^\prime,$ then $f(\mathcal{B}) \to f\left(x^\prime\right).$[^n21]

::: {#o89 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Grothendieck's Completeness Theorem[^n21].**

If $X$ is a Hausdorff topological vector space then its completion is linearly isomorphic to the set of all $\gamma$-continuous linear functions on $X^\prime.$

:::

### Properties preserved by completions {#Properties_preserved_by_completions}

If a TVS $X$ has any of the following properties then so does its completion:

[Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [Locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [Pseudometrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078)[^n14] [Metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078)[^n14] [Seminormable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminormed_space) [Normable](wikipedia://en/64278078)

Moreover, if $(X, p)$ is a normed space, then the completion can be chosen to be a Banach space $(B, q)$ such that the TVS-embedding of $(X, p)$ into $(B, q)$ is an isometry. [Hausdorff pre-Hilbert](wikipedia://en/14856). That is, a TVS induced by an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product).[^n22] [Nuclear](wikipedia://en/2702957)[^n23] [Barrelled](wikipedia://en/1733592)[^n24] [Mackey](wikipedia://en/1804899)[^n25] [DF-space](wikipedia://en/63703274)[^n26]

**Completions of Hilbert spaces**

Every inner product space $\left(H, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle\right)$ has a completion $\left(\overline{H}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\overline{H}}\right)$ that is a Hilbert space, where the inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\overline{H}}$ is the unique continuous extension to $\overline{H}$ of the original inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle.$ The norm induced by $\left(\overline{H}, \langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle_{\overline{H}}\right)$ is also the unique continuous extension to $\overline{H}$ of the norm induced by $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle.$[^n22][^n18]

**Other preserved properties**

If $X$ is a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) TVS, then the continuous dual space of $X$ is identical to the continuous dual space of the completion of $X.$[^n27] The completion of a locally convex [bornological space](wikipedia://en/1804457) is a [barrelled space](wikipedia://en/1733592).[^n24] If $X$ and $Y$ are [DF-spaces](wikipedia://en/63703274) then the [projective tensor product](wikipedia://en/63566617), as well as its completion, of these spaces is a DF-space.[^n28]

The completion of the [projective tensor product](wikipedia://en/63566617) of two nuclear spaces is nuclear.[^n23] The completion of a nuclear space is TVS-isomorphic with a projective limit of [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932).[^n23]

If $X = Y \oplus Z$ (meaning that the addition map $Y \times Z \to X$ is a TVS-isomorphism) has a Hausdorff completion $C$ then $\left(\operatorname{cl}_C Y\right) + \left(\operatorname{cl}_C Z\right) = C.$ If in addition $X$ is an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) and $Y$ and $Z$ are [orthogonal complements](wikipedia://en/1058833) of each other in $X$ (that is, $\langle Y, Z \rangle = \{0\}$), then $\operatorname{cl}_C Y$ and $\operatorname{cl}_C Z$ are orthogonal complements in the [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $C.$

### Properties of maps preserved by extensions to a completion {#Properties_of_maps_preserved_by_extensions_to_a_completion}

If $f : X \to Y$ is a [nuclear linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_linear_operator) between two locally convex spaces and if $C$ be a completion of $X$ then $f$ has a unique continuous linear extension to a nuclear linear operator $F : C \to Y.$[^n23]

Let $X$ and $Y$ be two Hausdorff TVSs with $Y$ complete. Let $C$ be a completion of $X.$ Let $L(X; Y)$ denote the vector space of continuous linear operators and let $I : L(X; Y) \to L(C; Y)$ denote the map that sends every $f \in L(X; Y)$ to its unique continuous linear extension on $C.$ Then $I : L(X; Y) \to L(C; Y)$ is a (surjective) vector space isomorphism. Moreover, $I : L(X; Y) \to L(C; Y)$ maps families of [equicontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equicontinuous) subsets onto each other. Suppose that $L(X; Y)$ is endowed with a [$\mathcal{G}$-topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologies_of_uniform_convergence) and that $\mathcal{H}$ denotes the closures in $C$ of sets in $\mathcal{G}.$ Then the map $I : L_{\mathcal{G}}(X; Y) \to L_{\mathcal{H}}(C; Y)$ is also a TVS-isomorphism.[^n23]

## Examples and sufficient conditions for a complete TVS {#Examples_and_sufficient_conditions_for_a_complete_TVS}

::: {#o109 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

[^n12] Let $d$ be any (not assumed to be translation-invariant) metric on a vector space $X$ such that the topology $\tau$ induced by $d$ on $X$ makes $(X, \tau)$ into a topological vector space. If $(X, d)$ is a complete metric space then $(X, \tau)$ is a complete-TVS.

:::

Any TVS endowed with the [trivial topology](wikipedia://en/382447) is complete and every one of its subsets is complete. Moreover, every TVS with the trivial topology is compact and hence locally compact. Thus a complete [seminormable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminormable_space) locally convex and locally compact TVS need not be finite-dimensional if it is not Hausdorff. An arbitrary product of complete (resp. sequentially complete, quasi-complete) TVSs has that same property. If all spaces are Hausdorff, then the converses are also true.[^n29] A product of Hausdorff completions of a family of (Hausdorff) TVSs is a Hausdorff completion of their product TVS.[^n29] More generally, an arbitrary product of complete subsets of a family of TVSs is a complete subset of the product TVS.[^n30] The projective limit of a projective system of Hausdorff complete (resp. sequentially complete, quasi-complete) TVSs has that same property.[^n29] A projective limit of Hausdorff completions of an inverse system of (Hausdorff) TVSs is a Hausdorff completion of their projective limit.[^n29] If $M$ is a closed vector subspace of a complete [pseudometrizable TVS](wikipedia://en/64278078) $X,$ then the quotient space $X / M$ is complete.[^n3] Suppose $M$ is a complete vector subspace of a [metrizable TVS](wikipedia://en/64278078) $X.$ If the quotient space $X / M$ is complete then so is $X.$[^n3][^n31] However, there exists a complete TVS $X$ having a closed vector subspace $M$ such that the quotient TVS $X / M$ is not complete.[^n15] Every [F-space](wikipedia://en/200440), [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801), [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), and [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) is a complete TVS. Strict [LF-spaces](wikipedia://en/2314852) and strict [LB-spaces](wikipedia://en/63848931) are complete.[^n32] Suppose that $D$ is a dense subset of a TVS $X.$ If every Cauchy filter on $D$ converges to some point in $X$ then $X$ is complete.[^n31] The [Schwartz space](wikipedia://en/4277567) of smooth functions is complete. The spaces of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) and test functions is complete. Suppose that $X$ and $Y$ are [locally convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_convex) TVSs and that the space of continuous linear maps $L_b(X; Y)$ is endowed with the [topology of uniform convergence on bounded subsets](wikipedia://en/41677409) of $X.$ If $X$ is a [bornological space](wikipedia://en/1804457) and if $Y$ is complete then $L_b(X; Y)$ is a complete TVS.[^n32] In particular, the strong dual of a [bornological space](wikipedia://en/1804457) is complete.[^n32] However, it need not be bornological. Every [quasi-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-complete) [DF-space](wikipedia://en/63703274) is complete.[^n26] Let $\omega$ and $\tau$ be Hausdorff TVS topologies on a vector space $X$ such that $\omega \subseteq \tau.$ If there exists a prefilter $\mathcal{B}$ such that $\mathcal{B}$ is a [neighborhood basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_basis) at the origin for $(X, \tau)$ and such that every $B \in \mathcal{B}$ is a complete subset of $(X, \omega),$ then $(X, \tau)$ is a complete TVS.[^n6]

## Properties {#Properties}

### Complete TVSs {#Complete_TVSs}

Every TVS has a [completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_TVS) and every Hausdorff TVS has a Hausdorff completion.[^n33] Every complete TVS is [quasi-complete space](wikipedia://en/63948433) and [sequentially complete](wikipedia://en/64126151).[^n34] However, the converses of the above implications are generally false.[^n34] There exists a [sequentially complete](wikipedia://en/64126151) locally convex TVS that is not [quasi-complete](wikipedia://en/63948433).[^n26]

If a TVS has a complete neighborhood of the origin then it is complete.[^n35] Every complete [pseudometrizable TVS](wikipedia://en/64278078) is a [barrelled space](wikipedia://en/1733592) and a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550) (and thus non-meager).[^n36] The dimension of a complete metrizable TVS is either finite or uncountable.[^n17]

### Cauchy nets and prefilters {#Cauchy_nets_and_prefilters}

Any [neighborhood basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_basis) of any point in a TVS is a Cauchy prefilter.

Every convergent net (respectively, prefilter) in a TVS is necessarily a Cauchy net (respectively, a Cauchy prefilter).[^n6] Any prefilter that is subordinate to (that is, finer than) a Cauchy prefilter is necessarily also a Cauchy prefilter[^n6] and any prefilter finer than a Cauchy prefilter is also a Cauchy prefilter. The filter associated with a sequence in a TVS is Cauchy if and only if the sequence is a Cauchy sequence. Every convergent prefilter is a Cauchy prefilter.

If $X$ is a TVS and if $x \in X$ is a cluster point of a Cauchy net (respectively, Cauchy prefilter), then that Cauchy net (respectively, that Cauchy prefilter) converges to $x$ in $X.$[^n3] If a Cauchy filter in a TVS has an [accumulation point](wikipedia://en/47516955) $x$ then it converges to $x.$

Uniformly continuous maps send Cauchy nets to Cauchy nets.[^n3] A Cauchy sequence in a Hausdorff TVS $X,$ when considered as a set, is not necessarily [relatively compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_compact) (that is, its closure in $X$ is not necessarily compact[^o141]) although it is precompact (that is, its closure in the completion of $X$ is compact).

Every Cauchy sequence is a [bounded subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_set_%28functional_analysis%29) but this is not necessarily true of Cauchy net. For example, let $\mathbb{N}$ have it usual order, let $\,\leq\,$ denote any [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582) on the non-[indiscrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_topology) TVS $X$ (that is, $X$ does not have the [trivial topology](wikipedia://en/382447); it is also assumed that $X \cap \mathbb{N} = \varnothing$) and extend these two preorders to the union $I ~\stackrel{\scriptscriptstyle\text{def}}{=}~ X \cup \mathbb{N}$ by declaring that $x \leq n$ holds for every $x \in X$ and $n \in \mathbb{N}.$ Let $f : I \to X$ be defined by $f(i) = i$ if $i \in X$ and $f(i) = 0$ otherwise (that is, if $i \in \mathbb{N}$), which is a net in $X$ since the preordered set $(I, \leq)$ is [directed](wikipedia://en/9165) (this preorder on $I$ is also [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) (respectively, a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330)) if this is true of $(X, \leq)$). This net $f$ is a Cauchy net in $X$ because it converges to the origin, but the set $\{f(i) : i \in I\} = X$ is not a bounded subset of $X$ (because $X$ does not have the trivial topology).

Suppose that $X_{\bullet} = \left(X_i\right)_{i \in I}$ is a family of TVSs and that $X$ denotes the product of these TVSs. Suppose that for every index $i,$ $\mathcal{B}_i$ is a prefilter on $X_i.$ Then the product of this family of prefilters is a Cauchy filter on $X$ if and only if each $\mathcal{B}_i$ is a Cauchy filter on $X_i.$[^n15]

### Maps {#Maps}

If $f : X \to Y$ is an injective [topological homomorphism](wikipedia://en/63987005) from a complete TVS into a Hausdorff TVS then the image of $f$ (that is, $f(X)$) is a closed subspace of $Y.$[^n31] If $f : X \to Y$ is a [topological homomorphism](wikipedia://en/63987005) from a complete [metrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078) TVS into a Hausdorff TVS then the range of $f$ is a closed subspace of $Y.$[^n31] If $f : X \to Y$ is a [uniformly continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_continuous) map between two Hausdorff TVSs then the image under $f$ of a totally bounded subset of $X$ is a totally bounded subset of $Y.$[^n37]

**Uniformly continuous extensions**

Suppose that $f : D \to Y$ is a uniformly continuous map from a dense subset $D$ of a TVS $X$ into a complete Hausdorff TVS $Y.$ Then $f$ has a unique uniformly continuous extension to all of $X.$[^n3] If in addition $f$ is a homomorphism then its unique uniformly continuous extension is also a homomorphism.[^n3] This remains true if "TVS" is replaced by "commutative topological group."[^n3] The map $f$ is not required to be a linear map and that $D$ is not required to be a vector subspace of $X.$

**Uniformly continuous linear extensions**

Suppose $f : X \to Y$ be a continuous linear operator between two Hausdorff TVSs. If $M$ is a dense vector subspace of $X$ and if the restriction $f\big\vert_M : M \to Y$ to $M$ is a [topological homomorphism](wikipedia://en/63987005) then $f : X \to Y$ is also a topological homomorphism.[^n38] So if $C$ and $D$ are Hausdorff completions of $X$ and $Y,$ respectively, and if $f : X \to Y$ is a topological homomorphism, then $f$'s unique continuous linear extension $F : C \to D$ is a topological homomorphism. (Note that it's possible for $f : X \to Y$ to be surjective but for $F : C \to D$ to not be injective.)[^n38]

Suppose $X$ and $Y$ are Hausdorff TVSs, $M$ is a dense vector subspace of $X,$ and $N$ is a dense vector subspaces of $Y.$ If $M$ are and $N$ are topologically isomorphic additive subgroups via a topological homomorphism $f$ then the same is true of $X$ and $Y$ via the unique uniformly continuous extension of $f$ (which is also a homeomorphism).[^n39]

### Subsets {#Subsets}

**Complete subsets**

Every complete subset of a TVS is [sequentially complete](wikipedia://en/64126151). A complete subset of a Hausdorff TVS $X$ is a closed subset of $X.$[^n3][^n35]

Every compact subset of a TVS is complete (even if the TVS is not Hausdorff or not complete).[^n3][^n35] Closed subsets of a complete TVS are complete; however, if a TVS $X$ is not complete then $X$ is a closed subset of $X$ that is not complete. The empty set is complete subset of every TVS. If $C$ is a complete subset of a TVS (the TVS is not necessarily Hausdorff or complete) then any subset of $C$ that is closed in $C$ is complete.[^n35]

**Topological complements**

If $X$ is a non-normable [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801) on which there exists a continuous norm then $X$ contains a closed vector subspace that has no [topological complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_complement).[^n26] If $X$ is a complete TVS and $M$ is a closed vector subspace of $X$ such that $X / M$ is not complete, then $H$ does not have a [topological complement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_complement) in $X.$[^n26]

**Subsets of completions**

Let $M$ be a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) locally convex [metrizable topological vector space](wikipedia://en/64278078) and let $C$ be its completion. If $S$ is a bounded subset of $C$ then there exists a bounded subset $R$ of $X$ such that $S \subseteq \operatorname{cl}_C R.$[^n26]

**Relation to compact subsets**

A subset of a TVS (not assumed to be Hausdorff or complete) is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) if and only if it is complete and [totally bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_bounded).[^n40][^o163] Thus a closed and [totally bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_bounded) subset of a complete TVS is compact.[^n41][^n3]

In a Hausdorff locally convex TVS, the convex hull of a [precompact](wikipedia://en/1528346#Topological_vector_spaces) set is again precompact.[^n42] Consequently, in a complete locally convex Hausdorff TVS, the closed convex hull of a compact subset is again compact.[^n43]

The convex hull of compact subset of a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) is not necessarily closed and so also not necessarily compact. For example, let $H$ be the separable Hilbert space [$\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$](wikipedia://en/45194) of square-summable sequences with the usual norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ and let $e_n = (0, \ldots, 0, 1, 0, \ldots)$ be the standard [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) (that is $1$ at the $n^{\text{th}}$-coordinate). The closed set $S = \{0\} \cup \left\{\tfrac{1}{n} e_n\right\}$ is compact but its convex hull $\operatorname{co} S$ is not a closed set because $h := \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \tfrac{1}{2^n} \tfrac{1}{n} e_n$ belongs to the closure of $\operatorname{co} S$ in $H$ but $h \not\in\operatorname{co} S$ (since every sequence $z \in \operatorname{co} S$ is a finite [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) of elements of $S$ and so is necessarily $0$ in all but finitely many coordinates, which is not true of $h$).[^n44] However, like in all complete Hausdorff locally convex spaces, the closed convex hull $K := \overline{\operatorname{co}} S$ of this compact subset is compact.[^n43] The vector subspace $X := \operatorname{span} S$ is a [pre-Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Hilbert_space) when endowed with the substructure that the Hilbert space $H$ induces on it but $X$ is not complete and $h \not\in K \cap X$ (since $h \not\in X$). The closed convex hull of $S$ in $X$ (here, "closed" means with respect to $X,$ and not to $H$ as before) is equal to $K \cap X,$ which is not compact (because it is not a complete subset). This shows that in a Hausdorff locally convex space that is not complete, the closed convex hull of compact subset might fail to be compact (although it will be [precompact/totally bounded](wikipedia://en/1528346)).

Every complete totally bounded set is relatively compact.[^n3] If $X$ is any TVS then the quotient map $q : X \to X / \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ is a [closed map](wikipedia://en/372090)[^n45] and thus $S + \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\} \subseteq \operatorname{cl}_X S$ A subset $S$ of a TVS $X$ is totally bounded if and only if its image under the canonical quotient map $q : X \to X / \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ is totally bounded.[^n17] Thus $S$ is totally bounded if and only if $S + \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ is totally bounded. In any TVS, the closure of a totally bounded subset is again totally bounded.[^n3] In a locally convex space, the convex hull and the [disked hull](wikipedia://en/1734292) of a totally bounded set is totally bounded.[^n33] If $S$ is a subset of a TVS $X$ such that every sequence in $S$ has a cluster point in $S$ then $S$ is totally bounded.[^n17] A subset $S$ of a Hausdorff TVS $X$ is totally bounded if and only if every ultrafilter on $S$ is Cauchy, which happens if and only if it is pre-compact (that is, its closure in the completion of $X$ is compact).[^n37]

If $S \subseteq X$ is compact, then $\operatorname{cl}_X S = S + \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ and this set is compact. Thus the closure of a compact set is compact[^o177] (that is, all compact sets are [relatively compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_compact)).[^n46] Thus the closure of a compact set is compact. Every relatively compact subset of a Hausdorff TVS is totally bounded.[^n37]

In a complete locally convex space, the convex hull and the disked hull of a compact set are both compact.[^n33] More generally, if $K$ is a compact subset of a locally convex space, then the convex hull $\operatorname{co} K$ (resp. the disked hull $\operatorname{cobal} K$) is compact if and only if it is complete.[^n33] Every subset $S$ of $\operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ is compact and thus complete.[^o182] In particular, if $X$ is not Hausdorff then there exist compact complete sets that are not closed.[^n3]

The proof uses the fact that $S$ is compact (but possibly not closed) and $\operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ is both closed and compact so that $S + \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\},$ which is the image of the compact set $S + \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ under the continuous addition map $\cdot + \cdot : X \times X \to X,$ is also compact. Recall also that the sum of a compact set (that is, $S$) and a closed set is closed so $S + \operatorname{cl}_X \{0\}$ is closed in $X.$
