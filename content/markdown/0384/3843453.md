# Strong prime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Strong prime"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **strong prime** is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) with certain special properties. The definitions of strong primes are different in [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527).

:::

## Definition in number theory {#Definition_in_number_theory}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **strong prime** is a prime number that is greater than the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of the nearest prime above and below (in other words, it is closer to the following than to the preceding prime). Or to put it algebraically, writing the [sequence](wikipedia://en/51421) of prime numbers as (*p*${}_{1}$, *p*${}_{2}$, *p*${}_{3}$, ...) = (2, 3, 5, ...), *p${}_{n}$* is a strong prime if $p_{n} > \tfrac{p_{n -\,1} + p_{n +\,1}}{2}$. For example, 17 is the seventh prime: the sixth and eighth primes, 13 and 19, add up to 32, and half that is 16; 17 is greater than 16, so 17 is a strong prime.

The first few strong primes are

[11](wikipedia://en/208156), [17](wikipedia://en/208288), [29](wikipedia://en/362212), [37](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/37_%28number%29), [41](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/41_%28number%29), [59](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/59_%28number%29), [67](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/67_%28number%29), [71](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/71_%28number%29), [79](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/79_%28number%29), [97](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/97_%28number%29), [101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101_%28number%29), [107](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/107_%28number%29), [127](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/127_%28number%29), [137](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/137_%28number%29), [149](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/149_%28number%29), [163](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/163_%28number%29), [179](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/179_%28number%29), [191](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/191_%28number%29), [197](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/197_%28number%29), [223](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/223_%28number%29), [227](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/227_%28number%29), [239](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/239_%28number%29), [251](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/251_%28number%29), [269](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/269_%28number%29), [277](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/277_%28number%29), [281](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/281_%28number%29), [307](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/307_%28number%29), 311, 331, 347, 367, 379, 397, 419, 431, 439, 457, 461, 479, 487, 499 .

In a [twin prime](wikipedia://en/41997) pair (*p*, *p* + 2) with *p* > 5, *p* is always a strong prime, since 3 must divide *p* − 2, which cannot be prime.

## Definition in cryptography {#Definition_in_cryptography}

In [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), a prime number *p* is said to be "strong" if the following conditions are satisfied.[^o4]

- *p* is sufficiently large to be useful in cryptography; typically this requires *p* to be too large for plausible computational resources to enable a [cryptanalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalyst) to [factorise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factorisation) products of *p* with other strong primes.
- *p* − 1 has large prime factors. That is, *p* = *a*${}_{1}$*q*${}_{1}$ + 1 for some integer *a*${}_{1}$ and large prime *q*${}_{1}$.
- *q*${}_{1}$ − 1 has large prime factors. That is, *q*${}_{1}$ = *a*${}_{2}$*q*${}_{2}$ + 1 for some integer *a*${}_{2}$ and large prime *q*${}_{2}$.
- *p* + 1 has large prime factors. That is, *p* = *a*${}_{3}$*q*${}_{3}$ − 1 for some integer *a*${}_{3}$ and large prime *q*${}_{3}$.

It is possible for a prime to be a strong prime both in the cryptographic sense and the number theoretic sense. For the sake of illustration, 439351292910452432574786963588089477522344331 is a strong prime in the number theoretic sense because the arithmetic mean of its two neighboring primes is 62 less. Without the aid of a computer, this number would be a strong prime in the cryptographic sense because 439351292910452432574786963588089477522344330 has the large prime factor 1747822896920092227343 (and in turn the number one less than that has the large prime factor 1683837087591611009), 439351292910452432574786963588089477522344332 has the large prime factor 864608136454559457049 (and in turn the number one less than that has the large prime factor 105646155480762397). Even using algorithms more advanced than [trial division](wikipedia://en/557660), these numbers would be difficult to factor by hand. For a modern [computer algebra system](wikipedia://en/54427), these numbers can be factored almost instantaneously. A [cryptographically strong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographically_strong) prime has to be much larger than this example.

## Application of strong primes in cryptography {#Application_of_strong_primes_in_cryptography}

### Factoring-based cryptosystems {#Factoring-based_cryptosystems}

Some people suggest that in the [key generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_generation) process in [RSA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RSA_%28algorithm%29) cryptosystems, the modulus *n* should be chosen as the product of two strong primes. This makes the factorization of *n* = *pq* using [Pollard's *p* − 1 algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pollard's_p_-_1_algorithm) computationally infeasible. For this reason, strong primes are required by the [ANSI X9.31](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ANSI_X9.31) standard for use in generating RSA keys for [digital signatures](wikipedia://en/59644). However, strong primes do not protect against modulus factorisation using newer algorithms such as [Lenstra elliptic curve factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lenstra_elliptic_curve_factorization) and [Number Field Sieve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_Field_Sieve) algorithm. Given the additional cost of generating strong primes [RSA Security](wikipedia://en/301240) do not currently recommend their use in [key generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_generation). Similar (and more technical) argument is also given by Rivest and Silverman.[^o4]

### Discrete-logarithm-based cryptosystems {#Discrete-logarithm-based_cryptosystems}

It is shown by Stephen Pohlig and [Martin Hellman](wikipedia://en/422387) in 1978 that if all the factors of *p* − 1 are less than log${}^{c}$ *p*, then the problem of solving [discrete logarithm](wikipedia://en/181334) modulo *p* is in [P](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P_=_NP_problem). Therefore, for cryptosystems based on discrete logarithm, such as [DSA](wikipedia://en/59470), it is required that *p* − 1 have at least one large prime factor.

## Miscellaneous facts {#Miscellaneous_facts}

A computationally large [safe prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Safe_prime) is likely to be a cryptographically strong prime.

Note that the criteria for determining if a pseudoprime is a [strong pseudoprime](wikipedia://en/1100422) is by congruences to powers of a base, not by inequality to the arithmetic mean of neighboring pseudoprimes.

When a prime is equal to the mean of its neighboring primes, it is called a [balanced prime](wikipedia://en/3949019). When it is less, it is called a **weak prime** (not to be confused with a [weakly prime number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakly_prime_number)).
