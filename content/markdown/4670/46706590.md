# Loewner order

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Loewner order"}

In mathematics, **[Loewner](wikipedia://en/9337483) order** is the [partial order defined by the convex cone](wikipedia://en/3558266#Partial_order_defined_by_a_convex_cone) of [positive semi-definite matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_matrix#Negative-definite,_semidefinite_and_indefinite_matrices). This order is usually employed to generalize the definitions of monotone and concave/convex scalar functions to [monotone and concave/convex Hermitian valued functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_function#Classes_of_matrix_functions). These functions arise naturally in matrix and operator theory and have applications in many areas of physics and engineering.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *A* and *B* be two [Hermitian matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_matrices) of order *n*. We say that *A ≥ B* if *A* − *B* is [positive semi-definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_matrix#Negative-definite,_semidefinite_and_indefinite_matrices). Similarly, we say that *A > B* if *A* − *B* is [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix).

Although it is commonly discussed on matrices (as a finite-dimensional case), the Loewner order is also well-defined on [operators](wikipedia://en/454936) (an infinite-dimensional case) in the analogous way.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

When *A* and *B* are real scalars (i.e. *n* = 1), the Loewner order reduces to the usual ordering of **R**. Although some familiar properties of the usual order of **R** are also valid when *n* ≥ 2, several properties are no longer valid. For instance, the [comparability](wikipedia://en/57495962) of two matrices may no longer be valid. In fact, if $A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}\$ and $B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}\$ then neither *A* ≥ *B* or *B* ≥ *A* holds true. In other words, the Loewner order is a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order), but not a [total order](wikipedia://en/30330).

Moreover, since *A* and *B* are Hermitian matrices, their [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) are all real numbers. If *λ*<sub>1</sub>(*B*) is the maximum eigenvalue of *B* and *λ*<sub>*n*</sub>(*A*) the minimum eigenvalue of *A*, a sufficient criterion to have *A* ≥ *B* is that *λ*<sub>*n*</sub>(*A*) ≥ *λ*<sub>1</sub>(*B*). If *A* or *B* is a multiple of the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), then this criterion is also necessary.

The Loewner order does *not* have the [least-upper-bound property](wikipedia://en/22038671), and therefore does not form a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304). It is bounded: for any finite set $S$ of matrices, one can find an "upper-bound" matrix *A* that is greater than all of S. However, there will be multiple upper bounds. In a lattice, there would exist a unique maximum $\max(S)$ such that any upper bound *U* on $S$ obeys $\max(S)$ ≤ *U*. But in the Loewner order, one can have two upper bounds *A* and *B* that are both minimal (there is no element *C* \< *A* that is also an upper bound) but that are incomparable (*A* - *B* is neither positive semidefinite nor negative semidefinite).
