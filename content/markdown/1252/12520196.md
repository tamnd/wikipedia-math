# Cross slope

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cross slope"}

*Image: Cross Slope.svg.* Cross section of a road. 1. Gutter; 2. Shoulder; 3. Sub-base; 4. Base course; 5. Asphalt

**Cross slope**, **cross fall** or **camber** is a geometric feature of pavement surfaces: the transverse slope with respect to the horizon. It is a very important safety factor. Cross slope is provided to provide a [drainage gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage_gradient) so that water will run off the surface to a [drainage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drainage) system such as a [street gutter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Street_gutter) or [ditch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ditch). Inadequate cross slope will contribute to [aquaplaning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquaplaning). On straight sections of normal two-lane roads, the pavement [cross section](wikipedia://en/1337370) is usually highest in the center and drains to both sides. In horizontal curves, the cross slope is banked into [superelevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cant_%28road/rail%29) to reduce steering effort and lateral force required to go around the curve.[^o2] All water drains to the inside of the curve.[^o3] If the cross slope magnitude oscillates within 1 \-, the body and payload of high (heavy) vehicles will experience high roll and lateral vibration.

:::

Cross slope is usually expressed as a percentage:

$$\text{cross slope} = \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}} \times 100\%.$$

Cross slope is the angle around a vertical axis between:

- the horizontal line that is perpendicular to the road's center line, and
- the surface.

Typical values range from 2 percent for straight segments to 10 percent for sharp superelevated curves. It may also be expressed as a fraction of an inch in rise over a one-[foot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foot_%28unit%29) run (e.g. $\tfrac{1}{4}$ inch per foot).
