# Lawvere theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lawvere theory"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a **Lawvere theory** (named after [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [William Lawvere](wikipedia://en/872760)) is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) that can be considered a categorical counterpart of the notion of an [equational theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equational_theory). Intuitively, it is a categorical generalization of algebraic structures (e.g., a group or a ring), where there exists a "generic" object and all objects are isomorphic to an integer power of $x$, representing the inputs for the $n$-ary operations on $x$ (i.e., of the form $x^n \mapsto x$, starting from the fact that $x := x^1$ and so $x \circ x := x^1 \circ x^1 := x^1x^1 := x^2$; trivially generalizing inductively, we get the rest of the objects) where the operations come from the algebraic structure at hand (e.g., addition and/or multiplication).

:::

The Lawvere theory of groups has as its generic object an underlying placeholder $x$ where the other objects are the inputs for $n$-ary operations from those integer powers of $x$ of the form ($x^n$) back to $x$, where a model, a finite-product preserving functor, from this theory into a target category $C$ such as the category of sets or topological spaces, would map the abstract theory $x$ onto the category to create a concrete, "combined," group-based structure. This model would provide a set with group structure (a *group*) or a topological space with group structure (a *topological group*), supplying appropriate names to the generic object $x$ and its mappings ($n$-ary operations) according to whatever the theory and model at play are; in the model of sets for the Lawvere theory of groups, the generic object is a group and its mappings are group operations.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\aleph_0$ be a [skeleton](wikipedia://en/1812151) of the category **[FinSet](wikipedia://en/25044975)** of [finite sets](wikipedia://en/11742) and [functions](wikipedia://en/185427). Formally, a **Lawvere theory** consists of a [small category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_category) $L$ with (strictly [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity)) finite [products](wikipedia://en/237868) and a strict identity-on-objects [functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functor_%28category_theory%29) $I:\aleph_0^\text{op}\rightarrow L$ preserving finite products.

A **model** of a Lawvere theory in a category $C$ with finite products is a finite-product preserving functor $M : L \rightarrow C$. A **morphism of models** $h : M \rightarrow N$ where $M$ and *$N$* are models of *$L$* is a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022) of functors.

:::

## Model examples {#Model_examples}

Some examples of models of the Lawvere theory of groups (i.e., $L$ = $\mathbf{LawGrp}$):

- $M : L \rightarrow \mathbf{Set}$ ($M$ is a classical [*group*](wikipedia://en/19447))
- $M : L \rightarrow \mathbf{Top}$ ($M$ is a [topological *group*](wikipedia://en/42315))
- $M : L \rightarrow \mathbf{Man}$ ($M$ is a [Lie *group*](wikipedia://en/17945)).

Some examples of models of the Lawvere theory of rings (i.e., $L$ = $\mathbf{LawRing}$):

- $M : L \rightarrow \mathbf{Set}$ ($M$ is a classical *[ring](wikipedia://en/48404)*)
- $M : L \rightarrow \mathbf{Top}$ ($M$ is a [topological *ring*](wikipedia://en/60097))
- $M : L \rightarrow \mathbf{Man}$ ($M$ is a [smooth *ring*](wikipedia://en/36088541)).

## Category of Lawvere theories {#Category_of_Lawvere_theories}

A **map** between Lawvere theories $(L, I)$ and $(L', I')$ is a finite-product preserving functor that commutes with $I$ and $I'$. Such a map is commonly seen as an interpretation of $(L, I)$ in $(L', I')$.

More formally, a map $K : (L, I) \rightarrow (L', I')$, such that $K \circ I = I'$, where $I(n) = x^n_{L}$ and $(K \circ I)(n) = I'(n) = x^n_{L'}$.

Lawvere theories together with maps between them form the category $\mathbf{Law}$.

## Variations {#Variations}

Variations include **multisorted** (or **multityped**) **Lawvere theory**, **infinitary Lawvere theory**, and **finite-product theory**.
