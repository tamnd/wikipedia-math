# Chung Kai-lai

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Chung Kai-lai"}

**Kai Lai Chung** ([traditional Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traditional_Chinese): 鍾開萊; [simplified Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplified_Chinese): 钟开莱; September 19, 1917 – June 2, 2009) was a Chinese-American mathematician known for his significant contributions to modern [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Chung was a native of [Hangzhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hangzhou), the capital city of [Zhejiang Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhejiang_Province). Chung entered [Tsinghua University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsinghua_University) in 1936, and initially studied [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) at its Department of Physics. In 1940, Chung graduated from the Department of Mathematics of the [National Southwestern Associated University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Southwestern_Associated_University), where he later worked as a [teaching assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaching_assistant). During this period, he first studied number theory with [Lo-Keng Hua](wikipedia://en/2337582) and then probability theory with [Pao-Lu Hsu](wikipedia://en/42380697).

In 1944, Chung was chosen to be one of the recipients of the 6th [Boxer Indemnity Scholarship Program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boxer_Indemnity_Scholarship_Program) for study in the United States. He arrived at [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) in December 1945 and obtained his [PhD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PhD) in 1947. Chung's dissertation was titled “On the maximum partial sum of sequences of independent random variables” and was under the supervision of [John Wilder Tukey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wilder_Tukey) and [Harald Cramér](wikipedia://en/223129).[^o4]

In 1950s, Chung taught at the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago), [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University), [UC-Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UC-Berkeley), [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University) and [Syracuse University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse_University). He then transferred to [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) in 1961, where he made fundamental contributions to the study of [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) and laid the framework for the general mathematical theory of [Markov chains](wikipedia://en/60876). Chung was later appointed Professor Emeritus of Mathematics of the Department of Mathematics at Stanford.

Chung was regarded as one of the leading probabilists after [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II). He was an Invited Speaker at the [ICM](wikipedia://en/627842) in 1958 in Edinburgh and in 1970 in Nice. Some of his most influential contributions have been in the form of his expositions in his textbooks on elementary probability and Markov chains. In addition, Chung also explored other branches of mathematics, such as probabilistic [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342) and gauge theorems for the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874).

Chung's visit to China in 1979 (together with [Joseph L. Doob](wikipedia://en/1547599) and Jacques Neveu), and his subsequent visits, served as points of renewed exchange between Chinese probabilists and their Western counterparts. He also served as an external examiner for several universities in the Asian region, including the [National University of Singapore](wikipedia://en/286880).

In 1981, with [Erhan Cinlar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erhan_Cinlar) and [Ronald Getoor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Getoor), Chung initiated the "Seminars on Stochastic Processes", a popular annual national meeting covering Markov processes, Brownian motion and probability.

Chung possessed a wide-ranging and intimate knowledge of literature and music, especially opera. He also had an interest in [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italians) culture and taught himself Italian after he retired. He spoke several languages and translated a probability book from [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_language) to English.

Chung died of natural causes on June 1, 2009, at the age of 91.

- *A Course in Probability Theory*; by Kai Lai Chung.[^o6]
- *Markov Processes with Stationary Transition Probabilities*, by Kai Lai Chung.[^o7]
- *Selected Works Of Kai Lai Chung*; [World Scientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Scientific) Publishing Company; .
