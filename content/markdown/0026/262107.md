# Minor (linear algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Minor"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a **minor** of a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $A$ is the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of some smaller [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) generated from $A$ by removing one or more of its rows and columns. Minors obtained by removing just one row and one column from square matrices (**first minors**) are useful for calculating matrix **cofactors**, which are useful for computing both the determinant and [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_matrix) of square matrices. The requirement that the square matrix be smaller than the original matrix is often omitted in the definition.

:::

The cofactors $C_{ij}$ are also the partial derivatives with respect to the matrix entries of the determinant map $\text{det}:\text{Mat}(n, \mathbb R) \to \mathbb R$ which takes a matrix $(a_{i, j})_{i, j \in \{1, \dots, n\}}$ to its determinant:

$$\frac{\text{d} (\text{det})}{\text{d}a_{ij}} = C_{ij}$$

## Definition and illustration {#Definition_and_illustration}

### First minors {#First_minors}

If $\mathbf{A}$ is a square matrix, then the *minor* of the entry in the $i$-th row and $j$-th column (also called the $(i, j)$ *minor*, or a *first minor*[^o4]) is the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the [submatrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submatrix) formed by deleting the $i$-th row and $j$-th column. This number is often denoted $M_{i, j}$. The $(i, j)$ **cofactor** is obtained by multiplying the minor by $(-1)^{i + j}$, and is often denoted $C_{i, j}$.

To illustrate these definitions, consider the following 3 × 3 matrix,

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & 7 \\ 3 & 0 & 5 \\ -1 & 9 & 11 \\ \end{bmatrix}$$

To compute the minor $M_{2,3}$ and the cofactor $C_{2,3}$, we find the determinant of the above matrix with row 2 and column 3 removed.

$$M_{2,3} = \det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 & \Box \\ \Box & \Box & \Box \\ -1 & 9 & \Box \\ \end{bmatrix}= \det \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ -1 & 9 \\ \end{bmatrix} = 9-(-4) = 13$$

So the cofactor of the (2,3) entry is

$$C_{2,3} = (-1)^{2+3}(M_{2,3}) = -13.$$

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="General definition"}

### General definition {#General_definition}

Let $\mathbf{A}$ be an $m \times n$ matrix and $k$ an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) with $0 < k \le m$, and $k \le n$. A $k \times k$ *minor* of $\mathbf{A}$, also called *minor determinant of order $k$* of $\mathbf{A}$ or, if $m = n$, the $(n - k)$*th* *minor determinant* of $\mathbf{A}$ (the word "determinant" is often omitted, and the word "degree" is sometimes used instead of "order") is the determinant of a $k \times k$ matrix obtained from $\mathbf{A}$ by deleting $m - k$ rows and $n - k$ columns. Sometimes the term is used to refer to the $k \times k$ matrix obtained from $\mathbf{A}$ as above (by deleting $m - k$ rows and $n - k$ columns), but this matrix should be referred to as a *(square) submatrix* of $\mathbf{A}$, leaving the term "minor" to refer to the determinant of this matrix. For a matrix $\mathbf{A}$ as above, there are a total of ${m \choose k} \cdot {n \choose k}$ minors of size $k \times k$. The *minor of order zero* is often defined to be 1. For a square matrix, the *zeroth minor* is just the determinant of the matrix.[^o7][^o17]

Let

$$\begin{aligned} I &= 1 \le i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_k \le m, \\[2pt] J &= 1 \le j_1 < j_2 < \cdots < j_k \le n, \end{aligned}$$

be ordered sequences (in natural order, as it is always assumed when talking about minors unless otherwise stated) of indexes. The minor $\det \bigl( (\mathbf A_{i_p, j_q})_{p,q = 1, \ldots, k} \bigr)$ corresponding to these choices of indexes is denoted $\det_{I,J} A$ or $\det \mathbf A_{I, J}$ or $[\mathbf A]_{I,J}$ or $M_{I,J}$ or $M_{i_1, i_2, \ldots, i_k, j_1, j_2, \ldots, j_k}$ or $M_{(i),(j)}$ (where the $(i)$ denotes the sequence of indexes $I$, etc.), depending on the source. Also, there are two types of denotations in use in literature: by the minor associated to ordered sequences of indexes $I$ and $J$, some authors[^o8] mean the determinant of the matrix that is formed as above, by taking the elements of the original matrix from the rows whose indexes are in $I$ and columns whose indexes are in $J$, whereas some other authors mean by a minor associated to $I$ and $J$ the determinant of the matrix formed from the original matrix by deleting the rows in $I$ and columns in $J$;[^o7] which notation is used should always be checked. In this article, we use the inclusive definition of choosing the elements from rows of $I$ and columns of $J$. The exceptional case is the case of the first minor or the $(i, j)$-minor described above; in that case, the exclusive meaning $M_{i,j} = \det \bigl( \left( \mathbf A_{p,q} \right)_{p \neq i, q \neq j} \bigr)$ is standard everywhere in the literature and is used in this article also.

:::

### Complement {#Complement}

The complement $B_{ijk..., pqr...}$ of a minor $M_{ijk..., pqr...}$ of a square matrix, $\mathbf{A}$, is formed by the determinant of the matrix $\mathbf{A}$ from which all the rows ($ijk...$) and columns ($pqr...$) associated with $M_{ijk..., pqr...}$ have been removed. The complement of the first minor of an element $a_{ij}$ is merely that element.[^o10]

## Applications of minors and cofactors {#Applications_of_minors_and_cofactors}

### Cofactor expansion of the determinant {#Cofactor_expansion_of_the_determinant}

The cofactors feature prominently in [Laplace's formula](wikipedia://en/3506553) for the expansion of determinants, which is a method of computing larger determinants in terms of smaller ones. Given an $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{A} = (a_{ij})$, the determinant of $\mathbf{A}$, denoted $\det(\mathbf{A})$, can be written as the sum of the cofactors of any row or column of the matrix multiplied by the entries that generated them. In other words, defining $C_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}$ then the cofactor expansion along the $j$-th column gives:

$$\begin{aligned} \det(\mathbf A) &= a_{1j}C_{1j} + a_{2j}C_{2j} + a_{3j}C_{3j} + \cdots + a_{nj}C_{nj} \\[2pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ij} C_{ij} \\[2pt] &= \sum_{i=1}^{n} a_{ij}(-1)^{i+j} M_{ij} \end{aligned}$$

The cofactor expansion along the $i$-th row gives:

$$\begin{aligned} \det(\mathbf A) &= a_{i1}C_{i1} + a_{i2}C_{i2} + a_{i3}C_{i3} + \cdots + a_{in}C_{in} \\[2pt] &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} C_{ij} \\[2pt] &= \sum_{j=1}^{n} a_{ij} (-1)^{i+j} M_{ij} \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Cofactor matrix"}

### Inverse of a matrix {#Inverse_of_a_matrix}

One can write down the inverse of an [invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122) by computing its cofactors by using [Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008), as follows. The matrix formed by all of the cofactors of a square matrix $\mathbf{A}$ is called the **cofactor matrix** (also called the **matrix of cofactors** or, sometimes, *comatrix*):

$$\mathbf C = \begin{bmatrix} C_{11} & C_{12} & \cdots & C_{1n} \\ C_{21} & C_{22} & \cdots & C_{2n} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ C_{n1} & C_{n2} & \cdots & C_{nn} \end{bmatrix}$$

Then the inverse of $\mathbf{A}$ is the transpose of the cofactor matrix times the reciprocal of the determinant of $\mathbf{A}$:

$$\mathbf A^{-1} = \frac{1}{\operatorname{det}(\mathbf A)} \mathbf C^\mathsf{T}.$$

The [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of the cofactor matrix is called the [adjugate matrix](wikipedia://en/202840) (also called the *classical adjoint*) of $\mathbf{A}$.

The above formula can be generalized as follows: Let

$$\begin{aligned} I &= 1 \le i_1 < i_2 < \ldots < i_k \le n, \\[2pt] J &= 1 \le j_1 < j_2 < \ldots < j_k \le n, \end{aligned}$$

be ordered sequences (in natural order) of indexes (here $\mathbf{A}$ is an $n \times n$ matrix). Then[^o15]

$$[\mathbf A^{-1}]_{I,J} = \pm\frac{[\mathbf A]_{J',I'}}{\det \mathbf A},$$

where $I', J'$ denote the ordered sequences of indices (the indices are in natural order of magnitude, as above) complementary to $I, J$, so that every index $1, ..., n$ appears exactly once in either $I$ or $I'$, but not in both (similarly for the $J$ and $J'$) and $[\mathbf{A}]_{I, J}$ denotes the determinant of the submatrix of $\mathbf{A}$ formed by choosing the rows of the index set $I$ and columns of index set $J$. Also, $[\mathbf A]_{I,J} = \det \bigl( (A_{i_p, j_q})_{p,q = 1, \ldots, k} \bigr).$ A simple proof can be given using the wedge product. Indeed,

$$\bigl[ \mathbf A^{-1} \bigr]_{I,J} (e_1\wedge\ldots \wedge e_n) = \pm(\mathbf A^{-1}e_{j_1})\wedge \ldots \wedge(\mathbf A^{-1}e_{j_k})\wedge e_{i'_1}\wedge\ldots \wedge e_{i'_{n-k}},$$

where $e_1, \ldots, e_n$ are the basis vectors. Acting by $\mathbf{A}$ on both sides, one gets

$$\begin{aligned} &\ \bigl[\mathbf A^{-1} \bigr]_{I,J} \det \mathbf A (e_1\wedge\ldots \wedge e_n) \\[2pt] =&\ \pm (e_{j_1})\wedge \ldots \wedge(e_{j_k})\wedge (\mathbf A e_{i'_1})\wedge\ldots \wedge (\mathbf A e_{i'_{n-k}}) \\[2pt] =&\ \pm [\mathbf A]_{J',I'}(e_1\wedge\ldots \wedge e_n). \end{aligned}$$

The sign can be worked out to be

$$(-1)^{\left( \sum_{s=1}^{k} i_s - \sum_{s=1}^{k} j_s \right)},$$

so the sign is determined by the sums of elements in $I$ and $J$.

:::

### Other applications {#Other_applications}

Given an $i \times j$ matrix with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) entries (or entries from any other [field](wikipedia://en/10603)) and [rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_%28matrix_theory%29) $r$, then there exists at least one non-zero $r \times r$ minor, while all larger minors are zero.

We will use the following notation for minors: if $\mathbf{A}$ is an $i \times j$ matrix, $I$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $\{1, ..., i\}$ with $m$ elements, and $J$ is a subset of $\{1, ..., j\}$ with $m$ elements, then we write $[\mathbf{A}]_{I, J}$ for the $m \times m$ minor of $\mathbf{A}$ that corresponds to the rows with index in $I$ and the columns with index in $J$.

- If $\mathbf{A}$ is square and $I = J$, then $[\mathbf{A}]_{I, J}$ is called a *principal minor*.
- If $\mathbf{A}$ is square and $I = J = \{1, ..., m\}$, then the principal minor $[\mathbf{A}]_{I, J}$ is called a *leading principal minor (of order $m$)* or *corner (principal) minor (of order $m$)*.[^o17] For an $i \times i$ square matrix, there are $i$ leading principal minors.
- A *basic minor* of a matrix with rank $r$ is an $r \times r$ minor with nonzero value.[^o17]
- For [Hermitian matrices](wikipedia://en/189682), the leading principal minors can be used to test for [positive definiteness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) and the principal minors can be used to test for [positive semidefiniteness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-semidefinite_matrix). See [Sylvester's criterion](wikipedia://en/11437896) for more details.

Both the formula for ordinary [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) and the [Cauchy–Binet formula](wikipedia://en/357354) for the determinant of the product of two matrices are special cases of the following general statement about the minors of a product of two matrices. Suppose that $\mathbf{A}$ is an $i \times k$ matrix, $\mathbf{B}$ is an $k \times j$ matrix, $I$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $\{1, ..., i\}$ with $m$ elements and $J$ is a subset of $\{1, ..., j\}$ with $m$ elements. Then

$$[\mathbf{AB}]_{I,J} = \sum_{K} [\mathbf{A}]_{I,K} [\mathbf{B}]_{K,J}\,$$

where the sum extends over all subsets $K$ of $\{1, ..., k\}$ with $m$ elements.

## Multilinear algebra approach {#Multilinear_algebra_approach}

A more systematic, algebraic treatment of minors is given in [multilinear algebra](wikipedia://en/243946), using the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product): the $k$-minors of a matrix are the entries in the $k$-th [exterior power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_power) map.

If the columns of a matrix are wedged together $k$ at a time, the $k \times k$ minors appear as the components of the resulting $k$-vectors. For example, the 2 × 2 minors of the matrix

$$\begin{pmatrix} 1 & 4 \\ 3 & \!\!-1 \\ 2 & 1 \\ \end{pmatrix}$$

are −13 (from the first two rows), −7 (from the first and last row), and 5 (from the last two rows). Now consider the wedge product

$$(\mathbf{e}_1 + 3\mathbf{e}_2 + 2\mathbf{e}_3) \wedge (4\mathbf{e}_1 - \mathbf{e}_2 + \mathbf{e}_3)$$

where the two expressions correspond to the two columns of our matrix. Using the properties of the wedge product, namely that it is [bilinear](wikipedia://en/4365) and [alternating](wikipedia://en/8200947),

$$\mathbf{e}_i \wedge \mathbf{e}_i = 0,$$

and [antisymmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutativity),

$$\mathbf{e}_i\wedge \mathbf{e}_j = - \mathbf{e}_j\wedge \mathbf{e}_i,$$

we can simplify this expression to

$$-13 \mathbf{e}_1\wedge \mathbf{e}_2 -7 \mathbf{e}_1\wedge \mathbf{e}_3 +5 \mathbf{e}_2\wedge \mathbf{e}_3$$

where the coefficients agree with the minors computed earlier.

## A remark about different notation {#A_remark_about_different_notation}

In some books, instead of *cofactor* the term *adjunct* is used.[^o20] Moreover, it is denoted as $\mathbf{A}_{ij}$ and defined in the same way as cofactor:

$$\mathbf{A}_{ij} = (-1)^{i+j} \mathbf{M}_{ij}$$

Using this notation the inverse matrix is written this way:

$$\mathbf{M}^{-1} = \frac{1}{\det(M)}\begin{bmatrix} A_{11} & A_{21} & \cdots & A_{n1} \\ A_{12} & A_{22} & \cdots & A_{n2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ A_{1n} & A_{2n} & \cdots & A_{nn} \end{bmatrix}$$

Keep in mind that *adjunct* is not [adjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjugate) or [adjoint](wikipedia://en/644032). In modern terminology, the "adjoint" of a matrix most often refers to the corresponding [adjoint operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_operator).
