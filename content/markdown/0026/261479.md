# Quipu

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quipu"}

*Image: Peru, Inca Period, 15th-16th Century - Inka Khipu (Fiber Recording Device) - 1940.469 - Cleveland Museum of Art.tif.* *Quipu* in the [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cleveland_Museum_of_Art) in Cleveland, Ohio.

***Quipu*** ( ), also spelled ***khipu*** (kipu, ; khipu, ), are record-keeping devices fashioned from knotted cords. They were historically used by various cultures in the central [Andes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andes) of South America, most prominently by the [Inca Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire).[^o37]

:::

A *quipu* usually consists of [cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton) or [camelid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camelidae) fiber cords, and contains categorized information based on dimensions like color, order, and number.[^o3] The Inca, in particular, used knots tied in a decimal [positional system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positional_system) to store numbers and other values in *quipu* cords. Depending on use and the amount of information stored, *quipus* can have anywhere from a few to several thousand cords.

Objects which can unambiguously be identified as *quipus* first appear in the archaeological record during the 1st millennium CE,[^o4] likely attributable to the [Wari Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wari_Empire).[^o60][^o61] *Quipus* subsequently played a key part in the administration of the [Kingdom of Cusco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Cusco) of the 13th to 15th centuries, and later of the [Inca Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Empire) (1438–1533), flourishing across the Andes from c. 1100 to 1532. Inca administration used *quipus* extensively for a variety of uses: monitoring tax obligations, collecting [census](wikipedia://en/6889) records, keeping calendrical information,[^o5] military organization,[^o6] and potentially for recording simple and stereotyped historical "annales".[^o3]

It is not known exactly how many intact *quipus* still remain, as many were deposited in ancient mausoleums[^o4] or later destroyed by the Spanish. However, a recent survey of both museum and private collection inventories places the total number of known extant pre-Columbian *quipus* at just under 1,400.[^o7]

After the [Spanish conquest of the Inca Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_the_Inca_Empire), *quipus* were slowly replaced by European writing and numeral systems. Many *quipus* were identified as idolatrous and destroyed, but some Spaniards promoted the adaptation of the *quipu* recording system to the needs of the colonial administration, and some priests advocated the use of *quipus* for ecclesiastical purposes.[^o8] In some cases *quipu* technology was even fused with writing to form hybrid objects called *quipu* boards, which were often used to track religious duties.[^o9] Today, *quipus* continue to serve as important items in several modern Andean villages.[^o71]

Various other cultures have used knotted strings, unrelated to Andean *quipus,* to record information, such as [Chinese knotting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_knotting), also practiced by [Tibetans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tibetan_people), [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_people), and [Polynesians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesians).[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13][^o14]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The word *quipu* is derived from a Quechua word meaning 'knot'.[^o16] The terms *quipu* and *khipu* are spelling variations on the same word. *Quipu* is the traditional spelling based on the [Spanish orthography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_orthography), while *khipu* reflects the [Quechuan and Aymaran spelling shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quechuan_and_Aymaran_spelling_shift). qu (pronounced ) comes from [Cusco Quechua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qusqu-Qullaw), while many other Quechua varieties use the term qu. The [hispanicized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hispanicized) spelling of *quipu* is the form most commonly used in both Spanish and English.[^o17]

## Purpose {#Purpose}

"The khipu were knotted-string devices that were used for recording both statistical and narrative information, most notably by the Inca but also by other peoples of the central Andes from pre-Incaic times, through the colonial and republican eras, and even – in a considerably transformed and attenuated form – down to the present day."

*Image: Nueva corónica y buen gobierno (1936 facsimile) p360.png.* A *quipucamayoc* depicted in *[El primer nueva corónica y buen gobierno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_primer_nueva_corónica_y_buen_gobierno)*. A *[yupana](wikipedia://en/24320887)*, an Inca calculating device, is also visible in the lower left.

*Quipus* held information, decipherable by officials called *quipucamayocs* (khipu kamayuq, ), classified in various categories, narrated from the most important to the least important category, according to color, number, and order.[^o3]

To date, most of the information recorded on the *quipus* studied by researchers consists of numbers in a decimal system,[^o20] such as "Indian chiefs ascertain\[ing\] which province had lost more than another and balanc\[ing\] the losses between them" after the Spanish invasion.[^o21] In the early years of the [Spanish conquest of Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_Peru), Spanish officials often relied on the *quipus* to settle disputes over local [tribute payments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tribute_payment) or goods production. The *quipucamayocs* could be summoned to court, where their bookkeeping was recognised as valid documentation of past payments.

Some knots — as well as other features, such as color, fiber type, cord attachments, etc. — are thought to compose an undeciphered non-numeric information system. The lack of a clear link between any indigenous Andean languages and the *quipus* has historically led to the supposition that *quipus* are not a [glottographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glottographic) writing system and have no [phonetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonetic) referent.[^o22] However, anthropologist [Gary Urton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Urton) has suggested that the *quipus* used a binary system that could record [phonological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonology) or [logographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logogram) data.[^o23] According to Martti Pärssinen, *quipucamayocs* would learn specific [oral texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oral_tradition), which in relation to the basic information contained in *quipu*, and pictorial representations, often painted on [quiru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qiru) vessels, similar to [Aztec pictograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec_pictograms), related simple "episodes".[^o3] Frank Salomon, meanwhile, has argued that *quipus* are actually a [semasiographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semasiographic) language, a system of representative symbols – such as [music notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_notation) or [numerals](wikipedia://en/21170) – that relay information but are not directly related to the speech sounds of a particular language,[^o24] like [ideograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideogram) and [proto-writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-writing).

In 2011, a potential match between six colonial-era [Santa Valley Quipus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Valley_Quipus) and a Spanish colonial document from the same region was identified.[^o4] Researchers believe this possible *quipu*-document match is the strongest [Rosetta Stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone)-like connection currently known, which could offer key clues needed to unlock the full extent of the quipu code. Subsequent studies have built on the proposed *quipu*-document connection, suggesting that the binary manner by which cords can be attached to the main body of the six *quipus* may encode [moiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moiety_%28kinship%29) affiliation,[^o25][^o26] and uncovering detailed Andean social structures encoded within the six *quipus*.[^o27]

[Sabine Hyland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sabine_Hyland) claims to have made the first phonetic decipherment through her analysis of [epistolary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistle) *quipus* from San Juan de Collata, Peru*,* challenging the assumption that *quipus* do not represent information phonetically.[^o28] However, the *quipus* in question date to the colonial period and are believed to have been exchanged during an 18th-century rebellion against the Spanish government, suggesting that their encoding may have been influenced by the introduction of European writing systems. With the help of local leaders, Hyland argues that the names of the two *[ayllus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ayllu)*, or family lineages, who received and sent the *quipus* can be translated using phonetic references to the animal fibers and colors of the relevant quipu cords.[^o29][^o30]

### Numeric usage {#Numeric_usage}

While Spanish colonial chroniclers, such as [Inca Garcilaso de la Vega](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_Garcilaso_de_la_Vega), hinted at the numerical system of *quipus*, it is [Leslie Leland Locke](wikipedia://en/78782339) who is often credited with first demonstrating that many *quipus* encode numbers using a base-10 positional notation.[^o32][^o33] Starting in the late 1960's and building on Locke's foundational work, [Marcia Ascher](wikipedia://en/63298947) and [Robert Ascher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Ascher) analyzed several hundred *quipus*, revealing that most of the information recorded by *quipu* knots is numerical and can be systematically interpreted. Most *quipus* use three main types of knots: simple [overhand knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overhand_knot); "long knots", consisting of an overhand knot with one or more additional [turns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28knot%29); and [figure-eight knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Figure-eight_knot). The Aschers also identified a fourth, and less common, type of knot—a figure-eight knot with an extra twist—which they refer to as an "EE" knot. On a given *quipu* cord, knots are grouped into clusters. Each cluster is tied at specific registers, or lengths, along the cord. These knot clusters represent digits in a [base-10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base-10) number system.[^o34] The units, or "ones" position is commonly tied at the bottom of a cord, followed by a space above it, then the "tens" position, then another space, then hundreds position, and so on. In other words:

- Powers of ten are denoted by position along the string, and this position is often aligned between successive strands.
- Digits in positions for 10 and higher powers are represented by clusters of simple knots (e.g., 40 is four simple knots in a row in the "tens" position).
- Digits 2–9 in the "ones" position are represented by long knots (e.g., 4 is a knot with four turns), and the digit 1 in the "ones" position is represented by a figure-eight knot.
- [Zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero) is represented by the absence of a knot in the appropriate position.

For example, if 4s represents four simple knots, 3L represents a long knot with three turns, E represents a figure-eight knot, and X represents a space:

- The number 731 would be represented by 7s, 3s, E.
- The number 804 would be represented by 8s, X, 4L.
- The number 1493 would be represented by 1s, 4s, 9s, 3L.

Since the ones position on *quipu* cords are shown in a distinctive way (i.e., using long knots and figure-eight knots), it is usually clear where a number ends. Thus, it is possible that a single *quipu* cord could contain several numbers. For example:

- The number 107 followed by the number 51 would be represented by 1s, X, 7L, 5s, E.

The "reading" of *quipu* knots as numbers in the way outlined above is bolstered by the fact that *quipus* regularly contain sums in systematic ways.[^o32][^o33][^o35][^o36] For instance, a cord may contain the sum of the next *n* cords, with this relationship being repeated throughout the *quipu*. In other cases, there are even cords which contain sums of sums. Such a relationship would be highly improbable if *quipu* knot values were being incorrectly interpreted.

Some data items are not numbers but what Ascher and Ascher call *number labels*.[^o37] They are still composed of digits, but the resulting number seems to be used as a code. For example, Carrie J. Brezine hypothesized that a particular three-number label at the beginning of some *quipus* may refer to [Puruchuco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puruchuco), similar to a [ZIP code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZIP_code).[^o38]

### Non-numeric usage {#Non-numeric_usage}

Some have argued that far more than numeric information is present and that *quipus* are a [writing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system). This would be an especially important discovery as there is no surviving written record of the Inca Empire predating the [Spanish invasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_conquest_of_Peru).[^o6]

Making deciphering more complex, the Inca 'kept separate "khipu" for each province, on which a pendant string recorded the number of people belonging to each category.'[^o40][^o41]

How exactly non-numeric information is encoded is disputed, though scholars are working to collect and analysis khipu data beyond just their knot values, exploring other variables—such as cord twist, color[^o42], fiber, attachment orientation[^o26][^o27], and knot variations[^o43][^o44][^o45]—as signifiers of non-numerical data.

Historians Edward Hyams and George Ordish claim, for example, that quipus were recording devices, similar to musical notation, in that the notes on the page present basic information, and the performer would then bring those details to life.[^o46]

A 2005 report in the journal *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_%28journal%29),* titled "Khipu Accounting in Ancient Peru", may represent the first identification of a *quipu* element for a non-numeric concept. The report details a sequence of three figure-eight knots at the start of a *quipu* that seems to be a unique signifier. It could be a [toponym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toponym) for the city of [Puruchuco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puruchuco) (near [Lima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lima)), or the name of the *quipu* keeper who made it, or its subject matter, or even a time designator.[^o47]

*Quipucamayocs* (knot makers/keepers, i.e., the Inca record keepers) supplied colonial administrators with a variety and quantity of information pertaining to censuses, tribute, ritual and calendrical organization, genealogies, and other such matters from Inca times. One study led by Alberto Sáez-Rodríguez discovered that the distribution and patterning of S- and Z-knots can organize the information system from a real star map of the [Pleiades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pleiades) cluster.[^o48]

Laura Minelli, a professor of [pre-Columbian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Columbian) studies at the [University of Bologna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bologna), has discovered something which she claims to be a seventeenth-century [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit) manuscript that describes literary *quipus*, titled Historia et Rudimenta Linguae Piruanorum. This manuscript consists of nine [folios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folios) with Spanish, [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin), and ciphered Italian texts. Owned by the family of Neapolitan historian Clara Miccinelli, the manuscript also includes a wool *quipu* fragment. Miccinelli claims that the text was written by two Italian Jesuit missionaries, Joan Antonio Cumis and Giovanni Anello Oliva, around 1610–1638, and [Blas Valera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blas_Valera), a [mestizo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mestizo) Jesuit sometime before 1618. Along with the details of reading literary *quipus*, the documents also discuss the events and people of the Spanish conquest of [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru). According to Cumis, since so many *quipus* were burned by the Spanish, very few remained for him to analyze. As related in the manuscript, the word [Pacha Kamaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacha_Kamaq), the Inca deity of earth and time, was used many times in these *quipus*, where the syllables were represented by symbols formed in the knots. Following the analysis of the use of "Pacha Kamaq", the manuscript offers a list of many words present in *quipus*.[^o49] However, both Bruce Mannheim, the director of the Center for Latin American Studies at the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan), and [Colgate University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colgate_University)'s Gary Urton, question its origin and authenticity. These documents seem to be inspired freely by a 1751 writing of [Raimondo di Sangro, Prince of Sansevero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raimondo_di_Sangro).[^o50][^o51][^o52]

## History {#History}

### Possible proto-*quipus* {#Possible_proto-quipus}

Claims of the earliest *quipu,* or possible proto-*quipu,* comes from the [Late Preceramic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodization_of_pre-Columbian_Peru) (c. 3000–1800 BCE) site of [Caral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caral),[^o55][^o56] though this claim has yet to be thoroughly evaluated. A more plausible candidate for the earliest known precursor to *quipus* may be the wrapped batons found at the site of Cerrillos from the Late Paracas Period (c. 350–200 BCE).[^o57]

### Wari Empire {#Wari_Empire}

The first undisputed evidence of *quipu* technology dates back to the [Middle Horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Horizon) (c. 600–1000 CE),[^o59] with these early *quipus* being used by the [Wari Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wari_Empire). Differing slightly from their Inca successors, extant Wari *quipu* specimens tend to be smaller, have brightly colored thread wrapped cords, and their own system of knots which scholars do not fully understand.[^o60][^o61]

### Inca Empire {#Inca_Empire}

*Image: Quipu MKL Bd. 13 1890 (128905524).jpg.* Representation of a *quipu* (1890)

*Quipucamayocs* (Quechua *khipu kamayuq*, "khipu-authority"), the accountants of [Tawantin Suyu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tawantin_Suyu), created and deciphered the *quipu* knots. *Quipucamayocs* could carry out basic [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) operations, such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division. They kept track of [mita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mita_%28Inca%29), a form of [taxation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxation). The *quipucamayocs* also tracked the type of [labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labour_%28economics%29) being performed, maintained a record of [economic output](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_output), and ran a [census](wikipedia://en/6889) that counted everyone from infants to "old blind men over 80". The system was also used to keep track of the calendar. According to Guaman Poma, *quipucamayocs* could "read" the *quipus* with their eyes closed.[^o50]

*Quipucamayocs* were from a class of people, "males, fifty to sixty",[^o63] and were not the only members of [Inca society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca#Society) to use *quipus*. Inca [historians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historians) used *quipus* when telling the Spanish about Tawantin Suyu history (whether they only recorded important numbers or actually contained the story itself is unknown). Members of the ruling class were usually taught to read *quipus* in the Inca equivalent of a university, the *yachay wasi* (literally, "house of teaching"), in the third year of schooling, for the higher classes who would eventually become the bureaucracy.[^o64]

### Spanish Empire {#Spanish_Empire}

In 1532, the [Spanish Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Empire)'s conquest of the Andean region began, with several Spanish conquerors making note of the existence of *quipus* in their written records about the invasion. The earliest known example comes from [Hernando Pizarro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hernando_Pizarro), the brother of the Spanish military leader [Francisco Pizarro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francisco_Pizarro), who recorded an encounter that he and his men had in 1533 as they traveled along the royal road from the highlands to the central coast.[^o66] It was during this journey that they encountered several *quipu* keepers, later relating that these keepers "untied some of the knots which they had in the deposits section \[of the khipu\], and they \[re-\]tied them in another section \[of the khipu\]."[^o67][^o68][^o69][^o70]

Christian officials of the [Third Council of Lima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Third_Council_of_Lima) banned and ordered the burning of some q*uipus* in 1583 because they were used to record offerings to non-Christian gods and were therefore considered idolatrous objects and an obstacle to religious conversion.[^o71]

### Contemporary social importance {#Contemporary_social_importance}

The *quipu* system operated as both a method of calculation and social organization, regulating regional governance and land use.[^o73] While evidence for the latter is still under the critical eye of scholars around the world, the very fact that they are kept to this day without any confirmed level of fluent literacy in the system is testament to its historical 'moral authority.'[^o74] Today, "khipu" is regarded as a powerful symbol of heritage, only 'unfurled' and handled by 'pairs of \[contemporary\] dignitaries,' as the system and its 'construction embed' modern 'cultural knowledge.'[^o74] Ceremonies in which they are 'curated, even though they can no longer be read,' is even further support for the case of societal honor and significance associated with the *quipu*.[^o74] Even today, 'the knotted cords must be present and displayed when village officers leave or begin service, and draping the cords over the incoming office holders instantiates the moral and political authority of the past.'[^o74] These examples are indicative of how the *quipu* system was not only fundamental mathematically and linguistically for the original Inca, but also for cultural preservation of the original empire's descendants.

[Anthropologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropologists) and [archaeologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeologists) carrying out research in [Peru](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru) have highlighted two known cases where *quipus* have continued to be used by contemporary communities, albeit as ritual items seen as "communal patrimony" rather than as devices for recording information.[^o75] The *quipu* system, being the useful method of social management it was for the Inca, is also a link to the Cuzco census, as it was one of the primary methods of population calculation.[^o76] This also has allowed historians and anthropologists to understand both the census and the "decimal hierarchy" system the Inca used, and that they were actually 'initiated together,' due to the fact that they were 'conceptually so closely linked.'[^o76]

#### Tupicocha, Peru {#Tupicocha,_Peru}

In 1994, the American cultural anthropologist Frank Salomon conducted a study in the Peruvian village of [Tupicocha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Andrés_de_Tupicocha_District), where *quipus* are still an important part of the social life of the village.[^o78] As of 1994, this was the only known village where *quipus* with a structure similar to pre-Columbian *quipus* were still used for official local government record-keeping and functions, although the villagers did not associate their *quipus* with Inca artifacts.[^o79]

#### San Cristóbal de Rapaz, Peru {#San_Cristóbal_de_Rapaz,_Peru}

The villagers of San Cristóbal de Rapaz (known as Rapacinos), located in the [Province of Oyón](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyón_Province), keep a *quipu* in an old ceremonial building, the *Kaha Wayi*, that is itself surrounded by a walled architectural complex. Also within the complex is a disused communal storehouse, known as the *Pasa Qullqa*, which was formerly used to protect and redistribute the local crops, and some Rapacinos believe that the *quipu* was once a record of this process of collecting and redistributing food. The entire complex was important to the villagers, being "the seat of traditional control over land use, and the centre of communication with the deified mountains who control weather".[^o75]

In 2004, the archaeologist Renata Peeters (of the [UCL Institute of Archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCL_Institute_of_Archaeology) in London) and the cultural anthropologist Frank Salomon (of the [University of Wisconsin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin)) undertook a project to conserve both the *quipus* in Rapaz and the building that it was in, due to their increasingly poor condition.[^o81]

#### Jucul, Peru {#Jucul,_Peru}

The remote village of Jucul, Peru, has kept *quipus* in the attic of its colonial church for centuries, only recently being discovered by outsiders in 2024.[^o83] These *quipus* are closely related to those of San Cristóbal de Rapaz, which is near by.[^o84]

## Collections and number of *quipus* {#Collections_and_number_of_quipus}

The total number of quipus is unknown. Their whereabouts range from [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe) to [North](wikipedia://en/21139) and [South America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_America). Most are housed in museums outside of their native countries, but some reside in their native locations under the care of the descendants of those who made the knot records.

The archaeologist [Gary Urton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Urton) noted in his 2003 book *Signs of the Inka Khipu* that he estimated "from my own studies and from the published works of other scholars that there are about 600 extant *quipu* in public and private collections around the world."[^o86]

The Khipu Database Project was started by Urton and Carrie Brezine with funding from [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) and the [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation) with the aim of centralizing data about known quipus.[^o87][^o88] The project later became the Open Khipu Repository, which introduced a new "KH" numbering system to distinguish between *quipus*, moving away from naming systems based on archaeologists' names.[^o89] As of January 2026, the Open Khipu Repository records 702 *quipus* with publicly available data.[^o90]

A 2021 survey of both museum and private collection inventories places the total number of known extant pre-Columbian *quipus* at just under 1,400.[^o91]

## Preservation {#Preservation}

*Quipus* are made of [fibers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber), either spun and plied thread such as [wool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wool) or [hair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hair) from [alpaca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alpaca), [llama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Llama), [guanaco](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guanaco) or [vicuña](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vicuña_wool), though are also commonly made of [cellulose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellulose) like [cotton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton). Archaeological evidence has also shown that, in some cases, finely carved wood was used as a supplemental base to which the color-coded cords could be attached.[^o105] The [knotted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot) strings of *quipus* were often made with an "elaborate system of knotted cords, dyed in various colors, the significance of which was known to the [magistrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magistrates)".[^o106] Fading of [color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color), natural or dyed, cannot be reversed, and may indicate further damage to the fibers. Colors can darken if damaged by dust or by certain [dyes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyes) and [mordants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordants).[^o107] *Quipus* have been found with adornments, such as dried potatoes and beans, attached to the cords, and these non-textile materials may require additional preservation measures.[^o108]

*Quipus* are now preserved using techniques that aim to minimize their future degradation. [Museums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum), [archives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archive) and special collections have adopted preservation guidelines from [textile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile) practices.[^o109]

Environmental controls are used to monitor and control [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050), [humidity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humidity) and [light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) exposure of storage areas. As with all textiles, cool, clean, dry and dark environments are most suitable. The heating, ventilating and air conditioning, or [HVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HVAC) systems, of buildings that house *quipu* knot records are usually automatically regulated. Relative humidity should be 60% or lower, with low temperatures, as high temperatures can damage the fibres and make them brittle. Damp conditions and high humidity can damage protein-rich material. [Textiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textiles) suffer damage from [ultraviolet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet) (UV) light, which can include fading and weakening of the fibrous material. When *quipus* are on display, their exposure to ambient conditions is usually minimized and closely monitored.[^o107][^o110]

Despite best efforts, damage can occur during storage, or be from the result of earlier conservation efforts.[^o111] The more accessible the items are during storage, the greater the chance of early detection.[^o110] Storing *quipus* horizontally on boards covered with a neutral [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) paper (paper that is neither [acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid) or [alkaline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkaline)) to prevent potential acid transfer is a preservation technique that extends the life of a collection. The fibers can be abraded by rubbing against each other or, for those attached to sticks or rods, by their own weight if held in an upright position. Extensive handling of *quipus* can also increase the risk of further damage.[^o112]

*Quipus* are also closely monitored for [mold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mold_%28fungus%29), as well as [insects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insect) and their [larvae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larva). As with all textiles, these are major problems. [Fumigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fumigation) may not be recommended for fiber textiles displaying mold or insect [infestations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ectoparasitic_infestation), although it is common practice for ridding [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper) of mold and insects.

Conservators in the field of [library science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_science) have the skills to handle a variety of situations. Even though some *quipus* have hundreds of cords, each cord should be assessed and treated individually. *Quipu* cords can be "mechanically cleaned with brushes, small tools and light vacuuming".[^o113] Just as the application of [fungicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fungicides) is not recommended to rid *quipus* of mold, neither is the use of [solvents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvents) to clean them.

Even when people have tried to preserve *quipus*, corrective care may still be required. If *quipus* are to be conserved close to their place of origin, local camelid or wool fibres in natural colors can be obtained and used to mend breaks and splits in the cords.[^o113] Rosa Choque Gonzales and Rosalia Choque Gonzales, conservators from southern Peru, worked to conserve the Rapaz patrimonial *quipus* in the Andean village of Rapaz, Peru. These *quipus* had undergone repair in the past, so this conservator team used new local camelid and wool fibers to spin around the area under repair in a similar fashion to the earlier repairs found on the *quipu*.[^o113]

When Gary Urton, professor of [Anthropology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropology) at Harvard, was asked "Are they \[*quipus*\] fragile?", he answered, "some of them are, and you can't touch them – they would break or turn into dust. Many are quite well preserved, and you can actually study them without doing them any harm. Of course, any time you touch an ancient fabric like that, you're doing some damage, but these strings are generally quite durable."[^o114]

[Ruth Shady](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Shady), a [Peruvian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peru) [archeologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archeologist), has discovered what she argues resembles a proto-*quipu* dating to approximately 5,000 years old in the coastal city of [Caral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caral). It was in quite good condition, with "brown cotton strings wound around thin sticks", along with "a series of offerings, including mysterious fiber balls of different sizes wrapped in 'nets' and pristine reed baskets. Piles of raw cotton – uncombed and containing seeds, though turned a dirty brown by the ages – and a ball of cotton thread" were also found preserved. The good condition of these articles can be attributed to the [arid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arid) climate of Caral.[^o115]
