# Edward Tufte

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Edward Tufte"}

**Edward Rolf Tufte** (;[^o4] born March 14, 1942),[^o5] sometimes known as "**ET**",[^o6] is an American [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661) and [professor emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_emeritus) of [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science), [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University).[^o7] He is noted for his writings on [information design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_design) and as a pioneer in the field of [data visualization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_visualization).[^o8]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Edward Rolf Tufte was born in 1942 in [Kansas City, Missouri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kansas_City,_Missouri), to [Virginia Tufte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virginia_Tufte) (1918–2020) and Edward E. Tufte (1912–1999). He grew up in [Beverly Hills, California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beverly_Hills,_California), where his father was a longtime city official. He graduated from the public [Beverly Hills High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beverly_Hills_High_School).[^o10]

Tufte received a BS and a MS in statistics from [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University), and a Doctor of Philosophy in political science from [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University).[^o17] His dissertation was completed in 1968 and titled *The Civil Rights Movement and Its Opposition*.[^o11]

## Career {#Career}

Tufte was hired in 1967 by the [Woodrow Wilson School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodrow_Wilson_School) of [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) as a lecturer in politics and public affairs, where he steadily moved up to the rank of full Professor in 1972.[^o13] He taught courses there in [political economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_economy) and [data analysis](wikipedia://en/2720954) while publishing three quantitatively inclined political science books.

In 1975, while at Princeton, Tufte was asked to teach a statistics course to a group of journalists who were visiting the school to study economics. He developed a set of readings and lectures on [statistical graphics](wikipedia://en/15934463), which he further developed in joint seminars he taught with renowned statistician [John Tukey](wikipedia://en/415974), a pioneer in the field of information design. These course materials became the foundation for Tufte's first book on information design, *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information*.[^o14][^n1]

In 1977, Tufte left Princeton University for Yale University, where he accepted an appointment as Professor of [Political Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science), [Statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), and [Computer Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), as well as a Senior Critic at the [Yale School of Art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_School_of_Art).[^o16]

After negotiations with major publishers failed, Tufte decided to self-publish the book *The Visual Display of Quantitative Information* in 1982, working closely with graphic designer Howard Gralla. Tufte financed the work by taking out a [second mortgage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_mortgage) on his home. The book quickly became a commercial success and secured Tufte's transition from political scientist to information expert.[^o14]

In 1999, after 22 years of service at Yale University, his professorship at Yale was made [Emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emeritus).[^o16]

On March 5, 2010, President [Barack Obama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barack_Obama) appointed Tufte to the [American Recovery and Reinvestment Act's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Recovery_and_Reinvestment_Act_of_2009) Recovery Independent Advisory Panel "to provide transparency in the use of Recovery-related funds".[^o17]

## Infographic work {#Infographic_work}

Tufte is an expert in the presentation of [infographics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infographic) such as [charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chart) and [diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagram), and is a fellow of the [American Statistical Association](wikipedia://en/341988). He has held fellowships from the [Guggenheim Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solomon_R._Guggenheim_Foundation) and the [Center for Advanced Study in the Behavioral Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_for_Advanced_Study_in_the_Behavioral_Sciences).

### Information design {#Information_design}

*Image: Minard.png.* Tufte described [Charles Joseph Minard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Joseph_Minard)'s 1869 graphic of [Napoleonic France's invasion of Russia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_invasion_of_Russia) as what "may well be the best statistical graphic ever drawn", noting that it captures six variables in two dimensions.[^o20]

Tufte's writing is important in such fields as [information design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_design) and [visual literacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visual_literacy), which deal with the visual communication of information. He coined the word *[chartjunk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chartjunk)* to refer to useless, non-informative, or information-obscuring elements of quantitative information displays. Tufte's other key concepts include what he calls the *lie factor*, the *data-ink ratio*, and the *data density* of a graphic.[^o21]

Tufte uses the term "data-ink ratio" to argue against using excessive decoration in visual displays of quantitative information.[^o22] In *Visual Display*, Tufte explains, "Sometimes decoration can help editorialize about the substance of the graphic. But it is wrong to distort the data measures—the ink locating values of numbers—in order to make an editorial comment or fit a decorative scheme."[^n2]

Tufte encourages the use of data-rich [illustrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illustration) that present all available data. When such illustrations are examined closely, every [data point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_point) has a value, but when they are looked at more generally, only trends and patterns can be observed. Tufte suggests these macro/micro readings be presented in the space of an eye-span, in the high resolution format of the printed page, and at the unhurried pace of the viewer's leisure.

Tufte uses several historical examples to make his case. These include [John Snow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Snow_%28physician%29)'s cholera outbreak map, [Charles Joseph Minard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Joseph_Minard)'s *Carte Figurative*, early [space debris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_debris) plots, [Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374)'s *[Sidereus Nuncius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius)*, and [Maya Lin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_Lin)'s [Vietnam Veterans Memorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vietnam_Veterans_Memorial). For instance, the listing of the names of deceased soldiers on the black granite of Lin's sculptural memorial is shown to be more powerful as a chronological list rather than as an alphabetical one. The sacrifice each fallen individual has made is thus highlighted within the overall time scope of the war.[^n3] In *Sidereus Nuncius*, Galileo presents the nightly observations of the moons of Jupiter in relation to the body itself, interwoven with the two-month narrative record.[^o25]

### Criticism of PowerPoint {#Criticism_of_PowerPoint}

Tufte has criticized the way [Microsoft PowerPoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_PowerPoint) is typically used. In his essay "The Cognitive Style of PowerPoint", Tufte criticizes many aspects of the software:

- Its use as a way to guide and reassure a presenter, rather than to enlighten the audience;
- Its unhelpfully simplistic tables and charts, a design decision holdover from the low resolution of early computer displays;
- The [outliner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outliner)'s causing ideas to be arranged in an artificially deep hierarchy, itself subverted by the need to restate the hierarchy on each slide;
- Enforcement of the audience's lockstep linear progression through that hierarchy (whereas with handouts, readers could browse and relate items at their leisure);
- Poor [typography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typography) and chart layout, from presenters who are poor designers or who use poorly designed templates and default settings (in particular, difficulty in using [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715));
- Simplistic thinking—from ideas being squashed into bulleted lists; and stories with a beginning, middle, and end being turned into a collection of disparate, loosely disguised points—presenting a misleading façade of objectivity and neutrality that people associate with science, technology, and "bullet points".

Tufte cites the way PowerPoint was used by [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA) engineers in the events leading to the [Space Shuttle Columbia disaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Shuttle_Columbia_disaster) as an example of PowerPoint's many problems. The software style is designed to [persuade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persuade) rather than to inform people of technical details. Tufte's analysis of a NASA PowerPoint slide is included in the [Columbia Accident Investigation Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_Accident_Investigation_Board)’s report -- including an engineering detail buried in small type on a crowded slide with six bullet points, that if presented in a regular engineering [white paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/White_paper), might have been noticed and the disaster prevented.[^o28][^o29]

Instead, Tufte argues that the most effective way of presenting information in a technical setting, such as an academic seminar or a meeting of industry experts, is by distributing a brief written report that can be read by all participants in the first 5 to 10 minutes of the meeting. Tufte believes that this is the most efficient method of transferring knowledge from the presenter to the audience and then the rest of the meeting is devoted to discussion and debate.[^o28]

### Small multiple {#Small_multiple}

One method Tufte encourages to allow quick visual comparison of multiple series is the [small multiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_multiple), a chart with many series shown on a single pair of axes that can often be easier to read when displayed as several separate pairs of axes placed next to each other. He suggests this is particularly helpful when the series are measured on quite different vertical (*y*-axis) scales, but over the same range on the horizontal *x*-axis (usually time).

### Sparkline {#Sparkline}

*Image: Screenshot of Sparklines in Medved QuoteTracker, 1998.png.* Earliest known implementation of sparklines, around January 1998 by interaction designer Peter Zelchenko for programmer Michael Medved and the QuoteTracker application (sold to TD Ameritrade).

[Sparklines](wikipedia://en/1743077) are a condensed way to present trends and variation, associated with a measurement such as average [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) or [stock market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock_market) activity, often embedded directly in the text; for example: The Dow Jones index for February 7, 2006

*Image: Sparkline dowjones new.svg.*

.[^o32][^o33] These are often used as elements of a [small multiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_multiple) with several lines used together. Tufte explains the sparkline as a kind of "word" that conveys rich information without breaking the flow of a sentence or paragraph made of other "words" both visual and conventional. To date, the earliest known implementation of sparklines was conceived by interaction designer Peter Zelchenko and implemented by programmer Mike Medved in early 1998.[^o34]

## Sculpture {#Sculpture}

Beyond his academic endeavors over the years, Tufte has created sculptures, often large outdoor ones made of metal or stone,[^o10] that were first primarily exhibited on his own rural [Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connecticut) property. In 2009–10, some of these artworks were exhibited at the [Aldrich Contemporary Art Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldrich_Contemporary_Art_Museum) in [Ridgefield, Connecticut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ridgefield,_Connecticut), in the one-man show *Edward Tufte: Seeing Around*.[^o36][^o37]

### Hogpen Hill Farms {#Hogpen_Hill_Farms}

Hogpen Hill Farms, the 234 acre Tufte [sculpture garden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture_garden) in Woodbury, Connecticut, is open to the public on summer weekends.[^o39]

### ET Modern {#ET_Modern}

In 2010, Edward Tufte opened a gallery, ET Modern, in [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City)'s [Chelsea Art District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chelsea_Art_District)[^o6] at 11th Avenue and 20th Street.[^o41] The gallery closed in 2013.[^o42]
