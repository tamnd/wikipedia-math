# Synthetic geometry

branches

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Synthetic geometry"}

**Synthetic geometry** (sometimes referred to as **axiomatic geometry** or even **pure geometry**) is [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) without the use of [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates). It relies on the [axiomatic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_method) for proving all results from a few basic properties, initially called [postulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postulate) and at present called [axioms](wikipedia://en/928).

:::

After the 17th-century introduction by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) of the coordinate method, which was called [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), the term "synthetic geometry" was coined to refer to the older methods that were, before Descartes, the only known ones.

According to [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887), [^o2]

The first systematic approach to synthetic geometry is [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107). However, it appeared at the end of the 19th century that [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s postulates were not sufficient for characterizing geometry. The first complete [axiom system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_system) for geometry was given only at the end of the 19th century by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302). At the same time, it appeared that both synthetic methods and analytic methods can be used to build geometry. The fact that the two approaches are equivalent has been proved by [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) in his book *[Geometric Algebra](wikipedia://en/31102294)*.

Because of this equivalence, the distinction between synthetic and analytic geometry is no more in use, except at elementary level, or for geometries that are not related to any sort of numbers, such as some [finite geometries](wikipedia://en/267061) and [non-Desarguesian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Desarguesian_geometry).

## Logical synthesis {#Logical_synthesis}

The process of logical synthesis begins with some arbitrary but definite starting point. This starting point is the introduction of primitive notions or primitives and axioms about these primitives:

- [Primitives](wikipedia://en/23706) are the most basic ideas. Typically they include both objects and relationships. In geometry, the objects are things such as points, lines and planes, while a fundamental relationship is that of incidence – of one object meeting or joining with another. The terms themselves are undefined. [Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) once remarked that instead of points, lines and planes one might just as well talk of tables, chairs and beer mugs,[^o4] the point being that the primitive terms are just empty [placeholders](wikipedia://en/147460) and have no intrinsic properties.
- [Axioms](wikipedia://en/928) are statements about these primitives; for example, any two points are together incident with just one line (i.e., that for any two points, there is just one line which passes through both of them). Axioms are assumed true, and not proven. They are the building blocks of geometric concepts, since they specify the properties that the primitives have.

From a given [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of axioms, synthesis proceeds as a carefully constructed logical argument. When a significant result is proved rigorously, it becomes a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977).

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="Properties of axiom sets"}

### Properties of axiom sets {#Properties_of_axiom_sets}

There is no fixed axiom set for geometry, as more than one [consistent set](wikipedia://en/75802) can be chosen. Each such set may lead to a different geometry, while there are also examples of different sets giving the same geometry. With this plethora of possibilities, it is no longer appropriate to speak of "geometry" in the singular.

Historically, Euclid's [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) has turned out to be [independent](wikipedia://en/1141208) of the other axioms. Simply discarding it gives [absolute geometry](wikipedia://en/699294), while negating it yields [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291). Other [consistent axiom sets](wikipedia://en/75802) can yield other geometries, such as [projective](wikipedia://en/243849), [elliptic](wikipedia://en/236020), [spherical](wikipedia://en/174026) or [affine](wikipedia://en/243890) geometry.

Axioms of continuity and "betweenness" are also optional; for example, [discrete geometries](wikipedia://en/386468) may be created by discarding or modifying them.

Following the [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382) of [Klein](wikipedia://en/41887), the nature of any given geometry can be seen as the connection between [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) and the content of the propositions, rather than the style of development.

:::

## History {#History}

Euclid's original treatment remained unchallenged for over two thousand years, until the simultaneous discoveries of the non-Euclidean geometries by [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125), [Bolyai](wikipedia://en/98912), [Lobachevsky](wikipedia://en/648527) and [Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) in the 19th century led mathematicians to question Euclid's underlying assumptions.[^o8]

The early French analyst [Sylvestre François Lacroix](wikipedia://en/2289727) (1816) summarized synthetic geometry this way:

The heyday of synthetic geometry is considered to have been the 19th century, when analytic methods based on [coordinates](wikipedia://en/81931) and [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) were ignored by some [geometers](wikipedia://en/161339) such as [Jakob Steiner](wikipedia://en/567445), in favor of a purely synthetic development of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849). For example, the treatment of the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) starting from axioms of incidence is actually a broader theory (with more [models](wikipedia://en/19858)) than is found by starting with a third dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). Projective geometry has in fact the simplest and most elegant synthetic expression of any geometry.[^o12]

In his [Erlangen program](wikipedia://en/243382), [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) played down the tension between synthetic and analytic methods:

The close axiomatic study of [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) led to the construction of the [Lambert quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3515519) and the [Saccheri quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/3818958). These structures introduced the field of [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) where Euclid's parallel axiom is denied. [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss), [Bolyai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolyai) and [Lobachevski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobachevski) independently constructed [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), where parallel lines have an [angle of parallelism](wikipedia://en/1341579) that depends on their separation. This study became widely accessible through the [Poincaré disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poincaré_disc) model where [motions](wikipedia://en/36132944) are given by [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493). Similarly, [Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980), a student of Gauss's, constructed [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243), of which [elliptic geometry](wikipedia://en/236020) is a particular case.

Another example concerns [inversive geometry](wikipedia://en/295844) as advanced by [Ludwig Immanuel Magnus](wikipedia://en/340313), which can be considered synthetic in spirit. The closely related operation of [reciprocation](wikipedia://en/229940) expresses analysis of the plane.

[Karl von Staudt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_von_Staudt) showed that algebraic axioms, such as [commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) and [associativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associativity) of addition and multiplication, were in fact consequences of [incidence](wikipedia://en/387224) of lines in [geometric configurations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_configuration). [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) showed[^o11] that the [Desargues configuration](wikipedia://en/7209841) played a special role. Further work was done by [Ruth Moufang](wikipedia://en/2003831) and her students. The concepts have been one of the motivators of [incidence geometry](wikipedia://en/1576323).

When [parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines) are taken as primary, synthesis produces [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890). Though Euclidean geometry is both an affine and [metric geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_geometry), in general [affine spaces](wikipedia://en/298834) may be missing a metric. The extra flexibility thus afforded makes affine geometry appropriate for the study of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758), as discussed in the [history of affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890#History).

## Proofs using synthetic geometry {#Proofs_using_synthetic_geometry}

Synthetic proofs of geometric theorems make use of auxiliary constructs (such as [helping lines](wikipedia://en/40054744)) and concepts such as equality of sides or angles, and [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377) and [congruence](wikipedia://en/39330) of triangles. Examples include the [Butterfly theorem](wikipedia://en/1335536), [Angle bisector theorem](wikipedia://en/2401128), [Apollonius' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollonius'_theorem), [British flag theorem](wikipedia://en/8300841), [Ceva's theorem](wikipedia://en/152428), [Equal incircles theorem](wikipedia://en/17538757), [Geometric mean theorem](wikipedia://en/37981515), [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123), [Isosceles triangle theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isosceles_triangle_theorem), and [Law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890).

## Computational synthetic geometry {#Computational_synthetic_geometry}

In conjunction with [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), a **computational synthetic geometry** has been founded, having close connection, for example, with [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) theory. [Synthetic differential geometry](wikipedia://en/3869427) is an application of [topos](wikipedia://en/21391464) theory to the foundations of [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) theory.
