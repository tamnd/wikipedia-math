# Evanescent field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Evanescent field"}

*Image: Electron density wave - plasmon excitations.png.* Schematic representation of a [surface wave](wikipedia://en/41763) ([surface plasmon polariton](wikipedia://en/30868906)) propagating along a metal-dielectric interface. The fields away from the surface die off exponentially (right hand graph) and those fields are thus described as *evanescent* in the *z* direction

In [electromagnetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetics), an **evanescent field**, or **evanescent wave**, is an oscillating electric and/or magnetic field that does not propagate as an [electromagnetic wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_wave) but whose energy is spatially concentrated in the vicinity of the source (oscillating charges and currents). Even when there is a propagating electromagnetic wave produced (e.g., by a transmitting [antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_%28radio%29)), one can still identify as an evanescent field the component of the electric or magnetic field that cannot be attributed to the propagating wave observed at a distance of many wavelengths (such as the [far field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Far_field) of a transmitting antenna).

:::

A hallmark of an evanescent field is that there is no net energy flow in that region. Since the net flow of electromagnetic energy is given by the average [Poynting vector](wikipedia://en/49718), this means that the Poynting vector in these regions, as averaged over a complete oscillation cycle, is zero.

## Use of the term {#Use_of_the_term}

In many cases one cannot simply say that a field is or is not "evanescent" – having the Poynting vector average to zero in some direction (or all directions). In most cases where they exist, evanescent fields are simply thought of and referred to the same as all other electric or magnetic fields involved, without any special recognition of those fields' evanescence. The term's use is mostly limited to distinguishing a part of a field or solution in those cases where one might only expect the fields of a propagating wave.

For instance, in the illustration at the top of the article, energy is indeed carried in the horizontal direction. However, in the vertical direction, the field strength drops off exponentially with increasing distance above the surface. This leaves most of the field concentrated in a thin boundary layer very close to the interface; for that reason, it is referred to as a *[surface wave](wikipedia://en/41763)*.[^o3] However, despite energy flowing horizontally, along the vertical there is *no* net propagation of energy *away* from (or toward) the surface, so that one could properly describe the field as being "evanescent in the vertical direction". This is one example of the context dependence of the term.

Everyday electronic devices and electrical appliances are surrounded by large fields which are evanescent; their operation involves alternating voltages (producing an electric field between them) and alternating currents (producing a magnetic field around them) which are expected to only carry power along internal wires, but not to the outsides of the devices. Even though the term "evanescent" is not mentioned in this ordinary context, the appliances' designers still may be concerned with *maintaining* evanescence, in order to prevent or limit production of a propagating electromagnetic wave, which would lead to [radiation loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current#Techniques_for_reducing_radiation_loss), since a propagating wave "steals" its power from the circuitry or donates unwanted [interference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_interference).

The term "evanescent field" *does* arise in various contexts where a propagating electromagnetic wave is involved (even if confined). The term then differentiates electromagnetic field components that accompany the propagating wave, but which do not themselves propagate. In other, similar cases, where a propagating electromagnetic wave would normally be expected (such as light [refracted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracted) at the interface between glass and air), the term is invoked to describe that part of the field where the wave is suppressed (such as light traveling through glass, impinging on a glass-to-air interface but beyond the [critical angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_angle_%28optics%29)).

Although all electromagnetic fields are classically governed according to [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737), different technologies or problems have certain types of expected solutions, and when the primary solutions involve wave propagation the term *evanescent* is frequently applied to field components or solutions which do not share the propagation property.

For instance, the [propagation constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propagation_constant) of a hollow metal [waveguide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waveguide) is a strong function of frequency (a [dispersion relation](wikipedia://en/853778)). Below a certain frequency (the [cut-off frequency](wikipedia://en/40986)) the propagation constant becomes an imaginary number. A solution to the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) having an imaginary wavenumber does *not* propagate as a wave but falls off exponentially, so the field excited at that lower frequency is considered evanescent. It can also be simply said that propagation is "disallowed" for that frequency.

The formal solution to the wave equation can describe modes having an identical form, but the change of the propagation constant from real to imaginary as the frequency drops below the cut-off frequency totally changes the physical nature of the result. The solution may be described as a "cut-off mode" or an "evanescent mode";[^o4][^o5] while a different author will just state that no such mode exists. Since the evanescent field corresponding to the mode was computed as a solution to the wave equation, it is often discussed as being an "evanescent wave" even though its properties (such as carrying no energy) are inconsistent with the definition of [wave](wikipedia://en/33516).

Although this article concentrates on electromagnetics, the term *evanescent* is used similarly in fields such as [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics) and [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), where the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691) arises from the physics involved. In these cases, solutions to the wave equation resulting in imaginary propagation constants are likewise called "evanescent", and have the essential property that no net energy is transferred, even though there is a non-zero field.

## Evanescent wave applications {#Evanescent_wave_applications}

In [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics) and [acoustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acoustics), evanescent waves are formed when waves traveling in a medium undergo [total internal reflection](wikipedia://en/30426) at its boundary because they strike it at an angle greater than the *[critical angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_angle_%28optics%29)*.[^o7][^o8] The physical explanation for the existence of the evanescent wave is that the electric and magnetic fields (or [pressure gradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure_gradients), in the case of acoustical waves) cannot be discontinuous at a boundary, as would be the case if there was no evanescent wave field. In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the physical explanation is exactly analogous—the [Schrödinger wave-function](wikipedia://en/145343) representing particle motion normal to the boundary cannot be discontinuous at the boundary.

Electromagnetic evanescent waves have been used to exert optical [radiation pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_pressure) on small particles to trap them for experimentation, or to [cool](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refrigeration) them to very low temperatures, and to illuminate very small objects such as [biological cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biological_cell) or [single protein and DNA molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single-molecule_experiment) for [microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopy) (as in the [total internal reflection fluorescence microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_internal_reflection_fluorescence_microscope)). The evanescent wave from an [optical fiber](wikipedia://en/3372377) can be used in a gas sensor, and evanescent waves figure in the [infrared spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infrared_spectroscopy) technique known as [attenuated total reflectance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Attenuated_total_reflectance). In x-ray and neutron [grazing-incidence small-angle scattering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grazing-incidence_small-angle_scattering), evanescent waves lead to strongly enhanced scattering from a thin layer ([Yoneda peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoneda_peak)), which can be used for calibration.

In [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering), evanescent waves are found in the [near-field region](wikipedia://en/9426#Near_and_far_fields) within one third of a wavelength of any radio antenna. During normal operation, an antenna emits electromagnetic fields into the surrounding nearfield region, and a portion of the field energy is reabsorbed, while the remainder is radiated as EM waves.

Recently, a graphene-based Bragg grating (one-dimensional [photonic crystal](wikipedia://en/355377)) has been fabricated and demonstrated its competence for excitation of surface electromagnetic waves in the periodic structure using a [prism coupling technique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_coupler).[^o9]

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the evanescent-wave solutions of the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) give rise to the phenomenon of [wave-mechanical tunneling](wikipedia://en/643769).

In [microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopy), systems that capture the information contained in evanescent waves can be used to create [super-resolution images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Super-resolution_microscopy). Matter radiates both propagating and evanescent electromagnetic waves. Conventional optical systems capture only the information in the propagating waves and hence are subject to the [diffraction limit](wikipedia://en/376885). Systems that capture the information contained in evanescent waves, such as the [superlens](wikipedia://en/3088223) and [near field scanning optical microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Near_field_scanning_optical_microscopy), can overcome the diffraction limit; however these systems are then limited by the system's ability to accurately capture the evanescent waves.[^o10] The limitation on their resolution is given by

$$k \propto \frac{1}{d} \ln \frac{1}{\delta},$$

where $k$ is the maximal [wave vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_vector) that can be resolved, $d$ is the distance between the object and the sensor, and $\delta$ is a measure of the [quality](wikipedia://en/229160) of the sensor.

More generally, practical applications of evanescent waves can be classified as (1) those in which the energy associated with the wave is used to excite some other phenomenon within the region of space where the original traveling wave becomes evanescent (for example, as in the [total internal reflection fluorescence microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_internal_reflection_fluorescence_microscope)) or (2) those in which the evanescent wave couples two media in which traveling waves are allowed, and hence permits the transfer of energy or a particle between the media (depending on the wave equation in use), even though no traveling-wave solutions are allowed in the region of space between the two media. An example of this is *[wave-mechanical tunnelling](wikipedia://en/643769)*, and is known generally as *[evanescent wave coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evanescent_wave_coupling)*.

## Total internal reflection of light {#Total_internal_reflection_of_light}

*Image: Total internal reflection.jpg.* [Total internal reflection](wikipedia://en/30426)

*Image: Evanescent wave.jpg.* Representation of a (top) [refracted incident wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction) and (bottom) evanescent wave at an interface in red (reflected waves omitted).

For example, consider [total internal reflection](wikipedia://en/30426) in two dimensions, with the interface between the media lying on the x axis, the [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_normal) along y, and the [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564) along z. One might expect that for angles leading to total internal reflection, the solution would consist of an incident wave and a reflected wave, with no transmitted wave at all, but there is no such solution that obeys [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737). Maxwell's equations in a dielectric medium impose a boundary condition of continuity for the components of the fields *E<sub>\||</sub>, H<sub>\||</sub>, D<sub>y</sub>*, and *B<sub>y</sub>*. For the polarization considered in this example, the conditions on *E<sub>\||</sub>* and *B<sub>y</sub>* are satisfied if the reflected wave has the same amplitude as the incident one, because these components of the incident and reflected waves superimpose destructively. Their *H<sub>x</sub>* components, however, superimpose constructively, so there can be no solution without a non-vanishing transmitted wave. The transmitted wave cannot, however, be a sinusoidal wave, since it would then transport energy away from the boundary, but since the incident and reflected waves have equal energy, this would violate [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088). We therefore conclude that the transmitted wave must be a non-vanishing solution to Maxwell's equations that is not a traveling wave, and the only such solutions in a dielectric are those that decay exponentially: evanescent waves.

Mathematically, evanescent waves can be characterized by a [wave vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_vector) where one or more of the vector's components has an [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) value. Because the vector has imaginary components, it may have a magnitude that is less than its real components.

For the plane of incidence as the $xy$ plane at $z = 0$ and the interface of the two mediums as the $xz$ plane at $y=0$, the wave vector of the transmitted wave has the form[^o12]

$$\mathbf{k_t} \ = \ k_y \hat{\mathbf{y}} + k_x \hat{\mathbf{x}}$$

with $k_x = k_t \sin \theta_t$ and $k_y = k_t \cos \theta_t$, where $k_t$ is the magnitude of the wave vector of the transmitted wave (so the [wavenumber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber)), $\theta_t$ is the angle of refraction, and $\hat{\mathbf{x}}$ and $\hat{\mathbf{y}}$ are the unit vectors along the $x$ axis direction and the $y$ axis direction respectively.

By using the [Snell's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snell's_law) $n_i \sin \theta_i = n_t \sin \theta_t$ where $n_i$, $n_t$, and $\theta_i$ are the refractive index of the medium where the incident wave and the reflected wave exist, the refractive index of the medium where the transmitted wave exists, and the angle of incidence respectively,

$$k_y =k_t \cos \theta_t = \pm k_t \left (1 - \frac{\sin^2 \theta_i}{n_{ti}^2}\right)^{1/2}.$$

with $n_{ti} = \frac{n_t}{n_i}$.

If a part of the condition of the [total internal reflection](wikipedia://en/30426) as $\sin \theta_i > \sin \theta_c = n_{ti}$, is satisfied, then

$$k_y = \pm i k_t \left( \frac{\sin^2 \theta_i}{n_{ti}^2} - 1\right)^{1/2} = \pm i \alpha.$$

If the [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564) is perpendicular to the plane of incidence (along the $z$ direction), then the electric field of any of the waves (incident, reflected, or transmitted) can be expressed as

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{r},t) = \operatorname{Re} \left \{ E(\mathbf{r}) e^{ i \omega t } \right \} \mathbf{\hat{z}}$$

where $\mathbf{\hat{z}}$ is the [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) in the $z$ axis direction.

By assuming plane waves as $E(\mathbf{r}) = E_0 e^{-i\mathbf{k}\cdot \mathbf{r}}$, and substituting the transmitted wave vector $\mathbf{k_t}$ into $\mathbf{k}$, we find for the transmitted wave:

$$E(\mathbf{r}) = E_o e^{-i ( -i \alpha y + \beta x ) } = E_o e^{-\alpha y - i \beta x }$$

where $\alpha = k_t \left( \frac{\sin^2 \theta_i}{n_{ti}^2} - 1\right)^{1/2}$ is the [attenuation constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propagation_constant#Attenuation_constant), and $\beta = k_x$ is the [phase constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_constant). $+i \alpha$ is ignored since it does not physically make sense (the wave amplification along *y* the direction in this case).

## Evanescent-wave coupling {#Evanescent-wave_coupling}

*Image: FITR penetration depth.svg.* Plot of 1/e-penetration depth of the evanescent wave against angle of incidence in units of wavelength for different refraction indices.

Especially in [optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics), *evanescent-wave coupling* refers to the coupling between two waves due to physical overlap of what would otherwise be described as the evanescent fields corresponding to the propagating waves.[^o14]

One classical example is [frustrated total internal reflection (FTIR)](wikipedia://en/30426#FTIR_%28Frustrated_Total_Internal_Reflection%29) in which the evanescent field very close (see graph) to the surface of a dense medium at which a wave normally undergoes [total internal reflection](wikipedia://en/30426) overlaps another dense medium in the vicinity. This disrupts the totality of the reflection, diverting some power into the second medium.

Coupling between two [optical waveguides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_waveguide) may be effected by placing the fiber cores close together so that the evanescent field generated by one element excites a wave in the other fiber. This is used to produce [fiber-optic splitters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber-optic_splitter) and in [fiber tapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_tapping). At radio (and even optical) frequencies, such a device is called a [directional coupler.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_dividers_and_directional_couplers#Directional_couplers) The device is usually called a power divider in the case of microwave transmission and modulation.

Evanescent-wave coupling is synonymous with [near field](wikipedia://en/271708) interaction in electromagnetic field theory. Depending on the nature of the source element, the evanescent field involved is either predominantly electric (capacitive) or magnetic (inductive), unlike (propagating) waves in the far field where these components are connected (identical phase, in the ratio of the [impedance of free space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impedance_of_free_space)). The evanescent wave coupling takes place in the non-radiative field near each medium and as such is always associated with matter; i.e., with the induced currents and charges within a partially reflecting surface. In quantum mechanics the wave function interaction may be discussed in terms of particles and described as [quantum tunneling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_tunneling).

### Applications {#Applications}

Evanescent wave coupling is commonly used in photonic and nanophotonic devices as waveguide sensors or couplers (see e.g., [prism coupler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_coupler)).[^o17]

Evanescent wave coupling is used to excite, for example, dielectric microsphere resonators.

Evanescent coupling, as near field interaction, is one of the concerns in [electromagnetic compatibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_compatibility).

Coupling of optical fibers without loss for [fiber tapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_tapping).

Evanescent wave coupling plays a major role in the theoretical explanation of [extraordinary optical transmission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extraordinary_optical_transmission).[^o18]

Evanescent wave coupling is used in powering devices wirelessly.[^o19][^o20][^o21]

A [total internal reflection fluorescence microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_internal_reflection_fluorescence_microscope) uses the evanescent wave produced by [total internal reflection](wikipedia://en/30426) to excite fluorophores close to a surface. This is useful when surface properties of biological samples need to be studied.[^o22]
