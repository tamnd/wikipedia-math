# Clipping (signal processing)

*Image: Clipping 1KHz 10V DIV clip A 5ohms-1-.jpg.* An [oscilloscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscilloscope) screen of an amplifier clipping. The amplifier should be outputting a clean [sine wave](wikipedia://en/324749) with rounded tops and bottoms, but instead they are cut off flat, or *clipped*.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Clipping"}

**Clipping** is a form of [distortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distortion) that limits a [signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal) once it exceeds a threshold. Clipping may occur when a signal is recorded by a [sensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor) that has constraints on the range of data it can measure, it can occur when a signal is [digitized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digitized), or it can occur any other time an [analog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analog_signal) or [digital signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_%28signal_processing%29) is transformed, particularly in the presence of [gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gain_%28electronics%29) or [overshoot and undershoot](wikipedia://en/22376472).

:::

Clipping may be described as hard, in cases where the signal is strictly limited at the threshold, producing a flat cutoff; or it may be described as soft, in cases where the clipped signal continues to follow the original at a reduced gain. Hard clipping results in many high-frequency [harmonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonics); soft clipping results in fewer higher-order harmonics and intermodulation distortion components.

## Audio {#Audio}

*Image: Clipping.svg.* This [PCM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulse-code_modulation) waveform is clipped between the red lines

In the [frequency domain](wikipedia://en/370346), clipping produces strong harmonics in the high-frequency range (as the clipped waveform comes closer to a [square wave](wikipedia://en/314652)). The extra high-frequency weighting of the signal could make [tweeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tweeter) damage more likely than if the signal was not clipped.

Many [electric guitar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_guitar) [players](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guitarist) intentionally **overdrive** their amplifiers (or insert a "fuzz box") to cause clipping in order to get a desired sound (see [guitar distortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distortion_%28music%29)).

In general, the distortion associated with clipping is unwanted, and is visible on an [oscilloscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscilloscope) even if it is inaudible.[^o3]

## Images {#Images}

*Image: blown-out highlights.jpg.* Example image exhibiting blown-out highlights. Top: original image, bottom: blown-out areas marked red

In the image domain, clipping is seen as [desaturated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturation_%28color_theory%29) (washed-out) bright areas that turn to pure white if all [color components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Channel_%28digital_image%29) clip. In digital colour photography, it is also possible for individual colour channels to clip, which results in inaccurate colour reproduction.

## Causes {#Causes}

### Analog circuitry {#Analog_circuitry}

A circuit designer may intentionally use a [clipper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clipper_%28electronics%29) or [clamper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clamper_%28electronics%29) to keep a signal within a desired range.

When an amplifier is pushed to create a signal with more power than it can support, it will amplify the signal only up to its maximum capacity, at which point the signal will be amplified no further.

- An [integrated circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit) or discrete [solid state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid_state_%28electronics%29) amplifier cannot give an output voltage larger than the voltage it is powered by (commonly a 24- or 30-volt spread for operational amplifiers used in [line-level](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line-level) equipment).
- A [vacuum tube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube) can only move a limited number of [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrons) in an amount of time, dependent on its size, temperature, and metals.
- A [transformer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer) (most commonly used between stages in tube equipment) will clip when its [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic) core becomes [electromagnetically saturated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturation_%28magnetic%29).

### Digital processing {#Digital_processing}

In [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing), clipping occurs when the signal is restricted by the range of a chosen representation. For example in a system using 16-bit [signed](wikipedia://en/1181818) integers, 32767 is the largest positive value that can be represented, and if during processing the amplitude of the signal is doubled, [sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_%28signal%29) values of 32000 should become 64000, but instead they are truncated to the maximum, 32767. Clipping is preferable to the alternative in digital systems — wrapping — which occurs if the [digital hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_hardware) is allowed to "[overflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_overflow)", ignoring the [most significant bits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Most_significant_bit) of the magnitude, and sometimes even the sign of the sample value, resulting in gross distortion of the signal.

The incidence of clipping may be greatly reduced by using [floating point numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_point_numbers) instead of integers. However, floating point numbers are usually less efficient to use, sometimes result in a loss of [precision](wikipedia://en/41932), and they can still clip if a number is extremely large or small.

## Avoiding clipping {#Avoiding_clipping}

Clipping can be detected by viewing the signal (on an oscilloscope, for example), and observing that the tops and bottoms of waves aren't smooth anymore. When working with images, some tools can highlight all [pixels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel) that are pure white, allowing the user to identify larger groups of white pixels and decide if too much clipping has occurred.

To avoid clipping, the signal can be dynamically reduced using a [limiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limiter). If not done carefully, this can still cause undesirable distortion, but it prevents any data from being completely lost.

## Repairing a clipped signal {#Repairing_a_clipped_signal}

When clipping occurs, part of the original signal is lost, so perfect restoration is impossible. Thus, it is much preferable to avoid clipping in the first place. However, when repair is the only option, the goal is to make up a plausible replacement for the clipped part of the signal.
