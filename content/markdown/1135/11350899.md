# Unduloid

*Image: Unduloid.svg.* Computer generated unduloid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unduloid"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **unduloid**, or **onduloid**, is a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) with [constant nonzero mean curvature](wikipedia://en/35544695) obtained as a [surface of revolution](wikipedia://en/329549) of an [elliptic catenary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_catenary): that is, by [rolling](wikipedia://en/846500) an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) along a fixed line, tracing the [focus](wikipedia://en/1032607), and revolving the resulting curve around the line. In 1841 [Delaunay](wikipedia://en/523533) proved that the only [surfaces of revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfaces_of_revolution) with constant mean curvature were the surfaces obtained by rotating the [roulettes](wikipedia://en/846500) of the conics. These are the plane, cylinder, sphere, the [catenoid](wikipedia://en/273329), the unduloid and [nodoid](wikipedia://en/11396581).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula"}

## Formula {#Formula}

Let $\operatorname{sn}(u,k)$ represent the normal [Jacobi sine function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_sine_function) and $\operatorname{dn}(u,k)$ be the normal [Jacobi elliptic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_elliptic_function) and let $\operatorname{F}(z,k)$ represent the normal [elliptic integral](wikipedia://en/9960) of the first kind and $\operatorname{E}(z,k)$ represent the normal elliptic integral of the second kind. Let *a* be the length of the ellipse's [major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_axis), and *e* be the [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/1239472) of the ellipse. Let *k* be a fixed value between 0 and 1 called the modulus.

Given these variables,

$$\operatorname{x}(u) = -a(1-e)( \operatorname{F}(\operatorname{sn}(u,k),k) + \operatorname{F}(1,k)) - a(1+e)( \operatorname{E}( \operatorname{sn}(u,k),k) + \operatorname{E}(1,k)) \,$$

$$\operatorname{y}(u) = a(1+e)\operatorname{dn}(u,k) \,$$

The formula for the surface of revolution that is the unduloid is then

$$\operatorname{X}(u,v) = \langle \operatorname{x}(u), \operatorname{y}(u) \cos(v), \operatorname{y}(u) \sin(v)\rangle \,$$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

One interesting property of the unduloid is that the [mean curvature](wikipedia://en/684210) is constant. In fact, the mean curvature across the entire surface is always the reciprocal of twice the major axis length: 1/(2*a*).

Also, [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics) on an unduloid obey the [Clairaut relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clairaut's_relation), and their behavior is therefore predictable.

:::

## Occurrence in material science {#Occurrence_in_material_science}

Unduloids are not a common pattern in nature. However, there are a few circumstances in which they form. First documented in 1970, passing a strong electric current through a thin (0.16—1.0mm), horizontally mounted, hard-drawn (non-[tempered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempering_%28metallurgy%29)) [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver) wire will result in unduloids forming along its length.[^o8] This phenomenon was later discovered to also occur in [molybdenum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molybdenum) wire. Unduloids have also been formed with [ferrofluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrofluid).[^o10] By passing a current axially through a cylinder coated with a viscous magnetic fluid film, the [magnetic dipoles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_dipole) of the fluid interact with the magnetic field of the current, creating a droplet pattern along the cylinder’s length.
