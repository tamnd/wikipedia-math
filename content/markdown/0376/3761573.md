# Paramartha (Chinese monk)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paramartha"}

**Paramārtha** ([Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit), [Devanagari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devanagari): परमार्थ; ) (499-569 CE) was an Indian monk from [Ujjain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ujjain), who is best known for his prolific [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language) translations of [Buddhist texts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist_texts) during the [Six Dynasties era](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Six_Dynasties).[^o2][^o3] He is known as one of the four great translators in [Chinese Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism) history (along with [Kumārajīva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kumārajīva) and [Xuanzang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanzang)).[^o4] He is also known for the various oral commentaries he gave on his translations which were written down by his disciples (and now only survive in fragmentary form).[^o2] Some of Paramārtha's influential translations include [Vasubandhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasubandhu)'s *[Abhidharmakośa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidharma-kosa)*, [Asaṅga’s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asanga) *[Mahāyānasaṃgraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahāyānasaṃgraha),* and [Dignāga](wikipedia://en/1280290)'s *[Ālambanaparīkṣā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ālambanaparīkṣā)* & *Hastavālaprakaraṇa*.[^o2][^o5]

:::

Paramārtha is associated with some unique doctrines. He is traditionally seen as having taught the doctrine of the "immaculate consciousness" (*amalavijñāna*, Ch: *amoluoshi* 阿摩羅識).[^o6][^o7] He is also seen as the source of the doctrine of “[original awakening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hongaku)” (benjue \[本覺\]).[^o3] Paramārtha is also associated with various works on [Buddha-nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha-nature) that became extremely influential in [Chinese Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_Buddhism). These include the *Treatise on Buddha Nature* (*Foxing lun* 佛性論) and the *[Mahayana Awakening of Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awakening_of_Faith_in_the_Mahayana)* (*Dasheng qi xin lun* 大乘起信論), a key work for [Huayan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huayan) and [Chan Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chan_Buddhism).[^o2][^o3] However, modern scholars have expressed doubts about the attribution of the *Awakening of Faith* to Paramārtha (as well as numerous other texts), and scholarly opinion remains divided, often due to discrepancies between ancient Chinese [catalogs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cataloging_%28library_science%29).[^o3]

Due to his teachings which synthesize [Yogacara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogachara) thought with [Buddha-nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha-nature) ideas, Paramārtha is traditionally seen as a key figure of the Shelun School (攝論宗), a major tradition of Chinese Buddhist thought in the 6th and 7th centuries as well as a major figure of the Faxing school (法性宗, “School of Dharma-nature”).[^o3] The distinctive doctrine of the Faxing school was "the existence of a pure and transcendent element within the mind, in which case liberation would simply be a matter of recovering that innate purity."[^o3] This was opposed to the view of [Xuanzang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanzang) and his school, which held that the mind was impure and had to be totally transformed.[^o3]

## Biography {#Biography}

*Image: Asia 500ad.jpg.* Asia in 500 CE

*Image: Northern and Southern Dynasties 3.png.* Status of China during the Northern and Southern Dynasties era

*Image: Liang Wudi.jpg.* [Emperor Wu of Liang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Wu_of_Liang) was Paramārtha's patron in [China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China)

Paramārtha was born in 499 CE in the autonomous kingdom of [Malwa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malwa) in central India, at the end of the [Gupta Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gupta_Dynasty).[^n1] His given name was Kulanātha, meaning "savior of the family", and his parents were [Brahmins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmin) belonging to the Bhāradvāja clan.[^n2] His Buddhist name of *Paramārtha* means "the ultimate meaning," *parama*: uppermost, *artha*: meaning. In the [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) context, this refers to the absolute, as opposed to merely conventional truth.

Paramārtha became a Buddhist monk in India, most likely in the [Sāṃmitīya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pudgalavada) Vinaya*.[^o3]* He received support from royalty for his travels to spread the teachings of Buddhism. He most likely received royal patronage from Bālāditya II or Kumāragupta III.[^n3] The [Maukhari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maukharis_of_Kannauj) ruler Dhruvasena I may have also supported Paramārtha, as his kingdom was a well-known bastion of the type of [Yogācāra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogacara) teachings advocated by Paramārtha.[^n3] Paramārtha also seems to have associated with Jivitagupta of the [Later Gupta dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Later_Gupta_dynasty) as the latter instructed Paramārtha to provide assistance in the translation of Mahayana texts to a Chinese delegation in 539 CE.[^o13]

The first destination of Paramārtha was the kingdom of [Funan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Funan), or pre-[Angkor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angkor) [Cambodia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambodia).[^n3] Here in Funan, Paramārtha's reputation grew to the extent that [Emperor Wu of Liang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emperor_Wu_of_Liang) sent ambassadors to bring Paramārtha to the Chinese imperial court.[^n4] Paramārtha arrived in China through [Guangdong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangdong) (then called Nanhai) on 25 September 546 CE.23 The conditions of Paramartha's arrival at the capital are described in a Chinese introduction written by Pao Kuei in 597 CE:[^n4]

During the Ta-t'ung period the emperor sent a rear guard Chang Szu to Funan to send back to China eminent monks and [Mahayana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahayana) [sastras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shastra) and [sutras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutra) of various kinds. This country \[Funan\] then yielded in turning over the western Indian Dharma Master from Ujjain, namely Paramartha, who in Liang was called Chen-ti, and many sutras and sastras in order to honor the emperor. After Dharma Master Paramartha had traveled to many kingdoms he had settled in Funan. His manner was lively and intelligent and he relished details in scriptural texts and profound texts, all of which he had studied. In the first year of T'ai-ch'ing (547) he went to the capital and had a visit with the emperor who himself bowed down to him in the Jeweled Cloud quarters of the palace in reverence to him, wishing for him to translate sutras and sastras.

In China, Paramārtha worked with a translation team of twenty accomplished monks.[^n5] Paramartha's work was interrupted by political events and the general chaotic state of China during this period, which included the murder of Emperor Wu. Several years later, Paramārtha was able to continue translation efforts in earnest with his translation team, beginning with the *[Golden Light Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Light_Sutra)* (Skt. *Suvarṇaprabhāsa Sūtra*).[^n6] Despite his success in China, Paramārtha wished to return to India toward the end of his life, but felt that this journey back to the west would be "impossible."[^n7] Instead, he accepted the patronage of Ouyang Ho and continued his translation efforts at a rapid pace.[^n7] During much of his later life, Paramārtha continued a pattern of continually translating texts while traveling from region to region in China. He also continued to review his older translations for any areas in which the words and the general meaning were in conflict.[^n6]

During his later years (562–569) Paramārtha finally attained a stable patronage and could remain in one single place to work - [Guangzhou](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guangzhou). It was during this late period that he and his main students, like Huikai, Sengzong (僧宗), Fazhun (法准), and Sengren (僧忍), produced the most important translations, like the *[Abhidharmakośabhāṣya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidharmakośa-bhāsya)* and the *[Mahāyānasaṃgraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahāyānasaṃgraha).[^o3]* In this later period, Paramārtha had become famous throughout southern China and had acquired a supportive following of disciples, many of whom traveled great distance to hear his teachings, especially those from the *Mahāyānasaṃgraha*.[^n8]

In 569 CE, at the age of 70, he died, and a [stūpa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stupa) was built in his honor.[^n9]

## Teaching {#Teaching}

Paramārtha's interest ranged across a wide variety of Buddhist teachings, from [Abhidharma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidharma), to [Yogacara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogachara) Buddhism, [Buddha-nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha-nature) teaching and [Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna)'s ethical teachings*.[^o3]*

### Pure consciousness {#Pure_consciousness}

However, Paramārtha is most well known for introducing his unique [Yogacara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogachara) doctrine of the "pure consciousness" or "immaculate consciousness" (*amalavijñāna*, Ch: *amoluoshi* 阿摩羅識 or *wugou shi* 無垢識).[^o6][^o26][^o7] This doctrine expands on the Yogacara school's doctrine of [eight consciousnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_Consciousnesses) by introducing the immaculate consciousness as a ninth consciousness.[^o6][^o26][^o7]

The term *amalavijñāna* was not a new term and had been used by [Vasubandhu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasubandhu) in his *[Abhidharmakośa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidharmakośa-bhāsya)* (at 5.29)*.* In this text, the term refers to a “consciousness without outflows” (anăsravavijñăna). This is a consciousness that has been purified of all defilement through insight into the four noble truths and which brings freedom from rebirth.[^o6] Likewise, the *[Yogacarabhumi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogācārabhūmi-Śāstra)* contains teachings on purified consciousness (*visuddha vijñāna*). It is likely that these earlier sources influenced Paramārtha's conception of immaculate consciousness.[^o6]

Paramārtha's concept of the *amalavijñāna* is a pure and permanent (*nitya*) consciousness that is unaffected by [suffering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duḥkha) or [mental afflictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kleshas_%28Buddhism%29).[^o6] This immaculate consciousness is not a basis for the defilements (unlike the *[ālayavijñāna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_Consciousnesses)*), but rather is a basis for the noble path (*āryamārga*).[^o6] It is thus a purified vijñāna [skandha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skandha) (consciousness aggregate). As Michael Radich notes, Paramārtha holds that there are two different types of basic consciousnesses, "one the basis for worldly and defiled dharmas, and the other the basis of transcendent (*lokôttara*) dharmas."[^o6] Furthermore, the phenomena produced by the immaculate consciousness act as the counteragent to all the defilements and the *amalavijñāna* is said to be attained by the cultivation of the wisdom that knows [Thusness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tathātā) (*tathatā*).[^o6] According to Paramārtha, [Buddhahood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhahood) is achieved when, after practicing the noble path, the mind experiences the “revolutionary transformation of the basis” (*āśrayaparāvṛtti*) during which the storehouse consciousness (*ālayavijñāna*) ceases to exist, leaving only the immaculate consciousness free of all evil (*dauṣṭhulya*), suffering and all outflows (*[asrava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asava)*).[^o6] Thus, according to Michael Radich "Paramărtha understood \*amalavijñăna to be the counteragent to ălayavijñăna, and the two to be in a temporal relationship to one another, whereby ălayavijñăna existed only until liberation, and was then succeeded by fully realised \*amalavijñăna."[^o6]

Some texts attributed to Paramārtha also identify the Yogacara idea of the [perfected nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three_natures) (*pariniṣpannasvabhāva*) with the *amalavijñāna.[^o6]* Some of these texts also see the teaching of the immaculate consciousness as a superior or higher version of the Yogacara doctrine of *vijñaptimātra* (*weishi*), which posits not just the unreality of non-mental phenomena, but also the unreality of the defiled consciousness itself*.[^o6]*

According to Radich, some sources attributed to Paramārtha also identify the immaculate consciousness with the “innate purity of the mind” (prakṛtiprabhāsvaracitta) and this links the concept with the pure [Thusness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tathātā) of the *[Ratnagotravibhāga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratnagotravibhāga)* and thus with the doctrine of [Buddha nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha-nature) (*foxing* 佛性). This purity is also linked with the [dharmadhātu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dharmadhatu) and, according to Radich, "this is the beginning of a process that links \*amalavijñāna into a chain of identifications for (aspects of) the Mahāyāna “absolute”.[^o6]

### Buddha nature {#Buddha_nature}

Some modern scholars also consider the "*Treatise on Buddha Nature" (Foxing lun* 佛性論, T. 1610*)* to be an original work of Paramārtha, based on his reading of the *[Ratnagotravibhāga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratnagotravibhāga)* (both texts share many similarities).[^o28] Because of this, Paramārtha is seen as an important figure in the development of the Yogacara-[tathagatagarbha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha-nature) synthesis.[^o29]

Since the status of the various texts attributed to Paramārtha are still up for debate, attempting to extract Paramārtha's original doctrine from later interpolations and the ideas of other figures in Paramārtha's tradition is quite difficult.[^o6]

## Works {#Works}

There are many disagreements and discrepancies between the main Chinese Buddhist catalogs regarding Paramārtha's translations and modern scholarly opinion on which works to attribute to him also remain divided.[^o3] Some scholars have also argued that the term “Paramārtha" should often be regarded not as a single individual, but as a group of scholars, the “Paramārtha group” or translation workshop. This helps explain why the various catalogs diverge in many ways.[^o3]

According to Keng Ching and Michael Radich, the following key texts are agreed upon by all catalogs (with minor differences in dating etc) as being translations of Paramārtha (and his team of translators):[^o3]

- Guangyi famen jing (廣義法門經, *\*Arthavistara-sūtra*, T. 97)
- Jin guangming jing (金光明經, *Suvarṇabhāsottama-sūtra* i.e. *[Golden Light Sutra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_Light_Sutra)*)
- Wushang yi jing (無上依經, *\*Anuttarāśraya-sūtra?*, T. 669)
- Jiejie jing (解節經, a part of the *[Saṃdhinirmocana-sūtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sandhinirmocana_Sutra)*, T. 677).
- Lü ershi’er mingliao lun (律二十二明了論, T. 1461).
- Fo apitan jing chujia xiang pin (佛阿毘曇經出家 相品, T. 1482).
- Apidamo jushe shilun (阿毘達磨俱舍釋論, *[Abhidharmakośabhāṣya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhidharmakośa-bhāsya)*, T. 1559)
- Dasheng weishi lun (大乘唯識論, *Viṃśikā*, T. 1589).
- She dasheng lun (攝大乘論, *[Mahāyānasaṃgraha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahāyānasaṃgraha)*, T. 1593).
- She dasheng lun shi (攝大乘論釋, *Mahāyānasaṃgrahabhāṣya* of Vasubandhu, T. 1595)
- Zhong bian fenbie lun (中邊分別論, *[Madhyānta-vibhāga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhyanta-vibhaga-karika),* T. 1599).
- Foxing lun (佛性論, T. 1610), The "*Treatise on Buddha Nature",* traditionally attributed to Vasubandhu, but this is questioned by modern scholars.[^o31]
- San wuxing lun (三無性論, T. 1617)
- Rushi lun fan zhinan pin (如實論反質難品, T. 1633).
- Suixiang lun jie shiliu di yi (隨相論解十六諦義, T. 1641).
- Lishi apitan lun (立世阿毘曇論, *\*Lokasthānābhidharma-śāstra*, T. 1644).
- Si di lun (四諦論, T. 1647).
- Baoxing wang zhenglun (寶行王正論, *Ratnāvalī* of [Nagarjuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagarjuna), T. 1656).
- Posoupandou fashi zhuan (婆藪槃豆法師傳, Biography of the Dharma Master Vasubandhu, T. 2049).

Regarding the famous *[Mahayana Awakening of Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awakening_of_Faith_in_the_Mahayana)* (*Dasheng qi xin lun* 大乘起信論, T. 1666), it is cited as "dubious" in one of the Chinese catalogs, hence the current scholarly debate as to its provenance.[^o3]

An important source for Paramārtha's doctrine of the immaculate consciousness is the *Jueding zang lun* (決定藏論, the beginning of the *Viniścayasaṃgrahaṇī* portion of the [*Yogācārabhūmi*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogācārabhūmi-Śāstra), T. 1584). This text is not included in all catalogs of Paramārtha's works but is considered to be by Paramārtha by various modern scholars including Michael Radich.[^o3][^o6]

There are numerous other works attributed to Paramārtha and there is still much scholarly debate regarding which works can be attributed to him.[^o3]

Scholars have noted that some of Paramārtha's translations contain deviations from their Indic or Tibetan counterparts. Some scholars such as Funayama Tōru, have argued that this difference is due to Paramārtha's "lecture notes" being included as part of the translations of the Indian source texts.[^o3]

Some of Paramārtha's various lost works, including some of his oral commentaries written by his students, have survived in fragmentary form as quotations in later texts. Modern scholars are still working on collecting these fragments.[^o3]

## Influence {#Influence}

After Paramārtha's death, his various students dispersed and attempted to spread his teachings, but they were not very successful*.[^o3]*

It was only due to the efforts of Tanqian (曇遷; 542–607) that Paramārtha's teachings flourished and became popular in the north. In spite of the fact that Tanqian had neither met Paramārtha, nor studied with any of Paramārtha’s students, it was Tanqian who really popularised Paramārtha's teachings, especially the *Mahāyānasaṃgrahabhāṣya,* which he taught together with the *Awakening of Faith.* Tanqian is also seen as a key figure of the Shelun School (攝論宗) and he possibly was the main force behind the promotion of the *Awakening of Faith* as Paramārtha's work*.[^o3]* The Shelun School based itself on Paramārtha's translation of Vasubandhu’s *Mahāyānasaṃgrahabhāṣya.*[^o33]

As Paramārtha's work became more influential, it also became central to the so called Faxing school (法性宗, “School of Dharma-nature”), which was a Chinese form of [Yogacara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogachara) that also placed much emphasis on the doctrine of [tathagatagarbha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddha-nature)*.[^o3]*

Paramārtha's doctrine of the immaculate consciousness was a particularly influential teaching which was widely adopted by many later Chinese Buddhist thinkers.[^o6] Beginning with the work of Huijun (慧均, d.u., fl. 574–580s?), the immaculate consciousness began to be widely called the untainted consciousness (wugoushi 無垢識) as well as the “ninth consciousness” (jiushi 九識), an extension of the Yogacara doctrine of [eight consciousnesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eight_Consciousnesses)).[^o6] While numerous later sources claim that Paramārtha taught the immaculate consciousness as a “ninth consciousness”, this is not found in any of Paramārtha's extant works and Michael Radich writes that the truth of the issue is impossible to determine.[^o6] Later sources also drew on a passage in the *[Laṃkâvatăra sūtra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laṅkāvatāra_Sūtra)* to defend the doctrine of immaculate consciousness as a ninth consciousness.[^o6]

The idea is used by numerous influential East Asian Buddhist authors like [Zhiyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhiyi) (智顗, 538–597), [Wŏnch’uk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woncheuk) (圓測, 613–696); [Wŏnhyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wonhyo) (元曉, 617–686); [Amoghavajra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amoghavajra) (不空金剛, 705–774), [Chengguan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chengguan_%28monk%29) (澄觀, 738–839); and [Zongmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guifeng_Zongmi) (宗密, 780–841).[^o6]

Many later authors interpreted Paramārtha's doctrine of the immaculate consciousness through other works, especially the *[Awakening of Faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Awakening_of_Faith_in_the_Mahayana)*. The influence of the *Awakening of Faith* on the immaculate consciousness doctrine can already be seen in the work of [Jingying Huiyuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jingying_Huiyuan) (淨影慧遠, 523–592). For Huiyuan, the amalavijñăna and the ălayavijñăna are both two aspects of the same "true" consciousness, reminiscent of the "One Mind" of the *Awakening of Faith*.[^o6] The doctrine is also further developed in the *[Vajrasamādhi sūtra.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vajrasamadhi-sutra)[^o6]*

[Faxiang school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_Yogācāra) thought also commented on the doctrines associated with Paramārtha, the immaculate consciousness and the "ninth consciousness". [Kuiji](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuiji), a key disciple of [Xuanzang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuanzang), saw the doctrine as heterodox and criticised it in his works.*[^o6]* [Wŏnch’uk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woncheuk) meanwhile used the term untainted consciousness as just a synonym for *ālayavijñāna.[^o6]*
