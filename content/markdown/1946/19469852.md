# Plane (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Plane"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **plane** is a [two-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/70691232) or [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_space) [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) that extends indefinitely. A plane is the two-dimensional analogue of a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) (zero dimensions), a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) (one dimension) and [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853). When working exclusively in two-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the definite article is used, so *the* [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) refers to the whole space.

:::

Several notions of a plane may be defined. The Euclidean plane follows [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), and in particular the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493). A [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) may be constructed by adding "points at infinity" where two otherwise parallel lines would intersect, so that every pair of lines intersects in exactly one point. The [elliptic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_plane) may be further defined by adding a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) to the real projective plane. One may also conceive of a [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane), which obeys [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) and has a negative [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770).

Abstractly, one may forget all structure except the topology, producing the topological plane, which is homeomorphic to an [open disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_disk). Viewing the plane as an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) produces the [affine plane](wikipedia://en/30764139), which lacks a notion of distance but preserves the notion of [collinearity](wikipedia://en/3189581). Conversely, in adding more structure, one may view the plane as a [1-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-dimensional) [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742), called the [complex line](wikipedia://en/7975294).

Many fundamental tasks in mathematics, [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), and [graphing](wikipedia://en/87352) are performed in a two-dimensional or *planar* space.[^o2]

## Further generalizations {#Further_generalizations}

In addition to its familiar [geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric) structure, with [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) that are [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) with respect to the usual inner product, the plane may be viewed at various other levels of [abstraction](wikipedia://en/454446). Each level of abstraction corresponds to a specific [category](wikipedia://en/53759).

At one extreme, all geometrical and [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) concepts may be dropped to leave the [topological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological) plane, which may be thought of as an idealized [homotopically](wikipedia://en/212250) trivial infinite rubber sheet, which retains a notion of proximity, but has no distances. The topological plane has a concept of a linear path, but no concept of a straight line. The topological plane, or its equivalent the open disc, is the basic topological neighborhood used to construct [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865) (or 2-manifolds) classified in [low-dimensional topology](wikipedia://en/621774). Isomorphisms of the topological plane are all [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942). The topological plane is the natural context for the branch of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) that deals with [planar graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planar_graphs), and results such as the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949).

The plane may also be viewed as an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), whose isomorphisms are combinations of translations and non-singular linear maps. From this viewpoint there are no distances, but [collinearity](wikipedia://en/3189581) and ratios of distances on any line are preserved.

[Differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) views a plane as a 2-dimensional real [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), a topological plane which is provided with a [differential structure](wikipedia://en/725272). Again in this case, there is no notion of distance, but there is now a concept of smoothness of maps, for example a [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) or [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) path (depending on the type of differential structure applied). The isomorphisms in this case are bijections with the chosen degree of differentiability.

In the opposite direction of abstraction, we may apply a compatible field structure to the geometric plane, giving rise to the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) and the major area of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759). The complex field has only two automorphisms that leave the real line fixed, the identity and [conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation).

In the same way as in the real case, the plane may also be viewed as the simplest, [one-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-dimensional) (in terms of [complex dimension](wikipedia://en/2783513), over the complex numbers) [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742), sometimes called the [complex line](wikipedia://en/7975294). However, this viewpoint contrasts sharply with the case of the plane as a 2-dimensional real manifold. The isomorphisms are all [conformal](wikipedia://en/50627) bijections of the complex plane, but the only possibilities are maps that correspond to the composition of a multiplication by a complex number and a translation.

In addition, the Euclidean geometry (which has zero [curvature](wikipedia://en/60770) everywhere) is not the only geometry that the plane may have. The plane may be given a [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026) by using the [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431). This can be thought of as placing a sphere tangent to the plane (just like a ball on the floor), removing the top point, and projecting the sphere onto the plane from this point. This is one of the projections that may be used in making a flat map of part of the Earth's surface. The resulting geometry has constant positive curvature.

Alternatively, the plane can also be given a metric which gives it constant negative curvature giving the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291). The latter possibility finds an application in the theory of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962) in the simplified case where there are two spatial dimensions and one time dimension. (The hyperbolic plane is a [timelike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timelike) [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101) in three-dimensional [Minkowski space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space).)

## Topological and differential geometric notions {#Topological_and_differential_geometric_notions}

The [one-point compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-point_compactification) of the plane is homeomorphic to a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) (see [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431)); the open disk is homeomorphic to a sphere with the "north pole" missing; adding that point completes the (compact) sphere. The result of this compactification is a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) referred to as the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) or the [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578). The projection from the Euclidean plane to a sphere without a point is a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) and even a [conformal map](wikipedia://en/50627).

The plane itself is homeomorphic (and diffeomorphic) to an open [disk](wikipedia://en/329542). For the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291) such diffeomorphism is conformal, but for the Euclidean plane it is not.
