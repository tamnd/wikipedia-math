# MAGIC criteria

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="MAGIC criteria"}

The **MAGIC criteria** are a set of guidelines put forth by [Robert Abelson](wikipedia://en/7720278) in his 1995 book *Statistics as Principled Argument*.[^o2] In this book he posits that the goal of statistical analysis should be to make compelling claims about the world[^o3] and he presents the MAGIC criteria as a way to do that.

:::

## What are the MAGIC criteria? {#What_are_the_MAGIC_criteria?}

MAGIC is a [backronym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Backronym) for:

1. Magnitude – How big is the effect? Large effects are more compelling than small ones.
1. Articulation – How specific is it?[^o5] Precise statements are more compelling than imprecise ones.
1. Generality – How generally does it apply?[^o3] More general effects are more compelling than less general ones. Claims that would interest a more general audience are more compelling.[^o5]
1. Interestingness – interesting effects are those that "have the potential, through empirical analysis, to change what people believe about an important issue".[^o3] More interesting effects are more compelling than less interesting ones. In addition, more surprising effects are more compelling than ones that merely confirm what is already known.[^o5]
1. Credibility – Credible claims are more compelling than incredible ones. The researcher must show that the claims made are credible.[^o3] Results that contradict previously established ones are less credible.[^o5]

## Reviews and applications of the MAGIC criteria {#Reviews_and_applications_of_the_MAGIC_criteria}

Song Qian noted that the MAGIC criteria could be of use to ecologists.[^o7] Claudia Stanny discussed them in a course on psychology.[^o8] Anne Boomsma noted that they are useful when presenting results of complex statistical methods such as [structural equation modelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_equation_modelling).[^o9]
