# Topological game

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Topological game"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **topological game** is an infinite game of [perfect information](wikipedia://en/321438) played between two players on a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450). Players choose objects with topological properties such as points, [open sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_sets), [closed sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_sets) and [open coverings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_coverings). Time is generally discrete, but the plays may have [transfinite](wikipedia://en/189734) lengths, and extensions to continuum time have been put forth. The conditions for a player to win can involve notions like [topological closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_closure) and convergence.

:::

It turns out that some fundamental topological constructions have a natural counterpart in topological games; examples of these are the [Baire property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baire_property), [Baire spaces](wikipedia://en/200550), completeness and convergence properties, separation properties, covering and base properties, continuous images, Suslin sets, and singular spaces. At the same time, some topological properties that arise naturally in topological games can be generalized beyond a [game-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game-theoretic) context: by virtue of this duality, topological games have been widely used to describe new properties of topological spaces, and to put known properties under a different light. There are also close links with [selection principles](wikipedia://en/50398478).

The term *topological game* was first introduced by [Claude Berge](wikipedia://en/5893760),[^o2][^o3][^o4] who defined the basic ideas and formalism in analogy with [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315). A different meaning for *topological game*, the concept of “topological properties defined by games”, was introduced in the paper of Rastislav Telgársky,[^o5] and later "spaces defined by topological games";[^o6] this approach is based on analogies with matrix games, [differential games](wikipedia://en/17507367) and statistical games, and defines and studies topological games within topology. After more than 35 years, the term “topological game” became widespread, and appeared in several hundreds of publications. The survey paper of Telgársky[^o7] emphasizes the origin of topological games from the [Banach–Mazur game](wikipedia://en/365811).

There are two other meanings of topological games, but these are used less frequently.

- The term *topological game* introduced by [Leon Petrosjan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Petrosjan)[^o8] in the study of antagonistic [pursuit–evasion](wikipedia://en/3857398) games. The trajectories in these topological games are continuous in time.
- The games of [Nash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Nash_%28mathematician%29) (the [Hex games](wikipedia://en/36703)), the [Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) games (Y games), the [Shapley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lloyd_Shapley) games (projective plane games), and Gale's games ([Bridg-It](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridg-It) games) were called *topological games* by [David Gale](wikipedia://en/1227675) in his invited address \[1979/80\]. The number of moves in these games is always finite. The discovery or rediscovery of these topological games goes back to years 1948–49.

## Basic setup for a topological game {#Basic_setup_for_a_topological_game}

Many frameworks can be defined for infinite [positional games](wikipedia://en/25981951) of perfect information.

The typical setup is a game between two players, **I** and **II**, who alternately pick subsets of a topological space *X*. In the *n*th round, player **I** plays a subset *I*<sub>*n*</sub> of *X*, and player II responds with a subset *J*<sub>*n*</sub>. There is a round for every natural number *n*, and after all rounds are played, player **I** wins if the sequence

*I*<sub>0</sub>, *J*<sub>0</sub>, *I*<sub>1</sub>, *J*<sub>1</sub>,...

satisfies some property, and otherwise player **II** wins.

The game is defined by the target property and the allowed moves at each step. For example, in the [Banach–Mazur game](wikipedia://en/365811) *BM*(*X*), the allowed moves are nonempty open subsets of the previous move, and player **I** wins if $\bigcap_n I_n \neq \emptyset$.

This typical setup can be modified in various ways. For example, instead of being a subset of *X*, each move might consist of a pair $(I, p)$ where $I \subseteq X$ and $p \in x$. Alternatively, the sequence of moves might have length some [ordinal number](wikipedia://en/26547932) other than [ω](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_infinite_ordinal).

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions and notation"}

### Definitions and notation {#Definitions_and_notation}

- A *play* of the game is a sequence of legal moves

*I*<sub>0</sub>, *J*<sub>0</sub>, *I*<sub>1</sub>, *J*<sub>1</sub>,...

The *result of a play* is either a win or a loss for each player.

- A *strategy* for player **P** is a function defined over every legal finite sequence of moves of **P'**s opponent. For example, a strategy for player **I** is a function *s* from sequences (*J*<sub>0</sub>, *J*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *J*<sub>*n*</sub>) to subsets of *X*. A game is said to be played *according to strategy s* if every player **P** move is the value of *s* on the sequence of their opponent's prior moves. So if *s* is a strategy for player **I**, the play

$$s(\lambda), J_0, s(J_0), J_1, s(J_0, J_1), J_2, s(J_0, J_1, J_2), \ldots$$

is *according to strategy s*. (Here λ denotes the empty sequence of moves.)

- A strategy for player **P** is said to be *winning* if for every play according to strategy *s* results in a win for player **P**, for any sequence of legal moves by **P'**s opponent. If player **P** has a winning strategy for game *G*, this is denoted $P \uparrow G$. If either player has a winning strategy for *G*, then *G* is said to be *determined.* It follows from the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) that there are non-determined topological games.
- A strategy for **P** is *stationary* if it depends only on the last move by **P'**s opponent; a strategy is ***Markov*** if it depends both on the last move of the opponent *and* on the ordinal number of the move.

:::

## The Banach–Mazur game {#The_Banach–Mazur_game}

The first topological game studied was the Banach–Mazur game, which is a motivating example of the connections between game-theoretic notions and topological properties.

Let *Y* be a topological space, and let *X* be a subset of *Y*, called the *winning set*. Player **I** begins the game by picking a nonempty open subset $I_0 \subseteq Y$, and player **II** responds with a nonempty open subset $J_0 \subseteq I_0$. Play continues in this fashion, with players alternately picking a nonempty open subset of the previous play. After an infinite sequence of moves, one for each natural number, the game is finished, and **I** wins if and only if

$$X \cap \bigcap_{n \in \omega} I_n \neq \emptyset.$$

The game-theoretic and topological connections demonstrated by the game include:

- **II** has a winning strategy in the game if and only if *X* is of the *first category* in *Y* (a set is of the [first category](wikipedia://en/200550) or [meagre](wikipedia://en/226975) if it is the countable union of [nowhere-dense sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowhere-dense_set)).
- If *Y* is a [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832), then **I** has a winning strategy if and only if *X* is [comeagre](wikipedia://en/226975) in some nonempty open subset of *Y*.
- If *X* has the [property of Baire](wikipedia://en/2213768) in *Y*, then the game is determined.

## Other topological games {#Other_topological_games}

Some other notable topological games are:

- the [binary game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_game) introduced by [Ulam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanislaw_Ulam) — a modification of the Banach–Mazur game;
- the [Banach game](wikipedia://en/48645657) — played on a subset of the real line;
- the [Choquet game](wikipedia://en/43265794) — related to siftable spaces;
- the point-open game — in which player **I** chooses [points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28topology%29) and player **II** chooses [open neighborhoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_neighborhood) of them;
- selection games — each round player **I** chooses a (topological) collection and **II** chooses a member or finite subset of that collection. See Selection principle.

Many more games have been introduced over the years, to study, among others: the [Kuratowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kuratowski) coreduction principle; separation and reduction properties of sets in close projective classes; [Luzin](wikipedia://en/576228) sieves; invariant [descriptive set theory](wikipedia://en/396619); [Suslin sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suslin_set); the [closed graph theorem](wikipedia://en/497834); [webbed spaces](wikipedia://en/41717785); MP-spaces; the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840); [computable functions](wikipedia://en/1139338). Topological games have also been related to ideas in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), [infinitely-long formulas](wikipedia://en/630478), infinite strings of alternating quantifiers, [ultrafilters](wikipedia://en/31911), [partially ordered sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partially_ordered_sets), and the [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) of infinite graphs.

For a longer list and a more detailed account see the 1987 survey paper of Telgársky.[^o7]
