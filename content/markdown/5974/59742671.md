# DSatur

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="DSatur"}

**DSatur** is a [graph colouring](wikipedia://en/426743) [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) put forward by [Daniel Brélaz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Brélaz) in 1979.[^o3] Similarly to the [greedy colouring algorithm](wikipedia://en/21051195), DSatur colours the [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) of a [graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_%28graph_theory%29) one after another, adding a previously unused colour when needed. Once a new [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) has been coloured, the algorithm determines which of the remaining uncoloured vertices has the highest number of colours in its neighbourhood and colours this vertex next. Brélaz defines this number as the *degree of saturation* of a given vertex.[^o3] The contraction of the term "degree of saturation" forms the name of the algorithm.[^o4] DSatur is a heuristic graph colouring algorithm, yet produces exact results for bipartite,[^o3] cycle, and wheel graphs.[^o4] DSatur has also been referred to as saturation LF in the literature.[^o5]

:::

## Pseudocode {#Pseudocode}

Let the "degree of saturation" of a vertex be the number of different colours being used by its neighbors. Given a [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph), [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) $G$ compromising a vertex set $V$ and edge set $E$, the algorithm assigns colors to all of the vertices using color labels $1,2,3,...$. The algorithm operates as follows:[^o7]

1. Let $v$ be the uncolored vertex in $G$ with the highest degree of saturation. In cases of ties, choose the vertex among these with the largest degree in the subgraph induced by the uncolored vertices.
1. Assign $v$ to the lowest color label not being used by any of its neighbors.
1. If all vertices have been colored, then end; otherwise return to Step 1.

Step 2 of this algorithm assigns colors to vertices using the same scheme as the [greedy colouring algorithm](wikipedia://en/21051195). The main differences between the two approaches arises in Step 1 above, where vertices seen to be the most "constrained" are coloured first.

This gives the final three-colored solution $\mathcal{S} = \{\{g\}, \{a, c, e\}, \{b, d, f\}\}$.

## Performance {#Performance}

The [worst-case complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worst-case_complexity) of DSatur is $O(n^2)$, where $n$ is the number of vertices in the graph. This is because the process of selecting the next vertex to colour takes $O(n)$ time, and this process is carried out $n$ times. The algorithm can also be implemented using a [binary heap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_heap) to store saturation degrees, operating in $O((n+m)\log n)$, or $O(m+n\log n)$ using [Fibonacci heap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_heap), where $m$ is the number of edges in the graph.[^o4] This produces much faster runs with sparse graphs.

DSatur is known to be exact for bipartite graphs,[^o3] as well as for cycle and wheel graphs.[^o4] In an empirical comparison by Lewis in 2021, DSatur produced significantly better vertex colourings than the [greedy algorithm](wikipedia://en/21051195) on random graphs with edge probability $p=0.5$, while in turn producing significantly worse colourings than the [recursive largest first algorithm](wikipedia://en/69680802).[^o4]
