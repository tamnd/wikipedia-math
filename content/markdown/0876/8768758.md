# Sylvester's formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sylvester's formula"}

In [matrix theory](wikipedia://en/20556859), **Sylvester's formula** or **Sylvester's matrix theorem** (named after [J. J. Sylvester](wikipedia://en/234861)) or **Lagrange−Sylvester interpolation** expresses an analytic [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_function) $f (A)$ of a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $A$ as a polynomial in $A$, in terms of the [eigenvalues and eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue,_eigenvector_and_eigenspace) of $A$.[^o2][^o3] It states that[^o4]

:::

$$f(A) = \sum_{i=1}^k f(\lambda_i) ~A_i ~,$$

where the $\lambda_{i}$ are the eigenvalues of $A$, and the matrices

$$A_i \equiv \prod_{j=1 \atop j \ne i}^k \frac{1}{\lambda_i - \lambda_j} \left(A - \lambda_j I\right)$$

are the corresponding [Frobenius covariants](wikipedia://en/12438057) of $A$, which are (projection) matrix [Lagrange polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_polynomials) of $A$.

## Conditions {#Conditions}

Sylvester's formula applies for any [diagonalizable matrix](wikipedia://en/246325) $A$ with $k$ distinct eigenvalues, $\lambda$<sub>1</sub>, ..., $\lambda$<sub>*k*</sub>, and any function $f$ defined on some subset of the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) such that $f(A)$ is well defined. The last condition means that every eigenvalue $\lambda_{i}$ is in the domain of $f$, and that every eigenvalue $\lambda_{i}$ with multiplicity $m$<sub>*i*</sub> > 1 is in the interior of the domain, with $f$ being ($m_{i} - 1$) times differentiable at $\lambda_{i}$.[^o2]

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider the two-by-two matrix:

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 3 \\ 4 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

This matrix has two eigenvalues, 5 and −2. Its Frobenius covariants are

$$\begin{aligned} A_1 &= c_1 r_1 = \begin{bmatrix} 3 \\ 4 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{7} & \frac{1}{7} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{7} & \frac{3}{7} \\ \frac{4}{7} & \frac{4}{7} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{A + 2I}{5 - (-2)}\\ A_2 &= c_2 r_2 = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{7} \\ -\frac{1}{7} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 4 & -3 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \frac{4}{7} & -\frac{3}{7} \\ -\frac{4}{7} & \frac{3}{7} \end{bmatrix} = \frac{A - 5I}{-2 - 5}. \end{aligned}$$

Sylvester's formula then amounts to

$$f(A) = f(5) A_1 + f(-2) A_2. \,$$

For instance, if $f$ is defined by $f(x) = x^{-1}$, then Sylvester's formula expresses the matrix inverse $f(A) = A^{-1}$ as

$$\frac{1}{5} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{3}{7} & \frac{3}{7} \\ \frac{4}{7} & \frac{4}{7} \end{bmatrix} - \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} \frac{4}{7} & -\frac{3}{7} \\ -\frac{4}{7} & \frac{3}{7} \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -0.2 & 0.3 \\ 0.4 & -0.1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Sylvester's formula is only valid for matrices with no repeated eigenvalues; an extension due to [Arthur Buchheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Buchheim), based on [Hermite interpolating polynomials](wikipedia://en/2085700), covers the general case:[^o10]

$$f(A) = \sum_{i=1}^{s} \left[ \sum_{j=0}^{n_{i}-1} \frac{1}{j!} \phi_i^{(j)}(\lambda_i)\left(A - \lambda_i I\right)^j \prod_{{j=1,j\ne i}}^{s}\left(A - \lambda_j I\right)^{n_j} \right],$$

where $\phi_i(t) := f(t)/\prod_{j\ne i}\left(t - \lambda_j\right)^{n_j}$.

A concise form is further given by [Hans Schwerdtfeger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Schwerdtfeger),[^o11]

$$f(A)=\sum_{i=1}^{s} A_{i} \sum_{j=0}^{n_{i}-1} \frac{f^{(j)}(\lambda_i)}{j!}(A-\lambda_iI)^{j},$$

where $A$<sub>*i*</sub> are the corresponding [generalized Frobenius covariants](wikipedia://en/12438057#Generalization) of $A$

:::

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special case"}

## Special case {#Special_case}

If a matrix $A$ is both [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682) and [unitary](wikipedia://en/129222), then it can only have eigenvalues of $\pm 1$, and therefore $A=A_+-A_-$, where $A_+$ is the projector onto the subspace with eigenvalue +1, and $A_-$ is the projector onto the subspace with eigenvalue $- 1$; By the completeness of the eigenbasis, $A_++A_-=I$. Therefore, for any analytic function $f$,

$$\begin{aligned} f(\theta A)&=f(\theta)A_{+}+f(-\theta)A_{-} \\ &=f(\theta)\frac{I+A}{2}+f(-\theta)\frac{I-A}{2}\\ &=\frac{f(\theta)+f(-\theta)}{2}I+\frac{f(\theta)-f(-\theta)}{2}A\\ \end{aligned} .$$

In particular, $e^{i\theta A}=(\cos \theta)I+(i\sin \theta) A$ and $A =e^{i\frac{\pi}{2}(I-A)}=e^{-i\frac{\pi}{2}(I-A)}$.
