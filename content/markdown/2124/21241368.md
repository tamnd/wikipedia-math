# Blade (geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Blade"}

*Image: Multivector.svg.* Relations between scalars, vectors, $k$-blades (simple $k$-vectors), and $k$-vectors.

In the study of [geometric algebras](wikipedia://en/12939), a **$k$-blade** or a **simple $k$-vector** is a generalization of the concept of [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331) and [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533) to include *simple* [bivectors](wikipedia://en/884040), [trivectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivector), etc. Specifically, a $k$-blade is a [$k$-vector](wikipedia://en/2727288) that can be expressed as the [exterior product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_product) (informally *wedge product*) of 1-vectors, and is of *[grade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra)* $k$.

:::

In detail:[^o2]

- A 0-blade is a [scalar](wikipedia://en/3588331).
- A 1-blade is a [vector](wikipedia://en/32370). Every vector is simple.
- A 2-blade is a *simple* [bivector](wikipedia://en/884040). Sums of 2-blades are also bivectors, but not always simple. A 2-blade may be expressed as the wedge product of two vectors $a$ and $b$:

$$a \wedge b .$$

- A 3-blade is a simple trivector, that is, it may be expressed as the wedge product of three vectors $a$, $b$, and $c$:

$$a \wedge b \wedge c.$$

- In vector spaces of dimension ≤ 3, every k-vector is a blade. In dimension ≥ 4, there exist k-vectors (starting already at grade 2) that are not blades. This makes the distinction important, because in higher dimensions most k-vectors do not correspond to any subspace, which requires defining blades separately from k-vectors to identify the k-vectors that actually do.
- In a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) $n$, a blade of grade $n - 1$ is called a *[pseudovector](wikipedia://en/166356)*[^o3] or an *[antivector](wikipedia://en/51709781)*.[^o4]
- The highest grade element in a space is called a *[pseudoscalar](wikipedia://en/475035)*, and in a space of dimension $n$ is an $n$-blade.[^o5]
- In a vector space of dimension $n$, there are $k(n - k) + 1$ dimensions of freedom in choosing a $k$-blade for $0 \le k \le n$, of which one dimension is an overall scaling multiplier.[^o6]

A [vector subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of finite dimension $k$ may be represented by the $k$-blade formed as a wedge product of all the elements of a basis for that subspace.[^o7] Indeed, a $k$-blade is naturally equivalent to a $k$-subspace, up to a scalar factor. When the space is endowed with a [volume form](wikipedia://en/1855811) (an alternating $k$-multilinear scalar-valued function), such a $k$-blade may be normalized to take unit value, making the correspondence unique up to a sign.

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In two-dimensional space, scalars are described as 0-blades, vectors are 1-blades, and area elements are 2-blades in this context known as [pseudoscalars](wikipedia://en/475035), in that they are elements of a one-dimensional space that is distinct from regular scalars.

In three-dimensional space, 0-blades are again scalars and 1-blades are three-dimensional vectors, while 2-blades are oriented area elements. In this case 3-blades are called pseudoscalars and represent three-dimensional volume elements, which form a one-dimensional vector space similar to scalars. Unlike scalars, 3-blades transform according to the [Jacobian determinant](wikipedia://en/195351) of a [change-of-coordinate](wikipedia://en/81931) function.
