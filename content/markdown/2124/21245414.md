# Line integral

Multivariable

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Line integral"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **line integral** is an [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) where the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) to be integrated is evaluated along a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246).[^o2] The terms ***path integral***, ***curve integral***, and ***curvilinear integral*** are also used; *[contour integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_integral)* is used as well, although that is typically reserved for line integrals in the complex plane.

:::

The function to be integrated may be a [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879) or a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641). The value of the line integral is the sum of values of the field at all points on the curve, weighted by some scalar function on the curve (commonly [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) or, for a vector field, the [scalar product](wikipedia://en/157093) of the vector field with a [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28infinitesimal%29) vector in the curve). This weighting distinguishes the line integral from simpler integrals defined on [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172). Many simple formulae in physics, such as the definition of [work](wikipedia://en/149861) as $W = \mathbf{F} \cdot \mathbf{s}$, have natural continuous analogues in terms of line integrals, in this case $W = \int_L \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{s}) \cdot d\mathbf{s}$, which computes the [work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_work) done on an object moving through an electric or gravitational field $\mathbf{F}$ along a path $L$.

## Vector calculus {#Vector_calculus}

In qualitative terms, a line integral in vector calculus can be thought of as a measure of the total effect of a given [tensor field](wikipedia://en/256738) along a given curve. For example, the line integral over a scalar field (rank 0 tensor) can be interpreted as the area under the field carved out by a particular curve. This can be visualized as the surface created by $z = f(x,y)$ and a curve *C* in the *xy* plane. The line integral of *f* would be the area of the "curtain" created—when the points of the surface that are directly over *C* are carved out.

### Line integral of a scalar field {#Line_integral_of_a_scalar_field}

*Image: Line integral of scalar field.gif.* The line integral over a scalar field *f* can be thought of as the area under the curve *C* along a surface $z = f(x,y)$, described by the field. The curve end points are at $(x,y)=\mathbf{r}(a)$ and $\mathbf{r}(b)$.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

#### Definition {#Definition}

For some [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879) $f\colon U\to\mathbb{R}$ where $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, the line integral along a [piecewise smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_smooth) [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) $\mathcal{C} \subset U$ is defined as

$$\int_{\mathcal{C}} f\, ds = \int_a^b f\left(\mathbf{r}(t)\right) \left|\mathbf{r}'(t)\right| \, dt,$$

where $\mathbf r\colon[a,b]\to\mathcal{C}$ is an arbitrary [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) [parametrization](wikipedia://en/576108) of the curve $\mathcal{C}$ such that $\mathbf{r}(a)$ and $\mathbf{r}(b)$ give the endpoints of $\mathcal{C}$ and $a < b$. Here, and in the rest of the article, the absolute value bars denote the [standard (Euclidean) norm](wikipedia://en/53932) of a vector.

The function $f$ is called the integrand, the curve $\mathcal{C}$ is the domain of integration, and the symbol $ds$ may be intuitively interpreted as an elementary [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) of the curve $\mathcal{C}$ (i.e., a differential length of $\mathcal{C}$). Line integrals of scalar fields over a curve $\mathcal{C}$ do not depend on the chosen parametrization $\mathbf{r}$ of $\mathcal{C}$.[^o7]

Geometrically, when the scalar field $f$ is defined over a plane $(n = 2)$, its graph is a surface $z = f(x, y)$ in space, and the line integral gives the (signed) [cross-sectional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-section_%28geometry%29) area bounded by the curve $\mathcal{C}$ and the graph of $f$. See the animation to the right.

:::

#### Derivation {#Derivation}

For a line integral over a scalar field, the integral can be constructed from a [Riemann sum](wikipedia://en/176478) using the above definitions of $f$, $C$ and a parametrization $\mathbf{r}$ of $C$. This can be done by partitioning the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a, b]$ into $n$ sub-intervals $[t_{i-1}, t_{i}]$ of length $\Delta t = (b - a)/n$, then $\mathbf{r}(t_{i})$ denotes some point, call it a sample point, on the curve $C$. We can use the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of sample points $\{\mathbf{r}(t_{i}): 1 \le i \le n\}$ to approximate the curve $C$ as a [polygonal path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygonal_path) by introducing the straight line piece between each of the sample points $\mathbf{r}(t_{i-1})$ and $\mathbf{r}(t_{i})$. (The approximation of a curve to a polygonal path is called *rectification of a curve,* see [here](wikipedia://en/1898401) for more details.) We then label the distance of the line segment between adjacent sample points on the curve as $\Delta s_{i}$. The product of $f(\mathbf{r}(t_{i}))$ and $\Delta s_{i}$ can be associated with the signed area of a rectangle with a height and width of $f(\mathbf{r}(t_{i}))$ and $\Delta s_{i}$, respectively. Taking the [limit](wikipedia://en/285773) of the [sum](wikipedia://en/246160) of the terms as the length of the partitions approaches zero gives us

$$I = \lim_{\Delta s_i \to 0} \sum_{i=1}^n f(\mathbf{r}(t_i)) \, \Delta s_i.$$

By the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662), the distance between subsequent points on the curve, is

$$\Delta s_i = \left|\mathbf{r}(t_i+\Delta t)-\mathbf{r}(t_i)\right| \approx \left|\mathbf{r}'(t_i) \Delta t\right|$$

Substituting this in the above Riemann sum yields

$$I = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \sum_{i=1}^n f(\mathbf{r}(t_i)) \left|\mathbf{r}'(t_i)\right| \Delta t$$

which is the Riemann sum for the integral

$$I = \int_a^b f(\mathbf{r}(t)) \left|\mathbf{r}'(t)\right| dt.$$

### Line integral of a vector field {#Line_integral_of_a_vector_field}

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

#### Definition {#Definition}

For a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) $\mathbf{F}: U \subseteq \mathbf{R}^{n} \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$, the line integral along a [piecewise smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_smooth) [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) $C \subset U$, in the direction of **r**, is defined as

$$\int_C \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) \,dt$$

where $\cdot$ is the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093), and $\mathbf{r}: [a, b] \to C$ is a regular [parametrization](wikipedia://en/576108) (i.e: $||\mathbf{r}'(t)|| \neq0 \;\;\forall t\in[a,b]$) of the curve *C* such that $\mathbf{r}(a)$ and $\mathbf{r}(b)$ give the endpoints of *C*.

A line integral of a scalar field is thus a line integral of a vector field, where the vectors are always [tangential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangential) to the line of the integration.

Line integrals of vector fields are independent of the parametrization **r** in [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991), but they do depend on its [orientation](wikipedia://en/3633706). Specifically, a reversal in the orientation of the parametrization changes the sign of the line integral.[^o7]

From the viewpoint of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the line integral of a vector field along a curve is the integral of the corresponding 1-form under the [musical isomorphism](wikipedia://en/3762980) (which takes the vector field to the corresponding [covector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covector) field), over the curve considered as an [immersed](wikipedia://en/6993953) 1-manifold.

:::

#### Derivation {#Derivation}

*Image: Line integral of vector field.gif.* The trajectory of a particle (in red) along a curve inside a vector field. Starting from *a*, the particle traces the path *C* along the vector field *F*. The dot product (green line) of its tangent vector (red arrow) and the field vector (blue arrow) defines an area under a curve, which is equivalent to the path's line integral. (Click on image for a detailed description.)

The line integral of a vector field can be derived in a manner very similar to the case of a scalar field, but this time with the inclusion of a dot product. Again using the above definitions of $\mathbf{F}$, $C$ and its parametrization $\mathbf{r}(t)$, we construct the integral from a [Riemann sum](wikipedia://en/176478). We partition the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a, b]$ (which is the range of the values of the [parameter](wikipedia://en/25065) $t$) into $n$ intervals of length $\Delta t = (b - a)/n$. Letting $t_{i}$ be the $i$th point on $[a, b]$, then $\mathbf{r}(t_{i})$ gives us the position of the $i$th point on the curve. However, instead of calculating up the distances between subsequent points, we need to calculate their [displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_%28vector%29) vectors, $\Delta\mathbf{r}_{i}$. As before, evaluating $\mathbf{F}$ at all the points on the curve and taking the dot product with each displacement vector gives us the [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) contribution of each partition of $\mathbf{F}$ on $C$. Letting the size of the partitions go to zero gives us a sum

$$I = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t_i)) \cdot \Delta\mathbf{r}_i$$

By the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662), we see that the displacement vector between adjacent points on the curve is

$$\Delta\mathbf{r}_i = \mathbf{r}(t_i + \Delta t)-\mathbf{r}(t_i) \approx \mathbf{r}'(t_i) \,\Delta t.$$

Substituting this in the above Riemann sum yields

$$I = \lim_{\Delta t \to 0} \sum_{i=1}^n \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t_i)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t_i)\,\Delta t,$$

which is the Riemann sum for the integral defined above.

### Path independence {#Path_independence}

If a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of a [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879) $G$ (i.e. if $\mathbf{F}$ is [conservative](wikipedia://en/461454)), that is,

$$\mathbf{F} = \nabla G ,$$

then by the [multivariable chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113#Multivariable_case) the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of $G$ and $\mathbf{r}(t)$ is

$$\frac{dG(\mathbf{r}(t))}{dt} = \nabla G(\mathbf{r} ) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t) = \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t)) \cdot \mathbf{r}'(t)$$

which happens to be the integrand for the line integral of $\mathbf{F}$ on $\mathbf{r}(t)$. It follows, given a path *C*, that

$$\int_C \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}) \cdot d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt = \int_a^b \frac{dG(\mathbf{r}(t))}{dt}\,dt = G(\mathbf{r}(b)) - G(\mathbf{r}(a)).$$

In other words, the integral of $\mathbf{F}$ over *C* depends solely on the values of $G$ at the points $\mathbf{r}(b)$ and $\mathbf{r}(a)$, and is thus independent of the path between them. For this reason, a line integral of a conservative vector field is called *path independent*.

::: {#o15 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

### Applications {#Applications}

The line integral has many uses in physics. For example, the [work](wikipedia://en/149861) done on a particle traveling on a curve *C* inside a force field represented as a vector field $\mathbf{F}$ is the line integral of $\mathbf{F}$ on *C*.[^o16]

For another example, see [Ampère's circuital law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_circuital_law).

:::

## Flow across a curve {#Flow_across_a_curve}

For a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) $\mathbf F \colon U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$, $\mathbf{F}(x, y) = (P(x, y), Q(x, y))$, the **line integral across a curve** *C* ⊂ *U*, also called the [*flux integral*](wikipedia://en/43590), is defined in terms of a [piecewise smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_smooth) [parametrization](wikipedia://en/576108) $\mathbf{r}: [a,b] \to C$, $\mathbf{r}(t) = (x(t), y(t))$, as:

$$\int_C \mathbf F(\mathbf r)\cdot d\mathbf r^\perp = \int_a^b \begin{bmatrix} P\big(x(t),y(t)\big) \\ Q\big(x(t),y(t)\big) \end{bmatrix} \cdot \begin{bmatrix} y'(t) \\ -x'(t) \end{bmatrix} ~dt = \int_a^b \left(-Q~dx + P~dy\right).$$

Here $\cdot$ is the dot product, and $\mathbf{r}'(t)^\perp = (y'(t), -x'(t))$ is the clockwise perpendicular of the velocity vector $\mathbf{r}'(t) = (x'(t), y'(t))$.

The flow is computed in an oriented sense: the curve $C$ has a specified forward direction from $\mathbf{r}(a)$ to $\mathbf{r}(b)$, and the flow is counted as positive when $\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))$ is on the clockwise side of the forward velocity vector $\mathbf{r'}(t)$.

## Complex line integral {#Complex_line_integral}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), the line integral is defined in terms of [multiplication](wikipedia://en/5826#Multiplication) and [addition](wikipedia://en/5826#Addition_and_subtraction) of complex numbers. Suppose *U* is an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) **C**, $f : U \to \mathbf{C}$ is a function, and $L \subset U$ is a curve of finite length, parametrized by $\gamma: [a,b] \to L$, where $\gamma(t) = x(t) + iy(t)$. The line integral

$$\int_L f(z) \, dz$$

may be defined by subdividing the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) \[*a*, *b*\] into *a* = *t*<sub>0</sub> \< *t*<sub>1</sub> \< ... \< *t*<sub>*n*</sub> = *b* and considering the expression

$$\sum_{k=1}^{n} f(\gamma(t_k)) \,[ \gamma(t_k) - \gamma(t_{k-1}) ] = \sum_{k=1}^n f(\gamma_k) \,\Delta\gamma_k.$$

The integral is then the limit of this [Riemann sum](wikipedia://en/176478) as the lengths of the subdivision intervals approach zero.

If the parametrization $\gamma$ is [continuously differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206#Differentiability_classes), the line integral can be evaluated as an integral of a function of a real variable:

$$\int_L f(z)\,dz = \int_a^b f(\gamma(t)) \gamma'(t)\,dt.$$

When $L$ is a closed curve (initial and final points coincide), the line integral is often denoted $\oint_L f(z)\,dz,$ sometimes referred to in engineering as a **cyclic integral**.

To establish a complete analogy with the line integral of a vector field, one must go back to the definition of differentiability in multivariable calculus. The gradient is defined from [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452), and inner products in complex analysis involve conjugacy (the gradient of a function $\gamma$ at some $z\in\mathbb{C}$ would be $\overline{\gamma'(z)}$, and the complex inner product would attribute twice a conjugate to $\gamma'$ in the vector field definition of a line integral).

The line integral with respect to the conjugate complex differential $\overline{dz}$ is defined[^o19] to be

$$\int_L f(z) \overline{dz} := \overline{\int_L \overline{f(z)} \,dz} = \int_a^b f(\gamma(t)) \overline{\gamma'(t)}\,dt.$$

The line integrals of complex functions can be evaluated using a number of techniques. The most direct is to split into real and imaginary parts, reducing the problem to evaluating two real-valued line integrals. The [Cauchy integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_integral_theorem) may be used to equate the line integral of an [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478) to the same integral over a more convenient curve. It also implies that over a closed curve enclosing a region where $f(z)$ is analytic without [singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity), the value of the integral is simply zero, or in case the region includes singularities, the [residue theorem](wikipedia://en/73390) computes the integral in terms of the singularities. This also implies the path independence of complex line integral for analytic functions.

::: {#o21 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

Consider the function $f(z) = 1/z$, and let the contour *L* be the counterclockwise [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) about 0, parametrized by $z(t) = e^{it}$ with $t$ in $[0, 2\pi]$ using the [complex exponential](wikipedia://en/9613). Substituting, we find:

$$\begin{aligned} \oint_L \frac {1}{z}\,dz &= \int_0^{2\pi} \frac{1}{e^{it}} ie^{it} \,dt = i\int_0^{2\pi} e^{-it}e^{it}\,dt \\ &= i \int_0^{2\pi} dt = i(2\pi-0)= 2\pi i. \end{aligned}$$

This is a typical result of [Cauchy's integral formula](wikipedia://en/72827) and the [residue theorem](wikipedia://en/73390).

:::

### Relation of complex line integral and line integral of vector field {#Relation_of_complex_line_integral_and_line_integral_of_vector_field}

Viewing complex numbers as 2-dimensional [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533), the line integral of a complex-valued function $f(z)$ has real and complex parts equal to the line integral and the flux integral of the vector field corresponding to the [conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) function $\overline{f(z)}.$ Specifically, if $\mathbf{r} (t) = (x(t), y(t))$ parametrizes *L*, and $f(z) = u(z) + iv(z)$ corresponds to the vector field $\mathbf{F}(x,y) = \overline{f(x + iy)} = (u(x + iy), -v(x + iy)),$ then:

$$\begin{aligned} \int_L f(z)\,dz &= \int_L (u+iv)(dx+i\,dy) \\ &= \int_L (u,-v)\cdot (dx,dy) + i\int_L (u,-v)\cdot (dy,-dx) \\ &= \int_L \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})\cdot d\mathbf{r} + i\int_L \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})\cdot d\mathbf{r}^\perp. \end{aligned}$$

By [Cauchy's theorem](wikipedia://en/61190), the left-hand integral is zero when $f(z)$ is analytic (satisfying the [Cauchy–Riemann equations](wikipedia://en/7583)) for any smooth closed curve L. Correspondingly, by [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), the right-hand integrals are zero when $\mathbf{F} = \overline{f(z)}$ is [irrotational](wikipedia://en/461454) ([curl](wikipedia://en/6123)-free) and [incompressible](wikipedia://en/461477) ([divergence](wikipedia://en/8328)-free). In fact, the Cauchy-Riemann equations for $f(z)$ are identical to the vanishing of curl and divergence for $\mathbf{F}$.

By [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), the area of a region enclosed by a smooth, closed, positively oriented curve $L$ is given by the integral $\frac{1}{2i} \int_L \overline{z} \, dz.$ This fact is used, for example, in the proof of the [area theorem](wikipedia://en/16796426).

## Quantum mechanics {#Quantum_mechanics}

The [path integral formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation) of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) actually refers not to path integrals in this sense but to [functional integrals](wikipedia://en/301521), that is, integrals over a space of paths, of a function *of* a possible path. However, path integrals in the sense of this article are important in quantum mechanics; for example, complex contour integration is often used in evaluating [probability amplitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude) in quantum [scattering](wikipedia://en/164483) theory.
