# Linear group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Linear group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **matrix group** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* consisting of [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) over a specified [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K*, with the operation of [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). A **linear group** is a group that is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to a matrix group (that is, admitting a [faithful](wikipedia://en/2617872), finite-dimensional [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) over *K*).

:::

Any [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) is linear, because it can be realized by [permutation matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permutation_matrices) using [Cayley's theorem](wikipedia://en/105967). Among [infinite groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_group_theory), linear groups form an interesting and tractable class. Examples of groups that are not linear include groups which are "too big" (for example, the group of permutations of an infinite set), or which exhibit some pathological behavior (for example, [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042) infinite [torsion groups](wikipedia://en/3568695)).

## Definition and basic examples {#Definition_and_basic_examples}

A group *G* is said to be *linear* if there exists a field *K*, an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *d* and an [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injectivity) [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from *G* to the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) GL<sub>*d*</sub>(*K*) (a faithful linear [representation](wikipedia://en/12695) of dimension *d* over *K*): if needed one can mention the field and dimension by saying that *G* is *linear of degree d over K*. Basic instances are groups which are defined as [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of a linear group, for example:

1. The group GL<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) itself;
1. The [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) SL<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) (the subgroup of matrices with [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) 1);
1. The group of invertible upper (or lower) [triangular matrices](wikipedia://en/374222);
1. If *g<sub>i</sub>* is a collection of elements in GL<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) [indexed](wikipedia://en/393055) by a set *I*, then the subgroup generated by the *g<sub>i</sub>* is a linear group.

In the study of [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945), it is sometimes pedagogically convenient to restrict attention to Lie groups that can be faithfully represented over the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). (Some authors require that the group be represented as a *closed* subgroup of the GL<sub>*n*</sub>(**C**).) Books that follow this approach include Hall (2015)[^o3] and Rossmann (2002).[^o4]

## Classes of linear groups {#Classes_of_linear_groups}

### Classical groups and related examples {#Classical_groups_and_related_examples}

The so-called [classical groups](wikipedia://en/10172878) generalize the examples 1 and 2 above. They arise as [linear algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/395885), that is, as subgroups of GL<sub>*n*</sub> defined by a finite number of equations. Basic examples are [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/173954), [unitary](wikipedia://en/173993) and [symplectic](wikipedia://en/173977) groups but it is possible to construct more using [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625) (for example the [unit group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group) of a [quaternion algebra](wikipedia://en/2776890) is a classical group). Note that the [projective groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_group) associated to these groups are also linear, though less obviously. For example, the group PSL<sub>2</sub>(**R**) is not a group of 2 × 2 matrices, but it has a faithful representation as 3 × 3 matrices (the [adjoint representation](wikipedia://en/302188)), which can be used in the general case.

Many [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) are linear, but not all of them. The [universal cover of SL<sub>2</sub>(**R**)](wikipedia://en/11105238#Topology_and_universal_cover) is not linear, as are many [solvable groups](wikipedia://en/105012), for instance the [quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) of the [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518) by a [central](wikipedia://en/4938766) cyclic subgroup.

[Discrete subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_subgroup) of classical Lie groups (for example [lattices](wikipedia://en/15782871) or [thin groups](wikipedia://en/42832340)) are also examples of interesting linear groups.

### Finite groups {#Finite_groups}

A finite group *G* of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) *n* is linear of degree at most *n* over any field *K*. This statement is sometimes called Cayley's theorem, and simply results from the fact that the action of *G* on the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) *K*\[*G*\] by left (or right) multiplication is linear and faithful. The [finite groups of Lie type](wikipedia://en/1691490) (classical groups over finite fields) are an important family of finite [simple groups](wikipedia://en/47600), as they take up most of the slots in the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445).

### Finitely generated matrix groups {#Finitely_generated_matrix_groups}

While example 4 above is too general to define a distinctive class (it includes all linear groups), restricting to a finite index set *I*, that is, to [finitely generated groups](wikipedia://en/963042) allows to construct many interesting examples. For example:

- The [ping-pong lemma](wikipedia://en/18786361) can be used to construct many examples of linear groups which are [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735) (for instance the group generated by $\bigl( {}_2^1 \,_1^0\bigr), \, \bigl( {}_0^1 \,_1^2\bigr)$ is free).
- [Arithmetic groups](wikipedia://en/630741) are known to be finitely generated. On the other hand, it is a difficult problem to find an explicit set of generators for a given arithmetic group.
- [Braid groups](wikipedia://en/550138) (which are defined as a [finitely presented group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group)) have faithful linear representation on a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) complex vector space where the generators act by explicit matrices.[^o9] The [mapping class group](wikipedia://en/31060586) of a genus 2 surface is also known to be linear.[^o10]

### Examples from geometry {#Examples_from_geometry}

In some cases the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) can be shown to be linear by using representations coming from a geometric structure. For example, all [closed surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_surface) of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) at least 2 are hyperbolic [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181). Via the [uniformization theorem](wikipedia://en/288270) this gives rise to a representation of its fundamental group in the [isometry group](wikipedia://en/1101694) of the [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane), which is isomorphic to PSL<sub>2</sub>(**R**) and this realizes the fundamental group as a [Fuchsian group](wikipedia://en/471365). A generalization of this construction is given by the notion of a [(*G*,*X*)-structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%28G,X%29-structure) on a manifold.

Another example is the fundamental group of [Seifert manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seifert_manifold). On the other hand, it is not known whether all fundamental groups of 3–manifolds are linear.[^o12]

## Properties {#Properties}

While linear groups are a vast class of examples, among all infinite groups they are distinguished by many remarkable properties. Finitely generated linear groups have the following properties:

- They are [residually finite](wikipedia://en/5780875);
- [Burnside's theorem](wikipedia://en/151452): a [torsion](wikipedia://en/3568695) group of finite [exponent](wikipedia://en/3568695) which is linear over a field of characteristic 0 must be finite;[^n1]
- Schur's theorem: a [torsion](wikipedia://en/3568695) linear group is [locally finite](wikipedia://en/4939270). In particular, if it is finitely generated then it is finite.[^n2]
- Selberg's lemma: any finitely generated linear group contains a [torsion-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion-free_group) subgroup of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581).[^o17]

The [Tits alternative](wikipedia://en/12704641) states that a linear group either contains a non-abelian free group or else is [virtually](wikipedia://en/10377971) solvable (that is, contains a [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012) of finite index). This has many further consequences, for example:

- the [Dehn function](wikipedia://en/20017026) of a finitely generated linear group can only be either polynomial or exponential;
- an [amenable](wikipedia://en/657430) linear group is virtually solvable, in particular [elementary amenable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_amenable);
- the [von Neumann conjecture](wikipedia://en/662256) is true for linear groups.

:::

## Examples of non-linear groups {#Examples_of_non-linear_groups}

It is not hard to give infinitely generated examples of non-linear groups: for example the infinite abelian group (**Z**/2**Z**)<sup>**N**</sup> x (**Z**/3**Z**)<sup>**N**</sup> cannot be linear.[^o19] Since the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on an infinite set contains this group it is also not linear. Finding finitely generated examples is subtler and usually requires the use of one of the properties listed above.

- Since any finitely linear group is residually finite, it cannot be both simple and infinite. Thus finitely generated infinite simple groups, for example [Thompson's group](wikipedia://en/612245) *F*, and the quotient of [Higman's group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higman's_group) by a maximal proper normal subgroup, are not linear.
- By the corollary to the Tits alternative mentioned above, groups of intermediate growth such as [Grigorchuk's group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigorchuk's_group) are not linear.
- Again by the Tits alternative, as mentioned above all counterexamples to the [von Neumann conjecture](wikipedia://en/662256) are not linear. This includes [Thompson's group](wikipedia://en/612245) *F* and [Tarski monster groups](wikipedia://en/727196).
- By Burnside's theorem, infinite, finitely generated torsion groups such as [Tarski monster groups](wikipedia://en/727196) cannot be linear.
- There are examples of [hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/3172817) which are not linear, obtained as quotients of lattices in the Lie groups Sp(*n*, 1).[^o20]
- The [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) [Out(F<sub>*n*</sub>)](wikipedia://en/12747184) of the free group is known not to be linear for *n* at least 4.[^o21]
- In contrast with the case of braid groups, it is an [open question](wikipedia://en/1333335) whether the mapping class group of a surface of genus > 2 is linear.

## Representation theory {#Representation_theory}

Once a group has been established to be linear it is interesting to try to find "optimal" faithful linear representations for it, for example of the lowest possible dimension, or even to try to classify all its linear representations (including those which are not faithful). These questions are the object of [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200). Salient parts of the theory include:

- [Representation theory of finite groups](wikipedia://en/874400);
- [Representation theory of Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_theory_of_Lie_groups) and more generally linear algebraic groups.

The representation theory of infinite finitely generated groups is in general mysterious; the object of interest in this case are the [character varieties](wikipedia://en/14688049) of the group, which are well understood only in very few cases, for example free groups, surface groups and more generally lattices in Lie groups (for example through Margulis' [superrigidity](wikipedia://en/15783129) theorem and other rigidity results).
