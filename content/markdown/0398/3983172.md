# Lattice of subgroups

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lattice of subgroups"}

*Image: Dih4 subgroups (cycle graphs).svg.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of the lattice of subgroups of the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) [Dih<sub>4</sub>](wikipedia://en/78749515), with the subgroups represented by their [cycle graphs](wikipedia://en/1681010)

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **lattice of subgroups** of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ is the [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) whose elements are the [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of $G$, with the [partial ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_ordering) being [set inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_inclusion). In this lattice, the [join](wikipedia://en/3956618) of two subgroups is the subgroup [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by their [union](wikipedia://en/47949), and the [meet](wikipedia://en/3956618) of two subgroups is their [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429).

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) [Dih<sub>4</sub>](wikipedia://en/78749515) has ten subgroups, counting itself and the [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) subgroup. Five of the eight group elements generate subgroups of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) two, and the other two non-[identity](wikipedia://en/14962) elements both generate the same [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) subgroup of order four. In addition, there are two subgroups of the form [**Z**<sub>2</sub> × **Z**<sub>2</sub>](wikipedia://en/41928), generated by pairs of [order](wikipedia://en/493527)-two elements. The lattice formed by these ten subgroups is shown in the illustration.

This example also shows that the lattice of all subgroups of a group is not a [modular lattice](wikipedia://en/1089311) in general. Indeed, this particular lattice contains the forbidden "pentagon" N<sub>5</sub> as a [sublattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublattice).

## Properties {#Properties}

For any *A*, *B*, and *C* subgroups of a group with *A* ≤ *C* (*A* a subgroup of *C*) then *AB* ∩ *C* = *A*(*B* ∩ *C*); the multiplication here is the [product of subgroups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_of_subgroups). This property has been called the *modular property of groups* (Aschbacher 2000) or *([Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106)'s) modular law* ((Robinson 1996), (Cohn 2000)). Since for two [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918) the product is actually the [smallest subgroup containing](wikipedia://en/99945) the two, the normal subgroups form a [modular lattice](wikipedia://en/1089311).

The [lattice theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theorem) establishes a [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411) between the lattice of subgroups of a group and that of its [quotients](wikipedia://en/11526).

The [Zassenhaus lemma](wikipedia://en/877209) gives an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) between certain combinations of quotients and products in the lattice of subgroups.

As groups are algebraic structures, it follows by a general Theorem (Burris Sankappanavar 2011, 33) that their lattices of subgroups are algebraic lattices. This means that they are complete and compactly generated. However in general, there is no restriction on the possible sublattices of the lattice of subgroups, in the sense that every lattice is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_isomorphism) to a sublattice of the subgroup lattice of some group. Furthermore, every [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) lattice is isomorphic to a sublattice of the subgroup lattice of some [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) (Schmidt 1994, 9). Every finite [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883) is also isomorphic to the normal subgroup lattice of some group (Silcock 1977).

:::

## Characteristic lattices {#Characteristic_lattices}

Subgroups with certain properties form lattices, but other properties do not.

- [Normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918) always form a [modular lattice](wikipedia://en/1089311). In fact, the essential property that guarantees that the lattice is modular is that subgroups commute with each other, i.e. that they are [quasinormal subgroups](wikipedia://en/2774267).
- [Nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) normal subgroups form a lattice, which is (part of) the content of [Fitting's theorem](wikipedia://en/4081611).
- A class of groups is called a *Fitting class* if it is closed under isomorphism, [subnormal subgroups](wikipedia://en/2774415), and products of subnormal subgroups. For any Fitting class *F*, both the subnormal *F*-subgroups and the normal *F*-subgroups form lattices. This generalizes the above with *F* the class of nilpotent groups, and another example is with *F* the class of [solvable groups](wikipedia://en/105012).
- [Central subgroups](wikipedia://en/4938766) form a lattice.

However, neither finite subgroups nor [torsion](wikipedia://en/3568695) subgroups form a lattice: for instance, the [free product](wikipedia://en/360036) $\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z} * \mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}$ is generated by two [torsion elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_element), but is [infinite](wikipedia://en/4587030) and contains elements of infinite order.

The fact that normal subgroups form a modular lattice is a particular case of a more general result, namely that in any [Maltsev variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maltsev_variety) (of which groups are an example), the [lattice of congruences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_lattice) is modular (Kearnes Kiss 2013).

## Characterizing groups by their subgroup lattices {#Characterizing_groups_by_their_subgroup_lattices}

[Lattice-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory) information about the lattice of subgroups can sometimes be used to infer information about the original group, an idea that goes back to the work of (txt). For instance, as Ore [proved](wikipedia://en/82285), a group is [locally cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_cyclic_group) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its lattice of subgroups is [distributive](wikipedia://en/310883). If additionally the lattice satisfies the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189), then the group is cyclic.

Groups whose lattice of subgroups is a [complemented lattice](wikipedia://en/753521) are called [complemented groups](wikipedia://en/5780991) (Zacher 1953), and groups whose lattice of subgroups are [modular lattices](wikipedia://en/1089311) are called [Iwasawa groups](wikipedia://en/16355158) or modular groups (Iwasawa 1941). Lattice-theoretic characterizations of this type also exist for [solvable groups](wikipedia://en/105012) and [perfect groups](wikipedia://en/585271) (Suzuki 1951).
