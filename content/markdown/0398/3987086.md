# Treewidth

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Treewidth"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **treewidth** of an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) number which specifies, informally, how far the graph is from being a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560). The smallest treewidth is 1; the graphs with treewidth 1 are exactly the trees and the [forests](wikipedia://en/48560#Forest). An example of graphs with treewidth at most 2 are the [series–parallel graphs](wikipedia://en/9938559). The maximal graphs with treewidth exactly $k$ are called *[$k$-trees](wikipedia://en/31104438)*, and the graphs with treewidth at most $k$ are called *[partial $k$-trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_k-tree)*.[^n1] Many other well-studied graph families also have bounded treewidth.

:::

Treewidth may be formally defined in several equivalent ways: in terms of the size of the largest vertex set in a [tree decomposition](wikipedia://en/159023) of the graph, in terms of the size of the largest [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) in a [chordal completion](wikipedia://en/48088710) of the graph, in terms of the maximum order of a [haven](wikipedia://en/5133456) describing a strategy for a [pursuit–evasion](wikipedia://en/3857398) game on the graph, or in terms of the maximum order of a [bramble](wikipedia://en/36601188), a collection of connected subgraphs that all touch each other.

Treewidth is commonly used as a parameter in the [parameterized complexity](wikipedia://en/603026) analysis of graph [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775). Many algorithms that are [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) for general graphs, become easier when the treewidth is bounded by a constant.

The concept of treewidth was originally introduced by (txt) under the name of *dimension*. It was later rediscovered by (txt), based on properties that it shares with a different graph parameter, the [Hadwiger number](wikipedia://en/1665366). Later it was again rediscovered by (txt) and has since been studied by many other authors.[^o6]

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Tree decomposition.svg.* A graph with eight vertices, and a tree decomposition of it onto a tree with six nodes. Each graph edge connects two vertices that are listed together at some tree node, and each graph vertex is listed at the nodes of a contiguous subtree of the tree. Each tree node lists at most three vertices, so the width of this decomposition is two.

A [tree decomposition](wikipedia://en/159023) of a graph $G=(V,E)$ is a tree $T$ in which each node is associated with a subset of vertices called a "bag". (The term *node* is used to refer to a vertex of $T$ to avoid confusion with vertices of $G$). The bags $X_1,\dots X_t$ must satisfy the following properties:[^o9]

1. Each graph vertex is contained in at least one bag: $\textstyle\bigcup_i X_i=V$
1. If bags $X_i$ and $X_j$ both contain a vertex $v$, then all bags $X_k$ associated with nodes in the (unique) path of $T$ between $X_i$ and $X_j$ also contain $v$ as well. Equivalently, the bags containing vertex $v$ are associated with a connected subtree of $T$.
1. For every edge $(v,w)$ in the graph, there is at least one bag $X_i$ that contains both $v$ and $w$. That is, vertices are adjacent in the graph only when their corresponding subtrees have a node in common. (However, two vertices may belong to a bag without being adjacent to each other.)

The *width* of a tree decomposition is the size of its largest bag $X_i$ minus one. The **treewidth** $\operatorname{tw}(G)$ of a graph $G$ is the minimum width among all possible tree decompositions of $G$. In this definition, the size of the largest set is diminished by one in order to make the treewidth of a tree equal to one.

Equivalently, the treewidth of $G$ is one less than the size of the largest [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) in the [chordal graph](wikipedia://en/744165) containing $G$ with the smallest [clique number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_clique). A chordal graph with this clique size may be obtained by adding to $G$ an edge between every two vertices for which at least one bag contains both vertices.

Treewidth may also be characterized in terms of [havens](wikipedia://en/5133456), functions describing an evasion strategy for a certain [pursuit–evasion](wikipedia://en/3857398) game defined on a graph. A graph $G$ has treewidth $k$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it has a haven of order $k+1$ but of no higher order, where a haven of order $k+1$ is a function $\beta$ that maps each set $X$ of at most $k$ vertices in $G$ into one of the connected components of $G\setminus X$and that obeys the [monotonicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonicity) property that $\beta(Y)\subseteq\beta(X)$ whenever $X\subset Y$.

*Image: 3x3 grid graph haven.svg.* A [bramble](wikipedia://en/36601188) of order four in a 3×3 grid graph, the existence of which shows that the graph has treewidth at least 3

A similar characterization can also be made using [brambles](wikipedia://en/36601188), families of connected subgraphs that all touch each other (meaning either that they share a vertex or are connected by an edge).[^o10] The order of a bramble is the smallest [hitting set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitting_set) for the family of subgraphs, and the treewidth of a graph is one less than the maximum order of a bramble.

:::

::: {#o12 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Every [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_n$ has treewidth $n-1$. This is most easily seen using the definition of treewidth in terms of [chordal graphs](wikipedia://en/744165): the complete graph is already chordal, and adding more edges cannot reduce the size of its largest clique.

A connected graph with at least two vertices has treewidth 1 if and only if it is a tree. A tree has treewidth one by the same reasoning as for complete graphs (namely, it is chordal, and has maximum clique size two). Conversely, if a graph has a cycle, then every [chordal completion](wikipedia://en/48088710) of the graph includes at least one triangle consisting of three consecutive vertices of the cycle, from which it follows that its treewidth is at least two.

:::

## Bounded treewidth {#Bounded_treewidth}

### Graph families with bounded treewidth {#Graph_families_with_bounded_treewidth}

For any fixed constant $k$, the graphs of treewidth at most $k$ are called the partial $k$-trees. Other families of graphs with bounded treewidth include the [cactus graphs](wikipedia://en/9944209), [pseudoforests](wikipedia://en/13511542), [series–parallel graphs](wikipedia://en/9938559), [outerplanar graphs](wikipedia://en/352343), [Halin graphs](wikipedia://en/19658548), and [Apollonian networks](wikipedia://en/31104610).[^o15] The [control-flow graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control-flow_graph) arising in the [compilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compiler) of [structured programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structured_programming) also have bounded treewidth, which allows certain tasks such as [register allocation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Register_allocation) to be performed efficiently on them.[^o16]

The [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) do not have bounded treewidth, because the $n\times n$ [grid graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_graph) is a planar graph with treewidth exactly $n$. Therefore, if $F$ is a [minor-closed graph family](wikipedia://en/353042#Minor-closed_graph_families) with bounded treewidth, it cannot include all planar graphs. Conversely, if some planar graph cannot occur as a minor for graphs in family $F$, then there is a constant $k$ such that all graphs in $F$ have treewidth at most $k$. That is, the following three conditions are equivalent to each other:[^o17]

1. $F$ is a minor-closed family of bounded-treewidth graphs;
1. One of the finitely many forbidden minors characterizing $F$ is planar;
1. $F$ is a minor-closed graph family that does not include all planar graphs.

### Forbidden minors {#Forbidden_minors}

*Image: Partial 3-tree forbidden minors.svg.* The four forbidden minors for treewidth 3: $K_{5}$ (top-left), the graph of the octahedron (bottom-left), the Wagner graph (top-right), and the graph of the pentagonal prism (bottom-right)

For every finite value of $k$, the graphs of treewidth at most $k$ may be characterized by a finite set of [forbidden minors](wikipedia://en/13547826). (That is, any graph of treewidth greater than $k$ includes one of the graphs in the set as a minor.) Each of these sets of forbidden minors includes at least one planar graph.

- For $k=1$, the unique forbidden minor is a 3-vertex [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962).[^o19]
- For $k=2$, the unique forbidden minor is the 4-vertex [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_4$.[^o19]
- For $k=3$, there are four forbidden minors: $K_5$, the graph of the [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), the [pentagonal](wikipedia://en/2859659) [prism graph](wikipedia://en/45012199), and the [Wagner graph](wikipedia://en/17598450). Of these, the two [polyhedral graphs](wikipedia://en/24656270) are planar.[^o20]

For larger values of $k$, the number of forbidden minors grows at least as quickly as an exponential function of $\sqrt k$.[^n2] However, known upper bounds on the size and number of forbidden minors are much higher than this lower bound.[^n3]

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

### Computing the treewidth {#Computing_the_treewidth}

It is $\mathsf{NP}$-complete to determine whether a given graph $G$ has treewidth at most a given variable $k$.[^n4] However, when $k$ is any fixed constant, the graphs with treewidth $k$ can be recognized, and a width $k$ tree decomposition constructed for them, in linear time.[^o27] The time dependence of this algorithm on $k$ is exponential.

Due to the roles the treewidth plays in an enormous number of fields, different practical and theoretical algorithms computing the treewidth of a graph were developed. Depending on the application on hand, one can prefer better [approximation ratio](wikipedia://en/563105), or better dependence in the running time from the size of the input or the treewidth. The table below provides an overview of some of the treewidth algorithms. Here $k$ is the treewidth and $n$ is the number of vertices of an input graph $G$. Each of the algorithms outputs in time $f(k)\cdot g(n)$ a decomposition of width given in the Approximation column. For example, the algorithm of (Bodlaender 1996) in time $2^{O(k^3)}\cdot n$ either constructs a tree decomposition of the input graph $G$ of width at most $k$ or reports that the treewidth of $G$ is more than $k$. Similarly, the algorithm of (Bodlaender Drange Dregi Fomin 2016) in time $2^{O(k)}\cdot n$ either constructs a tree decomposition of the input graph $G$ of width at most $5k+4$ or reports that the treewidth of $G$ is more than $k$. (Korhonen 2021) improved the width of the decomposition to $2k+1$ in the same running time.

::: {#o49 .problem cue=unsolved}

Can the treewidth of [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) be computed in polynomial time?

:::

It is not known whether determining the treewidth of [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314) is NP-complete, or whether their treewidth can be computed in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time).[^n5]

In practice, an algorithm of (Shoikhet Geiger 1997) can determine the treewidth of graphs with up to 100 vertices and treewidth up to 11, finding a chordal completion of these graphs with the optimal treewidth.

For larger graphs, one can use search-based techniques such as [branch and bound search](wikipedia://en/456580) to compute the treewidth. These algorithms are [anytime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anytime_algorithm) in that when stopped early, they will output an upper bound on the treewidth. An algorithm of this type was proposed in 2004 by Vibhav Gogate and [Rina Dechter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rina_Dechter). To provide a lower bound on treewidth for the branches of this search, they construct a [graph minor](wikipedia://en/353042) by repeatedly contracting an edge between a minimum degree vertex and one of its neighbors, until just one vertex remains. The maximum of the minimum degree over these constructed minors is guaranteed to be a lower bound on the treewidth of the graph.[^n6] Alex Dow and Rich Korf further improved this algorithm using [best-first search](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best-first_search).[^n7]

### Solving other problems on graphs of small treewidth {#Solving_other_problems_on_graphs_of_small_treewidth}

At the beginning of the 1970s, it was observed that a large class of [combinatorial optimization](wikipedia://en/420555) problems defined on graphs could be efficiently solved by non serial [dynamic programming](wikipedia://en/125297) as long as the graph had a bounded *dimension*,[^n8] a parameter shown to be equivalent to treewidth by (Bodlaender 1998). Later, several authors independently observed at the end of the 1980s[^o57] that many algorithmic problems that are [NP-complete](wikipedia://en/23385892) for arbitrary graphs may be solved efficiently by [dynamic programming](wikipedia://en/125297) for graphs of bounded treewidth, using the tree-decompositions of these graphs.

As an example, the problem of [coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) a graph of treewidth $k$ may be solved by using a [dynamic programming](wikipedia://en/125297) algorithm on a tree decomposition of the graph. For each bag $X_i$ of the tree decomposition, and each [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of the vertices of $X_i$ into color classes, the algorithm determines whether that coloring is valid and can be extended to all descendant nodes in the tree decomposition, by combining information of a similar type computed and stored at those nodes. The resulting algorithm finds an optimal coloring of an $n$-vertex graph in time $O(k^{k+O(1)}n)$, a time bound that makes this problem [fixed-parameter tractable](wikipedia://en/603026).

::: {#o59 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Courcelle's theorem"}

### Courcelle's theorem {#Courcelle's_theorem}

For a large class of problems, there is a linear time algorithm to solve a problem from the class if a [tree-decomposition](wikipedia://en/159023) with constant bounded treewidth is provided. Specifically, [Courcelle's theorem](wikipedia://en/35810608)[^o60] states that if a graph problem can be expressed in the [logic of graphs](wikipedia://en/47857242) using [monadic second order logic](wikipedia://en/14650395), then it can be solved in linear time on graphs with bounded treewidth. Monadic second order logic is a language to describe graph properties that uses the following constructions:

- Logic operations, such as $\wedge ,\vee ,\neg ,\Rightarrow$
- Membership tests, such as $e\in E$, $v\in V$
- Quantifications over vertices, edges, sets of vertices, and/or sets of edges, such as $\forall v\in V$, $\exists e\in E$, $\exists I\subset V$, $\forall F\subset E$
- Vertex–edge incidence tests ($u$ is an endpoint of $e$), and some extensions that allow for things such as optimization.

Consider for example the [3-coloring problem](wikipedia://en/426743) for graphs. For a graph $G=(V,E)$, this problem asks if it is possible to assign each vertex $v\in V$ one of three colors such that no two adjacent vertices are assigned the same color. This problem can be expressed in monadic second order logic as

$$\exists W_1 \subseteq V\ \exists W_2 \subseteq V\ \exists W_3 \subseteq V\ \left( \begin{aligned} & \forall v \in V\ (v \in W_1 \vee v \in W_2 \vee v \in W_3) \wedge{} \\ & \operatorname{independent}(W_1) \wedge{} \\ & \operatorname{independent}(W_2) \wedge{} \\ & \operatorname{independent}(W_3) \\ \end{aligned} \right),$$

where $W_1$,$W_2$, $W_3$ represent the subsets of vertices having each of the three colors, and where the subexpressions $\operatorname{independent}(W_i)$ are defined to mean

$$\operatorname{independent}(W_i)\equiv \forall e\in E\ \exists v\in V\ (\operatorname{endpoint}(v,e)\wedge \neg v\in W_i).$$

(It is not necessary to include in this formula the requirement that the sets $W_i$ be disjoint.) Therefore, by Courcelle's results, the 3-coloring problem can be solved in linear time for a graph given a tree-decomposition of bounded constant treewidth.

## Related parameters {#Related_parameters}

### Pathwidth {#Pathwidth}

The [pathwidth](wikipedia://en/5133142) of a graph has a very similar definition to treewidth via tree decompositions, but is restricted to tree decompositions in which the underlying tree of the decomposition is a [path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190). Alternatively, the pathwidth may be defined from [interval graphs](wikipedia://en/363423) analogously to the definition of treewidth from chordal graphs. As a consequence, the pathwidth of a graph is always at least as large as its treewidth, but it can only be larger by a logarithmic factor.[^o19] Another parameter, the [graph bandwidth](wikipedia://en/27414778), has an analogous definition from [proper interval graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_interval_graph), and is at least as large as the pathwidth. Other related parameters include the [tree-depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree-depth), a number that is bounded for a minor-closed graph family if and only if the family excludes a path, and the [degeneracy](wikipedia://en/27545816), a measure of the sparsity of a graph that is at most equal to its treewidth.

::: {#o64 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Grid minor theorem"}

### Grid minor size {#Grid_minor_size}

Because the treewidth of an $n\times n$ [grid graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grid_graph) is $n$, the treewidth of a graph $G$ is always greater than or equal to the size of the largest square grid [minor](wikipedia://en/353042) of $G$. In the other direction, the *grid minor theorem* by [Robertson](wikipedia://en/1575704) and [Seymour](wikipedia://en/1575708) shows that there exists an unbounded function $f$ such that the largest square grid minor of a graph of treewidth $k$ has size at least $f(k)$.[^n9] The best bounds known on$f$ are that $f$ must be at least $\Omega(k^d)$ for some fixed constant $d>0$, and at most[^o66]

$$O \left( \sqrt{ r / \log r} \right).$$

(For the $\Omega$ notation in the lower bound, see [big O notation](wikipedia://en/44578).) Tighter bounds are known for restricted graph families, leading to efficient algorithms for many graph optimization problems on those families through the theory of [bidimensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bidimensionality).[^n10] [Halin's grid theorem](wikipedia://en/38580904) provides an analogue of the relation between treewidth and grid minor size for infinite graphs.[^n11]

:::

### Diameter and local treewidth {#Diameter_and_local_treewidth}

A family $F$ of graphs closed under taking subgraphs is said to have **bounded local treewidth**, or the **diameter-treewidth property**, if the treewidth of the graphs in the family is [upper bounded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_bound) by a function of their [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341). If the class is also assumed to be closed under taking [minors](wikipedia://en/353042), then $F$ has bounded local treewidth if and only if one of the [forbidden minors](wikipedia://en/13547826) for $F$ is an [apex graph](wikipedia://en/28228942).[^n12] The original proofs of this result showed that treewidth in an apex-minor-free graph family grows at most doubly exponentially as a function of diameter;[^o71] later this was reduced to singly exponential[^n10] and finally to a linear bound.[^n13] Bounded local treewidth is closely related to the algorithmic theory of [bidimensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bidimensionality),[^o74] and every [graph property](wikipedia://en/1655142) definable in first order logic can be decided for an apex-minor-free graph family in an amount of time that is only slightly superlinear.[^n14]

It is also possible for a class of graphs that is not closed under minors to have bounded local treewidth. In particular this is trivially true for a class of bounded degree graphs, as bounded diameter subgraphs have bounded size. Another example is given by [1-planar graphs](wikipedia://en/38249632), graphs that can be drawn in the plane with one crossing per edge, and more generally for the graphs that can be drawn on a surface of bounded genus with a bounded number of crossings per edge. As with minor-closed graph families of bounded local treewidth, this property has pointed the way to efficient approximation algorithms for these graphs.[^n15]

### Hadwiger number and $S$-functions {#Hadwiger_number_and_-functions}

(Halin 1976) defines a class of graph parameters that he calls $S$-functions, which include the treewidth. These functions from graphs to integers are required to be zero on [graphs with no edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_graph), to be [minor-monotone](wikipedia://en/353042) (a function $f$ is referred to as "minor-monotone" if, whenever $H$ is a minor of $G$, one has $f(H)\le f(G)$), to increase by one when a new vertex is added that is [adjacent to all previous vertices](wikipedia://en/51352780), and to take the larger value from the two subgraphs on either side of a [clique](wikipedia://en/524466) [separator](wikipedia://en/1499165). The set of all such functions forms a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777) under the operations of elementwise minimization and maximization. The top element in this lattice is the treewidth, and the bottom element is the [Hadwiger number](wikipedia://en/1665366), the size of the largest [complete](wikipedia://en/85816) [minor](wikipedia://en/353042) in the given graph.
