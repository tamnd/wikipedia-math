# Covering graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Covering graph"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) discipline of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $C$ is a **covering graph** of another graph $G$ if there is a **covering map** from the [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) set of $C$ to the vertex set of $G$. A covering map $f$ is a [surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection) and a local [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828): the [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/7309251) of a vertex $v$ in $C$ is mapped [bijectively](wikipedia://en/3942) onto the neighbourhood of $f(v)$ in $G$.

:::

The term **lift** is often used as a synonym for a covering graph of a [connected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph).

Though it may be misleading, there is no (obvious) relationship between covering graph and [vertex cover](wikipedia://en/562782) or [edge cover](wikipedia://en/4577392).

The [combinatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial) formulation of covering graphs is immediately generalized to the case of [multigraphs](wikipedia://en/1774970). A covering graph is a special case of a covering complex.[^o2] Both covering complexes and multigraphs with a 1-dimensional cell complex, are nothing but examples of [covering spaces](wikipedia://en/250323) of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), so the terminology in the theory of covering spaces is available; say covering transformation group, universal covering, abelian covering, and maximal abelian covering.[^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *G* = (*V*<sub>1</sub>, *E*<sub>1</sub>) and *C* = (*V*<sub>2</sub>, *E*<sub>2</sub>) be two graphs, and let *f*: *V*<sub>2</sub> → *V*<sub>1</sub> be a [surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection). Then *f* is a *covering map* from *C* to *G* if for each *v* ∈ *V*<sub>2</sub>, the restriction of *f* to the [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/7309251) of *v* is a bijection onto the neighbourhood of *f*(*v*) in *G*. Put otherwise, *f* maps edges incident to *v* one-to-one onto edges incident to *f*(*v*).

If there exists a covering map from *C* to *G*, then *C* is a *covering graph*, or a *lift*, of *G*. An *h-lift* is a lift such that the covering map *f* has the property that for every vertex *v* of *G*, its *fiber* *f<sup>−1</sup>(v)* has exactly *h* elements.

:::

## Double cover {#Double_cover}

In the above example, each vertex of *H* has exactly 2 preimages in *C*. Hence *C* is a *2-fold cover* or a *double cover* of *H*.

For any graph *G*, it is possible to construct the [bipartite double cover](wikipedia://en/21241712) of *G*, which is a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) and a double cover of *G*. The bipartite double cover of *G* is the [tensor product of graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_graphs) *G* × *K*<sub>2</sub>:

*Image: Covering-graph-2.svg.*

If *G* is already bipartite, its bipartite double cover consists of two disjoint copies of *G*. A graph may have many different double covers other than the bipartite double cover.

## Universal cover {#Universal_cover}

For any connected graph *G*, it is possible to construct its **universal covering graph**.[^o10] This is an instance of the more general [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) concept from topology; the topological requirement that a universal cover be [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) translates in graph-theoretic terms to a requirement that it be acyclic and connected; that is, a [tree](wikipedia://en/48560). The universal covering graph is unique (up to isomorphism). If *G* is a tree, then *G* itself is the universal covering graph of *G*. For any other finite connected graph *G*, the universal covering graph of *G* is a countably infinite (but locally finite) tree.

The universal covering graph *T* of a connected graph *G* can be constructed as follows. Choose an arbitrary vertex *r* of *G* as a starting point. Each vertex of *T* is a non-backtracking walk that begins from *r*, that is, a sequence *w* = (*r*, *v*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *v*<sub>n</sub>) of vertices of *G* such that

- *v*<sub>*i*</sub> and *v*<sub>*i*+1</sub> are adjacent in *G* for all *i*, i.e., *w* is a walk
- *v*<sub>*i*-1</sub> ≠ *v*<sub>*i*+1</sub> for all *i*, i.e., w is non-backtracking.

Then, two vertices of *T* are adjacent if one is a [simple extension](wikipedia://en/4771726) of another: the vertex (*r*, *v*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *v*<sub>*n*</sub>) is adjacent to the vertex (*r*, *v*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *v*<sub>*n*-1</sub>). Up to isomorphism, the same tree *T* is constructed regardless of the choice of the starting point *r*.

The covering map *f* maps the vertex (*r*) in *T* to the vertex *r* in *G*, and a vertex (*r*, *v*<sub>1</sub>, *v*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *v*<sub>*n*</sub>) in *T* to the vertex *v*<sub>*n*</sub> in *G*.

## Topological crystal {#Topological_crystal}

An infinite-fold abelian covering graph of a finite (multi)graph is called a topological crystal, an abstraction of crystal structures. For example, the diamond crystal as a graph is the maximal abelian covering graph of the four-edge [dipole graph](wikipedia://en/6412575). This view combined with the idea of "standard realizations" turns out to be useful in a systematic design of (hypothetical) crystals.[^o3]

## Planar cover {#Planar_cover}

A [planar cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planar_cover) of a graph is a finite covering graph that is itself a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314). The property of having a planar cover may be characterized by [forbidden minors](wikipedia://en/13547826), but the exact characterization of this form remains unknown. Every graph with an [embedding](wikipedia://en/8149170) in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) has a planar cover coming from the [orientable double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable_double_cover) of the projective plane; in 1988, Seiya Nagami conjectured that these are the only graphs with planar covers, but this remains unproven.[^o14]

## Voltage graphs {#Voltage_graphs}

A common way to form covering graphs uses [voltage graphs](wikipedia://en/8802504), in which the darts of the given graph *G* (that is, pairs of directed edges corresponding to the undirected edges of *G*) are labeled with inverse pairs of elements from some [group](wikipedia://en/19447). The derived graph of the voltage graph has as its vertices the pairs (*v*,*x*) where *v* is a vertex of *G* and *x* is a group element; a dart from *v* to *w* labeled with the group element *y* in *G* corresponds to an edge from (*v*,*x*) to (*w*,*xy*) in the derived graph.

The universal cover can be seen in this way as a derived graph of a voltage graph in which the edges of a [spanning tree](wikipedia://en/455770) of the graph are labeled by the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of the group, and each remaining pair of darts is labeled by a distinct generating element of a [free group](wikipedia://en/59735). The bipartite double can be seen in this way as a derived graph of a voltage graph in which each dart is labeled by the nonzero element of the group of order two.
