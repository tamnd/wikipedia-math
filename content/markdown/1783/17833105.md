# Weak gravitational lensing

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weak gravitational lensing"}

In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), **weak gravitational lensing** is a technique to map the mass distribution of astronomical objects. As predicted by [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), the presence of any mass bends the path of light passing near it, producing [gravitational lensing](wikipedia://en/48824). This effect rarely produces the giant arcs and multiple images associated with [strong gravitational lensing](wikipedia://en/18117469). Most lines of sight in the universe are thoroughly in the weak lensing regime, in which the [deflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflection_%28physics%29) is impossible to detect in a single background source. However, even in these cases, the presence of the foreground mass can be detected, by way of a systematic alignment of background sources around the lensing mass. Weak gravitational lensing is thus an intrinsically statistical measurement, but it provides a way to measure the masses of astronomical objects without requiring assumptions about their composition or dynamical state.

:::

The field was pioneered by [J. Anthony Tyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Anthony_Tyson) in the 1990s.

## Methodology {#Methodology}

*Image: Shapenoise.svg.* Distortions of the type produced by lensing, acting on circles and a distribution of ellipses similar to that of real galaxies. The distortion shown here is greatly exaggerated relative to real astronomical systems.

[Gravitational lensing](wikipedia://en/48824) acts as a [coordinate transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_transformation) that distorts the images of background objects (usually galaxies) near a foreground mass. The transformation can be split into two terms, the [convergence and shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_Lensing_Formalism#Lensing_Jacobian). The convergence term magnifies the background objects by increasing their size, and the shear term stretches them tangentially around the foreground mass. Moreover, the higher order image distortion, which is called *flexion* can bend the galaxy images into arclets.

To measure this tangential alignment, it is necessary to measure the [ellipticities](wikipedia://en/2043875) of the background galaxies and construct a statistical estimate of their systematic alignment. The fundamental problem is that galaxies are not intrinsically circular, so their measured ellipticity is a combination of their intrinsic ellipticity and the gravitational lensing shear. Typically, the intrinsic ellipticity is much greater than the shear (by a factor of 3-300, depending on the foreground mass). The measurements of many background galaxies must be combined to average down this "shape noise". The orientation of intrinsic ellipticities of galaxies should be almost[^o3] entirely random, so any systematic alignment between multiple galaxies can generally be assumed to be caused by lensing.

Another major challenge for weak lensing is correction for the [point spread function](wikipedia://en/865348) (PSF) due to instrumental and atmospheric effects, which causes the observed images to be smeared relative to the "true sky". This smearing tends to make small objects more round, destroying some of the information about their true ellipticity. As a further complication, the PSF typically adds a small level of ellipticity to objects in the image, which is not at all random, and can in fact mimic a true lensing signal. Even for the most modern telescopes, this effect is usually at least the same order of magnitude as the gravitational lensing shear, and is often much larger. Correcting for the PSF requires building a model for the telescope for how it varies across the field. Stars in our own galaxy provide a direct measurement of the PSF, and these can be used to construct such a model, usually by [interpolating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpolating) between the points where stars appear on the image. This model can then be used to reconstruct the "true" ellipticities from the smeared ones. Ground-based and space-based data typically undergo distinct reduction procedures due to the differences in instruments and observing conditions.

[Angular diameter distances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_diameter_distance) to the lenses and background sources are important for converting the lensing observables to physically meaningful quantities. These distances are often estimated using [photometric redshifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photometric_redshift) when [spectroscopic redshifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy) are unavailable. Redshift information is also important in separating the background source population from other galaxies in the foreground, or those associated with the mass responsible for the lensing. With no redshift information, the foreground and background populations can be split by an [apparent magnitude](wikipedia://en/1962) or a [color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_index) cut, but this is much less accurate.

## Weak lensing by clusters of galaxies {#Weak_lensing_by_clusters_of_galaxies}

*Image: Gravitational-lensing-3d.png.* The effects of foreground galaxy cluster mass on background galaxy shapes. The upper left panel shows (projected onto the plane of the sky) the shapes of cluster members (in yellow) and background galaxies (in white), ignoring the effects of weak lensing. The lower right panel shows this same scenario, but includes the effects of lensing. The middle panel shows a 3-d representation of the positions of cluster and source galaxies, relative to the observer. Note that the background galaxies appear stretched tangentially around the cluster.

[Galaxy clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy_clusters) are the largest [gravitationally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation) bound structures in the [Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe) with approximately 80% of cluster content in the form of [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter).[^o5] The gravitational fields of these clusters deflect light-rays traveling near them. As seen from [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228), this effect can cause dramatic distortions of a background source object detectable by eye such as multiple images, arcs, and rings (cluster strong lensing). More generally, the effect causes small, but statistically coherent, distortions of background sources on the order of 10% (cluster weak lensing). [Abell 1689](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abell_1689), [CL0024+17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CL0024+17), and the [Bullet Cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet_Cluster) are among the most prominent examples of lensing clusters.

### History {#History}

The effects of cluster strong lensing were first detected by Roger Lynds of the [National Optical Astronomy Observatories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Optical_Astronomy_Observatory) and Vahe Petrosian of [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) who discovered giant luminous arcs in a survey of galaxy clusters in the late 1970s. Lynds and Petrosian published their findings in 1986 without knowing the origin of the arcs.[^o7] In 1987, Genevieve Soucail of the [Toulouse Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulouse_Observatory) and her collaborators presented data of a blue ring-like structure in [Abell 370](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abell_370) and proposed a gravitational lensing interpretation.[^o8] The first cluster weak lensing analysis was conducted in 1990 by [J. Anthony Tyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Anthony_Tyson) of [Bell Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Laboratories) and collaborators. Tyson et al. detected a coherent alignment of the [ellipticities](wikipedia://en/9277) of the [faint blue galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faint_blue_galaxy) behind both [Abell 1689](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abell_1689) and [CL 1409+524](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CL_1409+524).[^o9] Lensing has been used as a tool to investigate a tiny fraction of the thousands of [known galaxy clusters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_galaxy_clusters).

Historically, lensing analyses were conducted on galaxy clusters detected via their [baryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon) content (e.g. from [optical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible-light_astronomy) or [X-ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_astronomy) surveys). The sample of galaxy clusters studied with lensing was thus subject to various selection effects; for example, only the most [luminous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminous_intensity) clusters were investigated. In 2006, David Wittman of the [University of California at Davis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California_at_Davis) and collaborators published the first sample of galaxy clusters detected via their lensing signals, completely independent of their baryon content.[^o10] Clusters discovered through lensing are subject to mass selection effects because the more massive clusters produce lensing signals with higher [signal-to-noise ratio](wikipedia://en/41706).

### Observational products {#Observational_products}

The projected [mass density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) can be recovered from the measurement of the ellipticities of the lensed background galaxies through techniques that can be classified into two types: direct reconstruction[^o12] and [inversion](wikipedia://en/203956).[^o13] However, a [mass distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_distribution) reconstructed without knowledge of the [magnification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_Lensing_Formalism#Magnification) suffers from a limitation known as the mass sheet [degeneracy](wikipedia://en/232952), where the cluster surface mass density κ can be determined only up to a [transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_%28geometry%29) $\kappa \rightarrow \kappa^{\prime} = \lambda \kappa+(1-\lambda)$ where λ is an arbitrary constant.[^o14] This degeneracy can be broken if an independent measurement of the magnification is available because the magnification is not [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under the aforementioned degeneracy transformation.

Given a [centroid](wikipedia://en/187926) for the cluster, which can be determined by using a reconstructed mass distribution or optical or X-ray data, a model can be fit to the shear profile as a function of clustrocentric radius. For example, the [singular isothermal sphere (SIS) profile](wikipedia://en/17724517) and the [Navarro-Frenk-White (NFW) profile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navarro-Frenk-White_profile) are two commonly used [parametric models](wikipedia://en/2883137). Knowledge of the lensing cluster [redshift](wikipedia://en/26262) and the redshift distribution of the background galaxies is also necessary for estimation of the mass and size from a model fit; these redshifts can be measured precisely using [spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectroscopy) or [estimated using photometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photometric_redshift). Individual mass estimates from weak lensing can only be derived for the most massive clusters, and the accuracy of these mass estimates are limited by projections along the line of sight.[^o15]

### Scientific implications {#Scientific_implications}

*Image: Bullet cluster lensing.jpg.* Image of the Bullet Cluster from the Hubble Space Telescope with total mass contours (dominated by dark matter) from a lensing analysis overlaid.

Cluster mass estimates determined by lensing are valuable because the method requires no assumption about the dynamical state or [star formation history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_formation) of the cluster in question. Lensing mass maps can also potentially reveal "dark clusters," clusters containing overdense concentrations of dark matter but relatively insignificant amounts of baryonic matter. Comparison of the dark matter distribution mapped using lensing with the distribution of the baryons using optical and X-ray data reveals the interplay of the dark matter with the [stellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) and [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intracluster_medium) components. A notable example of such a joint analysis is the so-called [Bullet Cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bullet_Cluster).[^o17] The Bullet Cluster data provide constraints on models relating light, gas, and dark matter distributions such as [Modified Newtonian dynamics (MOND)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOND) and [Λ-Cold Dark Matter (Λ-CDM)](wikipedia://en/985963).

In principle, since the number density of clusters as a function of mass and redshift is sensitive to the underlying [cosmology](wikipedia://en/5378), cluster counts derived from large weak lensing [surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_survey) should be able to constrain cosmological parameters. In practice, however, projections along the line of sight cause many [false positives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/False_positives).[^o18] Weak lensing can also be used to [calibrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calibrate) the mass-observable relation via a stacked weak lensing signal around an ensemble of clusters, although this relation is expected to have an intrinsic [scatter](wikipedia://en/32344).[^o19] In order for lensing clusters to be a precision probe of cosmology in the future, the projection effects and the scatter in the lensing mass-observable relation need to be thoroughly characterized and modeled.

## Galaxy-galaxy lensing {#Galaxy-galaxy_lensing}

Galaxy-galaxy lensing is a specific type of weak (and occasionally strong) [gravitational lensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_lensing), in which the foreground object responsible for distorting the shapes of background galaxies is itself an individual [field galaxy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_galaxy) (as opposed to a [galaxy cluster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy_cluster) or the [large-scale structure of the cosmos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large-scale_structure_of_the_cosmos)). Of the three typical mass regimes in weak lensing, galaxy-galaxy lensing produces a "mid-range" signal (shear correlations of ~1%) that is weaker than the signal due to cluster lensing, but stronger than the signal due to cosmic shear.

### History {#History}

[J. Anthony Tyson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._Anthony_Tyson) and collaborators first postulated the concept of galaxy-galaxy lensing in 1984, though the observational results of their study were inconclusive.[^o22] It was not until 1996 that evidence of such distortion was tentatively discovered,[^o23] with the first statistically significant results not published until the year 2000.[^o24] Since those initial discoveries, the construction of larger, high resolution telescopes and the advent of dedicated wide field [galaxy surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift_survey) have greatly increased the observed number density of both background source and foreground lens galaxies, allowing for a much more robust statistical sample of galaxies, making the lensing signal much easier to detect. Today, measuring the shear signal due to galaxy-galaxy lensing is a widely used technique in [observational astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy) and [cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmology), often used in parallel with other measurements in determining physical characteristics of foreground galaxies.

### Stacking {#Stacking}

Much like in [cluster-scale weak lensing](wikipedia://en/17833105#Weak_lensing_by_clusters_of_galaxies), detection of a galaxy-galaxy shear signal requires one to measure the shapes of background source galaxies, and then look for statistical shape correlations (specifically, source galaxy shapes should be aligned tangentially, relative to the lens center.) In principle, this signal could be measured around any individual foreground lens. In practice, however, due to the relatively low mass of field lenses and the inherent randomness in intrinsic shape of background sources (the "shape noise"), the signal is impossible to measure on a galaxy-by-galaxy basis. However, by combining the signals of many individual lens measurements together (a technique known as "stacking"), the signal-to-noise ratio will improve, allowing one to determine a statistically significant signal, averaged over the entire lens set.

### Scientific applications {#Scientific_applications}

Galaxy-galaxy lensing (like all other types of gravitational lensing) is used to measure several quantities pertaining to [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass):

**Mass density profiles**

Using techniques similar to those in cluster-scale lensing, galaxy-galaxy lensing can provide information about the shape of mass density profiles, though these profiles correspond to galaxy-sized objects instead of larger clusters or groups. Given a high enough number density of background sources, a typical galaxy-galaxy mass density profile can cover a wide range of distances (from ~1 to ~100 [effective radii](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy_effective_radius)).[^o27] Since the effects of lensing are insensitive to the matter type, a galaxy-galaxy mass density profile can be used to probe a wide range of matter environments: from the central cores of galaxies where [baryons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon) dominate the total mass fraction, to the outer [halos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter_halo) where [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter) is more prevalent.

**Mass-to-light ratios**

Comparing the measured mass to the [luminosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luminosity) (averaged over the entire galaxy stack) in a specific [filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter_%28optics%29), galaxy-galaxy lensing can also provide insight into the [mass to light ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_to_light_ratio) of field galaxies. Specifically, the quantity measured through lensing is the total (or [virial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virial)) mass to light ratio – again due to the insensitivity of lensing to matter type. Assuming that luminous matter can trace dark matter, this quantity is of particular importance, since measuring the ratio of luminous (baryonic) matter to total matter can provide information regarding the overall ratio of baryonic to dark matter in the universe.[^o28]

**Galaxy mass evolution**

Since the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) is finite, an observer on the Earth will see distant galaxies not as they look today, but rather as they appeared at some earlier time. By restricting the lens sample of a galaxy-galaxy lensing study to lie at only one particular redshift, it is possible to understand the mass properties of the field galaxies that existed during this earlier time. Comparing the results of several such redshift-restricted lensing studies (with each study encompassing a different redshift), one can begin to observe changes in the mass features of galaxies over a period of several [epochs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_the_big_bang), leading towards a better understanding of the evolution of mass on the smallest cosmological scales.[^o29]

**Other mass trends**

Lens redshift is not the only quantity of interest that can be varied when studying mass differences between galaxy populations, and often there are several parameters used when segregating objects into galaxy-galaxy lens stacks.[^o30][^o31] Two widely used criteria are galaxy [color](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_index) and [morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphology_%28astronomy%29), which act as tracers of (among other things) [stellar population](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_population), galaxy age, and local mass environment. By separating lens galaxies based on these properties, and then further segregating samples based on redshift, it is possible to use galaxy-galaxy lensing to see how several different types of galaxies evolve through time.

## Cosmic shear {#Cosmic_shear}

The gravitational lensing by [large-scale structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large-scale_structure_of_the_universe) also produces intrinsic alignment (IA) - an observable pattern of alignments in background galaxies.[^o34][^o35] This distortion is only ~0.1%-1% - much more subtle than cluster or galaxy-galaxy lensing. The [thin lens approximation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_Lensing_Formalism#Thin_lens_approximation) usually used in cluster and galaxy lensing does not always work in this regime, because structures can be elongated along the line of sight. Instead, the distortion can be derived by assuming that the deflection angle is always small (see [Gravitational Lensing Formalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_Lensing_Formalism)). As in the thin lens case, the effect can be written as a mapping from the unlensed angular position $\vec{\beta}$ to the lensed position $\vec{\theta}$. The [Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351) of the transform can be written as an integral over the gravitational potential $\Phi$ along the line of sight

$$\frac{\partial \beta_i}{\partial \theta_j} = \delta_{ij} + \int_0^{r_\infty} dr g(r) \frac{\partial^2 \Phi(\vec{x}(r))}{\partial x^i \partial x^j}$$

where $r$ is the [comoving distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoving_distance), $x^i$ are the transverse distances, and

$$g(r) = 2 r \int^{r_\infty}_r dr^\prime \left(1-\frac{r^\prime}{r}\right)W(r^\prime)$$

is the *lensing kernel*, which defines the efficiency of lensing for a distribution of sources $W(r)$.

As in the thin-lens approximation, the Jacobian can be decomposed into [shear and convergence terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_Lensing_Formalism#Lensing_Jacobian).

### Shear correlation functions {#Shear_correlation_functions}

Because large-scale cosmological structures do not have a well-defined location, detecting cosmological gravitational lensing typically involves the computation of *shear correlation functions*, which measure the mean product of the shear at two points as a function of the distance between those points. Because there are two components of shear, three different correlation functions can be defined:

$$\xi_{++}(\Delta\theta) = \langle \gamma_+(\vec{\theta}) \gamma_+(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\Delta\theta}) \rangle$$

$$\xi_{\times\times}(\Delta\theta) = \langle \gamma_\times(\vec{\theta}) \gamma_\times(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\Delta\theta}) \rangle$$

$$\xi_{\times +}(\Delta\theta)=\xi_{+ \times}(\Delta\theta) = \langle \gamma_+(\vec{\theta}) \gamma_\times(\vec{\theta}+\vec{\Delta\theta}) \rangle$$

where $\gamma_{+~}$ is the component along or perpendicular to $\vec{\Delta\theta}$, and $\gamma_\times$ is the component at 45°. These correlation functions are typically computed by averaging over many pairs of galaxies. The last correlation function, $\xi_{\times +}$, is not affected at all by lensing, so measuring a value for this function that is inconsistent with zero is often interpreted as a sign of [systematic error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systematic_error).

The functions $\xi_{++~}$ and $\xi_{\times\times}$ can be related to projections (integrals with certain weight functions) of the dark matter density correlation function, which can be predicted from theory for a cosmological model through its Fourier transform, the [matter power spectrum](wikipedia://en/17780458).[^o37]

Because they both depend on a single scalar density field, $\xi_{++~}$ and $\xi_{\times\times}$ are not independent, and they can be decomposed further into *E-mode* and *B-mode* correlation functions.[^o38] In analogy with electric and magnetic fields, the E-mode field is curl-free and the B-mode field is divergence-free. Because gravitational lensing can only produce an E-mode field, the B-mode provides yet another test for systematic errors.

The E-mode correlation function is also known as the *aperture mass variance*

$$\langle M_{ap}^2 \rangle (\theta) = \int_0^{2\theta} \frac{\phi d\phi}{\theta^2} \left[\xi_{++}(\phi)+\xi_{\times\times}(\phi)\right] T_+\left(\frac{\phi}{\theta}\right) = \int_0^{2\theta} \frac{\phi d\phi}{\theta^2} \left[\xi_{++}(\phi)-\xi_{\times\times}(\phi)\right] T_-\left(\frac{\phi}{\theta}\right)$$

$$T_+(x) = 576\int^\infty_0 \frac{dt}{t^3}J_0(xt)[J_4(t)]^2$$

$$T_-(x) = 576\int^\infty_0 \frac{dt}{t^3}J_4(xt)[J_4(t)]^2$$

where $J_0~$ and $J_4~$ are [Bessel Functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_Functions).

An exact decomposition thus requires knowledge of the shear correlation functions at zero separation, but an approximate decomposition is fairly insensitive to these values because the filters $T_+~$ and $T_-~$ are small near $\theta=0~$.

### Weak lensing and cosmology {#Weak_lensing_and_cosmology}

The ability of weak lensing to constrain the [matter power spectrum](wikipedia://en/17780458) makes it a potentially powerful probe of cosmological parameters, especially when combined with other observations such as the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376), [supernovae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_Ia_supernova), and [galaxy surveys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redshift_survey). Detecting the extremely faint cosmic shear signal requires averaging over many background galaxies, so surveys must be both deep and wide, and because these background galaxies are small, the image quality must be very good. Measuring the shear correlations at small scales also requires a high density of background objects (again requiring deep, high quality data), while measurements at large scales push for wider surveys.

While weak lensing of large-scale structure was discussed as early as 1967,[^o40] due to the challenges mentioned above, it was not detected until more than 30 years later when large [CCD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge-coupled_device) cameras enabled surveys of the necessary size and quality. In 2000, four independent groups[^o41][^o42][^o43][^o44] published the first detections of cosmic shear, and subsequent observations have started to put constraints on cosmological parameters (particularly the [dark matter density $\Omega_m~$ and power spectrum amplitude $\sigma_8~$](wikipedia://en/985963)) that are competitive with other cosmological probes.

For current and future surveys, one goal is to use the redshifts of the background galaxies (often approximated using [photometric redshifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photometric_redshift)) to divide the survey into multiple redshift bins. The low-redshift bins will only be lensed by structures very near to us, while the high-redshift bins will be lensed by structures over a wide range of redshift. This technique, dubbed "cosmic [tomography](wikipedia://en/385523)", makes it possible to map out the 3D distribution of mass. Because the third dimension involves not only distance but cosmic time, tomographic weak lensing is sensitive not only to the matter power spectrum today, but also to its evolution over the history of the universe, and the expansion history of the universe during that time. This is a much more valuable cosmological probe, and many proposed experiments to measure the properties of [dark energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_energy) and [dark matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_matter) have focused on weak lensing, such as the [Dark Energy Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Dark_Energy_Survey), [Pan-STARRS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pan-STARRS), and the Legacy Survey of Space and Time (LSST) to be conducted by the [Vera C. Rubin Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vera_C._Rubin_Observatory).

Weak lensing also has an important effect on the [Cosmic Microwave Background](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmic_Microwave_Background) and diffuse [21cm line radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_line). Even though there are no distinct resolved sources, perturbations on the origining surface are sheared in a similar way to galaxy weak lensing, resulting in changes to the power spectrum and statistics of the observed signal. Since the source plane for the CMB and high-redshift diffuse 21 cm are at higher redshift than resolved galaxies, the lensing effect probes cosmology at higher redshifts than galaxy lensing.

### Negative weak lensing {#Negative_weak_lensing}

Minimal coupling of general relativity with [scalar fields](wikipedia://en/161879) allows solutions like [traversable wormholes](wikipedia://en/34043#Traversable_wormholes) stabilized by [exotic matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exotic_matter) of negative [energy density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_density). Moreover, [Modified Newtonian Dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_Newtonian_Dynamics) as well as some [bimetric theories of gravity](wikipedia://en/43590607) consider invisible [negative mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_mass) in cosmology as an alternative interpretation to dark matter, which classically has a positive mass.[^o46][^o47][^o48][^o49][^o50]

As the presence of exotic matter would bend spacetime and light differently than positive mass, a Japanese team at the [Hirosaki University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hirosaki_University) proposed to use "negative" weak gravitational lensing related to such negative mass.[^o51][^o52][^o53]

Instead of running statistical analysis on the distortion of galaxies based on the assumption of a positive weak lensing that usually reveals locations of positive mass "dark clusters", these researchers propose to locate "negative mass clumps" using negative weak lensing, i.e. where the deformation of galaxies is interpreted as being due to a diverging lensing effect producing radial distortions (similar to a [concave lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29#Types_of_simple_lenses) instead of the classical [azimuthal](wikipedia://en/47487) distortions of [convex lenses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lens_%28optics%29#Types_of_simple_lenses) similar to the image produced by a [fisheye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisheye_lens)). Such negative mass clumps would be located elsewhere than assumed dark clusters, as they would reside at the center of observed [cosmic voids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Void_%28astronomy%29) located between [galaxy filaments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy_filament) within the lacunar, web-like [large-scale structure of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe#Large-scale_structure). Such test based on negative weak lensing could help to falsify [cosmological models](wikipedia://en/5378) proposing exotic matter of negative mass as an alternative interpretation to dark matter.[^o54]
