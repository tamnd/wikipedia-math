# Equidimensionality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equidimensionality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the term **equidimensional** is used to indicate a particular property of a space or relation, though the exact meaning may vary by subfield. In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), **equidimensionality** is a property of a [space](wikipedia://en/27667) that the local [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) is the same everywhere.[^o2] In the classification of [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations) like the [Cauchy-Euler equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy-Euler_equation), an equation is called **equidimensional** when rescaling an independent or dependent coordinate leaves the equation unchanged.

:::

## Definition (topology) {#Definition_(topology)}

A [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* is said to be equidimensional if for all points *p* in *X*, the [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) at *p*, that is dim *p*(*X*), is [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132). The [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is an example of an equidimensional space. The [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of two spaces *X* and *Y* (as topological spaces) of different dimension is an example of a non-equidimensional space.

## Definition (algebraic geometry) {#Definition_(algebraic_geometry)}

A [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) *S* is said to be equidimensional if every irreducible component has the same [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079). For example, the affine scheme `Spec k[x,y,z]/(xy,xz)`, which intuitively looks like a line intersecting a plane, is not equidimensional.

An [affine algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_algebraic_variety) whose coordinate ring is a [Cohen–Macaulay ring](wikipedia://en/497407) is equidimensional.[^o5]

## Definition (differential equations) {#Definition_(differential_equations)}

A differential equation is said to be **equidimensional** if it is independent of the scale of measurement of one or more of its variables [^o7]. For example, consider the first-order homogeneous ordinary differential equation

$$x \frac{dy}{dx} + y = 0.$$

If we introduce a new function $z(t)$ such that $y(x) = \alpha z(\beta x)$, then $dy/dx = \alpha \beta \partial z/\partial t$ and

$$t \frac{\partial z}{\partial t} + z = 0.$$

Since this is equivalent to the differential equation we started with, we say that it is an equidimensional equation in both the independent variable $x$ and the dependent-variable $y$.

The third-order ordinary differential equation

$$y''' y' y - 2 y (y'')^2 + (y')^2 y'' = 0$$

is also equidimensional in both its independent and dependent variables. All homogeneous linear differential equations are equidimensional in their dependent variable. All [Cauchy-Euler equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy-Euler_equation) are equidimensional in both variables. In this context, the equidimensional label indicates the equation is the same under any rescaling of the units of that variable. Equidimensional equations play an important rule in [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267).
