# Rigidity (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rigidity"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **rigid** collection *C* of [mathematical objects](wikipedia://en/19453961) *c* (for instance sets or functions) is one in which every *c* [∈](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/∈) *C* is uniquely determined by less information about *c* than one would expect. The above statement does not define a [mathematical property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_property); instead, it describes in what sense the adjective "rigid" is typically used in mathematics, by mathematicians.

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Some examples include:

1. [Harmonic functions](wikipedia://en/55762) on the unit disk are rigid in the sense that they are uniquely determined by their boundary values.
1. [Holomorphic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_functions) are determined by the set of all derivatives at a single point. A smooth function from the real line to the complex plane is not, in general, determined by all its derivatives at a single point, but it is if we require additionally that it be possible to extend the function to one on a neighbourhood of the real line in the complex plane. The [Schwarz lemma](wikipedia://en/1432589) is an example of such a rigidity theorem.
1. By the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414), [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in **C** are rigid in the sense that any polynomial is completely determined by its values on any [infinite set](wikipedia://en/142679), say **N**, or the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738). By the previous example, a polynomial is also determined within the set of holomorphic functions by the finite set of its non-zero derivatives at any single point.
1. Linear maps **L**(*X*, *Y*) between vector spaces *X*, *Y* are rigid in the sense that any **L** ∈ **L**(*X*, *Y*) is completely determined by its values on any set of [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector) of *X*.
1. [Mostow's rigidity theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mostow's_rigidity_theorem), which states that the geometric structure of negatively curved manifolds is determined by their topological structure.
1. A [well-ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-ordered_set) is rigid in the sense that the only ([order-preserving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order-preserving)) [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) on it is the identity function. Consequently, an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) between two given well-ordered sets will be unique.
1. [Cauchy's theorem](wikipedia://en/8317687) on geometry of [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885) states that a convex polytope is uniquely determined by the geometry of its faces and combinatorial adjacency rules.
1. [Alexandrov's uniqueness theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexandrov's_uniqueness_theorem) states that a convex polyhedron in three dimensions is uniquely determined by the [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) of [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096) on its surface.
1. [Rigidity results in K-theory](wikipedia://en/63544354) show isomorphisms between various [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) groups.
1. Rigid groups in the [inverse Galois problem](wikipedia://en/739166).

:::

## Combinatorial use {#Combinatorial_use}

In [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), the term rigid is also used to define the notion of a **rigid surjection**, which is a [surjection](wikipedia://en/27873) $f: n \to m$ for which the following equivalent conditions hold:[^o6]

1. For every $i, j \in m$, $i < j \implies \min f^{-1}(i) < \min f^{-1}(j)$;
1. Considering $f$ as an $n$-[tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) $\big( f(0), f(1), \ldots, f(n-1) \big)$, the first occurrences of the elements in $m$ are in increasing order;
1. $f$ maps [initial segments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_set) of $n$ to initial segments of $m$.

This relates to the above definition of rigid, in that each rigid surjection $f$ uniquely defines, and is uniquely defined by, a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of $n$ into $m$ pieces. Given a rigid surjection $f$, the partition is defined by $n = f^{-1}(0) \sqcup \cdots \sqcup f^{-1}(m-1)$. Conversely, given a partition of $n = A_0 \sqcup \cdots \sqcup A_{m-1}$, order the $A_i$ by letting $A_i \prec A_j \iff \min A_i < \min A_j$. If $n = B_0 \sqcup \cdots \sqcup B_{m-1}$ is now the $\prec$-ordered partition, the function $f: n \to m$ defined by $f(i) = j \iff i \in B_j$ is a rigid surjection.
