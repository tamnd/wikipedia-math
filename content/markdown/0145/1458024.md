# Toy theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Toy theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **toy theorem** is a simplified instance ([special case](wikipedia://en/31308010)) of a more general [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977), which can be useful in providing a handy representation of the general theorem, or a framework for proving the general theorem. One way of obtaining a toy theorem is by introducing some simplifying assumptions in a theorem.

:::

In many cases, a toy theorem is used to illustrate the claim of a theorem, while in other cases, studying the [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) of a toy theorem (derived from a non-trivial theorem) can provide insight that would be hard to obtain otherwise.

Toy theorems can also have educational value as well. For example, after presenting a theorem (with, say, a highly non-trivial proof), one can sometimes give some assurance that the theorem really holds, by proving a toy version of the theorem.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

A toy theorem of the [Brouwer fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/4101) is obtained by restricting the [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) to one. In this case, the Brouwer fixed-point theorem follows almost immediately from the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884).

Another example of toy theorem is [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305), which is obtained from the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662) by equating the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) values at the endpoints.

:::
