# Exact trigonometric values

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Exact trigonometric values"}

In mathematics, the values of the [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) can be expressed approximately, as in $\cos (\pi/4) \approx 0.707$, or exactly, as in $\cos (\pi/ 4)= \sqrt 2 /2$. While [trigonometric tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_tables) contain many approximate values, the exact values for certain angles can be expressed by a combination of arithmetic operations and [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208). The angles with trigonometric values that are expressible in this way are exactly those that can be constructed with a [compass and straight edge](wikipedia://en/61229), and the values are called [constructible numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructible_numbers).

:::

## Common angles {#Common_angles}

The trigonometric functions of angles that are multiples of 15°, 18°, or 22.5° have simple algebraic values. These values are listed in the following table for angles from 0° to 45°[^o3][^o4] (see [below](wikipedia://en/17699968#Constructible_values) for proofs). In the table below, the label "Undefined" represents a ratio $1:0.$ If the codomain of the trigonometric functions is taken to be the [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) these entries are [undefined](wikipedia://en/9920876), whereas if the codomain is taken to be the [projectively extended real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectively_extended_real_numbers), these entries take the value $\infty$ (see [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663)).

::: {#o5 .table cue=table}

|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $0$ | $0^\circ$ | $0$ | $1$ | $0$ | Undefined | $1$ | Undefined |
| $\frac{\pi}{12}$ | $15^\circ$ | $\frac{\sqrt{2} (\sqrt{3} - 1)} {4}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{2} (\sqrt{3} + 1)} {4}$ | $2 - \sqrt3$ | $2 + \sqrt3$ | $\sqrt2(\sqrt3 - 1)$ | $\sqrt2(\sqrt3 + 1)$ |
| $\frac{\pi}{10}$ | $18^\circ$ | $\frac{\sqrt5 - 1} {4}$ | $\frac{\sqrt2 \sqrt{5 + \sqrt5}} {4}$ | $\frac{\sqrt5 \sqrt{5 - 2\sqrt5}} {5}$ | $\sqrt{5 + 2\sqrt5}$ | $\frac{\sqrt{10} \sqrt{5 - \sqrt5}} {5}$ | $\sqrt5 + 1$ |

For angles outside of this range, trigonometric values can be found by applying [reflection and shift identities](wikipedia://en/25804104#Reflections,_shifts,_and_periodicity), such as

$$\begin{alignedat}{3} &&\sin(\tfrac{\pi}{2} - \theta) &{}= \cos(\theta), \\[5mu] &&\sin(2\pi + \theta) &{}= \sin(\pi - \theta) &&{}= \sin(\theta), \quad &&\sin(\pi + \theta) &&{}= \sin(-\theta) &&{}= -\sin(\theta), \\[5mu] &&\cos(2\pi + \theta) &{}= \cos(-\theta) &&{}= \cos(\theta), \quad &&\cos(\pi + \theta) &&{}= \cos(\pi - \theta) &&{}= -\cos(\theta). \end{alignedat}$$

For example, for any $\theta$ greater than $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$, to find its sine, follow these steps to instead operate within the range of 0 to $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$:

1. While it is greater than 2π, subtract 2π from it. Now try to find $\sin(\theta)$.
1. If it is greater than π, subtract π from it and try to find $-\sin(\theta)$.
1. If it is greater than $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$, subtract $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ from it and try to find $\cos(\theta)$.
1. If it is greater than $\tfrac{\pi}{4}$, subtract it from $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$. If you are trying to find cosine due to step 3, now try to find $\sin(\theta)$. If you are trying to find sine because you did not enter step 3, try to find $\cos(\theta)$.

## Trigonometric numbers {#Trigonometric_numbers}

A **trigonometric number** is a number that can be expressed as the [sine or cosine](wikipedia://en/5010838) of a [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) multiple of $\pi$ [radians](wikipedia://en/26003).[^o7] Since $\sin(x)=\cos(x-\pi/2),$ the case of a sine can be omitted from this definition. Therefore any trigonometric number can be written as $\cos(2\pi k/n)$, where *k* and *n* are integers. This number can be thought of as the real part of the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $\cos(2\pi k/n) + i \sin(2\pi k/n)$. [De Moivre's formula](wikipedia://en/57326) shows that numbers of this form are [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity):

$$\left(\cos\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right) + i \sin\left(\frac{2\pi k}{n}\right)\right)^n = \cos(2\pi k) + i \sin(2\pi k) = 1.$$

(Conversely, the real part of any root of unity is a trigonometric number.) Since the root of unity is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_root) of the polynomial *x*<sup>*n*</sup> − 1, it is [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158). Since the trigonometric number is the average of the root of unity and its [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918), and algebraic numbers are closed under arithmetic operations, every trigonometric number is algebraic.[^o7] The minimal polynomials of trigonometric numbers can be [explicitly enumerated](wikipedia://en/66501922).[^o8] In contrast, by the [Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/348976), the sine or cosine of any non-zero algebraic number is always [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325).[^o9]

By [Niven's theorem](wikipedia://en/33937103), the only rational trigonometric numbers are 0, 1, −1, 1/2, and −1/2.[^o10]

## Constructibility {#Constructibility}

An angle can be constructed with a compass and straightedge if and only if its sine (or equivalently cosine) can be expressed by a combination of arithmetic operations and square roots applied to integers.[^o12] Additionally, an angle that is a rational multiple of $\pi$ radians is constructible if and only if, when it is expressed as $a\pi/b$ radians, where *a* and *b* are [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) integers, the [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization) of the denominator, *b*, is the product of some [power of two](wikipedia://en/376948) and any number of distinct [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime) (a Fermat prime is a prime number one greater than a power of two).[^o12]

Thus, for example, $2\pi/15 = 24^\circ$ is a constructible angle because 15 is the product of the Fermat primes 3 and 5. Similarly $\pi/12 = 15^\circ$ is a constructible angle because 12 is a power of two (4) times a Fermat prime (3). But $\pi/9 = 20^\circ$ is not a constructible angle, since $9 = 3 \cdot 3$ is not the product of *distinct* Fermat primes as it contains 3 as a factor twice, and neither is $\pi/7 \approx 25.714^\circ$, since 7 is not a Fermat prime.[^o13]

It results from the above characterisation that an angle of an integer number of degrees is constructible if and only if this number of degrees is a multiple of $3$.

## Constructible values {#Constructible_values}

### 45° {#45°}

From a [reflection identity](wikipedia://en/25804104#Reflections), $\cos(45^\circ) = \sin(90^\circ-45^\circ)=\sin(45^\circ)$. Substituting into the [Pythagorean trigonometric identity](wikipedia://en/1050741) $\sin(45^\circ)^2 + \cos(45^\circ)^2=1$, one obtains the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) $2 \sin(45^\circ)^2 - 1 = 0$. Taking the positive root, one finds $\sin(45^\circ) = \cos(45^\circ) = 1/\sqrt{2} = \sqrt{2}/2$.

A geometric way of deriving the sine or cosine of 45° is by considering an isosceles right triangle with leg length 1. Since two of the angles in an isosceles triangle are equal, if the remaining angle is 90° for a right triangle, then the two equal angles are each 45°. Then by the Pythagorean theorem, the length of the hypotenuse of such a triangle is $\sqrt{2}$. Scaling the triangle so that its hypotenuse has a length of 1 divides the lengths by $\sqrt{2}$, giving the same value for the sine or cosine of 45° given above.

### 30° and 60° {#30°_and_60°}

The values of sine and cosine of 30 and 60 degrees are derived by analysis of the [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285). In an equilateral triangle, the 3 angles are equal and sum to 180°, therefore each corner angle is 60°. Bisecting one corner, the [special right triangle](wikipedia://en/2278116) with angles 30-60-90 is obtained. By symmetry, the bisected side is half of the side of the equilateral triangle, so one concludes $\sin(30^\circ)=1/2$. The Pythagorean and reflection identities then give $\sin(60^\circ)=\cos(30^\circ)=\sqrt{1-(1/2)^2}=\sqrt{3}/2$.

### 18°, 36°, 54°, and 72° {#18°,_36°,_54°,_and_72°}

The value of $\sin(18^\circ)$ may be derived using the [multiple angle formulas](wikipedia://en/25804104#Multiple-angle_formulae) for sine and cosine.[^o18] By the double angle formula for sine:

$$\sin(36^\circ) = 2\sin(18^\circ)\cos(18^\circ)$$

By the triple angle formula for cosine:

$$\cos(54^\circ) = \cos^3(18^\circ) - 3\sin^2(18^\circ)\cos(18^\circ) = \cos(18^\circ)(1 - 4\sin^2(18^\circ))$$

Since sin(36°) = cos(54°), we equate these two expressions and cancel a factor of cos(18°):

$$2\sin(18^\circ) = 1 - 4\sin^2(18^\circ)$$

This quadratic equation has only one positive root:

$$\sin(18^\circ) = \frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{4}$$

The Pythagorean identity then gives $\cos(18^\circ)$, and the double and triple angle formulas give sine and cosine of 36°, 54°, and 72°. Then $\cos(36^\circ)=(\sqrt{5}+1)/4=\varphi/2$, where $\varphi$ is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386).

A geometric method of finding the sines of these angles comes from analyzing a regular pentagon. Two of its diagonals form an angle of 36° that can be inscribed in a circle, and one side of the pentagon forms a chord of the circle that subtends this inscribed angle. To compute the chord's length, form a quadrilateral from four points of the pentagon. If the diagonals each have a length of one, then applying [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971) to this quadrilateral gives the equation $x^2 + x - 1 = 0$, where *x* is the chord length. The positive root of this equation is this value:

$$\frac{\sqrt{5}-1}{2}$$

Bisecting the 36° angle then creates a right triangle with angles of 18° and 72°, and cuts the chord in half, giving the same value for the sine of 18° given above. The sine value of 72° then follows from the Pythagorean theorem, and the sine values for 36° and 54° follow from sin(18°) and the double- and triple-angle formulas.

### Remaining multiples of 3° {#Remaining_multiples_of_3°}

Exact\_trigonometric\_table\_for\_multiples\_of\_3°.jpg

The sines and cosines of all other angles between 0 and 90° that are multiples of 3° can be derived from the angles described above and the [sum and difference formulas](wikipedia://en/25804104#Angle_sum_and_difference_identities). Specifically,[^o20]

$$\begin{aligned} 3^\circ &= 18^\circ-15^\circ, & 24^\circ &= 60^\circ - 36^\circ, & 51^\circ &= 36^\circ + 15^\circ, & 78^\circ &= 60^\circ + 18^\circ, & \\ 6^\circ &= 36^\circ - 30^\circ, & 27^\circ &= 45^\circ - 18^\circ, & 57^\circ &= 75^\circ - 18^\circ, & 81^\circ &= 45^\circ + 36^\circ, & \\ 9^\circ &= 45^\circ - 36^\circ, & 33^\circ &= 18^\circ + 15^\circ, & 63^\circ &= 45^\circ + 18^\circ, & 84^\circ &= 54^\circ + 30^\circ, & \\ 12^\circ &= 30^\circ - 18^\circ, & 39^\circ &= 54^\circ - 15^\circ, & 66^\circ &= 36^\circ + 30^\circ, & 87^\circ &= 72^\circ + 15^\circ. & \\ 15^\circ &= 45^\circ - 30^\circ, & 42^\circ &= 60^\circ - 18^\circ, & 69^\circ &= 54^\circ + 15^\circ, & \\ 21^\circ &= 36^\circ - 15^\circ, & 48^\circ &= 30^\circ + 18^\circ, & 75^\circ &= 45^\circ + 30^\circ, & \end{aligned}$$

For example, since $24^\circ = 60^\circ - 36^\circ$, its cosine can be derived by the cosine difference formula:

$$\begin{aligned}\cos(24^\circ) &= \cos(60^\circ)\cos(36^\circ) + \sin(60^\circ)\sin(36^\circ) \\[4mu] &= \frac{1}{2}\frac{\sqrt{5}+1}{4}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\frac{\sqrt{10-2\sqrt{5}}}{4}\\[6mu] &= \frac{1 + \sqrt{5} + \sqrt{30-6\sqrt{5}}}{8}\end{aligned}$$

### Half angles {#Half_angles}

If the denominator, *b*, is multiplied by additional factors of 2, the sine and cosine can be derived with the [half-angle formulas](wikipedia://en/25804104#Half-angle_formulae). For example, 22.5° ($\pi$/8 rad) is half of 45°, so its sine and cosine are:[^o22]

$$\begin{aligned} \sin(22.5^\circ) &= \sqrt{\frac12\bigl(1 - \cos(45^\circ)\bigr)} = \sqrt{\frac12\left(1 - \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)} = \frac12\sqrt{2-\sqrt2} \\ \cos(22.5^\circ) &= \sqrt{\frac12\bigl(1 + \cos(45^\circ)\bigr)} = \sqrt{\frac12\left(1 + \frac{\sqrt2}{2}\right)} = \frac12\sqrt{2+\sqrt2} \end{aligned}$$

Repeated application of the half-angle formulas leads to [nested radicals](wikipedia://en/2503693), specifically nested [square roots of 2](wikipedia://en/341682) of the form $\sqrt{2 \pm \cdots}$. In general, the sine and cosine of most angles of the form $\beta / 2^n$ can be expressed using nested square roots of 2 in terms of $\beta$. Specifically, if one can write an angle as

$$\alpha = \pi \left(\frac{1}{2}-\sum_{i=1}^k \frac{\prod_{j=1}^i b_j}{2^{i+1}}\right) = \pi \left (\frac{1}{2} - \frac{b_1}{4} - \frac{b_1 b_2}{8} - \frac{b_1 b_2 b_3}{16} - \ldots - \frac{b_1 b_2 \ldots b_k}{2^{k+1}}\right)$$

where $b_k \in [-2,2]$ and $b_i$ is -1, 0, or 1 for $i<k$, then[^o23]

$$\cos(\alpha) = \frac{b_1}{2}\sqrt{2+b_2 \sqrt{2+b_3 \sqrt{2+\ldots+b_{k-1} \sqrt{2+2 \sin\left(\frac{\pi b_k}{4}\right)}}}}$$

and if $b_1 \neq 0$ then[^o23]

$$\sin(\alpha) = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2 - b_2 \sqrt{2+b_3 \sqrt{2+b_4 \sqrt{2+\ldots+b_{k-1} \sqrt{2+2 \sin\left(\frac{\pi b_k}{4}\right)}}}}}$$

For example, $\frac{13 \pi}{32} = \pi\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{4}+\frac{1}{8}+\frac{1}{16}-\frac{1}{32}\right)$, so one has $(b_1,b_2,b_3,b_4)=(1,-1,1,-1)$ and obtains:

$$\begin{aligned} \cos\left(\frac{13 \pi}{32}\right) &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2-\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+2 \sin\left(\frac{-\pi}{4}\right)}}} = \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2-\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2-\sqrt{2}}}}\\ \sin\left(\frac{13 \pi}{32}\right) &= \frac{1}{2}\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2+\sqrt{2-\sqrt 2}}} \end{aligned}$$

### Denominator of 17 {#Denominator_of_17}

Since 17 is a Fermat prime, a regular [17-gon](wikipedia://en/196890) is constructible, which means that the sines and cosines of angles such as $2\pi/17$ radians can be expressed in terms of square roots. In particular, in 1796, [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) showed that:[^o25][^o26]

$$\cos\left(\frac{2\pi}{17}\right) = \frac{-1+\sqrt{17}+\sqrt{34-2\sqrt{17}} + 2\sqrt{17+3\sqrt{17} - \sqrt{170+38\sqrt{17}}}}{16}$$

The sines and cosines of related constructible angles measuring $\textstyle k \pi / (17\cdot 2^n)$ radians (for integers $k$ and $n$) can be derived from this one.

## Non-constructibility of 1° {#Non-constructibility_of_1°}

As discussed in #Constructibility, only certain angles that are rational multiples of $\pi$ radians have trigonometric values that can be expressed with square roots. The angle 1°, being $\pi/180 = \pi/(2^2 \cdot 3^2 \cdot 5)$ radians, has a repeated factor of 3 in the denominator and therefore $\sin(1^\circ)$ cannot be expressed using only square roots. A related question is whether it can be expressed using cube roots. The following two approaches can be used, but both result in an expression that involves the [cube root of a complex number](wikipedia://en/99491#nth_roots_of_a_complex_number). [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) shows that the [casus irreducibilis](wikipedia://en/11153189) cannot be expressed in terms of solely real radicals.[^o28]

Using the triple-angle identity, we can identify $\sin(1^\circ)$ as a root of a cubic polynomial: $\sin(3^\circ) = -4x^3 + 3x$. The three roots of this polynomial are $\sin(1^\circ)$, $\sin(59^\circ)$, and $-\sin(61^\circ)$. Since $\sin(3^\circ)$ is constructible, an expression for it could be plugged into [Cardano's formula](wikipedia://en/180787#Cardano's_formula) to yield an expression for $\sin(1^\circ)$. However, since all three roots of the cubic are real, this is an instance of casus irreducibilis, and the expression would require taking the cube root of a complex number.[^o29][^o30]

Alternatively, by [De Moivre's formula](wikipedia://en/57326):

$$\begin{aligned} (\cos(1^\circ) + i \sin(1^\circ))^3 &= \cos(3^\circ) + i \sin(3^\circ), \\[4mu] (\cos(1^\circ) - i \sin(1^\circ))^3 &= \cos(3^\circ) - i \sin(3^\circ). \end{aligned}$$

Taking cube roots and adding or subtracting the equations, we have:[^o30]

$$\begin{aligned} \cos(1^\circ) &= \;\frac{1}{2} \left( \sqrt[3]{\cos(3^\circ) + i \sin(3^\circ)} + \sqrt[3]{\cos(3^\circ) - i \sin(3^\circ)} \right), \\[5mu] \sin(1^\circ) &= \frac{1}{2i}\left( \sqrt[3]{\cos(3^\circ) + i \sin(3^\circ)} - \sqrt[3]{\cos(3^\circ) - i \sin(3^\circ)} \right). \end{aligned}$$
