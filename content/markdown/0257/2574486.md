# Fubini–Study metric

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fubini–Study metric"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Fubini–Study metric** (IPA: /fubini-ʃtuːdi/) is a [Kähler metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_metric) on a [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977) **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> endowed with a [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form). This [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) was originally described in 1904 and 1905 by [Guido Fubini](wikipedia://en/349051) and [Eduard Study](wikipedia://en/663997).[^o2][^o3]

:::

A [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) in (the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370)) **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup> defines a [unitary subgroup](wikipedia://en/173993) U(*n* + 1) in GL(*n* + 1,**C**). A Fubini–Study metric is determined up to [homothety](wikipedia://en/386138) (overall scaling) by invariance under such a U(*n* + 1) action; thus it is [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/363325). Equipped with a Fubini–Study metric, **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a [symmetric space](wikipedia://en/1642877). The particular normalization on the metric depends on the application. In [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243), one uses a normalization so that the Fubini–Study metric simply relates to the standard metric on the [(2*n* + 1)-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782). In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), one uses a normalization making **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> a [Hodge manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_manifold).

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

The Fubini–Study metric arises naturally in the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) construction of [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977).

Specifically, one may define **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> to be the space consisting of all complex lines in **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup>, i.e., the quotient of **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup> \\ {0} by the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) relating all complex multiples of each point together. This agrees with the quotient by the diagonal [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of the multiplicative group **C**<sup>\*</sup> = **C** \\ {0}:

$$\mathbf{CP}^n = \left\{ \mathbf{Z} = [Z_0,Z_1,\ldots,Z_n] \in {\mathbf C}^{n+1}\smallsetminus\{0\} \right\} \big/ \{ \mathbf{Z} \sim c\mathbf{Z}, c \in \mathbf{C}^* \}.$$

A point of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is thus identified with an equivalence class of (*n* + 1)-tuples \[*Z*<sub>0</sub>,...,*Z*<sub>*n*</sub>\] modulo nonzero complex rescaling; the *Z*<sub>*i*</sub> are called [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) of the point.

Furthermore, one may realize this quotient mapping in two steps: since multiplication by a nonzero complex scalar *z* = *R* *e*<sup>iθ</sup> can be uniquely thought of as the composition of a dilation by the modulus *R* followed by a counterclockwise rotation about the origin by an angle $\theta$, the quotient mapping **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup> \\ {0} → **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> splits into two pieces,

$$\mathbf{C}^{n+1}\smallsetminus\{0\} \mathrel{\stackrel{(a)}\longrightarrow} S^{2n+1} \mathrel{\stackrel{(b)}\longrightarrow} \mathbf{CP}^n$$

where step (a) is a quotient by the dilation **Z** ~ *R***Z** for *R* ∈ **R**<sup>\+</sup>, the multiplicative group of [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571), and step (b) is a quotient by the rotations **Z** ~ *e*<sup>iθ</sup>**Z**.

The result of the quotient in (a) is the real hypersphere *S*<sup>2*n*+1</sup> defined by the equation |**Z**|<sup>2</sup> = |*Z*<sub>0</sub>|<sup>2</sup> + ... + |*Z*<sub>*n*</sub>|<sup>2</sup> = 1. The quotient in (b) realizes **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> = *S*<sup>2*n*+1</sup>/*S*<sup>1</sup>, where *S*<sup>1</sup> represents the group of rotations. This quotient is realized explicitly by the famous [Hopf fibration](wikipedia://en/580384) *S*<sup>1</sup> → *S*<sup>2*n*+1</sup> → **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>, the fibers of which are among the [great circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_circles) of $S^{2n+1}$.

### As a metric quotient {#As_a_metric_quotient}

When a quotient is taken of a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) (or [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) in general), care must be taken to ensure that the quotient space is endowed with a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) that is well-defined. For instance, if a group *G* acts on a Riemannian manifold (*X*,*g*), then in order for the [orbit space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_space) *X*/*G* to possess an induced metric, $g$ must be constant along *G*-orbits in the sense that for any element *h* ∈ *G* and pair of vector fields $X,Y$ we must have *g*(*Xh*,*Yh*) = *g*(*X*,*Y*).

The standard [Hermitian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_metric) on **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup> is given in the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) by

$$ds^2 = d\mathbf{Z} \otimes d\bar{\mathbf{Z}} = dZ_0 \otimes d\bar{Z}_0 + \cdots + dZ_n \otimes d\bar{Z}_n$$

whose realification is the standard [Euclidean metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_metric) on **R**<sup>2*n*+2</sup>. This metric is *not* invariant under the diagonal action of **C**<sup>\*</sup>, so we are unable to directly push it down to **CP**<sup>n</sup> in the quotient. However, this metric *is* invariant under the diagonal action of *S*<sup>1</sup> = U(1), the group of rotations. Therefore, step (b) in the above construction is possible once step (a) is accomplished.

The **Fubini–Study metric** is the metric induced on the quotient **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> = *S*<sup>2*n*+1</sup>/*S*<sup>1</sup>, where $S^{2n+1}$ carries the so-called "round metric" endowed upon it by *restriction* of the standard Euclidean metric to the unit hypersphere.

### In local affine coordinates {#In_local_affine_coordinates}

Corresponding to a point in **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> with homogeneous coordinates $[Z_0:\dots:Z_n]$, there is a unique set of *n* coordinates $(z_1,\dots,z_n)$ such that

$$[Z_0:\dots:Z_n] \sim [1,z_1,\dots,z_n],$$

provided $Z_0\neq 0$; specifically, $z_j=Z_j/Z_0$. The $(z_1,\dots,z_n)$ form an [affine coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_coordinates) for **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> in the coordinate patch $U_0=\{Z_0\neq 0\}$. One can develop an affine coordinate system in any of the coordinate patches $U_i=\{Z_i\neq 0\}$ by dividing instead by $Z_i$ in the obvious manner. The *n* + 1 coordinate patches $U_i$ cover **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>, and it is possible to give the metric explicitly in terms of the affine coordinates $(z_1,\dots,z_n)$ on $U_i$. The coordinate derivatives define a frame $\{\partial_1,\ldots,\partial_n\}$ of the holomorphic [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>, in terms of which the Fubini–Study metric has Hermitian components

$$g_{i\bar{j}} = h(\partial_i,\bar{\partial}_j) = \frac{\left(1+|\mathbf{z}|\vphantom{l}^2\right)\delta_{i\bar{j}} - \bar{z}_i z_j}{\left(1+|\mathbf{z}|\vphantom{l}^2\right)^2}.$$

where |**z**|<sup>2</sup> = |*z*<sub>1</sub>|<sup>2</sup> + ... + |*z*<sub>*n*</sub>|<sup>2</sup>. That is, the [Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/189682) of the Fubini–Study metric in this frame is

$$\bigl[g_{i\bar{j}}\bigr] = \frac{1}{\left(1+|\mathbf{z}|\vphantom{l}^2\right)^2} \left[ \begin{array}{cccc} 1+|\mathbf{z}|^2 - |z_1|^2 & -\bar{z}_1 z_2 & \cdots & -\bar{z}_1 z_n \\ -\bar{z}_2 z_1 & 1 + |\mathbf{z}|^2 - |z_2|^2 & \cdots & -\bar{z}_2 z_n \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ -\bar{z}_n z_1 & -\bar{z}_n z_2 & \cdots & 1 + |\mathbf{z}|^2 - |z_n|^2 \end{array} \right]$$

Note that each matrix element is unitary-invariant: the diagonal action $\mathbf{z} \mapsto e^{i\theta}\mathbf{z}$ will leave this matrix unchanged.

Accordingly, the [line element](wikipedia://en/855138) is given by

$$\begin{aligned} ds^2 &= g_{i\bar{j}} \, dz^i \, d\bar{z}^j \\[4pt] &= \frac{\left(1+|\mathbf{z}|\vphantom{l}^2\right)|d\mathbf{z}|^2 - (\bar{\mathbf{z}}\cdot d\mathbf{z})(\mathbf{z}\cdot d\bar{\mathbf{z}})}{\left(1+|\mathbf{z}|\vphantom{l}^2\right)^2} \\[4pt] &= \frac{(1+z_i\bar{z}^i)\,dz_j\,d\bar{z}^j - \bar{z}^j z_i\,dz_j\,d\bar{z}^i}{\left(1+z_i\bar{z}^i\right)^2}. \end{aligned}$$

In this last expression, the [summation convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summation_convention) is used to sum over Latin indices *i*,*j* that range from 1 to *n*.

The metric can be derived from the following [Kähler potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_potential):[^o8]

$$K = \ln(1 + z_i \bar{z}^i) = \ln(1 + \delta_{i\bar{j}} z^i \bar{z}^j)$$

as

$$g_{i\bar{j}}=K_{i\bar{j}}=\frac{\partial^2}{\partial z^i \, \partial\bar{z}^j} K$$

### Using homogeneous coordinates {#Using_homogeneous_coordinates}

An expression is also possible in the notation of [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316), commonly used to describe [projective varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_varieties) of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997): **Z** = \[*Z*<sub>0</sub>:...:*Z*<sub>*n*</sub>\]. Formally, subject to suitably interpreting the expressions involved, one has

$$\begin{aligned} ds^2 &= \frac{|\mathbf{Z}|^2|d\mathbf{Z}|^2 - (\bar{\mathbf{Z}}\cdot d\mathbf{Z})(\mathbf{Z}\cdot d\bar{\mathbf{Z}})}{|\mathbf{Z}|^4}\\ &=\frac{Z_\alpha\bar{Z}^\alpha dZ_\beta d\bar{Z}^\beta - \bar{Z}^\alpha Z_\beta dZ_\alpha d\bar{Z}^\beta}{\left(Z_\alpha\bar{Z}^\alpha\right)^2}\\ &= \frac {2Z_{[\alpha}\,dZ_{\beta]} \bar{Z}^{[\alpha}\,\overline{dZ}^{\beta]}} {\left( Z_\alpha \bar{Z}^\alpha \right)^2}. \end{aligned}$$

Here the summation convention is used to sum over Greek indices *α* *β* ranging from 0 to *n*, and in the last equality the standard notation for the skew part of a tensor is used:

$$Z_{[\alpha}W_{\beta]} = \tfrac12 \left( Z_{\alpha} W_{\beta} - Z_{\beta} W_{\alpha} \right).$$

Now, this expression for d*s*<sup>2</sup> apparently defines a tensor on the total space of the tautological bundle **C**<sup>*n*+1</sup> \\ {0}. It is to be understood properly as a tensor on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> by pulling it back along a holomorphic section σ of the tautological bundle of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>. It remains then to verify that the value of the pullback is independent of the choice of section: this can be done by a direct calculation.

The [Kähler form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_form) of this metric is

$$\omega = \frac{i}{2}\partial\bar{\partial}\log |\mathbf{Z}|^2$$

where the $\partial, \bar\partial$ are the [Dolbeault operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolbeault_operators). The pullback of this is clearly independent of the choice of holomorphic section. The quantity log|**Z**|<sup>2</sup> is the [Kähler potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_potential) (sometimes called the Kähler scalar) of **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>.

### In bra-ket coordinate notation {#In_bra-ket_coordinate_notation}

In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the Fubini–Study metric is also known as the [Bures metric](wikipedia://en/20086937).[^o11] However, the Bures metric is typically defined in the notation of [mixed states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_state_%28physics%29), whereas the exposition below is written in terms of a [pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state). The real part of the metric is (a quarter of) the [Fisher information metric](wikipedia://en/488687).[^o11]

The Fubini–Study metric may be written using the [bra–ket notation](wikipedia://en/4542) commonly used in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). To explicitly equate this notation to the homogeneous coordinates given above, let

$$\vert \psi \rangle = \sum_{k=0}^n Z_k \vert e_k \rangle = [Z_0:Z_1:\ldots:Z_n]$$

where $\{\vert e_k \rangle\}$ is a set of [orthonormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal) [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector) for [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), the $Z_k$ are complex numbers, and $Z_\alpha = [Z_0:Z_1:\ldots:Z_n]$ is the standard notation for a point in the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> in [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316). Then, given two points $\vert \psi \rangle = Z_\alpha$ and $\vert \varphi \rangle = W_\alpha$ in the space, the distance (length of a geodesic) between them is

$$\gamma (\psi, \varphi) = \arccos \sqrt \frac {\langle \psi \vert \varphi \rangle \; \langle \varphi \vert \psi \rangle } {\langle \psi \vert \psi \rangle \; \langle \varphi \vert \varphi \rangle}$$

or, equivalently, in projective variety notation,

$$\gamma (\psi, \varphi) =\gamma (Z,W) = \arccos \sqrt {\frac {Z_\alpha \bar{W}^\alpha \; W_\beta \bar{Z}^\beta} {Z_\alpha \bar{Z}^\alpha \; W_\beta \bar{W}^\beta}}.$$

Here, $\bar{Z}^\alpha$ is the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $Z_\alpha$. The appearance of $\langle \psi \vert \psi \rangle$ in the denominator is a reminder that $\vert \psi \rangle$ and likewise $\vert \varphi \rangle$ were not normalized to unit length; thus the normalization is made explicit here. In Hilbert space, the metric can be interpreted as the angle between two vectors; thus it is occasionally called the **quantum angle**. The angle is real-valued, and runs from 0 to $\pi/2$.

The infinitesimal form of this metric may be quickly obtained by taking $\varphi = \psi+\delta\psi$, or equivalently, $W_\alpha = Z_\alpha + dZ_\alpha$ to obtain

$$ds^2 = \frac{\langle \delta \psi \vert \delta \psi \rangle} {\langle \psi \vert \psi \rangle} - \frac {\langle \delta \psi \vert \psi \rangle \; \langle \psi \vert \delta \psi \rangle} {{\langle \psi \vert \psi \rangle}^2}.$$

In the context of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), **CP**<sup>1</sup> is called the [Bloch sphere](wikipedia://en/729572); the Fubini–Study metric is the natural [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) for the geometrization of quantum mechanics. Much of the peculiar behaviour of quantum mechanics, including [quantum entanglement](wikipedia://en/25336) and the [Berry phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berry_phase) effect, can be attributed to the peculiarities of the Fubini–Study metric.

:::

## The *n* = 1 case {#The_n_=_1_case}

When *n* = 1, there is a diffeomorphism $S^2\cong \mathbf{CP}^1$ given by [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431). This leads to the "special" Hopf fibration *S*<sup>1</sup> → *S*<sup>3</sup> → *S*<sup>2</sup>. When the Fubini–Study metric is written in coordinates on **CP**<sup>1</sup>, its restriction to the real tangent bundle yields an expression of the ordinary "round metric" of radius 1/2 (and [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) 4) on *S*<sup>2</sup>.

Namely, if *z* = *x* + i*y* is the standard affine coordinate chart on the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) **CP**<sup>1</sup> and *x* = *r* cos θ, *y* = *r* sin θ are polar coordinates on **C**, then a routine computation shows

$$ds^2= \frac{\operatorname{Re}(dz \otimes d\bar{z})}{\left(1+|\mathbf{z}|\vphantom{l}^2\right)^2} = \frac{dx^2+dy^2}{ \left(1+r^2\right)^2 } = \tfrac14(d\varphi^2 + \sin^2 \varphi\,d\theta^2) = \tfrac14 \, ds^2_{us}$$

where $ds^2_{us}$ is the round metric on the unit 2-sphere. Here φ, θ are "mathematician's [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates)" on *S*<sup>2</sup> coming from the stereographic projection *r* tan(φ/2) = 1, tan θ = *y*/*x*. (Many physics references interchange the roles of φ and θ.)

The [Kähler form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_form) is

$$K=\frac{i}{2}\frac{dz\wedge d\bar{z}}{\left(1+z\bar{z}\right)^2} = \frac{dx\wedge dy}{\left(1+x^2+y^2\right)^2}$$

Choosing as [vierbeins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vierbein) $e^1=dx/(1+r^2)$ and $e^2=dy/(1+r^2)$, the Kähler form simplifies to

$$K=e^1 \wedge e^2$$

Applying the [Hodge star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_star) to the Kähler form, one obtains

$$*K = 1$$

implying that *K* is [harmonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_form).

## The *n* = 2 case {#The_n_=_2_case}

The Fubini–Study metric on the [complex projective plane](wikipedia://en/737155) **CP**<sup>2</sup> has been proposed as a [gravitational instanton](wikipedia://en/2618270), the gravitational analog of an [instanton](wikipedia://en/296077).[^o14][^o8] The metric, the [connection form](wikipedia://en/706412) and the curvature are readily computed, once suitable real 4D coordinates are established. Writing $(x,y,z,t)$ for real Cartesian coordinates, one then defines polar coordinate one-forms on the [4-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) (the [quaternionic projective line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternionic_projective_line)) as

$$\begin{aligned} r\,dr &= +x\,dx+y\,dy+z\,dz+t\,dt \\ r^2\sigma_1 &= -t\,dx-z\,dy+y\,dz+x\,dt \\ r^2\sigma_2 &= +z\,dx-t\,dy-x\,dz+y\,dt \\ r^2\sigma_3 &= -y\,dx+x\,dy-t\,dz+z\,dt \end{aligned}$$

The $\sigma_1, \sigma_2, \sigma_3$ are the standard left-invariant one-form coordinate frame on the Lie group $SU(2)=S^3$; that is, they obey $d\sigma_i=2\sigma_j\wedge\sigma_k$ for $i,j,k=1,2,3$ and cyclic permutations.

The corresponding local affine coordinates are $z_1=x+iy$ and $z_2=z+it$ then provide

$$\begin{aligned} z_1\bar{z}_1+z_2\bar{z}_2 &= r^2 = x^2+y^2+z^2+t^2 \\ dz_1\,d\bar{z}_1 + dz_2\,d\bar{z}_2 &= dr^{\,2} + r^2(\sigma_1^{\,2}+\sigma_2^{\,2}+\sigma_3^{\,2}) \\ \bar{z}_1\,dz_1 + \bar{z}_2\,dz_2 &= rdr + i\, r^2 \sigma_3 \end{aligned}$$

with the usual abbreviations that $dr^{\,2}=dr\otimes dr$ and $\sigma_k^{\,2}=\sigma_k\otimes\sigma_k$.

The line element, starting with the previously given expression, is given by

$$\begin{aligned} ds^2 &= \frac{dz_j\,d\bar{z}^j}{1+z_i\bar{z}^i} - \frac{\bar{z}^j z_i\,dz_j\,d\bar{z}^i}{(1+z_i\bar{z}^i)^2} \\[5pt] &= \frac{dr^{\,2}+r^2(\sigma_1^{\,2}+\sigma_2^{\,2}+\sigma_3^{\,2})}{1+r^2} - \frac{r^2dr^{\,2} + r^4 \sigma_3^{\,2}}{\left(1+r^2\right)^2} \\[4pt] &= \frac{dr^{\,2}+r^2\sigma_3^{\,2}}{\left(1+r^2\right)^2} + \frac{r^2\left(\sigma_1^{\,2}+\sigma_2^{\,2}\right)}{1+r^2} \end{aligned}$$

The [vierbeins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vierbein) can be immediately read off from the last expression:

$$\begin{aligned} e^0 = \frac{dr}{1+r^2} & & & e^3 = \frac{r\sigma_3}{1+r^2} \\[5pt] e^1 = \frac{r\sigma_1}{\sqrt{1+r^2}} & & & e^2 = \frac{r\sigma_2}{\sqrt{1+r^2}} \end{aligned}$$

That is, in the vierbein coordinate system, using roman-letter subscripts, the metric tensor is Euclidean:

$$ds^2=\delta_{ab} e^a\otimes e^b = e^0 \otimes e^0 + e^1 \otimes e^1 + e^2 \otimes e^2 + e^3 \otimes e^3.$$

Given the vierbein, a [spin connection](wikipedia://en/2915190) can be computed; the Levi-Civita spin connection is the unique connection that is [torsion-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsion_form) and covariantly constant, namely, it is the one-form $\omega^a_{\;\;b}$ that satisfies the torsion-free condition

$$de^a + \omega^a_{\;\;b} \wedge e^b = 0$$

and is covariantly constant, which, for spin connections, means that it is antisymmetric in the vierbein indexes:

$$\omega_{ab} = -\omega_{ba}$$

The above is readily solved; one obtains

$$\begin{aligned} \omega^0_{\;\;1} &= - \omega^2_{\;\;3} = -\frac{e^1}{r} \\ \omega^0_{\;\;2} &= - \omega^3_{\;\;1} = -\frac{e^2}{r} \\ \omega^0_{\;\;3} &= \frac{r^2-1}{r} e^3 \quad\quad \omega^1_{\;\;2} = \frac{1+2r^2}{r} e^3 \\ \end{aligned}$$

The [curvature 2-form](wikipedia://en/130526) is defined as

$$R^a_{\;\,b} = d\omega^a_{\;\,b} + \omega^a_{\;c} \wedge \omega^c_{\;\,b}$$

and is constant:

$$\begin{aligned} R_{01} &= -R_{23} = e^0\wedge e^1 - e^2\wedge e^3 \\ R_{02} &= -R_{31} = e^0\wedge e^2 - e^3\wedge e^1 \\ R_{03} &= 4 e^0\wedge e^3 + 2 e^1\wedge e^2 \\ R_{12} &= 2 e^0\wedge e^3 + 4 e^1\wedge e^2 \end{aligned}$$

The [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) in vierbein indexes is given by

$$\operatorname{Ric}^a_{\;\;c}=R^a_{\;\,bcd} \delta^{bd}$$

where the curvature 2-form was expanded as a four-component tensor:

$$R^a_{\;\,b} = \tfrac12 R^a_{\;\,bcd}e^c\wedge e^d$$

The resulting [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) is constant

$$\operatorname{Ric}_{ab}=6\delta_{ab}$$

so that the resulting [Einstein equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_equation)

$$\operatorname{Ric}_{ab} - \tfrac12 \delta_{ab}R + \Lambda\delta_{ab} = 0$$

can be solved with the [cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992) $\Lambda=6$.

The [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848) for Fubini–Study metrics in general is given by

$$W_{abcd}=R_{abcd} - 2\left(\delta_{ac}\delta_{bd} - \delta_{ad}\delta_{bc}\right)$$

For the *n* = 2 case, the two-forms

$$W_{ab}=\tfrac12 W_{abcd} e^c \wedge e^d$$

are self-dual:

$$\begin{aligned} W_{01} &= W_{23} = -e^0\wedge e^1 - e^2\wedge e^3 \\ W_{02} &= W_{31} = -e^0\wedge e^2 - e^3\wedge e^1 \\ W_{03} &= W_{12} = 2 e^0\wedge e^3 + 2 e^1\wedge e^2 \end{aligned}$$

## Curvature properties {#Curvature_properties}

In the *n* = 1 special case, the Fubini–Study metric has constant [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) identically equal to 4, according to the equivalence with the 2-sphere's round metric (which given a radius *R* has sectional curvature $1/R^2$). However, for *n* > 1, the Fubini–Study metric does not have constant curvature. Its sectional curvature is instead given by the equation[^o16]

$$K(\sigma) = 1 + 3\langle JX,Y \rangle^2$$

where $\{X,Y\} \in T_p \mathbf{CP}^n$ is an orthonormal basis of the 2-plane σ, the mapping *J* : *T***CP**<sup>*n*</sup> → *T***CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is the [complex structure](wikipedia://en/1549805) on **CP**<sup>*n*</sup>, and $\langle \cdot , \cdot \rangle$ is the Fubini–Study metric.

A consequence of this formula is that the sectional curvature satisfies $1 \leq K(\sigma) \leq 4$ for all 2-planes $\sigma$. The maximum sectional curvature (4) is attained at a [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) 2-plane — one for which *J*(σ) ⊂ σ — while the minimum sectional curvature (1) is attained at a 2-plane for which *J*(σ) is orthogonal to σ. For this reason, the Fubini–Study metric is often said to have "constant *holomorphic* sectional curvature" equal to 4.

This makes **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> a (non-strict) [quarter pinched manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter-pinched_sphere_theorem); a celebrated theorem shows that a strictly quarter-pinched [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) *n*-manifold must be homeomorphic to a sphere.

The Fubini–Study metric is also an [Einstein metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_metric) in that it is proportional to its own [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor): there exists a constant $\Lambda$; such that for all *i*,*j* we have

$$\operatorname{Ric}_{ij} = \Lambda g_{ij}.$$

This implies, among other things, that the Fubini–Study metric remains unchanged up to a scalar multiple under the [Ricci flow](wikipedia://en/288291). It also makes **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> indispensable to the theory of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), where it serves as a nontrivial solution to the vacuum [Einstein field equations](wikipedia://en/424540).

The [cosmological constant](wikipedia://en/38992) $\Lambda$ for **CP**<sup>*n*</sup> is given in terms of the dimension of the space:

$$\operatorname{Ric}_{ij} = 2(n+1) g_{ij}.$$

## Product metric {#Product_metric}

The common notions of separability apply for the Fubini–Study metric. More precisely, the metric is separable on the natural product of projective spaces, the [Segre embedding](wikipedia://en/1623980). That is, if $\vert\psi\rangle$ is a [separable state](wikipedia://en/2833933), so that it can be written as $\vert\psi\rangle=\vert\psi_A\rangle\otimes\vert\psi_B\rangle$, then the metric is the sum of the metric on the subspaces:

$$ds^2 = {ds_A}^2+{ds_B}^2$$

where ${ds_A}^2$ and ${ds_B}^2$ are the metrics, respectively, on the subspaces *A* and *B*.

## Connection and curvature {#Connection_and_curvature}

The fact that the metric can be derived from the Kähler potential means that the [Christoffel symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoffel_symbol) and the curvature tensors contain a lot of symmetries, and can be given a particularly simple form: The Christoffel symbols, in the local affine coordinates, are given by

$$\Gamma^i_{\;jk}=g^{i\bar{m}}\frac{\partial g_{k\bar{m}}}{\partial z^j} \qquad \Gamma^\bar{i}_{\;\bar{j}\bar{k}}=g^{\bar{i}m}\frac{\partial g_{\bar{k}m}}{\partial \bar{z}^\bar{j}}$$

The Riemann tensor is also particularly simple:

$$R_{i\bar{j}k\bar{l}}=g^{i\bar{m}}\frac{\partial \Gamma^\bar{m}_{\;\;\bar{j}\bar{l}}}{\partial z^k}$$

The [Ricci tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ricci_tensor) is

$$R_{\bar{i}j}= R^{\bar{k}}_{\; \bar{i}\bar{k} j} = - \frac{\partial \Gamma^\bar{k}_{\;\bar{i}\bar{k}}}{\partial z^j} \qquad R_{i\bar{j}}= R^k_{\; ik \bar{j}} = - \frac{\partial \Gamma^k_{\;ik}}{\partial \bar{z}^\bar{j}}$$
