# Nachbin's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nachbin's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the area of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), **Nachbin's theorem** (named after [Leopoldo Nachbin](wikipedia://en/19050267)) is a result used to establish bounds on the growth rates for [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478). In particular, Nachbin's theorem may be used to give the domain of convergence of the **generalized Borel transform**, also called **Nachbin summation**.

:::

This article provides a brief review of growth rates, including the idea of a **function of exponential type**. Classification of growth rates based on type help provide a finer tool than [big O](wikipedia://en/44578) or [Landau notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau_notation), since a number of theorems about the analytic structure of the bounded function and its [integral transforms](wikipedia://en/430912) can be stated.

## Exponential type {#Exponential_type}

A function $f(z)$ defined on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) is said to be of exponential type if there exist constants $M$ and $\alpha$ such that

$$|f(re^{i\theta})|\le Me^{\alpha r}$$

in the limit of $r\to\infty$. Here, the [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable) $z$ was written as $z=re^{i\theta}$ to emphasize that the limit must hold in all directions $\theta$. Letting $\alpha$ stand for the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of all such $\alpha$, one then says that the function $f$ is of *exponential type $\alpha$*.

For example, let $f(z)=\sin(\pi z)$. Then one says that $\sin(\pi z)$ is of exponential type $\pi$, since $\pi$ is the smallest number that bounds the growth of $\sin(\pi z)$ along the imaginary axis. So, for this example, [Carlson's theorem](wikipedia://en/4065365) cannot apply, as it requires functions of exponential type less than $\pi$.

## Ψ type {#&Psi;_type}

Additional function types may be defined for other bounding functions besides the exponential function. In general, a function $\Psi(t)$ is a **comparison function** if it has a series

$$\Psi(t)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty \Psi_n t^n$$

with $\Psi_n>0$ for all $n$, and

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{\Psi_{n+1}}{\Psi_n} = 0.$$

Comparison functions are necessarily [entire](wikipedia://en/10372), which follows from the [ratio test](wikipedia://en/421341). If $\Psi(t)$ is such a comparison function, one then says that $f$ is of $\Psi$-type if there exist constants $M$ and $\tau$ such that

$$\left|f\left(re^{i\theta}\right)\right| \le M\Psi(\tau r)$$

as $r\to \infty$. If $\tau$ is the infimum of all such $\tau$ one says that $f$ is of $\Psi$-type $\tau$.

Nachbin's theorem states that a function $f(z)$ with the series

$$f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty f_n z^n$$

is of $\Psi$-type $\tau$ if and only if

$$\limsup_{n\to\infty} \left| \frac{f_n}{\Psi_n} \right|^{1/n} = \tau.$$

This is naturally connected to the [root test](wikipedia://en/1470603) and can be considered a relative of the [Cauchy–Hadamard theorem](wikipedia://en/13620523).

## Generalized Borel transform {#Generalized_Borel_transform}

Nachbin's theorem has immediate applications in [Cauchy theorem](wikipedia://en/72827)-like situations, and for [integral transforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_transforms). For example, the **generalized Borel transform** is given by

$$F(w)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{f_n}{\Psi_n w^{n+1}}.$$

If $f$ is of $\Psi$-type $\tau$, then the exterior of the domain of convergence of $F(w)$, and all of its singular points, are contained within the disk

$$|w| \le \tau.$$

Furthermore, one has

$$f(z)=\frac{1}{2\pi i} \oint_\gamma \Psi (zw) F(w)\, dw$$

where the [contour of integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_of_integration) γ encircles the disk $|w| \le \tau$. This generalizes the usual **Borel transform** for functions of exponential type, where $\Psi(t)=e^t$. The integral form for the generalized Borel transform follows as well. Let $\alpha(t)$ be a function whose first derivative is bounded on the interval $[0,\infty)$ and that satisfies the defining equation

$$\frac{1}{\Psi_n} = \int_0^\infty t^n\, d\alpha(t)$$

where $d\alpha(t)=\alpha^{\prime}(t)\,dt$. Then the integral form of the generalized Borel transform is

$$F(w)=\frac{1}{w} \int_0^\infty f \left(\frac{t}{w}\right) \, d\alpha(t).$$

The ordinary Borel transform is regained by setting $\alpha(t)=-e^{-t}$. Note that the integral form of the Borel transform is the [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610).

## Nachbin summation {#Nachbin_summation}

Nachbin summation can be used to sum divergent series that [Borel summation](wikipedia://en/2357705) does not, for instance to [asymptotically solve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_solution) integral equations of the form:

$$g(s)=s\int_0^\infty K(st) f(t)\,dt$$

where $g(s)= \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n s^{-n}$, $f(t)$ may or may not be of exponential type, and the kernel $K(u)$ has a [Mellin transform](wikipedia://en/387297). The solution can be obtained using Nachbin summation as $f(x)= \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{a_n}{M(n+1)}x^n$ with the $a_n$ from $g(s)$ and with $M(n)$ the Mellin transform of $K(u)$. An example of this is the Gram series $\pi (x) \approx 1+\sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\log^{n}(x)}{n\cdot n!\zeta (n+1)}.$

In some cases as an extra condition we require $\int_0^\infty K(t)t^{n}\,dt$ to be finite and nonzero for $n=0,1,2,3,....$

## Fréchet space {#Fréchet_space}

Collections of functions of exponential type $\tau$ can form a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) [uniform space](wikipedia://en/32339), namely a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801), by the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) induced by the countable family of [norms](wikipedia://en/990534)

$$\|f\|_{n} = \sup_{z \in \mathbb{C}} \exp \left[-\left(\tau + \frac{1}{n}\right)|z|\right]|f(z)|.$$
