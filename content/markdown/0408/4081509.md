# Exponential type

*Image: ExtremeGaussian.png.* The graph of the function in gray is $e^{-\pi z^{2}}$, the Gaussian restricted to the real axis. The Gaussian does not have exponential type, but the functions in red and blue are one sided approximations that have exponential type $2\pi$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Exponential type"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) is said to be of **exponential type C** if its [growth is bounded](wikipedia://en/35764367) by the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) $e^{C|z|}$ for some [real-valued](wikipedia://en/20646438) constant $C$ as $|z|\to\infty$. When a function is bounded in this way, it is then possible to express it as certain kinds of convergent summations over a series of other complex functions, as well as understanding when it is possible to apply techniques such as [Borel summation](wikipedia://en/2357705), or, for example, to apply the [Mellin transform](wikipedia://en/387297), or to perform approximations using the [Euler–Maclaurin formula](wikipedia://en/9637). The general case is handled by [Nachbin's theorem](wikipedia://en/4081361), which defines the analogous notion of **$\Psi$-type** for a general function $\Psi(z)$ as opposed to $e^z$.

:::

## Basic idea {#Basic_idea}

A function $f(z)$ defined on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) is said to be of exponential type if there exist real-valued constants $M$ and $\tau$ such that

$$\left|f\left(re^{i\theta}\right)\right| \le Me^{\tau r}$$

in the limit of $r\to\infty$. Here, the [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable) $z$ was written as $z=re^{i\theta}$ to emphasize that the limit must hold in all directions $\theta$. Letting $\tau$ stand for the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of all such $\tau$, one then says that the function $f$ is of *exponential type $\tau$*.

For example, let $f(z)=\sin(\pi z)$. Then one says that $\sin(\pi z)$ is of exponential type $\pi$, since $\pi$ is the smallest number that bounds the growth of $\sin(\pi z)$ along the imaginary axis. So, for this example, [Carlson's theorem](wikipedia://en/4065365) cannot apply, as it requires functions of exponential type less than $\pi$. Similarly, the [Euler–Maclaurin formula](wikipedia://en/9637) cannot be applied either, as it, too, expresses a theorem ultimately anchored in the theory of [finite differences](wikipedia://en/39147).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) $F(z)$ is said to be of **exponential type** $\sigma>0$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a real-valued constant $A_\varepsilon$ such that

$$|F(z)|\leq A_\varepsilon e^{(\sigma+\varepsilon)|z|}$$

for $|z|\to\infty$ where $z\in\mathbb{C}$. We say $F(z)$ is of exponential type if $F(z)$ is of exponential type $\sigma$ for some $\sigma>0$. The number

$$\tau(F)=\sigma=\displaystyle\limsup_{|z|\rightarrow\infty}|z|^{-1}\log|F(z)|$$

*Image: Example exponential type 1 function.png.* Behavior of $F(z)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{z^{10^{n!}}}{(10^{n!})!}.$ The ratio of $\log|F(z)|$ to $|z|$ at a given radius is maximized on the real axis.

is the exponential type of $F(z)$. The [limit superior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_superior) here means the limit of the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of the ratio outside a given radius as the radius goes to infinity. This is also the limit superior of the maximum of the ratio at a given radius as the radius goes to infinity. The limit superior may exist even if the maximum at radius $r$ does not have a limit as $r$ goes to infinity. For example, for the function

$$F(z)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{z^{10^{n!}}}{(10^{n!})!}$$

the value of

$$(\max_{|z|=r} \log|F(z)|) / r$$

at $r=10^{n!-1}$ is dominated by the $n$−1${}^{\operatorname{st}}$ term and this goes to zero as $n$ goes to infinity,[^o5] but $F(z)$ is nevertheless of exponential type 1, as can be seen by looking at the points $z=10^{n!}$.

:::

## Exponential type with respect to a symmetric convex body {#Exponential_type_with_respect_to_a_symmetric_convex_body}

(Stein 1957) has given a generalization of exponential type for [entire functions](wikipedia://en/10372) of [several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables). Suppose $K$ is a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292), [compact](wikipedia://en/680261), and [symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric) subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. It is known that for every such $K$ there is an associated [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) $\|\cdot\|_K$ with the property that

$$K=\{x\in\mathbb{R}^n : \|x\|_K \leq1\}.$$

In other words, $K$ is the unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^{n}$ with respect to $\|\cdot\|_K$. The set

$$K^{*}=\{y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}:x\cdot y \leq 1 \text{ for all }x\in{K}\}$$

is called the [polar set](wikipedia://en/1764022) and is also a [convex](wikipedia://en/6292), [compact](wikipedia://en/680261), and [symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric) subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Furthermore, we can write

$$\|x\|_K = \displaystyle\sup_{y\in K^{*}}|x\cdot y|.$$

We extend $\|\cdot\|_K$ from $\mathbb{R}^n$ to $\mathbb{C}^n$ by

$$\|z\|_K = \displaystyle\sup_{y\in K^{*}}|z\cdot y|.$$

An entire function $F(z)$ of $n$-complex variables is said to be of exponential type with respect to $K$ if for every $\varepsilon>0$ there exists a real-valued constant $A_\varepsilon$ such that

$$|F(z)|<A_\varepsilon e^{2\pi(1+\varepsilon)\|z\|_K}$$

for all $z\in\mathbb{C}^{n}$.

## Fréchet space {#Fréchet_space}

Collections of functions of exponential type $\tau$ can form a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) [uniform space](wikipedia://en/32339), namely a [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801), by the [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) induced by the countable family of [norms](wikipedia://en/990534)

$$\|f\|_n = \sup_{z \in \mathbb{C}} \exp \left[-\left(\tau + \frac{1}{n}\right)|z|\right]|f(z)|.$$
