# Novikov ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Novikov ring"}

In mathematics, given an additive subgroup $\Gamma \subset \mathbb{R}$, the **Novikov ring** $\operatorname{Nov}(\Gamma)$ of $\Gamma$ is the subring of $\mathbb{Z}[\![\Gamma]\!]$[^o2] consisting of [formal sums](wikipedia://en/498958) $\sum n_{\gamma_i} t^{\gamma_i}$ such that $\gamma_1 > \gamma_2 > \cdots$ and $\gamma_i \to -\infty$. The notion was introduced by [Sergei Novikov](wikipedia://en/628515) in the papers that initiated the generalization of [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) using a closed one-form instead of a function. The notion is used in [quantum cohomology](wikipedia://en/7781359), among the others.

:::

The Novikov ring $\operatorname{Nov}(\Gamma)$ is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039). Let *S* be the subset of $\mathbb{Z}[\Gamma]$ consisting of those with leading term 1. Since the elements of *S* are unit elements of $\operatorname{Nov}(\Gamma)$, the [localization](wikipedia://en/252216) $\operatorname{Nov}(\Gamma)[S^{-1}]$ of $\operatorname{Nov}(\Gamma)$ with respect to *S* is a subring of $\operatorname{Nov}(\Gamma)$ called the "rational part" of $\operatorname{Nov}(\Gamma)$; it is also a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039).

## Novikov numbers {#Novikov_numbers}

Given a [smooth function](wikipedia://en/1408000) *f* on a [smooth manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) $M$ with nondegenerate critical points, the usual [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) constructs a free [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404) $C_*(f)$ such that the (integral) rank of $C_p$ is the number of critical points of *f* of index *p* (called the Morse number). It computes the (integral) [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) of $M$ (cf. [Morse homology](wikipedia://en/2868120)):

$$H^*(C_*(f)) \cong H^*(M,\mathbb{Z})$$

In an analogy with this, one can define "Novikov numbers". Let *X* be a connected polyhedron with a base point. Each cohomology class $\xi \in H^1(X,\mathbb{R})$ may be viewed as a linear functional on the first homology group $H_1(X,\mathbb{R})$; when composed with the [Hurewicz homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurewicz_homomorphism), it can be viewed as a group homomorphism $\xi\colon \pi=\pi_1(X) \to \mathbb{R}$. By the universal property, this map in turns gives a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411),

$$\phi_\xi\colon \mathbb{Z}[\pi] \to \operatorname{Nov} = \operatorname{Nov}(\mathbb{R}),$$

making $\operatorname{Nov}$ a module over $\mathbb{Z}[\pi]$. Since *X* is a [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) polyhedron, a [local coefficient system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_coefficient_system) over it corresponds one-to-one to a $\mathbb{Z}[\pi]$-module. Let $L_\xi$ be a local coefficient system corresponding to $\operatorname{Nov}$ with module structure given by $\phi_\xi$. The homology group $H_p(X, L_\xi)$ is a finitely generated module over $\operatorname{Nov},$ which is, by the [structure theorem](wikipedia://en/9902787), the direct sum of its free part and its torsion part. The rank of the free part is called the Novikov Betti number and is denoted by $b_p(\xi)$. The number of cyclic modules in the torsion part is denoted by $q_p(\xi)$. If $\xi = 0$, $L_\xi$ is trivial and $b_p(0)$ is the usual Betti number of *X*.

The analog of [Morse inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_inequalities) holds for Novikov numbers as well (cf. the reference for now.)
