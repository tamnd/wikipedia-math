# Edmonds matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Edmonds matrix"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **Edmonds matrix** $A$ of a balanced [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) $G = (U, V, E)$ with sets of vertices $U = \{u_1, u_2, \dots , u_n \}$ and $V = \{v_1, v_2, \dots , v_n\}$ is defined by

:::

$$A_{ij} = \left\{ \begin{array}{ll} x_{ij} & (u_i, v_j) \in E \\ 0 & (u_i, v_j) \notin E \end{array}\right.$$

where the *x<sub>ij</sub>* are [indeterminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29). One application of the Edmonds matrix of a bipartite graph is that the graph admits a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) if and only if the [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) det(*A*) in the *x<sub>ij</sub>* is not identically zero. Furthermore, the number of perfect matchings is equal to the number of [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) in the polynomial det(*A*), and is also equal to the [permanent](wikipedia://en/215889) of $A$. In addition, the [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) of $A$ is equal to the [maximum matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_matching) size of $G$.

The Edmonds matrix is named after [Jack Edmonds](wikipedia://en/3625459). The [Tutte matrix](wikipedia://en/18305460) is a generalisation to non-bipartite graphs.
