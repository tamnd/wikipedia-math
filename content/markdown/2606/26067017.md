# Warburg coefficient

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Warburg coefficient"}

The **Warburg coefficient** (or **Warburg constant**; denoted $A_{W}$ or $\sigma$) is the [diffusion coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_coefficient) of [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) in [solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_%28chemistry%29), associated to the [Warburg element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warburg_element), $Z_{W}$. The Warburg coefficient has units of ${\Omega}/\sqrt{\text{seconds}}={\Omega} s^{-1/2}$

:::

The value of $A_{W}$ can be obtained by the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of the Warburg plot, a linear plot of the real [impedance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_impedance) ($R$) against the reciprocal of the square root of the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) (${1}/\sqrt{\omega}$). This relation should always yield a straight line, as it is unique for a Warburg.

Alternatively, the value of $A_{W}$ can be found by:

$$A_W={\frac{R T}{An^2F^2\sqrt2}}{\left(\frac{1}{C_\mathrm{O}^b\sqrt{D_\mathrm{O}}}+{\frac{1}{C_\mathrm{R}^b\sqrt{D_\mathrm{R}}}}\right)}=\frac{R T}{An^2F^2\Theta C\sqrt{2D}}$$

where

- $R$ is the [ideal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_gas_constant);
- $T$ is the [thermodynamic temperature](wikipedia://en/41789);
- $F$ is the [Faraday constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_constant);
- $n$ is the [valency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valence_%28chemistry%29);
- $D$ is the diffusion coefficient of the species, where subscripts $O$ and $R$ stand for the oxidized and reduced species respectively;
- $C^{b}$ is the concentration of the $O$ and $R$ species in the bulk;
- $C$ is the concentration of the electrolyte;
- $A$ denotes the surface area;
- $\Theta$ denotes the fraction of the $O$ and $R$ species present.

The equation for $A_{W}$ applies to both reversible and quasi-reversible reactions for which both halves of the couple are soluble.

The Warburg coefficient is used in engineering. By measuring the Warburg Coefficient engineers can calculate how quickly ions are moving through a material to characterise battery performance.[^o2]
