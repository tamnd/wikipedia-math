# Donald Kreider

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Donald Kreider"}

**Donald Lester Kreider** (December 5, 1931 – December 7, 2006) was an [American mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_mathematician) and educator who served as president of the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) (1993–1994).

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Kreider was born on December 5, 1931, in [Lancaster, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lancaster,_Pennsylvania).[^o4] He attended high school in [Lititz, Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lititz,_Pennsylvania), and college at [Lebanon Valley College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebanon_Valley_College), where he received a bachelor's degree in 1953.[^o5] In 1959, he received a PhD in mathematics from the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology) for a thesis in [recursive function theory](wikipedia://en/155414) supervised by [Hartley Rogers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_Rogers,_Jr.).[^o6]

## Career {#Career}

Kreider spent a postdoctoral year at MIT before joining the Department of Mathematics at [Dartmouth College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_College) in 1960. At both MIT and Dartmouth, Kreider was known for excellent teaching. His advisor [Hartley Rogers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_Rogers) at MIT commented that "he had a graduate student \[who\] was an extraordinary teacher \[and\] won the Goodwin Medal for teaching at MIT".[^o8][^o9] [John Kemeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_G._Kemeny) at Dartmouth remarked that it was difficult to find faculty who could teach well at both the undergraduate and graduate levels, but that "occasionally you are lucky—Don Kreider is an example. Don is spectacular at anything he teaches."[^o10]

Initially, Kreider continued research and writing in recursive function theory, working with Robert W. Ritchie. But he increasingly turned his attention to mathematical pedagogy,[^o11] writing textbooks in recursive function theory,[^o12] [differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_equations), and [linear analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) with colleagues in the Department of Mathematics.

Starting in 1960, Kreider became active in the Committee on the Undergraduate Program in Mathematics (CUPM) at the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) (MAA), where he later led the Subpanel on Calculus[^o13] and received one of the first grants in the Calculus Initiative launched by the [National Science Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Science_Foundation) in 1989.[^o14]

Kreider had a long-standing interest in the use of computation in teaching calculus. Working with John Kemeny and [Thomas E. Kurtz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_E._Kurtz), he developed programs that provided automatic feedback to students about four programs in [Dartmouth Basic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Basic) that they were assigned to write in freshman mathematics courses taken by 80% of all Dartmouth students.[^o15][^o16] Later he was lead author of a book supporting the use of the [HP-48G/GX](wikipedia://en/333248) calculator and co-author with his colleague [Dwight Lahr](wikipedia://en/66903005) of an interactive set of teaching modules.

Also in the 1960s, Kreider chaired the school board in [Norwich, Vermont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwich,_Vermont) and was instrumental in forming the [Dresden School District](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresden_School_District), the first inter-state school district in the United States, between Norwich and [Hanover, New Hampshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hanover,_New_Hampshire).[^o5] He spent three summers with the Entebbe Project in Africa, where he worked with local teachers on a high school mathematics curriculum, textbooks, and teacher training materials.[^o17]

In the 1970s, Kreider chaired the [College Board](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/College_Board) Calculus Development Committee and was a member of its Mathematical Science Advisory Committee, where he promoted the development of an Advanced Placement course in computer science.[^o18]

At Dartmouth, Kreider had a particularly close relationship with [John Kemeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_G._Kemeny). In 1967, he succeeded Kemeny as Chairman of the Department of Mathematics, which Kemeny had chaired since 1954. From 1972 to 1975, he was Vice President and Dean for Student Affairs. John Kemeny, who had become President of Dartmouth in 1970, sought help from Kreider and Ruth Adams,[^o19] a former President of [Wellesley College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wellesley_College), in managing the expansion of Dartmouth College as it began admitting women as well as significant numbers of Native American and minority students. A key factor in the expansion was the Dartmouth Plan, under which Dartmouth went to year-round operation to accommodate a larger student body without having to admit fewer men. And a key factor in the Dartmouth Plan was Project FIND (Forecasting Institutional Needs at Dartmouth), for which Kreider chaired the coordinating committee, that created an information management information system on the [Dartmouth Time-Sharing System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartmouth_Time-Sharing_System) and integrated it with all planning aspects of the College.[^o20][^o21]

After returning to the Dartmouth Mathematics Department in 1975, Kreider served another term as its chairman and became increasing active in the Mathematical Association of America. He served as its treasurer from 1986 to 1992[^o22] when, according to his successor Gerald J. Porter, "the MAA made remarkable strides toward putting both its financial and physical houses in order."[^o23] He also served as the MAA president-elect (1992), as its president (1993–1994), and on its Board of Governors (1995–1999).[^o14]

Kreider retired from Dartmouth in 1997. He died on December 7, 2006, at the age of 75.[^o24]
