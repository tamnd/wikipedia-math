# Lagrange's theorem (number theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lagrange's theorem"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Lagrange's theorem** is a statement named after [Joseph-Louis Lagrange](wikipedia://en/87793) about how frequently a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) over the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) may evaluate to a multiple of a fixed [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) *p*. More precisely, it states that for all integer polynomials $\textstyle f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$, either:

:::

- every coefficient of $f$ is divisible by *p*, or
- $p \mid f(x)$ has at most $\deg f$ solutions in $\{1, 2, ..., p\}$,

where $\deg f$ is the [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) of $f$.

This can be stated with [congruence classes](wikipedia://en/20087) as follows: for all polynomials $\textstyle f \in (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})[x]$ with *p* prime, either:

- every coefficient of $f$ is null, or
- $f(x)=0$ has at most $\deg f$ solutions in $\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$.

If *p* is not prime, then there can potentially be more than $\deg f(x)$ solutions. Consider for example *p=8* and the polynomial *f(x)=x*${}^{2}$*−1*, where *1, 3, 5, 7* are all solutions.

::: {#o3 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Let $\textstyle f \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ be an integer polynomial, and write $g \in (\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z})[x]$ the polynomial obtained by taking its coefficients $\bmod p$. Then, for all integers *x*,

$$f(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{p} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{p}.$$

Furthermore, by the [basic rules](wikipedia://en/20087#Basic_properties) of modular arithmetic,

$$f(x) \equiv 0 \pmod{p} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad f(x \bmod{p}) \equiv 0 \pmod{p} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad g(x \bmod{p}) \equiv 0 \pmod{p}.$$

Both versions of the theorem (over $\mathbf{Z}$ and over $\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}$) are thus equivalent. We prove the second version by induction on the degree, in the case where the coefficients of *f* are not all null.

If $\deg f = 0$ then $f$ has no roots and the statement is true.

If $\deg f \ge 1$ without roots then the statement is also trivially true.

Otherwise, $\deg f \ge 1$ and $f$ has a root $k\in\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$. The fact that $\mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z}$ is a field allows to apply the [division algorithm](wikipedia://en/314205) to $f$ and the polynomial $x - k$ (of degree *1*), which yields the existence of a polynomial $\textstyle g \in (\mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z})[x]$ (of degree lower than that of $f$) and of a constant $\textstyle r \in \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (of degree lower than *1*) such that

$$f(x) = g(x) (x-k) + r.$$

Evaluating at $x = k$ provides $r = 0$.[^o4] The other roots of *f* are then roots of *g* as well, which by the induction property are at most $\deg g \le \deg f - 1$ in number. This proves the result.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

Let $p(X)$ be a polynomial over an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) $R$ with degree $n > 0$. Then the polynomial equation $p(x) = 0$ has at most $n = \deg(p(X))$ roots in $R$.[^o7]

:::
