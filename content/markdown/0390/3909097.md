# Projection (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Projection"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **projection** is a [mapping](wikipedia://en/185427) from a set to itself—or an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) of a [mathematical structure](wikipedia://en/1809181)—that is [idempotent](wikipedia://en/14972), that is, equals its [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) with itself. The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a point or a subset $S$ under a projection is called the *projection of* $S$.

:::

An everyday example of a projection is the casting of shadows onto a plane (sheet of paper): the projection of a point is its shadow on the sheet of paper, and the projection (shadow) of a point on the sheet of paper is that point itself (idempotency). The shadow of a three-dimensional sphere is a disk. Originally, the notion of projection was introduced in [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) to denote the projection of the three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) onto a plane in it, like the shadow example. The two main projections of this kind are:

- The **projection from a point onto a plane** or **central projection**: If $C$ is a point, called the **centre of projection**, then the projection of a point $P$ different from $C$ onto a plane that does not contain $C$ is the intersection of the [line](wikipedia://en/946975) $CP$ with the plane. The points $P$ such that the line $CP$ is [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to the plane does not have any image by the projection, but one often says that they project to a point at infinity of the plane (see [Projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) for a formalization of this terminology). The projection of the point $C$ itself is not defined.
- The **projection parallel to a direction $D$, onto a plane** or **[parallel projection](wikipedia://en/2503009)**: The image of a point $P$ is the intersection of the plane with the line parallel to $D$ passing through $P$. See Affine space for an accurate definition, generalized to any dimension.

The concept of **projection** in mathematics is a very old one, and most likely has its roots in the phenomenon of the shadows cast by real-world objects on the ground. This rudimentary idea was refined and abstracted, first in a [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) context and later in other branches of mathematics. Over time different versions of the concept developed, but today, in a sufficiently abstract setting, we can unify these variations.

In [cartography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartography), a [map projection](wikipedia://en/51784) is a map of a part of the surface of the Earth onto a plane, which, in some cases, but not always, is the restriction of a projection in the above meaning. The [3D projections](wikipedia://en/313741) are also at the basis of the theory of [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405).

The need for unifying the two kinds of projections and of defining the image by a central projection of any point different of the center of projection are at the origin of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849).

## Definition {#Definition}

Generally, a mapping where the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) and [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) are the same [set](wikipedia://en/26691) (or [mathematical structure](wikipedia://en/1809181)) is a projection if the mapping is [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent), which means that a projection is equal to its [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) with itself. A projection may also refer to a mapping which has a [right inverse](wikipedia://en/14907#Right_inverses). Both notions are strongly related, as follows. Let $p$ be an idempotent mapping from a set $A$ into itself (thus $p \circ p = p$) and $B = p(A)$ be the image of $p$. If we denote by $\pi$ the map $p$ viewed as a map from $A$ onto $B$ and by $i$ the [injection](wikipedia://en/45196) of $B$ into $A$ (so that $p = i \circ \pi$), then we have $\pi \circ i = \operatorname{Id}_{B}$ (so that $\pi$ has a right inverse). Conversely, if $\pi$ has a right inverse $i$, then $\pi \circ i = \operatorname{Id}_{B}$ implies that $i \circ \pi \circ i \circ \pi = i \circ \operatorname{Id}_{B} \circ \pi = i \circ \pi$; that is, $p = i \circ \pi$ is idempotent.

## Applications {#Applications}

The original notion of projection has been extended or generalized to various mathematical situations, frequently, but not always, related to geometry, for example:

- In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553):
  - An operation typified by the $j$-<sup>th</sup> [projection map](wikipedia://en/3913145), written $\operatorname{proj}_{j}$, that takes an element $\mathbf{x} = (x_{1}, ..., x_{j}, ..., x_{n})$ of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $X_{1} \times \cdots \times X_{j} \times \cdots \times X_{n}$ to the value $\operatorname{proj}_{j}(\mathbf{x}) = x_{j}.$[^o6] This map is always [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) and, when each space $X_{k}$ has a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450), this map is also [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28topology%29) and [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map).[^o7]
  - A mapping that takes an element to its [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) under a given [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) is known as the [canonical projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_projection).[^o8]
  - The evaluation map sends a function $f$ to the value $f(x)$ for a fixed $x$. The space of functions $Y^{X}$ can be identified with the Cartesian product $\prod_{i\in X}Y$, and the evaluation map is a projection map from the Cartesian product.
- For [relational databases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_database) and [query languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Query_language), the [projection](wikipedia://en/2702833) is a [unary operation](wikipedia://en/161006) written as $\Pi_{a_1, \ldots,a_n}( R )$ where $a_1,\ldots,a_n$ is a set of attribute names. The result of such projection is defined as the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) that is obtained when all [tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) in $R$ are restricted to the set $\{a_1,\ldots,a_n\}$.[^o9][^o10][^o11] $R$ is a [database-relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_%28database%29).
- In [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026), projection of a sphere upon a plane was used by [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) (~150) in his [Planisphaerium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planisphaerium).[^o12] The method is called [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) and uses a plane [tangent](wikipedia://en/31482) to a sphere and a *pole* C diametrically opposite the point of tangency. Any point $P$ on the sphere besides $C$ determines a line $CP$ intersecting the plane at the projected point for $P$.[^o13] The correspondence makes the sphere a [one-point compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-point_compactification) for the plane when a [point at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139) is included to correspond to $C$, which otherwise has no projection on the plane. A common instance is the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) where the compactification corresponds to the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799). Alternatively, a [hemisphere](wikipedia://en/27859#Hemisphere) is frequently projected onto a plane using the [gnomonic projection](wikipedia://en/1331480).
- In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) that remains unchanged if applied twice: $p(u) = p(p(u))$. In other words, an [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) operator. For example, the mapping that takes a point $(x, y, z)$ in three dimensions to the point $(x, y, 0)$ is a projection. This type of projection naturally generalizes to any number of dimensions $n$ for the domain and $k \le n$ for the codomain of the mapping. See [Orthogonal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection), [Projection (linear algebra)](wikipedia://en/519182). In the case of orthogonal projections, the space admits a decomposition as a product, and the projection operator is a projection in that sense as well.[^o14][^o15]
- In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), a specific kind of projection is used in the construction of [projection-valued measures](wikipedia://en/1046155). In their sense, a projection is a [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint) idempotent linear operator.
- In [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562), any [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526) includes a projection map as part of its definition. Locally at least this map looks like a projection map in the sense of the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) and is therefore [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map) and surjective.
- In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a [retraction](wikipedia://en/2120001) is a [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map_%28topology%29) $r: X \to X$ which restricts to the [identity map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) on its image.[^o16] This satisfies a similar idempotency condition $r^{2} = r$ and can be considered a generalization of the projection map. The image of a retraction is called a retract of the original space. A retraction which is [homotopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopic) to the identity is known as a [deformation retraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retraction). This term is also used in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) to refer to any split epimorphism.
- The [scalar projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_resolute) (or resolute) of one [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) onto another.
- In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the above notion of Cartesian product of sets can be generalized to arbitrary [categories](wikipedia://en/53759). The [product](wikipedia://en/237868) of some objects has a **canonical projection** [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) to each factor. Special cases include the projection from the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) of [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) (which is always surjective and [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map)), or from the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), etc. Although these morphisms are often [epimorphisms](wikipedia://en/59539) and even surjective, they do not have to be.[^o17]

:::
