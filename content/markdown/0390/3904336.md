# Heat kernel

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Heat kernel"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) study of [heat conduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_conduction) and [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion), a **heat kernel** is the [fundamental solution](wikipedia://en/1820858) to the [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415) on a specified domain with appropriate [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions). It is also one of the main tools in the study of the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/506713) of the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706), and is thus of some auxiliary importance throughout [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416). The heat kernel represents the evolution of [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) in a region whose boundary is held fixed at a particular temperature (typically zero), such that an initial unit of heat energy is placed at a point at time $t = 0$.

:::

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Fundamental solution to the heat equation.gif.* Fundamental solution of the one-dimensional heat equation. Red: time course of $\Phi(x,t)$. Blue: time courses of $\Phi(x_0,t)$ for two selected points. [Interactive version.](https://www.geogebra.org/classic/SV6PruXx)

The most well-known heat kernel is the heat kernel of $d$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbf{R}^{d}$, which has the form of a time-varying [Gaussian function](wikipedia://en/245552),

$$K(t,x,y) = \frac{1}{\left(4\pi t\right)^{d/2}} \exp\left(-\frac{\left\|x - y\right\|^2}{4t}\right),$$

which is defined for all $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^d$ and $t > 0$.[^n1] This solves the heat equation

$$\left\{ \begin{aligned} & \frac{\partial K}{\partial t}(t,x,y) = \Delta_x K(t,x,y)\\ & \lim_{t \to 0} K(t,x,y) = \delta(x-y) = \delta_x(y) \end{aligned} \right.$$

for the unknown function K. Here $\delta$ is a [Dirac delta distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta_distribution), and the limit is taken in the sense of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29), that is, for every function $\phi$ in the space $C^{\infty}_{c}(\mathbf{R}^{d})$ of [smooth functions with compact support](wikipedia://en/340630#Functional_analysis), we have[^n2]

$$\lim_{t \to 0}\int_{\mathbb{R}^d} K(t,x,y)\phi(y)\,dy = \phi(x).$$

On a more general domain $\Omega$ in $\mathbf{R}^{d}$, such an explicit formula is not generally possible. The next simplest cases of a disc or square involve, respectively, [Bessel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessel_functions) and [Jacobi theta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobi_theta_function). Nevertheless, the heat kernel still exists and is [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) for $t > 0$ on arbitrary domains and indeed on any [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) [with boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_with_boundary), provided the boundary is sufficiently regular. More precisely, in these more general domains, the heat kernel is the solution of the initial boundary value problem

$$\begin{cases} \frac{\partial K}{\partial t}(t,x,y) = \Delta_x K(t,x,y) & \text{for all } t>0 \text{ and } x,y\in\Omega \\[6pt] \lim_{t \to 0} K(t,x,y) = \delta_x(y) & \text{for all } x,y\in\Omega\\[6pt] K(t,x,y) = 0 & x\in\partial\Omega \text{ or } y\in\partial\Omega \end{cases}$$

:::

## Spectral theory {#Spectral_theory}

To derive a formal expression for the heat kernel on an arbitrary domain, consider the Dirichlet problem in a connected domain (or manifold with boundary)$U$. Let $\lambda_{n}$ be the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) for the Dirichlet problem of the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian)[^n3]

$$\begin{cases} \Delta \phi + \lambda \phi = 0 & \text{in } U,\\ \phi=0 & \text{on }\ \partial U. \end{cases}$$

Let $\phi_{n}$ denote the associated [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601), normalized to be orthonormal in [$L^{2}(U)$](wikipedia://en/45194). The inverse Dirichlet Laplacian $\Delta^{-1}$ is a [compact](wikipedia://en/577441) and [selfadjoint operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selfadjoint_operator), and so the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) implies that the eigenvalues of $\Delta$ satisfy

$$0 < \lambda_1 \le \lambda_2\le \lambda_3\le\cdots,\quad \lambda_n\to\infty.$$

The heat kernel has the following expression:

$$K(t,x,y) = \sum_{n=0}^\infty e^{-\lambda_n t}\phi_n(x)\phi_n(y).$$

Formally differentiating the series under the sign of the summation shows that this should satisfy the heat equation. However, convergence and regularity of the series are quite delicate.

The heat kernel is also sometimes identified with the associated [integral transform](wikipedia://en/430912), defined for compactly supported smooth $\phi$ by

$$T\phi = \int_\Omega K(t,x,y)\phi(y)\,dy.$$

The [spectral mapping theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_mapping_theorem) gives a representation of $T$ in the form the [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799#Semigroup_methods_in_partial_differential_equations)[^n4][^n5]

$$T = e^{t\Delta}.$$

There are several geometric results on heat kernels on manifolds; say, short-time asymptotics, long-time asymptotics, and upper/lower bounds of Gaussian type.
