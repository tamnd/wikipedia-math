# Walter Burley

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Walter Burley"}

*Image: Burley - De intensione et remissione formarum, 1496 - 163728.jpg.* *De intensione et remissione formarum*, 1496

**Walter Burley** (or **Burleigh**; 1275 – 1344/45) was an English [scholastic philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholastic_philosopher) and [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician) with at least 50 works attributed to him. He studied under [Thomas Wilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Wilton)[^o2] and received his [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_%28Oxbridge%29) degree in 1301, and was a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of [Merton College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merton_College), Oxford until about 1310. He then spent sixteen years in Paris, becoming a fellow of the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) by 1324, before spending 17 years as a clerical courtier in England and [Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon). Burley disagreed with [William of Ockham](wikipedia://en/33617) on a number of points concerning logic and [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy). He was known as the *Doctor Planus et Perspicuus*.

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Burley was born in 1274 or 1275, possibly in [Burley-in-Wharfedale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burley-in-Wharfedale), [Yorkshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yorkshire), or in [Burley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burley,_Leeds) near [Leeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leeds). Little is known of his early life. He was made rector of [Welbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welbury) in Yorkshire in 1309, probably through the influence of Sir John de Lisle, a friend of [William Greenfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Greenfield).[^n1] As throughout his career, he did not act as rector, employing a substitute and using the income from the living to finance his study in Paris, where he completed his lectures on Peter Lombard's *[Sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentences)*, and probably encountered the work of his contemporary [William of Ockham](wikipedia://en/33617). Burley's commentary on the *Sentences* has not survived.[^n2]

## Political career {#Political_career}

Burley became a courtier during the political events that followed the deposition of [Edward II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_II) of England in 1327. His first assignment was to try and obtain the [canonisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonisation) of [Thomas, 2nd Earl of Lancaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas,_2nd_Earl_of_Lancaster), who had been one of the leaders of the baronial opposition to Edward II; Thomas had become venerated as a [martyr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martyr) within a few months of his death. Burley was sent to the [papal court at Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avignon_Papacy) to appeal directly to [Pope John XXII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_John_XXII). By coincidence, William of Ockham was also staying at Avignon, having been summoned there in 1324 to answer charges of possibly heretical statements (by 1326 there was a list of 51 charges against him).

Burley's associates were all closely involved in these attempts at canonisation (none of which was successful). One was [Richard de Bury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_de_Bury), a bibliophile and patron of the arts and sciences, who became Burley's patron and at whose request Bury translated some works of [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) into English.

## Philosophical work {#Philosophical_work}

Burley was one of the first medieval logicians to recognize the priority of the [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) over the [term logic](wikipedia://en/321652),[^o8] despite the fact that the latter had been the main focus of logicians until then. Burley also seems to have been the only 14th-century logician to have taken the position that, in line with modern views on the [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808), the principle that "from the impossible anything follows" ("*ex impossibili sequitur quodlibet*") is both a [necessary and sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) for explaining the logical relationship between [antecedent](wikipedia://en/804755) and [consequent](wikipedia://en/804778).[^n3] He was also known for his commentaries on Aristotle's [*Physics*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_%28Aristotle%29), which include the quodlibet *De Primo et ultimo instanti* (around 1320) and the longer work *Expositio in libros octo de physico auditu*. Burley wrote 39 Commentaries on Aristotle and 32 Treatises and Questions (many unedited).[^o10]

- *De Puritate Artis Logicae*, in two versions:
  - *Tractatus brevior* (before 1324)
  - *Tractatus longior* (1325–28)

This is Burley's main work, in which he covers such topics as the truth conditions for complex sentences, both [truth-functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth-functional) and [modal](wikipedia://en/333365), as well as providing rules of inferences for different types of inferences. This book is known to have been written after Ockham's *[Summa Logicae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summa_Logicae)* (c. 1323), possibly partly in response to it.[^n4] Other works include:

- *In Aristotelis Perihermenias* (*Questions on Aristotle's Perihermenias*, 1301)
- *De consequentiis* (1302)
- *De exclusivis* (1302)
- *De exceptivis* (1302)
- *De suppositionibus* (1302)
- *De obligationibus* (1302)
- *De ente* (ca. 1310)
- *De qualitatibus* (ca. 1310)
- *Expositio super libros De Anima* (after 1310)
- *Tractatus de formis* (1324–1326)
- *Tractatus de universalibus* (after 1337)
