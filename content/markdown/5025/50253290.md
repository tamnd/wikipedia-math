# Binary mass function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binary mass function"}

In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), the **binary mass function** or simply **mass function** is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that constrains the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) of the unseen component (typically a [star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) or [exoplanet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exoplanet)) in a single-lined spectroscopic [binary star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_star) or in a [planetary system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_system). It can be calculated from [observable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation) quantities only, namely the [orbital period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period) of the binary system, and the peak [radial velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_velocity) of the observed star. The velocity of one binary component and the orbital period provide information on the separation and gravitational force between the two components, and hence on the masses of the components.

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

*Image: orbit2.gif.* Two bodies orbiting a common center of mass, indicated by the red plus. The larger body has a higher mass, and therefore a smaller orbit and a lower orbital velocity than its lower-mass companion.

The binary mass function follows from [Kepler's third law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler's_third_law) when the radial velocity of one binary component is known.[^o3] Kepler's third law describes the motion of two bodies orbiting a common [center of mass](wikipedia://en/173961). It relates the [orbital period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_period) with the orbital separation between the two bodies, and the sum of their masses. For a given orbital separation, a higher total system mass implies higher [orbital velocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_speed). On the other hand, for a given system mass, a longer orbital period implies a larger separation and lower orbital velocities.

Because the orbital period and orbital velocities in the binary system are related to the masses of the binary components, measuring these parameters provides some information about the masses of one or both components.[^o4] However, the true orbital velocity is often unknown, because velocities in the plane of the sky are much more difficult to determine than velocities along the line of sight.[^o3]

Radial velocity is the velocity component of orbital velocity in the line of sight of the observer. Unlike true orbital velocity, radial velocity can be determined from [Doppler spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_spectroscopy) of [spectral lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_line) in the light of a star,[^o5] or from [variations in the arrival times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar_timing) of pulses from a [radio pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_pulsar).[^o6] A binary system is called a single-lined spectroscopic binary if the radial motion of only one of the two binary components can be measured. In this case, a lower limit on the mass of the other, unseen component can be determined.[^o3]

The true mass and true orbital velocity cannot be determined from the radial velocity because the [orbital inclination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_inclination) is generally unknown. (The inclination is the orientation of the orbit from the point of view of the observer, and relates true and radial velocity.[^o3]) This causes a degeneracy between mass and inclination.[^o7][^o8] For example, if the measured radial velocity is low, this can mean that the true orbital velocity is low (implying low mass objects) and the inclination high (the orbit is seen edge-on), or that the true velocity is high (implying high mass objects) but the inclination low (the orbit is seen face-on).

## Derivation for a circular orbit {#Derivation_for_a_circular_orbit}

*Image: Doppler Shift vs Time.svg.* Radial velocity curve with peak radial velocity *K*=1 m/s and orbital period 2 years.

The peak radial velocity $K$ is the semi-amplitude of the radial velocity curve, as shown in the figure. The orbital period $P_\text{orb}$ is found from the periodicity in the radial velocity curve. These are the two observable quantities needed to calculate the binary mass function.[^o4]

The observed object of which the radial velocity can be measured is taken to be object 1 in this article, its unseen companion is object 2.

Let $M_1$ and $M_2$ be the stellar masses, with $M_1 + M_2 = M_\mathrm{tot}$ the total mass of the binary system, $v_1$ and $v_2$ the orbital velocities, and $a_1$ and $a_2$ the distances of the objects to the center of mass. $a_1 + a_2 = a$ is the [semi-major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-major_axis) (orbital separation) of the binary system.

We start out with Kepler's third law, with $\omega_\mathrm{orb} = 2 \pi/P_\mathrm{orb}$ the [orbital frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) and $G$ the [gravitational constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_constant),

$$GM_\text{tot} = \omega_\text{orb}^2 a^3.$$

Using the definition of the center of mass location, $M_1 a_1 = M_2 a_2$,[^o3] we can write

$$a = a_1 + a_2 = a_1 \left(1 + \frac{a_2}{a_1}\right) = a_1 \left(1 + \frac{M_1}{M_2}\right) = \frac{a_1}{M_2} (M_1 + M_2) = \frac{a_1 M_\mathrm{tot}}{M_2}.$$

Inserting this expression for $a$ into Kepler's third law, we find

$$GM_\mathrm{tot} = \omega_\mathrm{orb}^2 \frac{a_1^3 M_\mathrm{tot}^3}{M_2^3}.$$

which can be rewritten to

$$\frac{M_2^3}{M_\mathrm{tot}^2} = \frac{\omega_\mathrm{orb}^2 a_1^3}{G}.$$

The peak radial velocity of object 1, $K$, depends on the orbital inclination $i$ (an inclination of 0° corresponds to an orbit seen face-on, an inclination of 90° corresponds to an orbit seen edge-on). For a circular orbit ([orbital eccentricity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_eccentricity) = 0) it is given by[^o10]

$$K = v_1 \sin i = \omega_\text{orb} a_1 \sin i.$$

After substituting $a_1$ we obtain

$$\frac{M_2^3}{M_\text{tot}^2} = \frac{K^3}{G \omega_\text{orb} \sin^3 i}.$$

The binary mass function $f$ (with [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement) of mass) is[^o11][^o10][^o4][^o12][^o3][^o8][^o13]

$$f = \frac{M_2^3 \sin^3 i }{(M_1 + M_2)^2} = \frac{P_\mathrm{orb}\ K^3}{2 \pi G}.$$

For an estimated or assumed mass $M_1$ of the observed object 1, a [minimum mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_mass) $M_\mathrm{2, min}$ can be determined for the unseen object 2 by assuming $i = 90^{\circ}$. The true mass $M_2$ depends on the orbital inclination. The inclination is typically not known, but to some extent it can be determined from observed [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_%28astronomy%29),[^o4] be constrained from the non-observation of eclipses,[^o11][^o12] or be modelled using ellipsoidal variations (the non-spherical shape of a star in binary system leads to variations in brightness over the course of an orbit that depend on the system's inclination).[^o14]

### Limits {#Limits}

In the case of $M_1 \gg M_2$ (for example, when the unseen object is an exoplanet[^o11]), the mass function simplifies to

$$f \approx \frac{M_2^3\ \sin^3 i }{M_1^2}.$$

In the other extreme, when $M_1 \ll M_2$ (for example, when the unseen object is a high-mass [black hole](wikipedia://en/4650)), the mass function becomes[^o4]

$$f \approx M_2 \sin^3 i,$$

and since $0 \leq \sin(i) \leq 1$ for $0^{\circ} \leq i \leq 90^{\circ}$, the mass function gives a lower limit on the mass of the unseen object 2.[^o8]

In general, for any $i$ or $M_1$,

$$M_2 > \max\left(f, f^{1/3} M_1^{2/3}\right).$$

## Eccentric orbit {#Eccentric_orbit}

In an orbit with eccentricity $e$, the mass function is given by[^o10][^o17]

$$f = \frac{M_2^3 \sin^3 i }{(M_1 + M_2)^2} = \frac{P_\mathrm{orb}\ K^3}{2 \pi G} \left(1 - e^2\right)^{3/2}.$$

## Applications {#Applications}

### X-ray binaries {#X-ray_binaries}

If the accretor in an [X-ray binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_binary) has a minimum mass that significantly exceeds the [Tolman–Oppenheimer–Volkoff limit](wikipedia://en/4180667) (the maximum possible mass for a [neutron star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star)), it is expected to be a black hole. This is the case in [Cygnus X-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cygnus_X-1), for example, where the radial velocity of the companion star has been measured.[^o21][^o22]

### Exoplanets {#Exoplanets}

An [exoplanet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exoplanet) causes its host star to move in a small orbit around the center of mass of the star-planet system. This 'wobble' can be observed if the radial velocity of the star is sufficiently high. This is the [radial velocity method](wikipedia://en/7290120) of detecting exoplanets.[^o7][^o5] Using the mass function and the radial velocity of the host star, the minimum mass of an exoplanet can be determined.[^o24][^o25]p. 9[^o17][^o26] Applying this method on [Proxima Centauri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxima_Centauri), the closest star to the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System), led to the discovery of [Proxima Centauri b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxima_Centauri_b), a [terrestrial planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrestrial_planet) with a minimum mass of 1.27.[^o27]

### Pulsar planets {#Pulsar_planets}

[Pulsar planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar_planet) are planets orbiting [pulsars](wikipedia://en/1227284), and [several](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_exoplanets_detected_by_timing) have been discovered using [pulsar timing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pulsar_timing). The radial velocity variations of the pulsar follow from the varying intervals between the arrival times of the pulses.[^o6] The first exoplanets were discovered this way in 1992 around the [millisecond pulsar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millisecond_pulsar) [PSR 1257+12](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSR_1257+12).[^o29] Another example is [PSR J1719-1438](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSR_J1719-1438), a millisecond pulsar whose companion, [PSR J1719-1438 b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSR_J1719-1438_b), has a minimum mass approximate equal to the mass of [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter), according to the mass function.[^o11]
