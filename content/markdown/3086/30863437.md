# Ward–Takahashi identity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ward–Takahashi identity"}

In [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), a **Ward–Takahashi identity** is an identity between [correlation functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_function_%28quantum_field_theory%29) that follows from the global or gauge [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_in_physics) of the theory, and which remains valid after [renormalization](wikipedia://en/291453).

:::

The Ward–Takahashi identity of [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) (QED) was originally used by [John Clive Ward](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Clive_Ward)[^o2] and [Yasushi Takahashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yasushi_Takahashi)[^o3] to relate the [wave function renormalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_function_renormalization) of the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) to its [vertex renormalization factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_function), guaranteeing the cancellation of the [ultraviolet divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraviolet_divergence) to all orders of [perturbation theory](wikipedia://en/102140). Later uses include the extension of the proof of [Goldstone's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldstone's_theorem) to all orders of perturbation theory.

More generally, a Ward–Takahashi identity is the quantum version of classical current conservation associated to a continuous symmetry by [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159). Such symmetries in quantum field theory (almost) always give rise to these generalized Ward–Takahashi identities which impose the symmetry on the level of the quantum mechanical amplitudes. This generalized sense should be distinguished when reading literature, such as [Michael Peskin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Peskin) and [Daniel Schroeder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_V._Schroeder)'s textbook,[^o4] from the original Ward–Takahashi identity.

The detailed discussion below concerns QED, an [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [theory](wikipedia://en/23912155) to which the Ward–Takahashi identity applies. The equivalent identities for [non-abelian](wikipedia://en/4791400) theories such as [quantum chromodynamics](wikipedia://en/25264) (QCD) are the [Slavnov–Taylor identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slavnov–Taylor_identities).

The **Ward operator** describes how a scalar term in a Lagrangian transforms under infinitesimal gauge transformations. It is closely related to the [BRST operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRST_operator) and plays a central role in providing a geometric description of the consistent quantization of [gauge theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_theories).

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Ward–Takahashi identity"}

## Ward–Takahashi identity {#Ward–Takahashi_identity}

The Ward–Takahashi identity applies to correlation functions in [momentum space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Momentum_space), which do not necessarily have all their external momenta [on-shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-shell). Let

$$\mathcal{M}(k; p_1 \cdots p_n; q_1 \cdots q_n) = \epsilon_{\mu}(k) \mathcal{M}^{\mu}(k; p_1 \cdots p_n; q_1 \cdots q_n)$$

be a [QED](wikipedia://en/25268) [correlation function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correlation_function_%28quantum_field_theory%29) involving an external [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) with momentum $k$ (where $\epsilon_{\mu}(k)$ is the [polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_polarization) vector of the photon and summation over $\mu=0,\ldots,3$ is implied), $n$ initial-state [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) with momenta $p_1 \cdots p_n$, and $n$ final-state electrons with momenta $q_1 \cdots q_n$. Also define $\mathcal{M}_0$ to be the simpler [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude) that is obtained by removing the photon with momentum $k$ from our original amplitude. Then the Ward–Takahashi identity reads

$$\begin{aligned} k_{\mu} \mathcal{M}^{\mu}(k; p_1 \cdots p_n; q_1 \cdots q_n) = e \sum_i \left[ \mathcal{M}_0\right. & (p_1 \cdots p_n; q_1 \cdots (q_i-k) \cdots q_n) \\ & \left. - \mathcal{M}_0(p_1 \cdots (p_i+k) \cdots p_n; q_1 \cdots q_n) \right] \end{aligned}$$

where $e$ is the [charge of the electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_charge) and is negative in sign. Note that if $\mathcal{M}$ has its external electrons on-shell, then the amplitudes on the right-hand side of this identity each have one external particle off-shell, and therefore they do not contribute to [S-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053) elements.

:::

::: {#o8 .statement cue=named-heading name="Ward identity"}

## Ward identity {#Ward_identity}

The Ward identity is a specialization of the Ward–Takahashi identity to [S-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053) elements, which describe physically possible [scattering](wikipedia://en/164483) processes and thus have all their external particles [on-shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On-shell). Again let $\mathcal{M}(k) = \epsilon_{\mu}(k) \mathcal{M}^{\mu}(k)$ be the amplitude for some QED process involving an external photon with momentum $k$, where $\epsilon_{\mu}(k)$ is the [polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_polarization) vector of the photon. Then the Ward identity reads:

$$k_{\mu} \mathcal{M}^{\mu}(k) = 0$$

Physically, what this identity means is the longitudinal polarization of the photon which arises in the [&xi; gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/&xi;_gauge) is unphysical and disappears from the S-matrix.

Examples of its use include constraining the [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965) structure of the [vacuum polarization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_polarization) and of the electron [vertex function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_function) in QED.

:::

## Derivation in the path integral formulation {#Derivation_in_the_path_integral_formulation}

In the path integral formulation, the Ward–Takahashi identities are a reflection of the invariance of the [functional measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_measure) under a [gauge transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_transformation). More precisely, if $\delta_\varepsilon$ represents a gauge transformation by $\varepsilon$ (and this applies even in the case where the physical symmetry of the system is [global](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_symmetry) or even nonexistent; we are only worried about the *invariance of the functional measure* here), then

$$\int \delta_\varepsilon \left(\mathcal{F} e^{iS}\right) \mathcal{D}\phi = 0$$

expresses the invariance of the functional measure where $S$ is the [action](wikipedia://en/312881) and $\mathcal{F}$ is a [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) of the [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29#Quantum_fields). If the gauge transformation corresponds to a *[global](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_symmetry)* symmetry of the theory, then,

$$\delta_\varepsilon S=\int \left(\partial_\mu\varepsilon\right)J^\mu\mathrm{d}^dx = -\int\varepsilon \partial_\mu J^\mu\mathrm{d}^dx$$

for some "[current](wikipedia://en/1097925)" $J$ (as a functional of the fields $\phi$) after [integrating by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) and assuming that the [surface terms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_term) can be neglected.

Then, the Ward–Takahashi identities become

$$\langle\delta_\varepsilon\mathcal{F}\rangle - i\int\varepsilon\langle\mathcal{F}\partial_\mu J^\mu \rangle\mathrm{d}^dx = 0$$

This is the QFT analog of the [Noether continuity equation](wikipedia://en/150159#Field_theory_version) $\partial_\mu J^\mu=0$.

If the gauge transformation corresponds to an actual [gauge symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_symmetry) then

$$\int\delta_\varepsilon\left(\mathcal{F}e^{i\left(S+S_{gf}\right)}\right)\mathcal{D}\phi = 0$$

where $S$ is the gauge invariant action and $S_{\mathrm{gf}}$ is a non-gauge-invariant [gauge fixing](wikipedia://en/2000736) term. Gauge-fixing terms are required so as to be able to perform [second quantization](wikipedia://en/418156) of a classical gauge theory. The path-integral (Lagrangian) formulation of quantum field theory does not entirely avoid the need for gauge-fixing, as there is still a need to compute the asymptotic states of the [scattering matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_matrix) (*e.g* in the [interaction picture](wikipedia://en/901382).) In short, gauge-fixing is required, but it breaks the overall gauge invariance of the theory. The Ward–Takahashi identities then describe exactly how all of the different fields are tied to one-another, under an infinitesimal gauge transformation. These Ward–Takahashi identities are generated by the Ward operator; in the linearized form, the Ward operator is the [BRST operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRST_operator). The corresponding [charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_%28physics%29) is the [BRST charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRST_charge). When the gauge theory is formulated on a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526), the Ward–Takahashi identities correspond to a (global) right-action in the [principle bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_bundle): they are generated by the [Lie derivative](wikipedia://en/294437) on the [vertical bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical_bundle).

When the functional measure is not gauge invariant, but happens to satisfy

$$\int\delta_\varepsilon\left(\mathcal{F}e^{iS}\right)\mathcal{D}\phi = \int\varepsilon\lambda\mathcal{F}e^{iS}\mathrm{d}^dx$$

with $\lambda$ is some functional of the fields $\phi$, the corresponding relation gives the **anomalous Ward–Takahashi identity**. The conventional example is the [chiral anomaly](wikipedia://en/312229). This example is prominent in the [sigma model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_model) theory of [nuclear forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_force). In this theory, the [neutron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron) and [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton), in an [isospin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isospin) doublet, feel forces mediated by [pions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion), in an isospin triplet. This theory has not one, but two distinct global symmetries: the vector $\overline\psi\gamma_\mu\psi$ and the axial vector $\overline\psi\gamma_5 \gamma_\mu\psi$ symmetries; equivalently, the left and right [chiral symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiral_symmetry). The corresponding currents are the [isovector current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isovector_current) (the [rho meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rho_meson)) and the [axial vector current](wikipedia://en/72988466). It is not possible to quantize both at the same time (due to the anomalous Ward–Takahashi identity); by convention, the vector symmetry is quantized so that the vector current is conserved, while the axial vector current is not conserved. The [rho meson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rho_meson) is then interpreted as the [gauge boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_boson) of the vector symmetry, whereas the axial symmetry is [spontaneously broken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spontaneously_broken). The breaking is due to quantization, that is, due to the anomalous Ward–Takahashi identity (rather than to a Higgs-style Mexican-hat potential, which results in an entirely different kind of symmetry breaking). The divergence of the axial current relates the [pion-nucleon interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion-nucleon_interaction) to pion decay, fixing $g_A\approx 1.267$ as the [axial coupling constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axial_coupling_constant). The [Goldberger–Treiman relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goldberger–Treiman_relation) $f_\pi g_{\pi N \overline N} \simeq g_A m_N$ relates $g_A$ to the [pion decay constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pion_decay_constant) $f_\pi$. In this way, the chiral anomaly provides the canonical description of the pion-nucleon interaction.
