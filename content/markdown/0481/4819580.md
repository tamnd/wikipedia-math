# Capstone (cryptography)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Capstone"}

**Capstone** is a [United States government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_government) long-term project to develop [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432) standards for public and government use. Capstone was authorized by the [Computer Security Act of 1987](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_Security_Act_of_1987),[^o6] driven by the [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology) (NIST) and the [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) (NSA); the project began in 1993.[^o4]

:::

## Project {#Project}

The initiative involved four standard algorithms: a data [encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypt) algorithm called [Skipjack](wikipedia://en/474702), along with the [Clipper chip](wikipedia://en/409665) that included the Skipjack algorithm, a digital signature algorithm, [Digital Signature Algorithm](wikipedia://en/59470) (DSA), a hash function, [SHA-1](wikipedia://en/26672), and a [key exchange](wikipedia://en/578092) protocol.[^o5] Capstone's first implementation was in the [Fortezza](wikipedia://en/797842) [PCMCIA card](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PCMCIA_card). All Capstone components were designed to provide 80-bit security.[^o6]

The initiative encountered massive resistance from the cryptographic community, and eventually the US government abandoned the effort. The main reasons for this resistance were concerns about Skipjack's design, which was [classified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classified_information), and the use of [key escrow](wikipedia://en/17114) in the Clipper chip.
