# Introductio in analysin infinitorum

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Introductio in analysin infinitorum"}

*Image: hyperbola E.svg.* [Euler's number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_number) **e** corresponds to shaded area (under the hyperbola y = 1/x) equal to 1, introduced in chapter VII

***Introductio in analysin infinitorum*** ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin):[^o2] ***Introduction to the Analysis of the Infinite***) is a two-volume work by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) which lays the foundations of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396). Written in Latin and published in 1748, the *Introductio* contains 18 chapters in the first part and 22 chapters in the second. It has [Eneström numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eneström_index) E101 and E102.[^o3][^o4] It is considered the first [precalculus](wikipedia://en/725821) book.

:::

## Contents {#Contents}

Chapter 1 is on the concepts of [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) and [functions](wikipedia://en/185427). Chapters 2 and 3 are concerned with the transformation of functions. Chapter 4 introduces [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) through [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210).

According to [Henk Bos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henk_Bos_%28historian%29),

The *Introduction* is meant as a survey of concepts and methods in analysis and analytic geometry preliminary to the study of the differential and integral calculus. \[Euler\] made of this survey a masterly exercise in introducing as much as possible of analysis without using differentiation or integration. In particular, he introduced the elementary transcendental functions, the logarithm, the exponential function, the trigonometric functions and their inverses without recourse to integral calculus — which was no mean feat, as the logarithm was traditionally linked to the quadrature of the hyperbola and the trigonometric functions to the arc-length of the circle.[^o6]

Euler accomplished this feat by introducing [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) *a*<sup>*x*</sup> for arbitrary constant *a* in the [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571). He noted that mapping *x* this way is *not* an [algebraic function](wikipedia://en/974169), but rather a [transcendental function](wikipedia://en/297021). For *a* > 1 these functions are monotonic increasing and form [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942) of the real line with positive real numbers. Then each base *a* corresponds to an [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) called the logarithm to base *a*, in chapter 6. In chapter 7, Euler introduces e as the number whose hyperbolic logarithm is 1, using the terminology established by [Gregoire de Saint-Vincent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gregoire_de_Saint-Vincent), who defined what is now called the natural logarithm through [quadrature](wikipedia://en/1618671) of the hyperbola *y* = 1/*x*. Section 122 labels the logarithm to base e the "natural or hyperbolic logarithm...since the quadrature of the hyperbola can be expressed through these logarithms". Here he also gives the exponential series:

$$\exp(z) = \sum_{k = 0}^{\infty} {z^k \over k!} = 1 + z + {z^2 \over 2} + {z^3 \over 6} + {z^4 \over 24} + \cdots$$

Then in chapter 8 Euler is prepared to address the classical trigonometric functions as "transcendental quantities that arise from the circle." He uses the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) and presents [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613). Chapter 9 considers trinomial factors in [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000). Chapter 16 is concerned with [partitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_%28number_theory%29), a topic in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). [Continued fractions](wikipedia://en/739199) are the topic of chapter 18.

## Impact {#Impact}

*Image: Houghton GC7 Eu536 748i - Introductio in analysin infinitorum.jpg.* Page from *Introductio in analysin infinitorum*, 1748

[Carl Benjamin Boyer](wikipedia://en/6694636)'s lectures at the 1950 [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) compared the influence of Euler's *Introductio* to that of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s *[Elements](wikipedia://en/244107)*, calling the *Elements* the foremost [textbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textbook) of ancient times, and the *Introductio* "the foremost textbook of modern times".[^o8] Boyer also wrote:

The analysis of Euler comes close to the modern orthodox discipline, the study of functions by means of infinite processes, especially through infinite series.

It is doubtful that any other essentially didactic work includes as large a portion of original material that survives in the college courses today...Can be read with comparative ease by the modern student...The prototype of modern textbooks.

## English translations {#English_translations}

The first translation into English was that by John D. Blanton, published in 1988.[^o10] The second, by Ian Bruce, is available online.[^o11] A list of the editions of *Introductio* has been assembled by [V. Frederick Rickey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V._Frederick_Rickey).[^o12]
