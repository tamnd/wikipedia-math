# Monad (philosophy)

*Image: Monad.svg.* The circled dot was used by the Pythagoreans and later Greeks to represent the first metaphysical being, the *Monad* or the [Absolute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_%28philosophy%29).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Monad"}

The term ***monad*** (grc)[^o2] is used in some [cosmic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_cosmos) and [cosmogony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmogony) to refer to a most basic or original substance. As originally conceived by the [Pythagoreans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoreans), the Monad is, therefore, the Supreme Being, [divinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity), or the totality of all things.

:::

According to some philosophers of the [early modern period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_modern_period), most notably [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281), there are infinite monads, which are the basic and immense forces, [elementary particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle), or [simplest units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomism) that make up the universe.[^o3]

## Historical background {#Historical_background}

### Ancient Greece {#Ancient_Greece}

According to [Hippolytus of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippolytus_of_Rome), the [Pythagoreans](wikipedia://en/291170) called the first thing that came into existence the "monad", which begat (bore) the [dyad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dyad_%28philosophy%29) (from the Greek word for two), which begat the [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690), which begat the [point](wikipedia://en/593693), begetting [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) (or finiteness), and so on.[^o6] [Diogenes Laërtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes_Laërtius) similarly held that from the monad evolved the dyad; from it numbers; from numbers, points; then lines, two-dimensional [entities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Entity), three-dimensional entities, bodies, culminating in the four [classical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_element) of earth, water, fire and air, from which the rest of our world is built up.[^o6]

For the Pythagoreans, the generation of number series bridged the gap between objects of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [cosmogony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmogony) (creation theories).[^o7] They thus held the monad as a symbol of [divinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divinity), the first being, or the totality of all beings, referring in cosmogony variously to source acting alone or an indivisible origin and [equivalent comparators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstraction_%28philosophy%29).[^o8]

In the [Neoplatonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonic) metaphysics of [Proclus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclus), reality is organized in a fractal-like structure, where the role of the monad is to bind together each layer with the next, such that a monad at a higher level folds out into a multiplicity at a lower level; this lower multiplicity is itself a multiplicity of monads at the next level down.[^o9][^n1] Proclus thus writes in the *[Elements of Theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elements_of_Theology)* (21.1–3): "Every order of reality has its beginning in a monad and proceeds to a multiplicity coordinate with that monad; and the multiplicity in any order may be referred back to a single monad."[^n2]

Some Neoplatonic philosophers, such as [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) and [Porphyry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porphyry_%28philosopher%29), were [opposed to Gnosticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism_and_Neoplatonism#Neoplatonist_objections), which they considered broadly synonymous with Christianity as a whole.[^n3] In *Against the Gnostics* (*[Enn.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enneads)* II.9), Plotinus contends that they "contract the divine into one",[^n4] thereby accusing them of misapplying Platonic theory about the monad in defense of [monotheism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism).[^n5]

The idea of the monad is also reflected in the [demiurge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demiurge), or the belief of one supreme being that brought about the [creation of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_myth).

### Medieval Christianity {#Medieval_Christianity}

In his [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin)-language treaty Maximae theologiae, [Alain de Lille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alain_de_Lille) affirms that "God is an intelligible [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), whose center is everywhere and whose circumference is nowhere." The French philosopher [François Rabelais](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/François_Rabelais) ascribed this proposition to [Hermes Trismegistus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes_Trismegistus).[^o16] This [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9331) [symbolism](wikipedia://en/37673) of the centered sphere also concerns the [secular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secular) debate on the existence of a [center of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_mundi). The symbolism is a free [exegesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exegesis) related to the [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity) in [Christian theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_theology) and influenced by Plotinus.[^o16] Alain also mentions the *[Book of the Twenty-Four Philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_the_24_Philosophers)*, attributed to Hermes Trismegistus, wherein it is stated that a Monad will beget only another Monad;[^o16] some Christians saw in this the generation, or creation, of [God the Son](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Son) from [God the Father](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_the_Father). This view might be similar to that of the [pagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagan) author of the *[Asclepius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asclepius_%28treatise%29),*[^o16] who sometimes has also been identified with Hermes Trismegistus. The *Book of the Twenty-Four Philosophers* completes the scheme adding that the ardor of the second Monad to the first Monad would be the [Holy Spirit in Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit_in_Christianity).[^o16] It closes a physical circle in a logical triangle (with a [retroaction](wikipedia://en/2120001)).

## Modern philosophy {#Modern_philosophy}

The term monad was adopted from [Greek philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophy) by a number of [modern philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_philosophy), including [Giordano Bruno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giordano_Bruno), [Anne Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anne_Conway_%28philosopher%29), [John Dee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dee) (*[The Hieroglyphic Monad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monas_Hieroglyphica)*), and [Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) (*[Monadology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monadology)*). The concept of the monad as a universal substance is also used by [Theosophists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theosophy) as a synonym for the [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) term "[svabhavat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Svabhava)"; [the Mahatma Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Mahatma_Letters_to_A.P._Sinnett) make frequent use of the term.[^o18]
