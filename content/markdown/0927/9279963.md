# Thom–Mather stratified space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thom–Mather stratified space"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a branch of mathematics, an **abstract [stratified space](wikipedia://en/25886609)**, or a **Thom–Mather stratified space** is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* that has been decomposed into pieces called **strata**; these strata are [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) and are required to fit together in a certain way. Thom–Mather stratified spaces provide a purely topological setting for the study of singularities analogous to the more differential-geometric theory of [Whitney](wikipedia://en/391794). They were introduced in 1969 by [René Thom](wikipedia://en/61633), who showed that every [Whitney stratified space](wikipedia://en/9322242) was also a topologically stratified space, with the same strata. Another proof was given by [John Mather](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Mather_%28mathematician%29) in 1970, inspired by Thom's proof.

:::

Basic examples of Thom–Mather stratified spaces include [manifolds with boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifolds_with_boundary) (top dimension and codimension 1 boundary) and [manifolds with corners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_with_corners) (top dimension, codimension 1 boundary, codimension 2 corners), real or complex analytic varieties, or orbit spaces of smooth transformation groups.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A Thom–Mather stratified space is a triple $(V, {\mathcal S}, {\mathfrak J})$ where $V$ is a topological space (often we require that it is [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact), [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), and [second countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countable)), ${\mathcal S}$ is a decomposition of $V$ into **strata,**

$$V = \bigsqcup_{X\in {\mathcal S}} X,$$

and ${\mathfrak J}$ is the set of **control data** $\{ (T_X), (\pi_X), (\rho_X)\ | X\in S\}$ where $T_X$ is an open neighborhood of the stratum $X$ (called the tubular neighborhood), $\pi_X: T_X \to X$ is a continuous retraction, and $\rho_X: T_X \to [0, +\infty)$ is a continuous function. These data need to satisfy the following conditions.

1. Each stratum $X$ is a [locally closed subset](wikipedia://en/26136898) and the decomposition $S$ is [locally finite](wikipedia://en/11611098).
1. The decomposition $S$ satisfies the axiom of the frontier: if $X, Y \in {\mathcal S}$ and $Y \cap \overline{X} \neq \emptyset$, then $Y \subseteq \overline{X}$. This condition implies that there is a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) among strata: $Y<X$ if and only if $Y \subset\overline{X}$ and $Y \neq X$.
1. Each stratum $X$ is a smooth manifold.
1. $X = \{ v \in T_X\ |\ \rho_X(v) = 0\}$. So $\rho_X$ can be viewed as the *distance function* from the stratum $X$.
1. For each pair of strata $Y<X$, the restriction $(\pi_Y, \rho_Y): T_Y \cap X \to Y \times (0, +\infty)$ is a [submersion](wikipedia://en/356413).
1. For each pair of strata $Y<X$, there holds $\pi_Y \circ \pi_X = \pi_Y$ and $\rho_Y \circ \pi_X = \rho_Y$ (both over the common domain of both sides of the equation).

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

One of the original motivations for stratified spaces were decomposing singular spaces into smooth chunks. For example, given a singular variety $X$, there is a naturally defined subvariety, $\mathrm{Sing}(X)$, which is the singular locus. This may not be a smooth variety, so taking the iterated singularity locus $\mathrm{Sing}(\mathrm{Sing}(X))$ will eventually give a natural stratification. A simple algebreo-geometric example is the singular [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101)

$$\text{Spec}\left(\mathbb{C}[x,y,z]/\left(x^4 + y^4 + z^4\right)\right) \xleftarrow{(0,0,0)} \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C})$$

where $\text{Spec}(-)$ is the [prime spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291).
