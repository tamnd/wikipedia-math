# Berlin Papyrus 6619

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Berlin Papyrus 6619"}

The **Berlin Papyrus 6619**, simply called the **Berlin Papyrus** when the context makes it clear,[^o3] is one of the primary sources of [ancient Egyptian mathematics](wikipedia://en/1180105) and part of the [Berlin Papyrus Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin_Papyrus_Collection).[^o4] One of the two mathematics problems on the Papyrus may suggest that the ancient Egyptians knew the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).

:::

## Description, dating, and provenance {#Description,_dating,_and_provenance}

The Berlin Papyrus 6619 is an [ancient Egyptian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egypt) papyrus document from the [Middle Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Kingdom_of_Egypt),[^o6] second half of the [12th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twelfth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) (c. 1990–1800 BC) or [13th Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirteenth_Dynasty_of_Egypt) (c. 1800 BC – 1649 BC).[^o7] It is written in the [hieratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hieratic) script.[^o8] The two readable fragments were published by Hans Schack-Schackenburg in 1900 and 1902.[^o9][^o10]

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Connection to the Pythagorean theorem"}

## Connection to the Pythagorean theorem {#Connection_to_the_Pythagorean_theorem}

The Berlin Papyrus contains two problems, the first stated as "the area of a square of 100 is equal to that of two smaller squares. The side of one is ½ + ¼ the side of the other."[^o13] The interest in the question may suggest some knowledge of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034), though the papyrus only shows a straightforward solution to a single second degree equation in one unknown. In modern terms, the simultaneous equations *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = 100 and *x* = (3/4)*y* reduce to the single equation in *y*: ((3/4)*y*)<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> = 100, giving the solution *y* = 8 and *x* = 6.

:::
