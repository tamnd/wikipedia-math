# Classical Heisenberg model

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Classical Heisenberg model"}

In [statistical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_physics), the **classical Heisenberg model**, developed by [Werner Heisenberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Werner_Heisenberg), is the $n = 3$ case of the [*n*-vector model](wikipedia://en/1165549), one of the models used to model [ferromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) and other phenomena.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The classical Heisenberg model can be formulated as follows: take a d-dimensional [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246), and place a set of spins of unit length,

$$\vec{s}_i \in \mathbb{R}^3, |\vec{s}_i|=1\quad (1),$$

on each lattice node.

The model is defined through the following [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319):

$$\mathcal{H} = -\sum_{i,j} \mathcal{J}_{ij} \vec{s}_i \cdot \vec{s}_j\quad (2)$$

where

$$\mathcal{J}_{ij} = \begin{cases} J & \mbox{if }i, j\mbox{ are neighbors} \\ 0 & \mbox{else.}\end{cases}$$

is a coupling between spins.

The classical Heisenberg model is the [semiclassical limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiclassical_physics) of the isotropic spin-*s* [quantum Heisenberg model](wikipedia://en/4899779) in the limit as *s* becomes large.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The general mathematical formalism used to describe and solve the Heisenberg model and certain generalizations is developed in the article on the [Potts model](wikipedia://en/1162226).
- In the [continuum limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_limit) the Heisenberg model (2) gives the following equation of motion

$$\vec{S}_{t}=\vec{S}\wedge \vec{S}_{xx}.$$

This equation is called the [continuous classical Heisenberg ferromagnet equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_classical_Heisenberg_ferromagnet_equation) or, more shortly, the Heisenberg model and is [integrable](wikipedia://en/2573213) in the sense of soliton theory. It admits several integrable and nonintegrable generalizations like the [Landau-Lifshitz equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau–Lifshitz_model), the [Ishimori equation](wikipedia://en/16617601), and so on.

### One dimension {#One_dimension}

- In the case of a long-range interaction, $J_{x,y}\sim |x-y|^{-\alpha}$, the thermodynamic limit is well defined if $\alpha >1$; the magnetization remains zero if $\alpha \ge 2$; but the magnetization is positive, at a low enough temperature, if $1< \alpha < 2$ (infrared bounds).
- As in any 'nearest-neighbor' [n-vector model](wikipedia://en/1165549) with free boundary conditions, if the external field is zero, there exists a simple exact solution.

### Two dimensions {#Two_dimensions}

- In the case of a long-range interaction, $J_{x,y}\sim |x-y|^{-\alpha}$, the thermodynamic limit is well defined if $\alpha >2$; the magnetization remains zero if $\alpha \ge 4$; but the magnetization is positive at a low enough temperature if $2< \alpha < 4$ (infrared bounds).
- Polyakov has conjectured that, as opposed to the [classical XY model](wikipedia://en/954006), there is no [dipole phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipole_phase) for any $T>0$; namely, at non-zero temperatures the correlations cluster exponentially fast.[^o8]

### Three and higher dimensions {#Three_and_higher_dimensions}

Independently of the range of the interaction, at a low enough temperature the magnetization is positive.

Conjecturally, in each of the low temperature extremal states the truncated correlations decay algebraically.
