# Logarithmic derivative

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Logarithmic derivative"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), the **logarithmic derivative** of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ is defined by the formula

$$\frac{f'}{f}$$

where $f'$ is the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of $f$.[^o2] Intuitively, this is the infinitesimal [relative change](wikipedia://en/10202429) in $f$; that is, the infinitesimal absolute change in $f$, namely $f'$ scaled by the current value of $f$.

:::

When $f$ is a function $f(x)$ of a real variable $x$, and takes [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), strictly [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) values, this is equal to the derivative of $\ln f(x)$, or the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) of $f$. This follows directly from the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113):[^o2]

$$\frac{d}{dx}\ln f(x) = \frac{1}{f(x)} \frac{df(x)}{dx}$$

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

Many properties of the real logarithm also apply to the logarithmic derivative, even when the function does *not* take values in the positive reals. For example, since the logarithm of a product is the sum of the logarithms of the factors, we have

$$(\log uv)' = (\log u + \log v)' = (\log u)' + (\log v)' .$$

So for positive-real-valued functions, the logarithmic derivative of a product is the sum of the logarithmic derivatives of the factors. But we can also use the [Leibniz law](wikipedia://en/1116115) for the derivative of a product to get

$$\frac{(uv)'}{uv} = \frac{u'v + uv'}{uv} = \frac{u'}{u} + \frac{v'}{v} .$$

Thus, it is true for *any* function that the logarithmic derivative of a product is the sum of the logarithmic derivatives of the factors (when they are defined).

A [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) to this is that the logarithmic derivative of the reciprocal of a function is the negation of the logarithmic derivative of the function:

$$\frac{(1/u)'}{1/u} = \frac{-u'/u^{2}}{1/u} = -\frac{u'}{u} ,$$

just as the logarithm of the reciprocal of a positive real number is the negation of the logarithm of the number.

More generally, the logarithmic derivative of a quotient is the difference of the logarithmic derivatives of the dividend and the divisor:

$$\frac{(u/v)'}{u/v} = \frac{(u'v - uv')/v^{2}}{u/v} = \frac{u'}{u} - \frac{v'}{v} ,$$

just as the logarithm of a quotient is the difference of the logarithms of the dividend and the divisor.

Generalising in another direction, the logarithmic derivative of a power (with constant real exponent) is the product of the exponent and the logarithmic derivative of the base:

$$\frac{(u^{k})'}{u^{k}} = \frac {ku^{k-1}u'}{u^{k}} = k \frac{u'}{u} ,$$

just as the logarithm of a power is the product of the exponent and the logarithm of the base.

Finally, the logarithmic derivative of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) of $u$ is just the derivative of $u$:

$$\frac{(e^u)'}{e^u} = \frac {u' e^u}{e^u} = u' ,$$

just as the logarithm of the exponential function of a function is just the original function.

In summary, both derivatives and logarithms have a [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988), a [reciprocal rule](wikipedia://en/4300321), a [quotient rule](wikipedia://en/209128), and a [power rule](wikipedia://en/147912) (compare the [list of logarithmic identities](wikipedia://en/18019)); each pair of rules is related through the logarithmic derivative.

:::

## Computing ordinary derivatives using logarithmic derivatives {#Computing_ordinary_derivatives_using_logarithmic_derivatives}

Logarithmic derivatives can simplify the computation of derivatives requiring the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) while producing the same result. The procedure is as follows: Suppose that $f(x) = u(x)v(x)$ and that we wish to compute $f'(x)$. Instead of computing it directly as $f' = u'v + v'u$, we compute its logarithmic derivative. That is, we compute:

$$\frac{f'}{f} = \frac{u'}{u} + \frac{v'}{v}.$$

Multiplying through by $f$ computes $f'$:

$$f' = f\cdot\left(\frac{u'}{u} + \frac{v'}{v}\right).$$

This technique is most useful when $f$ is a product of a large number of factors. This technique makes it possible to compute $f'$ by computing the logarithmic derivative of each factor, summing, and multiplying by $f$.

For example, we can compute the logarithmic derivative of

$$(e^x)^2(x-2)^3(x-3)(x-1)^{-1}$$

to be

$$2 + \frac{3}{x-2} + \frac{1}{x-3} - \frac{1}{x-1} .$$

## Integrating factors {#Integrating_factors}

The logarithmic derivative idea is closely connected to the [integrating factor](wikipedia://en/1912874) method for [first-order differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_differential_equation). In [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) terms, write

$$D = \frac{d}{dx}$$

and let *M* denote the operator of multiplication by some given function *G*(*x*). Then

$$M^{-1} D M$$

can be written (by the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988)) as

$$D + M^{*}$$

where $M^{*}$ now denotes the multiplication operator by the logarithmic derivative

$$\frac{G'}{G}$$

In practice we are given an operator such as

$$D + F = L$$

and wish to solve equations

$$L(h) = f$$

for the function *h*, given *f*. This then reduces to solving

$$\frac{G'}{G} = F$$

which has as solution

$$\exp \textstyle ( \int F )$$

with any [indefinite integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integral) of *F*.

## Complex analysis {#Complex_analysis}

The formula as given can be applied more widely; for example if *f*(*z*) is a [meromorphic function](wikipedia://en/80857), it makes sense at all complex values of *z* at which *f* has neither a [zero nor a pole](wikipedia://en/81560). Further, at a zero or a pole the logarithmic derivative behaves in a way that is easily analysed in terms of the particular case

with *n* an integer, $n \ne 0$. The logarithmic derivative is then

$$n/z$$

and one can draw the general conclusion that for *f* meromorphic, the singularities of the logarithmic derivative of *f* are all *simple* poles, with [residue](wikipedia://en/73102) *n* from a zero of order *n*, residue −*n* from a pole of order *n*. See [argument principle](wikipedia://en/1138322). This information is often exploited in [contour integration](wikipedia://en/704359).[^o8][^o9]

In the field of [Nevanlinna theory](wikipedia://en/393135), an important lemma states that the proximity function of a logarithmic derivative is small with respect to the Nevanlinna characteristic of the original function, for instance $m(r,h'/h) = S(r,h) = o(T(r,h))$.[^o10]

## The multiplicative group {#The_multiplicative_group}

Behind the use of the logarithmic derivative lie two basic facts about *GL*<sub>1</sub>, that is, the multiplicative group of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) or other [field](wikipedia://en/10603). The [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886)

$$X\frac{d}{dX}$$

is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under dilation (replacing *X* by *aX* for *a* constant). And the [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519)

$$\frac{dx}{X}$$

is likewise invariant. For functions *F* into GL<sub>1</sub>, the formula

$$\frac{dF}{F}$$

is therefore a *[pullback](wikipedia://en/494995)* of the invariant form.

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- [Exponential growth](wikipedia://en/191933) and [exponential decay](wikipedia://en/330320) are processes with constant logarithmic derivative.
- In [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680), the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Greeks_%28finance%29) *λ* is the logarithmic derivative of derivative price with respect to underlying price.
- In [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506), the [condition number](wikipedia://en/6934) is the infinitesimal relative change in the output for a relative change in the input, and is thus a ratio of logarithmic derivatives.
- The [digamma function](wikipedia://en/406935), and by extension the [polygamma function](wikipedia://en/406932), is defined in terms of the logarithmic derivative of the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316).
