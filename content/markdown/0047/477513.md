# Helmholtz's theorems

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Helmholtz's theorems"}

In [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988), **Helmholtz's theorems**, named after [Hermann von Helmholtz](wikipedia://en/53311), describe the three-dimensional motion of fluid in the vicinity of [vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vortex) lines. These theorems apply to [inviscid flows](wikipedia://en/5877457) and flows where the influence of [viscous forces](wikipedia://en/18963754) are small and can be ignored.

:::

Helmholtz's three theorems are as follows:[^o2]

**Helmholtz's first theorem**

The strength of a vortex line is constant along its length.

**Helmholtz's second theorem**

A vortex line cannot end in a fluid; it must extend to the boundaries of the fluid or form a closed path.

**Helmholtz's third theorem**

A fluid element that is initially irrotational remains irrotational.

Helmholtz's theorems apply to inviscid flows. In observations of vortices in real fluids the strength of the vortices always decays gradually due to the dissipative effect of [viscous forces](wikipedia://en/18963754).

Alternative expressions of the three theorems are as follows:

1. The strength of a vortex tube does not vary with time.[^o3]
1. Fluid elements lying on a vortex line at some instant continue to lie on that vortex line. More simply, vortex lines move with the fluid. Also vortex lines and tubes must appear as a closed loop, extend to infinity or start/end at solid boundaries.
1. Fluid elements initially free of [vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010) remain free of vorticity.

Helmholtz's theorems have application in understanding:

- [Generation of lift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lift_%28force%29#Stages_of_lift_production) on an [airfoil](wikipedia://en/290053)
- [Starting vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starting_vortex)
- [Horseshoe vortex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horseshoe_vortex)
- [Wingtip vortices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wingtip_vortices).

Helmholtz's theorems are now generally proven with reference to [Kelvin's circulation theorem](wikipedia://en/7086534). However Helmholtz's theorems were published in 1858,[^o4] nine years before the 1867 publication of Kelvin's theorem.

The convention for defining the sense of circulation and the normal to the surface **A** is given by the [right-hand screw rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule). The third theorem states that this strength is the same for all cross-sections A of the tube and is independent of time. This is equivalent to saying $\frac{D \Gamma}{Dt} = 0$
