# Pseudoscalar

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pseudoscalar"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a **pseudoscalar** is a quantity that behaves like a [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_%28physics%29), except that it changes sign under a [parity inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29)[^o2][^o3] while a true scalar does not.

:::

A pseudoscalar, when multiplied by an ordinary [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956), becomes a *[pseudovector](wikipedia://en/166356)* (or *axial vector*); a similar construction creates the [pseudotensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudotensor). A pseudoscalar also results from any scalar product between a pseudovector and an ordinary vector. The prototypical example of a pseudoscalar is the [scalar triple product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_triple_product), which can be written as the [scalar product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_product) between one of the vectors in the triple product and the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) between the two other vectors, where the latter is a pseudovector.

## In physics {#In_physics}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a pseudoscalar denotes a [physical quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity) analogous to a [scalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_%28physics%29). Both are [physical quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity) which assume a single value which is invariant under [proper rotations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_rotation). However, under the [parity transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_transformation), pseudoscalars flip their signs while scalars do not. As [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829) through a plane are the combination of a rotation with the parity transformation, pseudoscalars also change signs under reflections.

### Motivation {#Motivation}

One of the most powerful ideas in physics is that physical laws do not change when one changes the [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931) used to describe these laws. That a pseudoscalar reverses its sign when the coordinate axes are inverted suggests that it is not the best object to describe a physical quantity. In 3D-space, quantities described by a pseudovector are antisymmetric tensors of order 2, which are invariant under inversion. The pseudovector may be a simpler representation of that quantity, but suffers from the change of sign under inversion. Similarly, in 3D-space, the [Hodge dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_dual) of a scalar is equal to a constant times the 3-dimensional [Levi-Civita pseudotensor](wikipedia://en/195982) (or "permutation" pseudotensor); whereas the Hodge dual of a pseudoscalar is an antisymmetric (pure) tensor of order three. The Levi-Civita pseudotensor is a completely [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/696955) pseudotensor of order 3. Since the dual of the pseudoscalar is the product of two "pseudo-quantities", the resulting tensor is a true tensor, and does not change sign upon an inversion of axes. The situation is similar to the situation for pseudovectors and antisymmetric tensors of order 2. The dual of a pseudovector is an antisymmetric tensor of order 2 (and vice versa). The tensor is an invariant physical quantity under a coordinate inversion, while the pseudovector is not invariant.

The situation can be extended to any dimension. Generally in an *n*-dimensional space the Hodge dual of an order *r* tensor will be an antisymmetric pseudotensor of order (*n* − *r*) and vice versa. In particular, in the four-dimensional spacetime of [special relativity](wikipedia://en/26962), a pseudoscalar is the dual of a fourth-order tensor and is proportional to the four-dimensional [Levi-Civita pseudotensor](wikipedia://en/195982).

### Examples {#Examples}

- The [stream function](wikipedia://en/218091) $\psi(x,y)$ for a two-dimensional, incompressible fluid flow $\mathbf{v}(x,y)=\langle \partial_{y}\psi,-\partial_{x}\psi\rangle$.
- [Magnetic charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_charge) is a pseudoscalar as it is mathematically defined, regardless of whether it exists physically.
- [Helicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helicity_%28particle_physics%29) is the projection (dot product) of a [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) pseudovector onto the direction of [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) (a true vector).
- Pseudoscalar particles, i.e. particles with spin 0 and odd parity, that is, a particle with no intrinsic spin with [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) that changes sign under [parity inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parity_%28physics%29). Examples are [pseudoscalar mesons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscalar_meson).

:::

## In geometric algebra {#In_geometric_algebra}

A pseudoscalar in a [geometric algebra](wikipedia://en/12939) is a highest-[grade](wikipedia://en/1155734) element of the algebra. For example, in two dimensions there are two [orthogonal basis](wikipedia://en/755400) vectors, $e_1$, $e_2$ and the associated highest-grade basis element is

$$e_1 e_2 = e_{12}.$$

So a pseudoscalar is a multiple of $e_{12}$. The element $e_{12}$ squares to −1 and commutes with all even elements – behaving therefore like the imaginary scalar $i$ in the [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers). It is these scalar-like properties which give rise to its name.

In this setting, a pseudoscalar changes sign under a parity inversion, since if

$$(e_1,e_2) \mapsto (u_1, u_2)$$

is a [change of basis](wikipedia://en/1179451) representing an [orthogonal transformation](wikipedia://en/1382381), then

$$e_1 e_2 \mapsto u_1 u_2 = \pm e_1 e_2,$$

where the sign depends on the determinant of the transformation. Pseudoscalars in geometric algebra thus correspond to the pseudoscalars in physics.
