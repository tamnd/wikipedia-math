# Ryszard Winiarski

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Ryszard Winiarski"}

**Ryszard Winiarski** (March 2, 1936 – December 4, 2006) was a Polish painter and [set designer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scenic_design) best known for his [monochromatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monochrome) [abstract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_art) geometric compositions and spatial forms.[^o3] He has been associated with [Conceptualist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_art) tendencies in Poland during 1960s and 1970s and is considered one of the key representatives of [indeterminism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminism) in Polish postwar art. Winiarski's work was informed by his interest in the relationship between art and science.[^o3]

:::

## Life and work {#Life_and_work}

*Image: Ryszard Winiarski, Sto zdarzeń 10x10.jpg.* *One hundred events 10x10,* 1977

Ryszard Winiarski was born in [Lwów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lwów), then part of the [Second Polish Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_Polish_Republic), on March 2, 1936.[^o3] He studied at the [Warsaw University of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_University_of_Technology) from 1953 to 1959, graduating with a master's degree in precision mechanics. Subsequently, he pursued studies in painting under [Aleksander Kobzdej](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleksander_Kobzdej) at the [Warsaw Academy of Fine Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Fine_Arts,_Warsaw) from 1958 to 1966. He later worked at the Warsaw Academy of Fine Arts as a lecturer and, between 1985 and 1990, as the vice-dean.[^o3] In his art, Winiarski combined [constructivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructivism_%28art%29) principles focused on geometry with elements of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). This approach included the use of certain mathematical systems to compose and create visual forms.[^o5]p. 426

Winiarski experimented with chance and probability by composing [abstract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_art) arrangements of small squares, initially in black-and-white and later in color.[^o6] Winiarski regarded the anonymous building blocks of a small square as "statistical material" from which to build the surface of the work.[^o7] In 1966, Winiarski wrote: "My ambition involves attempts at visually recording events and presenting their [statistical distributions](wikipedia://en/23543) visually. The multitude of possibilities that such treatment of the image offers fascinates me".[^o5]p. 404–405 Between 1967 and 1977, Winiarski created set designs for several plays performed in Poland, including *Medea* by [Euripides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euripides), [William Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shakespeare)'s *[Othello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Othello)* and *[Macbeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeth)*, among others.[^o3]

In 1987, he initiated the creation of geometric compositions using lit candles, a series titled *Geometry, or a Chance for Meditation*. Winiarski's works are included in permanent collections of major Polish museums, including the [Museum of Art in Łódź](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Art,_Łódź) and the [National Museum in Kraków](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Museum_in_Kraków).[^o3] Similarly to his contemporary [Roman Opałka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Opałka), Winiarski dedicated the majority of his career to exploring a singular [conceptual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conceptual_art) project.[^o8] In 1995 he received [Jan Cybis Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Cybis_Award). According to art historian and critic Bożena Kowalska, Ryszard Winiarski was among the most influential Polish artists in the second half of the twentieth century.[^o10] Winiarski died in Warsaw on December 14, 2006, at the age of 70 following a long illness.[^o11]
