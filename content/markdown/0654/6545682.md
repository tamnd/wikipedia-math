# Honeycomb theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Honeycomb theorem"}

*Image: Tiling Regular 6-3 Hexagonal.svg.* A regular [hexagonal grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_grid)

*Image: Triangular tiling circle packing.svg.* This honeycomb forms a [circle packing](wikipedia://en/16879665), with circles centered on each hexagon.

The **honeycomb theorem,** formerly the **honeycomb conjecture**, states that a regular [hexagonal grid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_grid) or [honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb) has the least total [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) of any subdivision of the plane into regions of equal area. The conjecture was proven in 1999 by mathematician [Thomas C. Hales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_C._Hales).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

Let $\Gamma$ be any system of smooth curves in $\mathbb{R}^2$, subdividing the plane into regions (connected components of the complement of $\Gamma$), all of which are bounded and have unit area. Then, averaged over large disks in the plane, the average length of $\Gamma$ per unit area is at least as large as for the hexagon tiling. The theorem applies even if the complement of $\Gamma$ has additional components that are unbounded or whose area is not one; allowing these additional components cannot shorten $\Gamma$. Formally, let $B(0,r)$ denote the disk of radius $r$ centered at the origin, let $L_r$ denote the total length of $\Gamma\cap B(0,r)$, and let $A_r$ denote the total area of $B(0,r)$ covered by bounded unit-area components. (If these are the only components, then $A_r=\pi r^2$.) Then the theorem states that

$$\limsup_{r\to\infty} \frac{L_r}{A_r}\ge\sqrt[4]{12}.$$

The value on the right hand side of the inequality is the limiting length per unit area of the hexagonal tiling.

:::

## History {#History}

The first record of the conjecture dates back to 36 BC, from [Marcus Terentius Varro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Terentius_Varro), but is often attributed to [Pappus of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/509300) (c. 290).[^o7] In the 17th century, [Jan Brożek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Brożek) used a similar theorem to argue why [bees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bee) create [hexagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal) [honeycombs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb). In 1943, [László Fejes Tóth](wikipedia://en/2388442) published a proof for a special case of the conjecture, in which each cell is required to be a [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019).[^o6] The full conjecture was proven in 1999 by mathematician [Thomas C. Hales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_C._Hales), who mentions in his work that there is reason to believe that the conjecture may have been present in the minds of mathematicians before Varro.[^o2][^o7]

It is also related to the densest [circle packing](wikipedia://en/16879665#Packings_in_the_plane) of the plane, in which every circle is tangent to six other circles, which fill just over 90% of the area of the plane.

The case when the problem is restricted to a square grid was solved in 1989 by Jaigyoung Choe who proved that the optimal figure is an irregular hexagon.[^o8][^o9]
