# Representation on coordinate rings

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Representation on coordinate rings"}

In mathematics, a **representation on coordinate rings** is a [representation of a group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_a_group) on coordinate rings of affine varieties.

:::

Let *X* be an [affine algebraic variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_algebraic_variety) over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) *k* of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) zero with the action of a [reductive algebraic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductive_algebraic_group) *G*.[^o2] *G* then [acts](wikipedia://en/12781) on the coordinate ring $k[X]$ of *X* as a [left regular representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_regular_representation): $(g \cdot f)(x) = f(g^{-1} x)$. This is a representation of *G* on the coordinate ring of *X*.

The most basic case is when *X* is an affine space (that is, *X* is a finite-dimensional representation of *G*) and the coordinate ring is a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065). The most important case is when *X* is a [symmetric variety](wikipedia://en/37635994); i.e., the quotient of *G* by a [fixed-point subgroup](wikipedia://en/43166060) of an involution.

## Isotypic decomposition {#Isotypic_decomposition}

Let $k[X]_{(\lambda)}$ be the sum of all *G*-submodules of $k[X]$ that are isomorphic to the [simple module](wikipedia://en/29430) $V^{\lambda}$; it is called the $\lambda$-[isotypic component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotypic_component) of $k[X]$. Then there is a direct sum decomposition:

$$k[X] = \bigoplus_{\lambda} k[X]_{(\lambda)}$$

where the sum runs over all simple *G*-modules $V^{\lambda}$. The existence of the decomposition follows, for example, from the fact that the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) of *G* is semisimple since *G* is reductive.

*X* is called *multiplicity-free* (or [spherical variety](wikipedia://en/39305846)[^o4]) if every irreducible representation of *G* appears at most one time in the coordinate ring; i.e., $\operatorname{dim} k[X]_{(\lambda)} \le \operatorname{dim} V^{\lambda}$. For example, $G$ is multiplicity-free as $G \times G$-module. More precisely, given a closed subgroup *H* of *G*, define

$$\phi_{\lambda}: V^{{\lambda}*} \otimes (V^{\lambda})^H \to k[G/H]_{(\lambda)}$$

by setting $\phi_{\lambda}(\alpha \otimes v)(gH) = \langle \alpha, g \cdot v \rangle$ and then extending $\phi_{\lambda}$ by linearity. The functions in the image of $\phi_{\lambda}$ are usually called [matrix coefficients](wikipedia://en/11017946). Then there is a direct sum decomposition of $G \times N$-modules (*N* the normalizer of *H*)

$$k[G/H] = \bigoplus_{\lambda} \phi_{\lambda}(V^{{\lambda}*} \otimes (V^{\lambda})^H),$$

which is an algebraic version of the [Peter–Weyl theorem](wikipedia://en/294474) (and in fact the analytic version is an immediate consequence.) Proof: let *W* be a simple $G \times N$-submodules of $k[G/H]_{(\lambda)}$. We can assume $V^{\lambda} = W$. Let $\delta_1$ be the [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) of *W* such that $\delta_1(w) = w(1)$. Then $w(gH) = \phi_{\lambda}(\delta_1 \otimes w)(gH)$. That is, the image of $\phi_{\lambda}$ contains $k[G/H]_{(\lambda)}$ and the opposite inclusion holds since $\phi_{\lambda}$ is equivariant.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Let $v_{\lambda} \in V^{\lambda}$ be a *B*-[eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) and *X* the closure of the orbit $G \cdot v_\lambda$. It is an affine variety called the highest weight vector variety by Vinberg–Popov. It is multiplicity-free.

:::
