# Geometric Poisson distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geometric Poisson distribution"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **geometric Poisson distribution** (also called the **Pólya–Aeppli distribution**) is used for describing objects that come in clusters, where the number of clusters follows a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) and the number of objects within a cluster follows a [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922).[^n1] It is a particular case of the [compound Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/949562).[^n2]

:::

The [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725) of a random variable *N* distributed according to the geometric Poisson distribution $\mathcal{PG}(\lambda,\theta)$ is given by

$$f_N(n) = \mathrm{Pr}(N=n)= \begin{cases} \sum_{k=1}^n e^{-\lambda}\frac{\lambda^k}{k!}(1-\theta)^{n-k}\theta^k\binom{n-1}{k-1}, & n>0 \\ e^{-\lambda}, & n=0 \end{cases}$$

where *λ* is the parameter of the underlying [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) and θ is the parameter of the geometric distribution.[^n2]

The distribution was described by [George Pólya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya) in 1930. Pólya credited his student [Alfred Aeppli](wikipedia://en/43228330)'s 1924 dissertation as the original source. It was called the geometric Poisson distribution by Sherbrooke in 1968, who gave probability tables with a precision of four decimal places.[^n3]

The geometric Poisson distribution has been used to describe systems modelled by a [Markov model](wikipedia://en/22022581), such as biological processes[^n2] or traffic accidents.[^n4]
