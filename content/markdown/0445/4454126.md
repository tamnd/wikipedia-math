# Joseph Keller

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Joseph Keller"}

**Joseph Bishop Keller** (July 31, 1923 – September 7, 2016) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who specialized in [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969). He was best known for his work on the geometrical theory of diffraction.[^o4]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Born in [Paterson, New Jersey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paterson,_New_Jersey) on July 31, 1923, Keller attended [Eastside High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastside_High_School_%28Paterson,_New_Jersey%29), where he was a member of the math team.[^o6] After earning his undergraduate degree in 1943 at [New York University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_University), Keller obtained his PhD in 1948 from NYU under the supervision of [Richard Courant](wikipedia://en/715238). He was a professor of mathematics in the [Courant Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courant_Institute) at New York University until 1979. Then he was Professor of Mathematics and Mechanical Engineering at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) until 1993, when he became [professor emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor_emeritus).

## Research {#Research}

Keller worked on the application of mathematics to problems in science and engineering, such as [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation). He contributed to the [Einstein–Brillouin–Keller method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein–Brillouin–Keller_method) for computing [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) in [quantum mechanical](wikipedia://en/25202) systems.

## Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

Keller was awarded a [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford_Award) (shared with [David W. McLaughlin](wikipedia://en/83200082)) in 1976[^o9] and (not shared) in 1977.[^o10] In 1988 he was awarded the U.S. [National Medal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Medal_of_Science), and in 1997 he was awarded the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize) by the Israel-based [Wolf Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Foundation). In 1996, he was awarded the [Nemmers Prize in Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1252473). In 1999 he was awarded the [Ig Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ig_Nobel_Prize) for calculating how to make a teapot spout that does not drip. With Patrick B. Warren, Robin C. Ball and [Raymond E. Goldstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raymond_E._Goldstein), Keller was awarded an [Ig Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ig_Nobel_Prize) in 2012 for calculating the forces that shape and move [ponytail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ponytail) hair.[^o11][^o12] In 2012 he became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o13]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Keller's second wife, [Alice S. Whittemore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alice_S._Whittemore), started her career as a pure mathematician but shifted her interests to [epidemiology](wikipedia://en/66997) and [biostatistics](wikipedia://en/3878). Keller had a brother who was also a mathematician, [Herbert B. Keller](wikipedia://en/24424009), who studied numerical analysis, [scientific computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_computing), [bifurcation theory](wikipedia://en/2326042), path following and homotopy methods, and [computational fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/305924); Keller was the co-advisor of his brother Herbert's PhD thesis. Herbert Keller was a professor at [Caltech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caltech). Both brothers contributed to the fields of [electromagnetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetics) and [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034). Joseph Keller died in [Stanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford,_California), [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California) on September 7, 2016, from a recurrence of kidney cancer first diagnosed in 2003.[^o15]
