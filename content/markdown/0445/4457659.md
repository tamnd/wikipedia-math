# Period-doubling bifurcation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Period-doubling bifurcation"}

In [dynamical systems theory](wikipedia://en/990632), a **period-doubling bifurcation** occurs when a slight change in a system's parameters causes a new periodic trajectory to emerge from an existing periodic trajectory—the new one having double the period of the original. With the doubled period, it takes twice as long (or, in a [discrete](wikipedia://en/40240263) dynamical system, twice as many iterations) for the numerical values visited by the system to repeat themselves.

:::

A **period-halving bifurcation** occurs when a system switches to a new behavior with half the period of the original system.

A **period-doubling cascade** is an infinite sequence of period-doubling bifurcations. Such cascades are one route by which dynamical systems can develop chaos.[^o2] In [hydrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrodynamics), they are one of the possible routes to [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664).[^o3]

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Logistic Bifurcation map High Resolution.png.* [Bifurcation diagram](wikipedia://en/79173) for the logistic map. It shows the [attractor values](wikipedia://en/191094), like $x_*$ and $x'_*$, as a function of the parameter $r$.

### Logistic map {#Logistic_map}

The [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137) is

$$x_{n+1} = r x_n (1 - x_n)$$

where $x_n$ is a function of the (discrete) time $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots$.[^o7] The parameter $r$ is assumed to lie in the interval $[0,4]$, in which case $x_n$ is bounded on $[0,1]$.

For $r$ between 1 and 3, $x_n$ converges to the stable fixed point $x_* = (r-1)/r$. Then, for $r$ between 3 and 3.44949, $x_n$ converges to a permanent oscillation between two values $x_*$ and $x'_*$ that depend on $r$. As $r$ grows larger, oscillations between 4 values, then 8, 16, 32, etc. appear. These period doublings culminate at $r \approx 3.56995$, beyond which more complex regimes appear. As $r$ increases, there are some intervals where most starting values will converge to one or a small number of stable oscillations, such as near $r=3.83$, where there is a stable period-three solution.

In the interval where the period is $2^n$ for some positive integer $n$, not all the points actually have period $2^n$. These are single points, rather than intervals. These points are said to be in unstable orbits, since nearby points do not approach the same orbit as them.

### Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation {#Kuramoto–Sivashinsky_equation}

*Image: K-S equation period doubling.png.* Period doubling in the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation with periodic boundary conditions. The curves depict solutions of the Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation projected onto the energy phase plane *(E, dE/dt)*, where *E* is the [*L<sup>2</sup>*-norm](wikipedia://en/45194) of the solution. For *ν* = 0.056, there exists a periodic orbit with period *T* ≈ 1.1759. Near *ν* ≈ 0.0558, this solution splits into 2 orbits, which further separate as *ν* is decreased. Exactly at the transitional value of *ν*, the new orbit (red-dashed) has double the period of the original. (However, as *ν* increases further, the ratio of periods deviates from exactly 2.)

The [Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation](wikipedia://en/53788372) is an example of a spatiotemporally continuous dynamical system that exhibits period doubling. It is one of the most well-studied nonlinear [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564), originally introduced as a model of flame front propagation.[^o9]

The one-dimensional Kuramoto–Sivashinsky equation is

$$u_t + u u_x + u_{xx} + \nu \, u_{xxxx} = 0$$

A common choice for boundary conditions is spatial periodicity: $u(x + 2 \pi, t) = u(x,t)$.

For large values of $\nu$, $u(x,t)$ evolves toward steady (time-independent) solutions or simple periodic orbits. As $\nu$ is decreased, the dynamics eventually develops chaos. The transition from order to chaos occurs via a cascade of period-doubling bifurcations,[^o10][^o11] one of which is illustrated in the figure.

### Logistic map for a modified Phillips curve {#Logistic_map_for_a_modified_Phillips_curve}

Consider the following logistical map for a modified [Phillips curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillips_curve):

$$\pi_{t} = f(u_{t}) + b \pi_{t}^e$$

$$\pi_{t+1} = \pi_{t}^e + c (\pi_{t} - \pi_{t}^e)$$

$$f(u) = \beta_{1} + \beta_{2} e^{-u} \,$$

$$b > 0, 0 \leq c \leq 1, \frac {df} {du} < 0$$

where :

- $\pi$ is the actual [inflation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation)
- $\pi^e$ is the expected inflation,
- u is the level of unemployment,
- $m - \pi$ is the [money supply](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_supply) growth rate.

Keeping $\beta_{1} = -2.5, \ \beta_{2} = 20, \ c = 0.75$ and varying $b$, the system undergoes period-doubling bifurcations and ultimately becomes chaotic.

## Experimental observation {#Experimental_observation}

Period doubling has been observed in a number of experimental systems.[^o14] There is also experimental evidence of period-doubling cascades. For example, sequences of 4 period doublings have been observed in the dynamics of [convection rolls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convection_rolls) in water and [mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28element%29).[^o15][^o16] Similarly, 4-5 doublings have been observed in certain nonlinear [electronic circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_circuit).[^o17][^o18][^o19] However, the experimental precision required to detect the *i*<sup>th</sup> doubling event in a cascade increases exponentially with *i*, making it difficult to observe more than 5 doubling events in a cascade.[^o20]
