# Boussinesq approximation (water waves)

*Image: Bous2d berk03 z.gif.* Simulation of periodic waves over an underwater [shoal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shoal) with a Boussinesq-type model. The waves propagate over an elliptic-shaped underwater shoal on a plane beach. This example combines several effects of [waves and shallow water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waves_and_shallow_water), including [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction), [diffraction](wikipedia://en/8603), shoaling and weak [non-linearity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linearity).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Boussinesq approximation"}

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), the **Boussinesq approximation** for [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves) is an [approximation](wikipedia://en/336271) valid for weakly [non-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear) and fairly long waves. The approximation is named after [Joseph Valentin Boussinesq](wikipedia://en/2373094), who first derived them in response to the observation by [John Scott Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Scott_Russell) of the [wave of translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_of_translation) (also known as [solitary wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solitary_wave_%28water_waves%29) or [soliton](wikipedia://en/51654)). The 1872 paper by Boussinesq introduces the equations now known as the **Boussinesq equations**.[^o2]

:::

The Boussinesq approximation for [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves) takes into account the vertical structure of the horizontal and vertical [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity). This results in [non-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear) [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations), called **Boussinesq-type equations**, which incorporate [frequency dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650) (as opposite to the [shallow water equations](wikipedia://en/8631522), which are not frequency-dispersive). In [coastal engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_engineering), Boussinesq-type equations are frequently used in [computer models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_models) for the [simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation) of [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves) in [shallow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waves_and_shallow_water) [seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea) and [harbours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harbours).

While the Boussinesq approximation is applicable to fairly long waves – that is, when the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) is large compared to the water depth – the [Stokes expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stokes_expansion) is more appropriate for short waves (when the wavelength is of the same order as the water depth, or shorter).

## Boussinesq approximation {#Boussinesq_approximation}

*Image: Boussinesq T06H18d5.svg.* Periodic waves in the Boussinesq approximation, shown in a vertical [cross section](wikipedia://en/1337370) in the [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) direction. Notice the flat [troughs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trough_%28physics%29) and sharp [crests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crest_%28physics%29), due to the wave nonlinearity. This case (drawn on [scale](wikipedia://en/49526)) shows a wave with the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) equal to 39.1 [m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre), the wave height is 1.8 m (*i.e.* the difference between crest and trough elevation), and the mean water depth is 5 m, while the [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_gravity) is 9.81 m/s<sup>2</sup>.

The essential idea in the Boussinesq approximation is the elimination of the vertical [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) from the flow equations, while retaining some of the influences of the vertical structure of the flow under [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves). This is useful because the waves propagate in the horizontal plane and have a different (not wave-like) behaviour in the vertical direction. Often, as in Boussinesq's case, the interest is primarily in the wave propagation.

This elimination of the vertical coordinate was first done by [Joseph Boussinesq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Boussinesq) in 1871, to construct an approximate solution for the solitary wave (or [wave of translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_of_translation)). Subsequently, in 1872, Boussinesq derived the equations known nowadays as the Boussinesq equations.

The steps in the Boussinesq approximation are:

- a [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) is made of the horizontal and vertical [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) (or [velocity potential](wikipedia://en/2448955)) around a certain [elevation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation),
- this [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) is truncated to a finite number of terms,
- the conservation of mass (see [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793)) for an [incompressible flow](wikipedia://en/461477) and the zero-[curl](wikipedia://en/6123) condition for an [irrotational flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational_flow) are used, to replace vertical [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives) of quantities in the [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) with horizontal [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives).

Thereafter, the Boussinesq approximation is applied to the remaining flow equations, in order to eliminate the dependence on the vertical coordinate. As a result, the resulting [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) are in terms of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) of the horizontal [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates) (and [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time)).

As an example, consider [potential flow](wikipedia://en/58320) over a horizontal bed in the $(x,z)$ plane, with $x$ the horizontal and $z$ the vertical [coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate). The bed is located at $z=-h$, where $h$ is the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) water depth. A [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) is made of the [velocity potential](wikipedia://en/2448955) $\varphi(x,z,t)$ around the bed level $z=-h$:[^o4]

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi\, =\, & \varphi_b\, +\, (z+h)\, \left[ \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial z } \right]_{z=-h}\, +\, \frac{1}{2}\, (z+h)^2\, \left[ \frac{\partial^2 \varphi}{\partial z^2} \right]_{z=-h}\, \\ & +\, \frac{1}{6}\, (z+h)^3\, \left[ \frac{\partial^3 \varphi}{\partial z^3} \right]_{z=-h}\, +\, \frac{1}{24}\, (z+h)^4\, \left[ \frac{\partial^4 \varphi}{\partial z^4} \right]_{z=-h}\, +\, \cdots, \end{aligned}$$

where $\varphi_b(x,t)$ is the velocity potential at the bed. Invoking [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) for $\varphi$, as valid for [incompressible flow](wikipedia://en/461477), gives:

$$\begin{aligned} \varphi\, =\, & \left\{\, \varphi_b\, -\, \frac{1}{2}\, (z+h)^2\, \frac{\partial^2 \varphi_b}{\partial x^2}\, +\, \frac{1}{24}\, (z+h)^4\, \frac{\partial^4 \varphi_b}{\partial x^4}\, +\, \cdots\, \right\}\, \\ & +\, \left\{\, (z+h)\, \left[ \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial z} \right]_{z=-h}\, -\, \frac16\, (z+h)^3\, \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} \left[ \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial z} \right]_{z=-h}\, +\, \cdots\, \right\} \\ =\, & \left\{\, \varphi_b\, -\, \frac{1}{2}\, (z+h)^2\, \frac{\partial^2 \varphi_b}{\partial x^2}\, +\, \frac{1}{24}\, (z+h)^4\, \frac{\partial^4 \varphi_b}{\partial x^4}\, +\, \cdots\, \right\}, \end{aligned}$$

since the vertical velocity $\partial\varphi/\partial z$ is zero at the – impermeable – horizontal bed $z=-h$. This series may subsequently be truncated to a finite number of terms.

## Original Boussinesq equations {#Original_Boussinesq_equations}

### Derivation {#Derivation}

For [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves) on an [incompressible fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompressible_fluid) and [irrotational flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational_flow) in the $(x,z)$ plane, the [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions) at the [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) elevation $z=\eta(x,t)$ are:[^o7]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial t}\, &+\, u\, \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial x}\, -\, w\, =\, 0 \\ \frac{\partial \varphi}{\partial t}\, &+\, \frac{1}{2}\, \left( u^2 + w^2 \right)\, +\, g\, \eta\, =\, 0, \end{aligned}$$

where:

- $u$ is the horizontal [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) component: $u=\partial\varphi/\partial x$,
- $w$ is the vertical [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) component: $w=\partial\varphi/\partial z$,
- $g$ is the [acceleration](wikipedia://en/2443) by [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity).

Now the Boussinesq approximation for the [velocity potential](wikipedia://en/2448955) $\varphi$, as given above, is applied in these [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions). Further, in the resulting equations only the [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear) and [quadratic](wikipedia://en/25175) terms with respect to $\eta$ and $u_b$ are retained (with $u_b=\partial\varphi_b/\partial x$ the horizontal velocity at the bed $z=-h$). The [cubic](wikipedia://en/180787) and higher order terms are assumed to be negligible. Then, the following [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) are obtained:

**set A – Boussinesq (1872), equation (25)**

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial t}\, & +\, \frac{\partial}{\partial x}\, \left[ \left( h + \eta \right)\, u_b \right]\, =\, \frac{1}{6}\, h^3\, \frac{\partial^3 u_b}{\partial x^3}, \\ \frac{\partial u_b}{\partial t}\, & +\, u_b\, \frac{\partial u_b}{\partial x}\, +\, g\, \frac{\partial \eta}{\partial x}\, =\, \frac{1}{2}\, h^2\, \frac{\partial^3 u_b}{\partial t\, \partial x^2}. \end{aligned}$$

This set of equations has been derived for a flat horizontal bed, *i.e.* the mean depth $h$ is a constant independent of position $x$. When the right-hand sides of the above equations are set to zero, they reduce to the [shallow water equations](wikipedia://en/8631522).

Under some additional approximations, but at the same order of accuracy, the above set **A** can be reduced to a single [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) for the [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) elevation $\eta$:

**set B – Boussinesq (1872), equation (26)**

$$\frac{\partial^2 \eta}{\partial t^2}\, -\, g h\, \frac{\partial^2 \eta}{\partial x^2}\, -\, g h\, \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} \left( \frac{3}{2}\, \frac{\eta^2}{h}\, +\, \frac{1}{3}\, h^2\, \frac{\partial^2 \eta}{\partial x^2} \right)\, =\, 0.$$

From the terms between brackets, the importance of nonlinearity of the equation can be expressed in terms of the [Ursell number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursell_number). In [dimensionless quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantities), using the water depth $h$ and gravitational acceleration $g$ for non-dimensionalization, this equation reads, after [normalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalisable_wave_function):[^o8]

$$\frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial \tau^2}\, -\, \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial \xi^2}\, -\, \frac{\partial^2}{\partial \xi^2} \left(\, 3\, \psi^2\, +\, \frac{\partial^2 \psi}{\partial \xi^2}\, \right)\, =\, 0,$$

with:

| $\psi\, =\, \frac12\, \frac{\eta}{h}$ | : the dimensionless surface elevation, |
|---|---|
| $\tau\, =\, \sqrt{3}\, t\, \sqrt{\frac{g}{h}}$ | : the dimensionless time, and |
| $\xi\, =\, \sqrt{3}\, \frac{x}{h}$ | : the dimensionless horizontal position. |

*Image: disp\_bouss.svg.* Linear phase speed squared $c^2/(gh)$ as a function of relative wave number $kh$. **A** = Boussinesq (1872), equation (25), **B** = Boussinesq (1872), equation (26), **C** = full linear wave theory, see [dispersion (water waves)](wikipedia://en/1744650)

### Linear frequency dispersion {#Linear_frequency_dispersion}

[Water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_waves) of different [wave lengths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_length) travel with different [phase speeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_speed), a phenomenon known as [frequency dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650). For the case of [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) wave [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude), the terminology is *linear frequency dispersion*. The frequency dispersion characteristics of a Boussinesq-type of equation can be used to determine the range of wave lengths, for which it is a valid [approximation](wikipedia://en/336271).

The linear [frequency dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650) characteristics for the above set **A** of equations are:[^o10]

$$c^2\, =\; g h\, \frac{ 1\, +\, \frac{1}{6}\, k^2 h^2 }{ 1\, +\, \frac{1}{2}\, k^2 h^2 },$$

with:

- $c$ the [phase speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_speed),
- $k$ the [wave number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_number) ($k=2\pi/\lambda$, with $\lambda$ the [wave length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_length)).

The [relative error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_error) in the phase speed $c$ for set **A**, as compared with [linear theory for water waves](wikipedia://en/19620471), is less than 4% for a relative wave number $kh<\pi/2$. So, in [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) applications, set **A** is valid for wavelengths $\lambda$ larger than 4 times the water depth $h$.

The linear [frequency dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650) characteristics of equation **B** are:[^o10]

$$c^2\, =\, g h\, \left( 1\, -\, \frac{1}{3}\, k^2 h^2 \right).$$

The relative error in the phase speed for equation **B** is less than 4% for $kh<2\pi/7$, equivalent to wave lengths $\lambda$ longer than 7 times the water depth $h$, called **fairly long waves**.[^o11]

For short waves with $k^2 h^2>3$ equation **B** become physically meaningless, because there are no longer [real-valued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-valued) [solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solution_%28mathematics%29) of the [phase speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_speed). The original set of two [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations) (Boussinesq, 1872, equation 25, see set **A** above) does not have this shortcoming.

The [shallow water equations](wikipedia://en/8631522) have a relative error in the phase speed less than 4% for wave lengths $\lambda$ in excess of 13 times the water depth $h$.

## Boussinesq-type equations and extensions {#Boussinesq-type_equations_and_extensions}

There are an overwhelming number of [mathematical models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_models) which are referred to as Boussinesq equations. This may easily lead to confusion, since often they are loosely referenced to as *the* Boussinesq equations, while in fact a variant thereof is considered. So it is more appropriate to call them **Boussinesq-type equations**. Strictly speaking, *the* Boussinesq equations is the above-mentioned set **B**, since it is used in the analysis in the remainder of his 1872 paper.

Some directions, into which the Boussinesq equations have been extended, are:

- varying [bathymetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathymetry),
- improved [frequency dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650),
- improved [non-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear) behavior,
- making a [Taylor expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_expansion) around different vertical [elevations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevations),
- dividing the fluid domain in layers, and applying the Boussinesq approximation in each layer separately,
- inclusion of [wave breaking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_breaking),
- inclusion of [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension),
- extension to [internal waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internal_waves) on an [interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interface_%28chemistry%29) between fluid domains of different [mass density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_density),
- derivation from a [variational principle](wikipedia://en/491097).

## Further approximations for one-way wave propagation {#Further_approximations_for_one-way_wave_propagation}

While the Boussinesq equations allow for waves traveling simultaneously in opposing directions, it is often advantageous to only consider waves traveling in one direction. Under small additional assumptions, the Boussinesq equations reduce to:

- the [Korteweg–de Vries equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korteweg–de_Vries_equation) for [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) in one horizontal [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398),
- the [Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation](wikipedia://en/4211219) for (near uni-directional) [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) in two horizontal [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398),
- the [nonlinear Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/1422748) (NLS equation) for the [complex-valued amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex-valued_amplitude) of [narrowband](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrowband) waves (slowly [modulated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulated) waves).

Besides solitary wave solutions, the Korteweg–de Vries equation also has periodic and exact solutions, called [cnoidal waves](wikipedia://en/22463969). These are approximate solutions of the Boussinesq equation.

## Numerical models {#Numerical_models}

*Image: Bouss-2D simulation of nearshore waves.jpg.* A simulation with a Boussinesq-type wave model of nearshore waves travelling towards a harbour entrance. The simulation is with the BOUSS-2D module of [SMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_%28hydrology_software%29).

*Image: Celeris Wave Model.gif.* Faster than real-time simulation with the Boussinesq module of Celeris, showing wave breaking and refraction near the beach. The model provides an interactive environment.

For the simulation of wave motion near coasts and harbours, numerical models – both commercial and academic – employing Boussinesq-type equations exist. Some commercial examples are the Boussinesq-type wave modules in [MIKE 21](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIKE_21) and [SMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMS_%28hydrology_software%29). Some of the free Boussinesq models are Celeris,[^o15] COULWAVE,[^o16] and FUNWAVE.[^o17] Most numerical models employ [finite-difference](wikipedia://en/6054681), [finite-volume](wikipedia://en/345017) or [finite element](wikipedia://en/18233581) techniques for the [discretization](wikipedia://en/330017) of the model equations. Scientific reviews and intercomparisons of several Boussinesq-type equations, their numerical approximation and performance are e.g. (Kirby 2003), (Dingemans 1997, Part 2, Chapter 5) and (Hamm Madsen Peregrine 1993).
