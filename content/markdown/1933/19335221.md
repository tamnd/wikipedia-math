# Miquel's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Miquel's theorem"}

*Image: Miquel Circles.svg.* A diagram showing circles passing through the vertices of a triangle *ABC* and points *A´*, *B´* and *C´* on the adjacent sides of the triangle intersecting at a common point, *M*.

**Miquel's theorem** is a result in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), named after [Auguste Miquel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Miquel),[^o2] concerning the intersection of three circles, each drawn through one vertex of a triangle and two points on its adjacent sides. It is one of several results concerning circles in [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) due to Miquel, whose work was published in [Liouville's](wikipedia://en/341810) newly founded journal *[Journal de mathématiques pures et appliquées](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_de_mathématiques_pures_et_appliquées)*.

:::

Formally, let *ABC* be a triangle, with arbitrary points *A´*, *B´* and *C´* on sides *BC*, *AC*, and *AB* respectively (or their [extensions](wikipedia://en/43958459)). Draw three [circumcircles](wikipedia://en/1926432) (**Miquel's circles**) to triangles *AB´C´*, *A´BC´*, and *A´B´C*. Miquel's theorem states that these circles intersect in a single point *M*, called the **Miquel point**. In addition, the three angles *MA´B*, *MB´C* and *MC´A* (green in the diagram) are all equal, as are the three supplementary angles *MA´C*, *MB´A* and *MC´B*.[^o3][^o4]

The theorem (and its corollary) follow from the properties of [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131). Let the circumcircles of A'B'C and AB'C' meet at $M \ne B'.$ Then $\angle A'MC' = 2\pi - \angle B'MA' - \angle C'MB' = 2\pi - (\pi - C) - (\pi - A) = A + C = \pi - B,$ hence BA'MC' is cyclic as desired.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Pivot theorem"}

## Pivot theorem {#Pivot_theorem}

*Image: Pivot theorem.svg.* The Pivot Theorem for various triangles

If in the statement of Miquel's theorem the points *A´*, *B´* and *C´* form a triangle (that is, are not [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear)) then the theorem was named the **Pivot theorem** in (Forder 1960, 17).[^o8] (In the diagram these points are labeled *P*, *Q* and *R*.)

If *A´*, *B´* and *C´* are collinear then the Miquel point is on the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) of ∆ABC and conversely, if the Miquel point is on this circumcircle, then *A´*, *B´* and *C´* are on a line.[^o9]

:::

## Trilinear coordinates of the Miquel point {#Trilinear_coordinates_of_the_Miquel_point}

If the fractional distances of *A´*, *B´* and *C´* along sides *BC* (*a*), *CA* (*b*) and *AB* (*c*) are *d*<sub>*a*</sub>, *d*<sub>*b*</sub> and *d*<sub>*c*</sub>, respectively, the Miquel point, in [trilinear coordinates](wikipedia://en/3837790) (*x* : *y* : *z*), is given by:

$$x=a \left(-a^2 d_a d_a' + b^2 d_a d_b + c^2 d_a' d_c' \right)$$

$$y=b \left(a^2 d_a' d_b' - b^2 d_b d_b' + c^2 d_b d_c \right)$$

$$z=c \left(a^2 d_a d_c + b^2 d_b' d_c' - c^2 d_c d_c' \right),$$

where *d**<sub>*a*</sub> = 1 -*****d*<sub>*a*</sub>,*etc.**

In the case *d*<sub>*a*</sub> = *d*<sub>*b*</sub> = *d*<sub>*c*</sub> = ½ the Miquel point is the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) $(\cos \alpha : \cos \beta : \cos \gamma)$.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="A converse of Miquel's theorem"}

## A converse of Miquel's theorem {#A_converse_of_Miquel's_theorem}

The theorem can be reversed to say: for three circles intersecting at *M*, a line can be drawn from any point *A* on one circle, through its intersection *C´* with another to give *B* (at the second intersection). *B* is then similarly connected, via intersection at *A´* of the second and third circles, giving point *C*. Points *C*, *A* and the remaining point of intersection, *B´*, will then be collinear, and triangle *ABC* will always pass through the circle intersections *A´*, *B´* and *C´*.

:::

## Similar inscribed triangle {#Similar_inscribed_triangle}

If the inscribed triangle *XYZ* is similar to the reference triangle *ABC*, then the point *M* of concurrence of the three circles is fixed for all such *XYZ*.[^o14]

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Miquel and Steiner's quadrilateral theorem"}

## Miquel and Steiner's quadrilateral theorem {#Miquel_and_Steiner's_quadrilateral_theorem}

*Image: Miquelquad.svg.* Miquel and Steiner's **Quadrilateral Theorem**

The circumcircles of all four triangles of a [complete quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/2605272) meet at a point *M*.[^o17] In the diagram above these are ∆ABF, ∆CDF, ∆ADE and ∆BCE.

This result was announced, in two lines, by [Jakob Steiner](wikipedia://en/567445) in the 1827/1828 issue of [Gergonne's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Diaz_Gergonne) *Annales de Mathématiques*,[^o18] but a detailed proof was given by Miquel.[^o17]

:::

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Miquel's pentagon theorem"}

## Miquel's pentagon theorem {#Miquel's_pentagon_theorem}

Let ABCDE be a convex pentagon. Extend all sides until they meet in five points F,G,H,I,K and draw the circumcircles of the five triangles CFD, DGE, EHA, AIB and BKC. Then the second intersection points (other than A,B,C,D,E), namely the new points M,N,P,R and Q are concyclic (lie on a circle).[^o21] See diagram.

The converse result is known as the [Five circles theorem](wikipedia://en/19334397).

*Image: Miquel5.svg.* **Miquel's Pentagon Theorem**

:::

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Miquel's six circle theorem"}

## Miquel's six circle theorem {#Miquel's_six_circle_theorem}

*Image: Miquel's Theorem 2.svg.* **Miquel's six circles theorem** states that if five circles share four triple-points of intersection then the remaining four points of intersection lie on a sixth circle

Given points, *A*, *B*, *C*, and *D* on a circle, and circles passing through each adjacent pair of points, the alternate intersections of these four circles at *W*, *X*, *Y* and *Z* then lie on a common circle. This is known as the **six circles theorem**.[^o24] It is also known as the **four circles theorem** and while generally attributed to [Jakob Steiner](wikipedia://en/567445) the only known published proof was given by Miquel.[^o25] [David G. Wells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_G._Wells) refers to this as **Miquel's theorem**.[^o26]

:::

::: {#o28 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Three-dimensional version of Miquel's theorem"}

## Three-dimensional version of Miquel's theorem {#Three-dimensional_version_of_Miquel's_theorem}

There is also a three-dimensional analog, in which the four spheres passing through a point of a tetrahedron and points on the edges of the tetrahedron intersect in a common point.[^o4]
