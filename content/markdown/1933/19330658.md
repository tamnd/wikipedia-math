# High-dimensional statistics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="High-dimensional statistics"}

In [statistical theory](wikipedia://en/27579), the field of **high-dimensional statistics** studies data whose [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) is larger (relative to the number of datapoints) than typically considered in classical [multivariate analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_analysis). The area arose owing to the emergence of many modern data sets in which the dimension of the data vectors may be comparable to, or even larger than, the [sample size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size), so that justification for the use of traditional techniques, often based on asymptotic arguments with the dimension held fixed as the sample size increased, was lacking.[^o2][^o3]

:::

There are several notions of high-dimensional analysis of statistical methods including:

- Non-asymptotic results which apply for finite $n,p$ (number of data points and dimension size, respectively).
- Kolmogorov asymptotics which studies the asymptotic behavior where the ratio $n/p$ is converges to a specific finite value.[^o4]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Parameter estimation in linear models {#Parameter_estimation_in_linear_models}

*Image: High-dimensional linear model.svg.* Illustration of the [linear model](wikipedia://en/48758386) in high-dimensions: a data set consists of a response vector $Y \in \mathbb{R}^n$ and a [design matrix](wikipedia://en/949189) $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ with $p \gg n$. Our goal is to estimate the unknown vector $\beta=(\beta_1, \dots, \beta_p) \in \mathbb{R}^{p}$ of regression coefficients where $\beta$ is often assumed to be [sparse](wikipedia://en/341015), in the sense that the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of the set $S := \{j : \beta_j \neq 0 \}$ is small by comparison with $p$.

The most basic statistical model for the relationship between a [covariate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariate) vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^p$ and a [response variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Response_variable) $y \in \mathbb{R}$ is the [linear model](wikipedia://en/48758386)

$$y = x^\top \beta + \epsilon,$$

where $\beta \in \mathbb{R}^p$ is an unknown parameter vector, and $\epsilon$ is random noise with mean zero and variance $\sigma^2$. Given independent responses $Y_1,\ldots,Y_n$, with corresponding covariates $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, from this model, we can form the response vector $Y = (Y_1,\ldots,Y_n)^\top$, and [design matrix](wikipedia://en/949189) $X = (x_1,\ldots,x_n)^\top \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$. When $n \geq p$ and the design matrix has full [column rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_rank) (i.e. its columns are [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent)), the [ordinary least squares](wikipedia://en/1651906) estimator of $\beta$ is

$$\hat{\beta} := (X^\top X)^{-1} X^\top Y.$$

When $\epsilon \sim N(0,\sigma^2)$, it is [known](wikipedia://en/1651906) that $\hat{\beta} \sim N_p\bigl(\beta,\sigma^2(X^\top X)^{-1}\bigr)$. Thus, $\hat{\beta}$ is an [unbiased estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbiased_estimator) of $\beta$, and the [Gauss-Markov theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss-Markov_theorem) tells us that it is the [Best Linear Unbiased Estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Best_Linear_Unbiased_Estimator).

However, [overfitting](wikipedia://en/173332) is a concern when $p$ is of comparable magnitude to $n$: the matrix $X^\top X$ in the definition of $\hat{\beta}$ may become [ill-conditioned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ill-conditioned), with a small minimum [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue). In such circumstances $\mathbb{E}(\|\hat{\beta} - \beta\|^2) = \sigma^2 \mathrm{tr}\bigl((X^\top X)^{-1}\bigr)$ will be large (since the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of a matrix is the sum of its eigenvalues). Even worse, when $p > n$, the matrix $X^\top X$ is [singular](wikipedia://en/217122). (See Section 1.2 and Exercise 1.2 in [^o2].)

The deterioration in estimation performance in high dimensions observed in the previous paragraph is not limited to the ordinary least squares estimator. In fact, statistical inference in high dimensions is intrinsically hard, a phenomenon known as the [curse of dimensionality](wikipedia://en/787776), and it can be shown that no estimator can do better in a worst-case sense without additional information (see Example 15.10[^o3]). Nevertheless, the situation in high-dimensional statistics may not be hopeless when the data possess some low-dimensional structure. One common assumption for high-dimensional linear regression is that the vector of regression coefficients is [sparse](wikipedia://en/341015), in the sense that most coordinates of $\beta$ are zero. Many statistical procedures, including the [Lasso](wikipedia://en/22349350), have been proposed to fit high-dimensional linear models under such sparsity assumptions.

### Covariance matrix estimation {#Covariance_matrix_estimation}

Another example of a high-dimensional statistical phenomenon can be found in the problem of [covariance matrix estimation](wikipedia://en/974723). Suppose that we observe $X_1,\ldots,X_n \in \mathbb{R}^p$, which are [i.i.d.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I.i.d.) draws from some zero mean distribution with an unknown covariance matrix $\Sigma \in \mathbb{R}^{p \times p}$. A natural [unbiased estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unbiased_estimator) of $\Sigma$ is the [sample covariance matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_covariance_matrix)

$$\widehat{\Sigma} := \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n X_iX_i^\top.$$

In the low-dimensional setting where $n$ increases and $p$ is held fixed, $\widehat{\Sigma}$ is a [consistent estimator](wikipedia://en/1497569) of $\Sigma$ in any [matrix norm](wikipedia://en/1543735). When $p$ grows with $n$, on the other hand, this consistency result may fail to hold. As an illustration, suppose that each $X_i \sim N_p(0,I)$ and $p/n \rightarrow \alpha \in (0,1)$. If $\widehat{\Sigma}$ were to consistently estimate $\Sigma = I$, then the eigenvalues of $\widehat{\Sigma}$ should approach one as $n$ increases. It turns out that this is not the case in this high-dimensional setting. Indeed, the largest and smallest eigenvalues of $\widehat{\Sigma}$ concentrate around $(1 + \sqrt{\alpha})^2$ and $(1 - \sqrt{\alpha})^2$, respectively, according to the limiting distribution derived by [Tracy and Widom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tracy-Widom_distribution), and these clearly deviate from the unit eigenvalues of $\Sigma$. Further information on the asymptotic behaviour of the eigenvalues of $\widehat{\Sigma}$ can be obtained from the [Marchenko–Pastur law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marchenko–Pastur_law). From a non-asymptotic point of view, the maximum eigenvalue $\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\widehat{\Sigma})$ of $\widehat{\Sigma}$ satisfies

$$\mathbb{P}\left(\lambda_{\mathrm{max}}(\widehat{\Sigma}) \geq (1 + \sqrt{p/n} + \delta)^2\right) \leq e^{-n\delta^2/2},$$

for any $\delta \geq 0$ and all choices of pairs of $n,p$.[^o3]

Again, additional low-dimensional structure is needed for successful covariance matrix estimation in high dimensions. Examples of such structures include [sparsity](wikipedia://en/341015), [low rankness](wikipedia://en/26561) and [bandedness](wikipedia://en/1451476). Similar remarks apply when estimating an inverse covariance matrix [(precision matrix)](wikipedia://en/24467258).

## History {#History}

From an applied perspective, research in high-dimensional statistics was motivated by the realisation that advances in computing technology had dramatically increased the ability to collect and store [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Data), and that traditional statistical techniques such as those described in the examples above were often ill-equipped to handle the resulting challenges. Theoretical advances in the area can be traced back to the remarkable result of [Charles Stein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Stein_%28statistician%29) in 1956,[^o10] where he proved that the usual estimator of a multivariate normal mean was [inadmissible](wikipedia://en/1776726) with respect to squared error loss in three or more dimensions. Indeed, the [James-Stein estimator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James-Stein_estimator)[^o11] provided the insight that in high-dimensional settings, one may obtain improved estimation performance through shrinkage, which reduces variance at the expense of introducing a small amount of bias. This [bias-variance tradeoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bias-variance_tradeoff) was further exploited in the context of high-dimensional [linear models](wikipedia://en/48758386) by Hoerl and Kennard in 1970 with the introduction of [ridge regression](wikipedia://en/954328).[^o12] Another major impetus for the field was provided by [Robert Tibshirani](wikipedia://en/31404681)'s work on the [Lasso](wikipedia://en/22349350) in 1996, which used $\ell_1$ regularisation to achieve simultaneous model selection and parameter estimation in high-dimensional sparse linear regression.[^o13] Since then, a large number of other [shrinkage](wikipedia://en/8301951) estimators have been proposed to exploit different low-dimensional structures in a wide range of high-dimensional statistical problems.

## Topics in high-dimensional statistics {#Topics_in_high-dimensional_statistics}

The following are examples of topics that have received considerable attention in the high-dimensional statistics literature in recent years:

- Linear models in high dimensions. Linear models are one of the most widely used tools in statistics and its applications. As such, sparse linear regression is one of the most well-studied topics in high-dimensional statistical research. Building upon earlier works on [ridge regression](wikipedia://en/954328) and the [Lasso](wikipedia://en/22349350), several other [shrinkage](wikipedia://en/8301951) estimators have been proposed and studied in this and related problems. They include
  - The Dantzig selector, which minimises the maximum covariate-residual correlation, instead of the residual sum of squares as in the Lasso, subject to an $\ell_1$ constraint on the coefficients.[^o15]
  - [Elastic net](wikipedia://en/34021763), which combines $\ell_1$ regularisation of the [Lasso](wikipedia://en/22349350) with $\ell_2$ regularisation of [ridge regression](wikipedia://en/954328) to allow highly correlated covariates to be simultaneously selected with similar regression coefficients.[^o16]
  - The [Group Lasso](wikipedia://en/22349350#Group_lasso), which allows predefined groups of covariates to be selected jointly.[^o17]
  - The [Fused lasso](wikipedia://en/22349350#Fused_lasso), which regularises the difference between nearby coefficients when the regression coefficients reflect spatial or temporal relationships, so as to enforce a piecewise constant structure.[^o18]
- [High-dimensional variable selection](wikipedia://en/1179950). In addition to estimating the underlying parameter in regression models, another important topic is to seek to identify the non-zero coefficients, as these correspond to variables that are needed in a final model. Each of the techniques listed under the previous heading can be used for this purpose, and are sometimes combined with ideas such as [subsampling](wikipedia://en/3763850#Subsampling) through Stability Selection.[^o19][^o20]
- High-dimensional covariance and precision matrix estimation. These problems were introduced above; see also [shrinkage estimation](wikipedia://en/974723#Shrinkage_estimation). Methods include tapering estimators [^o21] and the constrained $\ell_1$ minimisation estimator.[^o22]
- [Sparse principal component analysis](wikipedia://en/18566488). [Principal Component Analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_Component_Analysis) is another technique that breaks down in high dimensions; more precisely, under appropriate conditions, the leading eigenvector of the sample covariance matrix is an inconsistent estimator of its population counterpart when the ratio of the number of variables $p$ to the number of observations $n$ is bounded away from zero.[^o23] Under the assumption that this leading eigenvector is sparse (which can aid interpretability), consistency can be restored.[^o24]
- [Matrix completion](wikipedia://en/35683165). This topic, which concerns the task of filling in the missing entries of a partially observed matrix, became popular owing in large part to the [Netflix prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Netflix_prize) for predicting user ratings for films.
- High-dimensional classification. [Linear discriminant analysis](wikipedia://en/1470657) cannot be used when $p > n$, because the sample covariance matrix is [singular](wikipedia://en/217122). Alternative approaches have been proposed based on [naive Bayes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naive_Bayes),[^o25] feature selection [^o26] and [random projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_projections).[^o27]
- [Graphical models for high-dimensional data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_models). Graphical models are used to encode the conditional dependence structure between different variables. Under a Gaussianity assumption, the problem reduces to that of estimating a sparse precision matrix, discussed above.
