# Language equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Language equation"}

**Language equations** are mathematical statements that resemble [numerical equations](wikipedia://en/9284), but the variables assume values of [formal languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_languages) rather than numbers. Instead of arithmetic operations in numerical equations, the variables are joined by language operations. Among the most common operations on two languages *A* and *B* are the [set union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_union) *A* ∪ *B*, the [set intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_intersection) *A* ∩ *B*, and the [concatenation](wikipedia://en/64474#Concatenation_of_sets_of_strings) *A*⋅*B*. Finally, as an operation taking a single [operand](wikipedia://en/22656), the set *A*<sup>\*</sup> denotes the [Kleene star](wikipedia://en/16750) of the language *A*. Therefore, language equations can be used to represent [formal grammars](wikipedia://en/18020716), since the languages generated by the grammar must be the solution of a system of language equations.

:::

## Language equations and context-free grammars {#Language_equations_and_context-free_grammars}

[Ginsburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seymour_Ginsburg) and [Rice](wikipedia://en/15461434)[^o3] gave an alternative definition of [context-free grammars](wikipedia://en/6759) by language equations. To every context-free grammar $G = (V, \Sigma, R, S)$, is associated a system of equations in variables $V$. Each variable $X \in V$ is an unknown language over $\Sigma$ and is defined by the equation $X=\alpha_1 \cup \ldots \cup \alpha_m$ where $X \to \alpha_1$, ..., $X \to \alpha_m$ are all productions for $X$. Ginsburg and Rice used a [fixed-point iteration](wikipedia://en/7344320) argument to show that a solution always exists, and proved that the assignment $X=L_G(X)$ is the *least solution* to this system, i.e. any other solution must be a subset of this one.

For example, the grammar $S \to a S c \mid b \mid S$ corresponds to the equation system $S = ( \{ a \} \cdot S \cdot \{ c \} ) \cup \{ b \} \cup S$ which has as solution every superset of $\{ a^n b c^n \mid n \in \mathcal{N} \}$.

Language equations with added intersection analogously correspond to [conjunctive grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunctive_grammars).

## Language equations and finite automata {#Language_equations_and_finite_automata}

[Brzozowski](wikipedia://en/23616030) and Leiss[^o5] studied *left language equations* where every concatenation is with a singleton constant language on the left, e.g. $\{a\} \cdot X$ with variable $X$, but not $X \cdot Y$ nor $X \cdot \{a\}$. Each equation is of the form $X_i=F(X_1, ..., X_k)$ with one variable on the right-hand side. Every [nondeterministic finite automaton](wikipedia://en/653406) has such corresponding equation using left-concatenation and union, see Fig. 1. If intersection operation is allowed, equations correspond to [alternating finite automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_finite_automaton).

*Image: DFAexample.svg.* Fig. 1: A [finite automaton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_automaton) with associated system of equations $S_1 = 1 \cdot S_1 \cup 0 \cdot S_2 \cup \{\epsilon\}$, $S_2 = 1 \cdot S_2 \cup 0 \cdot S_1$ where $\epsilon$ is the empty word.[^o5]

[Baader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franz_Baader) and Narendran[^o6] studied equations $F(X_1, \ldots, X_k)=G(X_1, \ldots, X_k)$ using left-concatenation and union and proved that their satisfiability problem is [EXPTIME-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EXPTIME-complete).

## Conway's problem {#Conway's_problem}

[Conway](wikipedia://en/15807)[^o8] proposed the following problem: given a constant finite language $L$, is the greatest solution of the equation $LX=XL$ always regular? This problem was studied by [Karhumäki](wikipedia://en/53552649) and Petre[^o9][^o10] who gave an affirmative answer in a special case. A strongly negative answer to Conway's problem was given by [Kunc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michal_Kunc)[^o11] who constructed a finite language $L$ such that the greatest solution of this equation is not recursively enumerable.

Kunc[^o12] also proved that the greatest solution of inequality $LX \subseteq XL$ is always regular.

## Language equations with Boolean operations {#Language_equations_with_Boolean_operations}

Language equations with concatenation and Boolean operations were first studied by [Parikh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rohit_Parikh), [Chandra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashok_K._Chandra), [Halpern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Halpern) and [Meyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albert_R._Meyer) [^o14] who proved that the satisfiability problem for a given equation is undecidable, and that if a system of language equations has a unique solution, then that solution is recursive. Later, Okhotin[^o15] proved that the unsatisfiability problem is [RE-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RE-complete) and that every recursive language is a unique solution of some equation.

## Language equations over a unary alphabet {#Language_equations_over_a_unary_alphabet}

For a one-letter alphabet, Leiss[^o17] discovered the first language equation with a nonregular solution, using complementation and concatenation operations. Later, Jeż[^o18] showed that non-regular unary languages can be defined by language equations with union, intersection and concatenation, equivalent to [conjunctive grammars](wikipedia://en/3237776). By this method Jeż and Okhotin[^o19] proved that every recursive unary language is a unique solution of some equation.
