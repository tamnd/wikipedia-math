# Permutation polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Permutation polynomial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **permutation polynomial** (for a given [ring](wikipedia://en/48404)) is a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) that acts as a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of the elements of the ring, i.e. the map $x \mapsto g(x)$ is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942). In case the ring is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), the [Dickson polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dickson_polynomials), which are closely related to the [Chebyshev polynomials](wikipedia://en/184539), provide examples. [^o2] Over a finite field, every function, so in particular every permutation of the elements of that field, can be written as a polynomial function.

:::

In the case of finite rings **Z**/*n***Z**, such polynomials have also been studied and applied in the [interleaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interleaver) component of [error detection and correction](wikipedia://en/10375) algorithms.[^o3][^o4]

## Single variable permutation polynomials over finite fields {#Single_variable_permutation_polynomials_over_finite_fields}

Let $\mathbf{F}_{q} = \operatorname{GF}(q)$ be the finite field of [characteristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_%28field_theory%29) $p$, that is, the field having $q$ elements where $q = p^{e}$ for some prime $p$. A polynomial $f$ with coefficients in $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ (symbolically written as $f \in \mathbf{F}_{q}[x]$) is a *permutation polynomial* of $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ if the function from $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ to itself defined by $c \mapsto f(c)$ is a permutation of $\mathbf{F}_{q}$.[^o6]

Due to the finiteness of $\mathbf{F}_{q}$, this definition can be expressed in several equivalent ways:[^o7]

- the function $c \mapsto f(c)$ is *onto* ([surjective](wikipedia://en/27873));
- the function $c \mapsto f(c)$ is *one-to-one* ([injective](wikipedia://en/45196));
- $f(x) = a$ has a solution in $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ for each $a$ in $\mathbf{F}_{q}$;
- $f(x) = a$ has a *unique* solution in $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ for each $a$ in $\mathbf{F}_{q}$.

A characterization of which polynomials are permutation polynomials is given by

(*[Hermite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite)'s Criterion*)[^o8][^o9] $f \in \mathbf{F}_{q}[x]$ is a permutation polynomial of $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the following two conditions hold:

1. $f$ has exactly one root in $\mathbf{F}_{q}$;
1. for each integer $t$ with $1 \le t \le q - 2$ and $t \not \equiv 0 \!\pmod p$, the reduction of $f(x)^{t} \bmod (x^{q} - x)$ has degree $\le q - 2$.

If $f(x)$ is a permutation polynomial defined over the finite field $\operatorname{GF}(q)$, then so is $g(x) = a f(x + b) + c$ for all $a \ne 0, b$ and $c$ in $\operatorname{GF}(q)$. The permutation polynomial $g(x)$ is in **normalized form** if $a, b$ and $c$ are chosen so that $g(x)$ is [monic](wikipedia://en/314730), $g(0) = 0$ and (provided the characteristic $p$ does not divide the degree $n$ of the polynomial) the coefficient of $x^{n-1}$ is 0.

There are many open questions concerning permutation polynomials defined over finite fields.[^o10][^o11]

### Small degree {#Small_degree}

Hermite's criterion is computationally intensive and can be difficult to use in making theoretical conclusions. However, [Dickson](wikipedia://en/1790247) was able to use it to find all permutation polynomials of degree at most five over all finite fields. These results are:[^o13][^o9]

::: {#o14 .table cue=table}

| Normalized Permutation Polynomial of $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ | $q$ |
|---|---|
| $x$ | any $q$ |
| $x^2$ | $q \equiv 0\! \pmod 2$ |
| $x^3$ | $q \not \equiv 1 \! \pmod 3$ |
| $x^3 - ax$ ($a$ not a square) | $q \equiv 0 \! \pmod 3$ |
| $x^4 \pm 3x$ | $q = 7$ |
| $x^4 + a_1 x^2 + a_2 x$ (if its only root in $\mathbf{F}_{q}$ is 0) | $q \equiv 0 \! \pmod 2$ |
| $x^5$ | $q \not \equiv 1 \! \pmod 5$ |
| $x^5 - ax$ ($a$ not a fourth power) | $q \equiv 0 \! \pmod 5$ |
| $x^5 + ax \,(a^2 = 2)$ | $q = 9$ |
| $x^5 \pm 2x^2$ | $q = 7$ |
| $x^5 + ax^3 \pm x^2 + 3a^2 x$ ($a$ not a square) | $q = 7$ |
| $x^5 + ax^3 + 5^{-1} a^2 x$ ($a$ arbitrary) | $q \equiv \pm 2 \! \pmod 5$ |
| $x^5 + ax^3 + 3a^2 x$ ($a$ not a square) | $q = 13$ |
| $x^5 - 2ax^3 + a^2x$ ($a$ not a square) | $q \equiv 0 \! \pmod 5$ |

:::

A list of all monic permutation polynomials of degree six in normalized form can be found in (Shallue Wanless 2013).[^o16]

### Some classes of permutation polynomials {#Some_classes_of_permutation_polynomials}

Beyond the above examples, the following list, while not exhaustive, contains almost all of the known major classes of permutation polynomials over finite fields.[^o18]

- $x^{n}$ permutes $\operatorname{GF}(q)$ if and only if $n$ and $q - 1$ are [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556) (notationally, $(n, q - 1) = 1$).[^o19]
- If $a$ is in $\operatorname{GF}(q)$ and $n \ge 1$ then the [Dickson polynomial](wikipedia://en/15640469) (of the first kind) $D_{n}(x,a)$ is defined by $$D_n(x,a)=\sum_{j=0}^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}\frac{n}{n-j} \binom{n-j}{j} (-a)^j x^{n-2j}.$$

These can also be obtained from the [recursion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive_relation)

$$D_n(x,a) = xD_{n-1}(x,a)-a D_{n-2}(x,a),$$

with the initial conditions $D_0(x,a) = 2$ and $D_1(x,a) = x$. The first few Dickson polynomials are:

- $$D_2(x,a) = x^2 - 2a$$
- $$D_3(x,a) = x^3 - 3ax$$
- $$D_4(x,a) = x^4 - 4ax^2 + 2a^2$$
- $$D_5(x,a) = x^5 - 5ax^3 + 5a^2 x.$$

If $a \ne 0$ and $n > 1$ then $D_{n}(x, a)$ permutes GF(*q*) if and only if $(n, q^{2} - 1) = 1$.[^o20] If $a = 0$ then $D_{n}(x, 0) = x^{n}$ and the previous result holds.

- If $\operatorname{GF}(q^{r})$ is an [extension](wikipedia://en/11634) of $\operatorname{GF}(q)$ of degree $r$, then the [linearized polynomial](wikipedia://en/37384831) $$L(x) = \sum_{s=0}^{r-1} \alpha_s x^{q^s},$$ with $\alpha_{s}$ in $\operatorname{GF}(q^{r})$, is a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) on $\operatorname{GF}(q^{r})$ over $\operatorname{GF}(q)$. A linearized polynomial $L(x)$ permutes $\operatorname{GF}(q^{r})$ if and only if 0 is the only root of $L(x)$ in $\operatorname{GF}(q^{r})$.[^o19] This condition can be expressed algebraically as[^o21] $$\det\left ( \alpha_{i-j}^{q^j} \right ) \neq 0 \quad (i, j= 0,1,\ldots,r-1).$$

The linearized polynomials that are permutation polynomials over $\operatorname{GF}(q^{r})$ form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) under the operation of composition modulo $x^{q^r} - x$, which is known as the Betti-Mathieu group, isomorphic to the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $\operatorname{GL}(r, \mathbf{F}_{q})$.[^o21]

- If $g(x)$ is in the polynomial ring $\mathbf{F}_{q}[x]$ and $g(x^{s})$ has no nonzero root in $\operatorname{GF}(q)$ when $s$ divides $q - 1$, and $r > 1$ is relatively prime (coprime) to $q - 1$, then $x^{r}(g(x^{s}))^{(q - 1)/s}$ permutes $\operatorname{GF}(q)$.[^o9]
- Only a few other specific classes of permutation polynomials over $\operatorname{GF}(q)$ have been characterized. Two of these, for example, are: $$x^{(q + m - 1)/m} + ax$$ where $m$ divides $q - 1$, and $$x^r \left(x^d - a\right)^{\left(p^n - 1\right)/d}$$ where $d$ divides $p^{n} - 1$.

### Exceptional polynomials {#Exceptional_polynomials}

An **exceptional polynomial** over $\operatorname{GF}(q)$ is a polynomial in $\mathbf{F}_{q}[x]$ which is a permutation polynomial on $\operatorname{GF}(q^{m})$ for infinitely many $m$.[^o23]

A permutation polynomial over $\operatorname{GF}(q)$ of degree at most $q^{1/4}$ is exceptional over $\operatorname{GF}(q)$.[^o24]

Every permutation of $\operatorname{GF}(q)$ is induced by an exceptional polynomial.[^o24]

If a polynomial with integer coefficients (i.e., in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$) is a permutation polynomial over $\operatorname{GF}(p)$ for infinitely many primes $p$, then it is the composition of linear and Dickson polynomials.[^o25] (See Schur's conjecture below).

## Geometric examples {#Geometric_examples}

In [finite geometry](wikipedia://en/267061) coordinate descriptions of certain point sets can provide examples of permutation polynomials of higher degree. In particular, the points forming an [oval](wikipedia://en/4255174) in a finite [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350), $\operatorname{PG}(2,q)$ with $q$ a power of 2, can be coordinatized in such a way that the relationship between the coordinates is given by an *[o-polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/O-polynomial)*, which is a special type of permutation polynomial over the finite field $\operatorname{GF}(q)$.

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

The problem of testing whether a given polynomial over a finite field is a permutation polynomial can be solved in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time).[^o28]

## Permutation polynomials in several variables over finite fields {#Permutation_polynomials_in_several_variables_over_finite_fields}

A polynomial $f \in \mathbb{F}_q[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is a **permutation polynomial in $n$ variables over** $\mathbb{F}_q$ if the equation $f(x_1,\ldots,x_n) = \alpha$ has exactly $q^{n-1}$ solutions in $\mathbb{F}_q^n$ for each $\alpha \in \mathbb{F}_q$.[^o30]

## Quadratic permutation polynomials (QPP) over finite rings {#Quadratic_permutation_polynomials_(QPP)_over_finite_rings}

For the [finite ring](wikipedia://en/23706953) **Z**/*n***Z** one can construct quadratic permutation polynomials. Actually it is possible if and only if *n* is divisible by *p*<sup>2</sup> for some prime number *p*. The construction is surprisingly simple, nevertheless it can produce permutations with certain good properties. That is why it has been used in the [interleaver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interleaver) component of [turbo codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turbo_codes) in [3GPP Long Term Evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3GPP_Long_Term_Evolution) mobile telecommunication standard (see 3GPP technical specification 36.212 [^o32] e.g. page 14 in version 8.8.0).

::: {#o34 .example cue=heading name="Simple examples"}

### Simple examples {#Simple_examples}

Consider $g(x) = 2x^2+x$ for the ring **Z**/4**Z**. One sees: $g(0) = 0$; $g(1) = 3$; $g(2) = 2$; $g(3) = 1$, so the polynomial defines the permutation

$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 &1 & 2 & 3 \\ 0 &3 & 2 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .$$

Consider the same polynomial $g(x) = 2x^2+x$ for the other ring **Z**/*8***Z**. One sees: $g(0) = 0$; $g(1) = 3$; $g(2) = 2$; $g(3) = 5$; $g(4) = 4$; $g(5) = 7$; $g(6) = 6$; $g(7) = 1$, so the polynomial defines the permutation

$$\begin{pmatrix} 0 &1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 \\ 0 &3 & 2 & 5 & 4 & 7 & 6 & 1 \end{pmatrix} .$$

:::

### Rings Z/*p<sup>k</sup>*Z {#Rings_Z/pkZ}

Consider $g(x) = ax^2+bx+c$ for the ring **Z**/*p<sup>k</sup>***Z**.

::: {#o36 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=inline-head name="Lemma"}

**Lemma:** for *k*=1 (i.e. **Z**/*p***Z**) such polynomial defines a permutation only in the case *a*=0 and *b* not equal to zero. So the polynomial is not quadratic, but linear.

:::

::: {#o37 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=inline-head name="Lemma"}

**Lemma:** for *k*>1, *p*>2 (**Z**/*p<sup>k</sup>***Z**) such polynomial defines a permutation if and only if $a \equiv 0 \pmod p$ and $b \not \equiv 0 \pmod p$.

:::

### Rings Z/*n*Z {#Rings_Z/nZ}

Consider $n=p_1^{k_1}p_2^{k_2}...p_l^{k_l}$, where *p<sub>t</sub>* are prime numbers.

Lemma: any polynomial $g(x) = a_0+ \sum_{0 < i \leq M} a_i x^i$ defines a permutation for the ring **Z**/*n***Z** if and only if all the polynomials $g_{p_t}(x) = a_{0,p_t}+ \sum_{0 < i \leq M} a_{i,p_t} x^i$ defines the permutations for all rings $Z/p_t^{k_t}Z$, where $a_{j,p_t}$ are remainders of $a_{j}$ modulo $p_t^{k_t}$.

As a corollary one can construct plenty quadratic permutation polynomials using the following simple construction. Consider $n = p_1^{k_1} p_2^{k_2} \dots p_l^{k_l}$, assume that *k*<sub>1</sub> >1.

Consider $ax^2+bx$, such that $a= 0 \bmod p_1$, but $a\ne 0 \bmod p_1^{k_1}$; assume that $a = 0 \bmod p_i^{k_i}$, *i* > 1. And assume that $b\ne 0 \bmod p_i$ for all $i = 1, ..., l$. (For example, one can take $a=p_1 p_2^{k_2}...p_l^{k_l}$ and $b=1$). Then such polynomial defines a permutation.

To see this we observe that for all primes *p<sub>i</sub>*, *i* > 1, the reduction of this quadratic polynomial modulo *p<sub>i</sub>* is actually linear polynomial and hence is permutation by trivial reason. For the first [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) we should use the lemma discussed previously to see that it defines the permutation.

For example, consider $\mathbf{Z}/12\mathbf{Z}$ and polynomial $6x^2+x$. It defines a permutation $\begin{pmatrix} 0 &1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & 5 & 6 & 7 & 8 & \cdots \\ 0 &7 & 2 & 9 & 4 & 11 & 6 & 1 & 8 & \cdots \end{pmatrix} .$

## Higher degree polynomials over finite rings {#Higher_degree_polynomials_over_finite_rings}

A polynomial *g*(*x*) for the ring **Z**/*p<sup>k</sup>***Z** is a permutation polynomial if and only if it permutes the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) **Z**/*p***Z** and $g'(x) \ne 0 \bmod p$ for all *x* in **Z**/*p<sup>k</sup>***Z**, where *g*′(*x*) is the [formal derivative](wikipedia://en/3832917) of *g*(*x*).[^o40]

::: {#o42 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Schur's conjecture"}

## Schur's conjecture {#Schur's_conjecture}

Let *K* be an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) with *R* the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613). The term "Schur's conjecture" refers to the assertion that, if a polynomial *f* defined over *K* is a permutation polynomial on *R*/*P* for infinitely many [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) *P*, then *f* is the composition of Dickson polynomials, degree-one polynomials, and polynomials of the form *x*<sup>*k*</sup>. In fact, [Schur](wikipedia://en/617522) did not make any conjecture in this direction. The notion that he did is due to Fried,[^o43] who gave a flawed proof of a false version of the result. Correct proofs have been given by Turnwald[^o44] and Müller.[^o45]

:::
