# Noncommutative ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Noncommutative ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **noncommutative ring** is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) whose multiplication is not [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390); that is, there exist *a* and *b* in the ring such that *ab* and *ba* are different. Equivalently, a *noncommutative ring* is a ring that is not a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346).

:::

**Noncommutative algebra** is the part of [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424) devoted to study of properties of the noncommutative rings, including the properties that apply also to commutative rings.

Sometimes the term *noncommutative ring* is used instead of *ring* to refer to an unspecified ring which is not necessarily commutative, and hence may be commutative. Generally, this is for emphasizing that the studied properties are not restricted to commutative rings, as, in many contexts, *ring* is used as a shorthand for *commutative ring*.

Although some authors do not assume that rings have a multiplicative identity, in this article we make that assumption unless stated otherwise.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Some examples of noncommutative rings:

- The [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) of *n*-by-*n* matrices over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), where *n* > 1
- Hamilton's [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440)
- Any [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) constructed from a group that is not [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974)

Some examples of rings that are not typically commutative (but may be commutative in simple cases):

- The [free ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_ring) $\mathbb{Z}\langle x_1,\ldots,x_n\rangle$ generated by a finite set, an example of two non-equal elements being $2x_1x_2 + x_2x_1 \neq 3 x_1x_2$
- The [Weyl algebra](wikipedia://en/491095) $A_n(\mathbb{C})$, being the ring of polynomial differential operators defined over affine space; for example, $A_1(\mathbb{C}) \cong \mathbb{C} \langle x, y \rangle / (xy - yx - 1)$, where the ideal corresponds to the [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193#Ring_theory)
- The quotient ring $\mathbb{C}\langle x_1,\ldots,x_n\rangle / (x_ix_j - q_{ij} x_jx_i)$, called a [quantum plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_plane), where $q_{ij} \in \mathbb{C}$
- Any [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) can be described explicitly using an algebra presentation: given an $\mathbb{F}$-vector space $V$ of dimension $n$ with a quadratic form $q: V\otimes V \to \mathbb{F}$, the associated Clifford algebra has the presentation $\mathbb{F}\langle e_1,\ldots, e_n \rangle /(e_ie_j + e_je_i - q(e_i,e_j))$ for any basis $e_1,\ldots,e_n$ of $V$,
- [Superalgebras](wikipedia://en/469950) are another example of noncommutative rings; they can be presented as $\mathbb{C}[x_1,\ldots,x_n]\langle \theta_1,\ldots, \theta_m \rangle / (\theta_i\theta_j + \theta_j\theta_i)$
- There are finite noncommutative rings: for example, the $n$-by-$n$ matrices over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), for $n > 1$. The smallest noncommutative ring is the ring of the [upper triangular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular_matrices) over the field with two elements; it has eight elements and all noncommutative rings with eight elements are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to it or to its [opposite](wikipedia://en/3129323).

:::

## History {#History}

Beginning with [division rings](wikipedia://en/9067) arising from geometry, the study of noncommutative rings has grown into a major area of modern algebra. The theory and exposition of noncommutative rings was expanded and refined in the 19th and 20th centuries by numerous authors. An incomplete list of such contributors includes [E. Artin](wikipedia://en/245351), [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134), [P. M. Cohn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P._M._Cohn), [W. R. Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817), [I. N. Herstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I._N._Herstein), [N. Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Jacobson), [K. Morita](wikipedia://en/12034472), [E. Noether](wikipedia://en/149896), [Ø. Ore](wikipedia://en/1643733), [J. Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494) and others.

## Differences between commutative and noncommutative algebra {#Differences_between_commutative_and_noncommutative_algebra}

Because noncommutative rings of scientific interest are more complicated than commutative rings, their structure, properties and behavior are less well understood. A great deal of work has been done successfully generalizing some results from commutative rings to noncommutative rings. A major difference between rings which are and are not commutative is the necessity to separately consider [right ideals and left ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_ideal#Definitions). It is common for noncommutative ring theorists to enforce a condition on one of these types of ideals while not requiring it to hold for the opposite side. For commutative rings, the left–right distinction does not exist.

## Important classes {#Important_classes}

### Division rings {#Division_rings}

A division ring, also called a skew field, is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) in which [division](wikipedia://en/53696) is possible. Specifically, it is a [nonzero](wikipedia://en/1872874) ring[^o9] in which every nonzero element *a* has a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940), i.e., an element *x* with *a* **·** *x* = *x* **·** *a* = 1. Stated differently, a ring is a division ring if and only if its [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) is the set of all nonzero elements.

Division rings differ from [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) only in that their multiplication is not required to be [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative). However, by [Wedderburn's little theorem](wikipedia://en/7132002) all finite division rings are commutative and therefore [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615). Historically, division rings were sometimes referred to as fields, while fields were called "commutative fields".

### Semisimple rings {#Semisimple_rings}

A [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over a (not necessarily commutative) ring with unity is said to be semisimple (or completely reducible) if it is the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/58899) of [simple](wikipedia://en/29430) (irreducible) submodules.

A ring is said to be (left)-semisimple if it is semisimple as a left module over itself. Surprisingly, a left-semisimple ring is also right-semisimple and vice versa. The left/right distinction is therefore unnecessary.

### Semiprimitive rings {#Semiprimitive_rings}

A semiprimitive ring or Jacobson semisimple ring or J-semisimple ring is a ring whose [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) is zero. This is a type of ring more general than a [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring), but where [simple modules](wikipedia://en/29430) still provide enough information about the ring. Rings such as the ring of integers are semiprimitive, and an [artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) semiprimitive ring is just a [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring). Semiprimitive rings can be understood as [subdirect products](wikipedia://en/17395221) of [primitive rings](wikipedia://en/485862), which are described by the [Jacobson density theorem](wikipedia://en/488391).

### Simple rings {#Simple_rings}

A simple ring is a non-zero [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) that has no two-sided [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) besides the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) and itself. A simple ring can always be considered as a [simple algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_algebra). Rings which are simple as rings but not as [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) do exist: the full [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) does not have any nontrivial ideals (since any ideal of M(*n*,*R*) is of the form M(*n*,*I*) with *I* an ideal of *R*), but has nontrivial left ideals (namely, the sets of matrices which have some fixed zero columns).

According to the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem), every simple ring that is left or right [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) is a [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) over a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067). In particular, the only simple rings that are a finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) are rings of matrices over either the real numbers, the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), or the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440).

Any quotient of a ring by a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) is a simple ring. In particular, a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is a simple ring. A ring *R* is simple if and only if its [opposite ring](wikipedia://en/3129323) *R*<sup>o</sup> is simple.

An example of a simple ring that is not a matrix ring over a division ring is the [Weyl algebra](wikipedia://en/491095).

## Important theorems {#Important_theorems}

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Wedderburn's little theorem"}

### Wedderburn's little theorem {#Wedderburn's_little_theorem}

Wedderburn's little theorem states that every [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) [domain](wikipedia://en/487627) is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). In other words, for [finite rings](wikipedia://en/23706953), there is no distinction between domains, [division rings](wikipedia://en/9067) and fields.

The [Artin–Zorn theorem](wikipedia://en/14818089) generalizes the theorem to [alternative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternative_ring): every finite simple alternative ring is a field.[^o17]

:::

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Artin–Wedderburn theorem"}

### Artin–Wedderburn theorem {#Artin–Wedderburn_theorem}

The Artin–Wedderburn theorem is a [classification theorem](wikipedia://en/4576710) for [semisimple rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring) and [semisimple algebras](wikipedia://en/3876151). The theorem states that an (Artinian)[^o20] semisimple ring *R* is isomorphic to a [product](wikipedia://en/25063) of finitely many *n<sub>i</sub>*-by-*n<sub>i</sub>* [matrix rings](wikipedia://en/487541) over [division rings](wikipedia://en/9067) *D<sub>i</sub>*, for some integers *n<sub>i</sub>*, both of which are uniquely determined up to permutation of the index *i*. In particular, any [simple](wikipedia://en/443387) left or right [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273) is isomorphic to an *n*-by-*n* [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) over a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) *D*, where both *n* and *D* are uniquely determined.[^o21]

As a direct corollary, the Artin–Wedderburn theorem implies that every simple ring that is finite-dimensional over a division ring (a simple algebra) is a [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541). This is [Joseph Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494)'s original result. [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351) later generalized it to the case of Artinian rings.

:::

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Jacobson density theorem"}

### Jacobson density theorem {#Jacobson_density_theorem}

The **Jacobson density theorem** is a theorem concerning [simple modules](wikipedia://en/29430) over a ring $R$.[^o24]

The theorem can be applied to show that any [primitive ring](wikipedia://en/485862) can be viewed as a "dense" subring of the ring of [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of a vector space.[^o25][^o26] This theorem first appeared in the literature in 1945, in the famous paper "Structure Theory of Simple Rings Without Finiteness Assumptions" by [Nathan Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Jacobson).[^o27] This can be viewed as a kind of generalization of the [Artin-Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin-Wedderburn_theorem)'s conclusion about the structure of [simple](wikipedia://en/443387) [Artinian rings](wikipedia://en/489273).

More formally, the theorem can be stated as follows:

**The Jacobson Density Theorem.** Let $U$ be a simple right $R$-module, $D = \operatorname{End}(U_{R})$, and $X \subset U$ a finite and $D$-linearly independent set. If $A$ is a $D$-linear transformation on $U$ then there exists $r \in R$ such that $A(x) = x \cdot r$ for all $x$ in $X$.[^o28]

:::

::: {#o30 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Nakayama's lemma"}

### Nakayama's lemma {#Nakayama's_lemma}

Let J(*R*) be the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) of *R*. If *U* is a right module over a ring, *R*, and *I* is a right ideal in *R*, then define *U*·*I* to be the set of all (finite) sums of elements of the form *u*·*i*, where **·** is simply the action of *R* on *U*. Necessarily, *U*·*I* is a submodule of *U*.

If *V* is a [maximal submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_submodule) of *U*, then *U*/*V* is [simple](wikipedia://en/29430). So *U*·J(*R*) is necessarily a subset of *V*, by the definition of J(*R*) and the fact that *U*/*V* is simple.[^o31] Thus, if *U* contains at least one (proper) maximal submodule, *U*·J(*R*) is a proper submodule of *U*. However, this need not hold for arbitrary modules *U* over *R*, for *U* need not contain any maximal submodules.[^o32] Naturally, if *U* is a [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) module, this holds. If *R* is Noetherian, and *U* is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430), then *U* is a Noetherian module over *R*, and the conclusion is satisfied.[^o33] Somewhat remarkable is that the weaker assumption, namely that *U* is finitely generated as an *R*-module (and no finiteness assumption on *R*), is sufficient to guarantee the conclusion. This is essentially the statement of Nakayama's lemma.[^o34]

Precisely, one has the following.

**Nakayama's lemma**: Let *U* be a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) right module over a ring *R*. If *U* is a non-zero module, then *U*·J(*R*) is a proper submodule of *U*.[^o34]

A version of the lemma holds for right modules over non-commutative [unitary rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_ring) *R*. The resulting theorem is sometimes known as the **Jacobson–Azumaya theorem**.[^o35]

:::

::: {#o37 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Non-commutative localization"}

### Noncommutative localization {#Noncommutative_localization}

Localization is a systematic method of adding multiplicative inverses to a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), and is usually applied to commutative rings. Given a ring *R* and a subset *S*, one wants to construct some ring *R*\* and [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) from *R* to *R*\*, such that the image of *S* consists of *[units](wikipedia://en/289666)* (invertible elements) in *R*\*. Further one wants *R*\* to be the 'best possible' or 'most general' way to do this – in the usual fashion this should be expressed by a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245). The localization of *R* by *S* is usually denoted by *S*<sup>−1</sup>*R*; however other notations are used in some important special cases. If *S* is the set of the non zero elements of an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), then the localization is the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) and thus usually denoted Frac(*R*).

Localizing [non-commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_ring) is more difficult; the localization does not exist for every set *S* of prospective units. One condition which ensures that the localization exists is the [Ore condition](wikipedia://en/3100560).

One case for non-commutative rings where localization has a clear interest is for rings of differential operators. It has the interpretation, for example, of adjoining a formal inverse *D*<sup>−1</sup> for a differentiation operator *D*. This is done in many contexts in methods for [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309). There is now a large mathematical theory about it, named [microlocalization](wikipedia://en/6108841), connecting with numerous other branches. The *micro-* tag is to do with connections with [Fourier theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_theory), in particular.

:::

### Morita equivalence {#Morita_equivalence}

Morita equivalence is a relationship defined between [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) that preserves many ring-theoretic properties. It is named after Japanese mathematician [Kiiti Morita](wikipedia://en/12034472) who defined equivalence and a similar notion of duality in 1958.

Two rings *R* and *S* (associative, with 1) are said to be (**Morita**) **equivalent** if there is an equivalence of the category of (left) modules over *R*, *R-Mod*, and the category of (left) modules over *S*, *S-Mod*. It can be shown that the left module categories *R-Mod* and *S-Mod* are equivalent if and only if the right module categories *Mod-R* and *Mod-S* are equivalent. Further it can be shown that any functor from *R-Mod* to *S-Mod* that yields an equivalence is automatically [additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_functor).

### Brauer group {#Brauer_group}

The Brauer group of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) whose elements are [Morita equivalence](wikipedia://en/3296134) classes of [central simple algebras](wikipedia://en/404082) of finite rank over *K* and addition is induced by the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of algebras. It arose out of attempts to classify [division algebras](wikipedia://en/26549625) over a field and is named after the algebraist [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134). The group may also be defined in terms of [Galois cohomology](wikipedia://en/1111315). More generally, the Brauer group of a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) is defined in terms of [Azumaya algebras](wikipedia://en/3125089).

### Ore conditions {#Ore_conditions}

The Ore condition is a condition introduced by [Øystein Ore](wikipedia://en/1643733), in connection with the question of extending beyond [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) the construction of a [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271), or more generally [localization of a ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring). The *right Ore condition* for a [multiplicative subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_subset) *S* of a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* is that for *a* ∈ *R* and *s* ∈ *S*, the intersection *aS* ∩ *sR* ≠ ∅.[^o41] A domain that satisfies the right Ore condition is called a **right Ore domain**. The left case is defined similarly.

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Goldie's theorem"}

### Goldie's theorem {#Goldie's_theorem}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Goldie's theorem** is a basic structural result in [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), proved by [Alfred Goldie](wikipedia://en/20962143) during the 1950s. What is now termed a right **Goldie ring** is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* that has finite [uniform dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_dimension) (also called "finite rank") as a right module over itself, and satisfies the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189) on right [annihilators](wikipedia://en/328684) of subsets of *R*.

Goldie's theorem states that the [semiprime](wikipedia://en/7739701) right Goldie rings are precisely those that have a [semisimple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring) [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) right [classical ring of quotients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_ring_of_quotients). The structure of this ring of quotients is then completely determined by the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem).

In particular, Goldie's theorem applies to semiprime right [Noetherian rings](wikipedia://en/56129), since by definition right Noetherian rings have the ascending chain condition on *all* right ideals. This is sufficient to guarantee that a right-Noetherian ring is right Goldie. The converse does not hold: every right [Ore domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ore_domain) is a right Goldie domain, and hence so is every commutative [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462).

A consequence of Goldie's theorem, again due to Goldie, is that every semiprime [principal right ideal ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_right_ideal_ring) is isomorphic to a finite direct sum of [prime](wikipedia://en/487540) principal right ideal rings. Every prime principal right ideal ring is isomorphic to a [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) over a right Ore domain.
