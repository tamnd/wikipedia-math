# Joseph Berkson

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Joseph Berkson"}

**Joseph Berkson** (14 May 1899 – 12 September 1982)[^o3] was an American biostatistian, who is best known for having identified a source of bias in [observational studies](wikipedia://en/6326483) caused by selection effects. This bias eventually became known as [Berkson's paradox](wikipedia://en/1409541).[^o4]

:::

Berkson was trained as a physicist (BSc 1920, [College of City of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York) (CCNY), M.A., 1922, Columbia), physician (M.D., 1927, Johns Hopkins), and statistician (Dr.Sc., 1928, Johns Hopkins).[^o5]

## Works and views {#Works_and_views}

In 1950, as Head (1934–1964) of the Division of Biometry and Medical Statistics[^o5] of the [Mayo Clinic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayo_Clinic), [Rochester, Minnesota](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rochester,_Minnesota), Berkson wrote a key paper entitled *Are there two regressions?*[^o7] In this paper Berkson proposed an error model for [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997) that contradicted the [classical error model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_error_model) until that point assumed to generally apply and this has since been termed the [Berkson error model](wikipedia://en/17521621). Whereas the classical error model is statistically independent of the true variable, Berkson's model is statistically independent of the observed variable.[^o8] Carroll et al. (1995) refer to the two types of error models as follows:[^o9]

- *error models* including the Classical Measurement Error models and Error Calibration Models, where the conditional distribution of *W* given (*Z*, *X*) is modeled — use of such a model is appropriate when attempting to determine *X* directly, but this is prevented by various errors in measurement.
- *regression calibration models* (also known as controlled-variable or [Berkson error models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkson_error_models)), where the conditional distribution of *X* given (*Z*, *W*) is modeled.

Berkson is also widely recognised as the key proponent in the use of the [logistic](wikipedia://en/542666) in preference to the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) in probabilistic techniques.[^o10] He is credited with the introduction of the [logit](wikipedia://en/172068) model in 1944,[^o11] and with coining this term. The term was borrowed by analogy from the very similar [probit](wikipedia://en/1689101) model developed by [Chester Ittner Bliss](wikipedia://en/14264865) in 1934.

Berkson was a prominent opponent of the idea that cigarette smoking causes cancer. In the 1957 [Liggett & Myers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liggett_&_Myers) annual report, he was quoted as saying "the evidence, taken as a whole, does not establish, on any reasonable scientific basis, that cigarette smoking causes lung cancer."[^o12] Following the issuance of the famous report *[Smoking and Health: Report of the Advisory Committee to the Surgeon General of the Public Health Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking_and_Health:_Report_of_the_Advisory_Committee_to_the_Surgeon_General_of_the_Public_Health_Service)*, he was quoted in [*Life* magazine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_magazine) as saying it was "very doubtful that smoking causes cancer of the lung."[^o13]
