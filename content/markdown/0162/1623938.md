# László Lovász

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="László Lovász"}

Lovász László

**László Lovász** (; born March 9, 1948) is a Hungarian mathematician and professor emeritus at [Eötvös Loránd University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eötvös_Loránd_University), best known for his work in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), for which he was awarded the 2021 [Abel Prize](wikipedia://en/159285) jointly with [Avi Wigderson](wikipedia://en/6570979). He was the president of the [International Mathematical Union](wikipedia://en/14868) from 2007 to 2010 and the president of the [Hungarian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Academy_of_Sciences) from 2014 to 2020.

:::

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), Lovász's notable contributions include the proofs of [Kneser's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kneser's_conjecture) and the [Lovász local lemma](wikipedia://en/2211763), as well as the formulation of the [Erdős–Faber–Lovász conjecture](wikipedia://en/691803). He is also one of the eponymous authors of the [LLL lattice reduction algorithm](wikipedia://en/1735228).

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Lovász was born on March 9, 1948, in [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest), Hungary.[^o20][^o21][^o10]

Lovász attended the [Fazekas Mihály Gimnázium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fazekas_Mihály_Gimnázium_%28Budapest%29) in Budapest.[^o4] He won three gold medals (1964–1966) and one silver medal (1963) at the [International Mathematical Olympiad](wikipedia://en/82208).[^o20][^o21][^o4] He also participated in a Hungarian game show about math prodigies.[^o21] [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) helped introduce Lovász to [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) at a young age.[^o21][^o22]

Lovász received his [Candidate of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Candidate_of_Sciences) (C.Sc.) degree in 1970 at the [Hungarian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Academy_of_Sciences).[^o21][^o6][^o10] His advisor was [Tibor Gallai](wikipedia://en/1651618).[^o6] He received his first doctorate ([Dr.Rer.Nat.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._rer._nat.)) degree from [Eötvös Loránd University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eötvös_Loránd_University) in 1971 and his second doctorate (Dr.Math.Sci.) from the Hungarian Academy of Sciences in 1977.[^o10]

## Career {#Career}

From 1971 to 1975, Lovász worked at Eötvös Loránd University as a research associate.[^o10] From 1975 to 1978, he was a [docent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Docent#Central_and_Eastern_Europe) at the [University of Szeged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Szeged), and then served as a professor and the Chair of Geometry there until 1982.[^o10] He then returned to Eötvös Loránd University as a professor and the Chair of Computer Science until 1993.[^o10]

Lovász was a professor at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) from 1993 to 1999, when he moved to the [Microsoft Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Research_Center) where he worked as a senior researcher until 2006.[^o10] He returned to [Eötvös Loránd University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eötvös_Loránd_University) where he was the director of the Mathematical Institute (2006–2011)[^o9] and a professor in the Department of Computer Science (2006–2018).[^o10] He retired in 2018.[^o10]

Lovász was the president of the [International Mathematical Union](wikipedia://en/14868) between January 1, 2007, and December 31, 2010.[^o11][^o22] In 2014, he was elected the president of the [Hungarian Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Academy_of_Sciences) (MTA) and served until 2020.[^o12][^o13][^o22]

## Research {#Research}

In collaboration with Erdős in the 1970s, Lovász developed complementary methods to Erdős's existing [probabilistic graph theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic_graph_theory) techniques.[^o21] This included the [Lovász local lemma](wikipedia://en/2211763), which has become a standard technique for [proving the existence](wikipedia://en/251177) of rare [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806).[^o21] Also in graph theory, Lovász proved [Kneser's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kneser's_conjecture) and helped formulate the [Erdős–Faber–Lovász conjecture](wikipedia://en/691803).[^o21]

With [Arjen Lenstra](wikipedia://en/6850292) and [Hendrik Lenstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lenstra) in 1982, Lovász developed the [LLL algorithm](wikipedia://en/1735228) for approximating points in [lattices](wikipedia://en/469246) and [reducing their bases](wikipedia://en/4786593).[^o21][^o15] The LLL algorithm has been described by [Gil Kalai](wikipedia://en/19709056) as "one of the fundamental algorithms" and has been used in several practical applications, including [polynomial factorization](wikipedia://en/3776351) algorithms and [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432).[^o21]

[Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) named Lovász as one of his combinatorial heroes in a 2023 interview.[^o16]

## Awards {#Awards}

Lovász was awarded the [Pólya Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pólya_Prize_%28SIAM%29) in 1979, the [Fulkerson Prize](wikipedia://en/1425916) in 1982 and 2012, the [Brouwer Medal](wikipedia://en/42578624) in 1993, the [Wolf Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolf_Prize) and [Knuth Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knuth_Prize) in 1999, the [Gödel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel_Prize) in 2001, the [John von Neumann Theory Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann_Theory_Prize) in 2006, the János Bolyai Creative Prize in 2007, the [Széchenyi Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Széchenyi_Prize) in 2008, and the [Kyoto Prize in Basic Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kyoto_Prize_in_Basic_Sciences) in 2010.[^o10][^o18][^o19] In March 2021, he shared the [Abel Prize](wikipedia://en/159285) with [Avi Wigderson](wikipedia://en/6570979) from the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) "for their foundational contributions to [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392) and [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492), and their leading role in shaping them into central fields of modern mathematics".[^o20][^o21][^o22] In 2017 he received [John von Neumann Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann_Professor) title from the [Budapest University of Technology and Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest_University_of_Technology_and_Economics) (BME) and the [John von Neumann Computer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_von_Neumann_Computer_Society).[^o23] In 2021, he received Hungary's highest order, the [Hungarian Order of Saint Stephen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_Order_of_Saint_Stephen).[^o24]

He was elected a foreign member of the [Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Netherlands_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 2006[^o25] and the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences) in 2007, and an honorary member of the [London Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/591097) in 2009. Lovász was elected as a member of the U.S. [National Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Academy_of_Sciences) in 2012.[^o27] In 2012 he became a fellow of the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).[^o28]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Lovász is married to fellow mathematician [Katalin Vesztergombi](wikipedia://en/56553444),[^o30] with whom he participated in a program for high school students gifted in mathematics,[^o31] and has four children.[^o32][^o10] He is a dual citizen of Hungary and the United States.[^o10]
