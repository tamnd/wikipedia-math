# Algebraic graph theory

*Image: Petersen1 tiny.svg.* A highly symmetrical graph, the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926), which is [vertex-transitive](wikipedia://en/581175), [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1577896), [distance-transitive](wikipedia://en/7768222), and [distance-regular](wikipedia://en/7768943). It has [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) 2. Its [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/15094186) has 120 elements, and is in fact the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_5$.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic graph theory"}

**Algebraic [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401)** is a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) in which [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923) methods are applied to problems about [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806). This is in contrast to [geometric](wikipedia://en/3313527), [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170#Graph_theory), or [algorithmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_graph_theory) approaches. There are three main branches of algebraic graph theory, involving the use of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the use of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), and the study of [graph invariants](wikipedia://en/1655142).

:::

## Branches of algebraic graph theory {#Branches_of_algebraic_graph_theory}

### Using linear algebra {#Using_linear_algebra}

The first branch of algebraic graph theory involves the study of graphs in connection with [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422). Especially, it studies the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/13576645) of the [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463), or the [Laplacian matrix](wikipedia://en/1448472) of a graph (this part of algebraic graph theory is also called [spectral graph theory](wikipedia://en/534914)). For the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926), for example, the spectrum of the adjacency matrix is (−2, −2, −2, −2, 1, 1, 1, 1, 1, 3). Several theorems relate properties of the spectrum to other [graph properties](wikipedia://en/1655142). As a simple example, a [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) graph with [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) *D* will have at least *D*+1 distinct values in its spectrum.[^o4] [Aspects](wikipedia://en/3396069) of graph spectra have been used in analysing the [synchronizability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronizability) of [networks](wikipedia://en/766409).

### Using group theory {#Using_group_theory}

The second branch of algebraic graph theory involves the study of graphs in connection to [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), particularly [automorphism groups](wikipedia://en/15094186) and [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164). The focus is placed on various families of graphs based on [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) (such as [symmetric graphs](wikipedia://en/1577896), [vertex-transitive graphs](wikipedia://en/581175), [edge-transitive graphs](wikipedia://en/584406), [distance-transitive graphs](wikipedia://en/7768222), [distance-regular graphs](wikipedia://en/7768943), and [strongly regular graphs](wikipedia://en/1399856)), and on the inclusion relationships between these families. Certain of such categories of graphs are sparse enough that [lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foster_census) of graphs can be drawn up. By [Frucht's theorem](wikipedia://en/26259599), all [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) can be represented as the automorphism group of a connected graph (indeed, of a [cubic graph](wikipedia://en/980508)).[^o6] Another connection with group theory is that, given any group, symmetrical graphs known as [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) can be generated, and these have properties related to the structure of the group.[^o4]

*Image: Tuncated tetrahedral graph.png.* A [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) for the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) *A*<sub>4</sub>, forming a [truncated tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/279651) in three dimensions. All Cayley graphs are [vertex-transitive](wikipedia://en/581175), but some vertex-transitive graphs (like the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926)) are not Cayley graphs.

*Image: Petersen graph 3-coloring.svg.* A proper vertex coloring of the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) with 3 colors, the minimum number possible. According to the [chromatic polynomial](wikipedia://en/1460126), there are 120 such colorings with 3 colors.

This second branch of algebraic graph theory is related to the first, since the symmetry properties of a graph are reflected in its spectrum. In particular, the spectrum of a highly symmetrical graph, such as the Petersen graph, has few distinct values[^o4] (the Petersen graph has 3, which is the minimum possible, given its diameter). For Cayley graphs, the spectrum can be related directly to the structure of the group, in particular to its [irreducible characters](wikipedia://en/691741).[^o4][^o7]

### Studying graph invariants {#Studying_graph_invariants}

Finally, the third branch of algebraic graph theory concerns algebraic properties of [invariants](wikipedia://en/1655142) of graphs, and especially the [chromatic polynomial](wikipedia://en/1460126), the [Tutte polynomial](wikipedia://en/3892303) and [knot invariants](wikipedia://en/166898). The chromatic polynomial of a graph, for example, counts the number of its proper [vertex colorings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_coloring). For the Petersen graph, this polynomial is $t(t-1)(t-2)(t^7-12t^6+67t^5-230t^4+529t^3-814t^2+775t-352)$.[^o4] In particular, this means that the Petersen graph cannot be properly colored with one or two colors, but can be colored in 120 different ways with 3 colors. Much work in this area of algebraic graph theory was motivated by attempts to prove the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949). However, there are still many [open problems](wikipedia://en/426743#Open_problems), such as characterizing graphs which have the same chromatic polynomial, and determining which polynomials are chromatic.
