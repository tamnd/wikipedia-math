# Geophysical fluid dynamics

*Image: Mitch 01.jpg.* Model forecast of [Hurricane Mitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Mitch) created by the [Geophysical Fluid Dynamics Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysical_Fluid_Dynamics_Laboratory). The arrows are wind vectors and the grey shading indicates an [equivalent potential temperature](wikipedia://en/304799) surface that highlights the surface inflow layer and [eyewall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eye_%28cyclone%29) region.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geophysical fluid dynamics"}

**Geophysical fluid dynamics**, in its broadest meaning, is the application of [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034) to naturally occurring flows, such as lava, [oceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean), and [atmospheres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere), on [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228) and other [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet).[^o2]

:::

Two physical features that are common to many of the phenomena studied in geophysical fluid dynamics are [rotation of the fluid](wikipedia://en/1209823) due to the planetary rotation and [stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratification_%28water%29) (layering).

The applications of geophysical fluid dynamics do not generally include the circulation of the [mantle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantle_%28geology%29), which is the subject of *[geodynamics](wikipedia://en/9134092)*, or fluid phenomena in the [magnetosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetosphere). [Ocean circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_circulation) and [air circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_circulation) are typically studied in oceanography and meteorology.

## Fundamentals {#Fundamentals}

To describe the flow of geophysical fluids, equations are needed for [conservation of momentum](wikipedia://en/20431#Conservation) (or [Newton's second law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_second_law)) and [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088). The former leads to the [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395) which cannot be solved analytically (yet). Therefore, further approximations are generally made in order to be able to solve these equations. First, the fluid is assumed to be [incompressible](wikipedia://en/461477). Remarkably, this works well even for a highly compressible fluid like air as long as [sound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound) and [shock waves](wikipedia://en/146253) can be ignored.[^o4] Second, the fluid is assumed to be a [Newtonian fluid](wikipedia://en/363903), meaning that there is a linear relation between the [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) $\tau$ and the [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29#Strain) $u$, for example

$$\tau = \mu \frac{d u}{d x},$$

where $\mu$ is the [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754).[^o4] Under these assumptions the Navier-Stokes equations are

$$\overbrace{\rho \Big( \underbrace{\frac{\partial \mathbf{v}}{\partial t}}_{ \begin{smallmatrix} \text{Eulerian}\\ \text{acceleration} \end{smallmatrix}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{v}}_{ \begin{smallmatrix} \text{Advection} \end{smallmatrix}}\Big)}^{\text{Inertia (per volume)}} = \overbrace{\underbrace{-\nabla p}_{ \begin{smallmatrix} \text{Pressure} \\ \text{gradient} \end{smallmatrix}} + \underbrace{\mu \nabla^2 \mathbf{v}}_{\text{Viscosity}}}^{\text{Divergence of stress}} + \underbrace{\mathbf{f}}_{ \begin{smallmatrix} \text{Other} \\ \text{body} \\ \text{forces} \end{smallmatrix}}.$$

The left hand side represents the acceleration that a small parcel of fluid would experience in a reference frame that moved with the parcel (a [Lagrangian frame of reference](wikipedia://en/5289693)). In a stationary (Eulerian) frame of reference, this acceleration is divided into the local rate of change of velocity and [advection](wikipedia://en/166377), a measure of the rate of flow in or out of a small region.[^o4]

The equation for energy conservation is essentially an equation for heat flow. If heat is transported by [conduction](wikipedia://en/72536), the heat flow is governed by a [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) equation. If there are also [buoyancy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buoyancy) effects, for example hot air rising, then [natural convection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_convection), also known as free convection, can occur.[^o4] Convection in the Earth's [outer core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outer_core) drives the [geodynamo](wikipedia://en/255217) that is the source of the [Earth's magnetic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_magnetic_field).[^o5] In the ocean, convection can be *thermal* (driven by heat), *haline* (where the buoyancy is due to differences in salinity), or *[thermohaline](wikipedia://en/387457)*, a combination of the two.[^o6]

## Buoyancy and stratification {#Buoyancy_and_stratification}

*Image: Messina-waves-image.jpg.* Internal waves in the [Strait of Messina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strait_of_Messina) (photographed by [ASTER](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_Spaceborne_Thermal_Emission_and_Reflection_Radiometer)).

Fluid that is less dense than its surroundings tends to rise until it has the same density as its surroundings. If there is not much energy input to the system, it will tend to become [stratified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratification_%28water%29). On a large scale, Earth's atmosphere is [divided into a series of layers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmosphere_of_Earth#Principal_layers). Going upwards from the ground, these are the [troposphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Troposphere), [stratosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stratosphere), [mesosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesosphere), [thermosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermosphere), and [exosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exosphere).[^o8]

The density of air is mainly determined by temperature and [water vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_vapor) content, the density of [sea water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seawater) by temperature and [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity), and the density of lake water by temperature. Where stratification occurs, there may be thin layers in which temperature or some other property changes more rapidly with height or depth than the surrounding fluid. Depending on the main sources of buoyancy, this layer may be called a [pycnocline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pycnocline) (density), [thermocline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermocline) (temperature), [halocline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halocline) (salinity), or [chemocline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemocline) (chemistry, including oxygenation).

The same buoyancy that gives rise to stratification also drives [gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_wave). If the gravity waves occur within the fluid, they are called [internal waves](wikipedia://en/900160).[^o4]

In modeling buoyancy-driven flows, the Navier-Stokes equations are modified using the [Boussinesq approximation](wikipedia://en/393813). This ignores variations in density except where they are multiplied by the [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration) $g$.[^o4]

If the pressure depends only on density and vice versa, the fluid dynamics are called [barotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barotropic_fluid). In the atmosphere, this corresponds to a lack of fronts, as in the [tropics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropics). If there are fronts, the flow is [baroclinic](wikipedia://en/166371), and instabilities such as [cyclones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclone) can occur.[^o9]
