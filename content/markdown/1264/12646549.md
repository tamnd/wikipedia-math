# Filter on a set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Filter on a set"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **filter** on a set is a [collection](wikipedia://en/723043) of nonempty subsets which is closed under taking supersets and finite intersections. An example of filter is the collection of [neighborhoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29) of a point in a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450).

:::

Filters were introduced by [Henri Cartan](wikipedia://en/362536) in 1937[^n1][^n2] in the context of general topological spaces and were subsequently developed by [Nicolas Bourbaki](wikipedia://en/167394) in their book *[Topologie Générale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologie_Générale)* (first edition in 1940) as an alternative to the related notion of a [net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_%28topology%29) developed in 1922 by [E. H. Moore](wikipedia://en/1532860) and [Herman L. Smith](wikipedia://en/29651449). They later found applications in many fields outside of topology, including [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) ([ultraproducts](wikipedia://en/273642) for example), [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), and others.

Filters on a set were later generalized to [order filters](wikipedia://en/19719). Specifically, a filter on a set $X$ is an order filter on the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $X$ ordered by inclusion.

The notion dual to a filter is an [ideal](wikipedia://en/12633368). [Ultrafilters](wikipedia://en/67416519) are a particularly important subclass of filters.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a set $X$, a **filter** $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$ is a set of subsets of $X$ such that:[^n3][^n4][^n5]

- $\mathcal{F}$ is [upwards-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwards-closed_set): If $A, B \subseteq X$ are such that $A \in \mathcal{F}$ and $A \subseteq B$ then $B \in \mathcal{F}$,
- $\mathcal{F}$ is closed under finite intersections: $X \in \mathcal{F}$,, and if $A \in \mathcal{F}$ and $B \in \mathcal{F}$ then $A \cap B \in \mathcal{F}$.

A *proper* (or *non-degenerate*) filter is a filter which is proper as a subset of the powerset $\mathcal{P}(X)$ (i.e., the only improper filter is $\mathcal{P}(X)$, consisting of all possible subsets). By upwards-closure, a filter is proper if and only if it does not contain the empty set.[^n4] Many authors adopt the convention that a filter must be proper by definition.[^n6][^n7][^n8][^n9]

When $\mathcal{F}$ and $\mathcal{G}$ are two filters on the same set such that $\mathcal{F} \subseteq \mathcal{G}$ holds, $\mathcal{F}$ is said to be *coarser*[^n10] than $\mathcal{G}$ (or a *subfilter* of $\mathcal{G}$) while $\mathcal{G}$ is said to be *finer*[^n10] than $\mathcal{F}$ (or *subordinate* to $\mathcal{F}$ or a *superfilter*[^n11] of $\mathcal{F}$).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The singleton set $\mathcal{F} = \{X\}$ is called the *trivial* or *indiscrete* filter on $X$.[^n12]
- If $Y$ is a subset of $X$, the subsets of $X$ which are supersets of $Y$ form a *principal filter*.[^n3]
- If $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) and $x \in X$, then the set of neighborhoods of $x$ is a filter on $X$, the *[neighborhood filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_filter)*[^n13] or *vicinity filter*[^n14] of $x$.
- Many examples arise from various "largeness" conditions:
  - If $X$ is a set, the set of all [cofinite subsets](wikipedia://en/382708) of $X$ (i.e., those sets whose complement in $X$ is finite) is a filter on $X$, the [Fréchet filter](wikipedia://en/1586291)[^n12][^n15][^n5] (or *cofinite filter*[^n13]).
  - Similarly, if $X$ is a set, the [cocountable subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocountable_subset) of $X$ (those whose complement is countable) form a filter, the *cocountable filter*[^n14] which is finer than the Fréchet filter. More generally, for any infinite [cardinal](wikipedia://en/6173) $\kappa$, the subsets whose complement has cardinality at most $\kappa$ form a filter.
  - If $X$ is a metric space, e.g., $\mathbb{R}^n$, the co-bounded subsets of $X$ (those whose complement is [bounded set](wikipedia://en/48258)) form a filter on $X$.[^n16]
  - If $X$ is a [complete measure space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_measure_space) (e.g., $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)), the [conull](wikipedia://en/735067) subsets of $X$, i.e., the subsets whose complement has measure zero, form a filter on $X$. (For a non-complete measure space, one can take the subsets which, while not necessarily measurable, are contained in a measurable subset of measure zero.)
  - Similarly, if $X$ is a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270), the subsets whose complement is contained in a measurable subset of finite measure form a filter on $X$.
  - If $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), the [comeager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comeager_set) subsets of $X$, i.e., those whose complement is [meager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meager_set), form a filter on $X$.
  - The subsets of $\mathbb{N}$ which have a [natural density](wikipedia://en/1819983) of 1 form a filter on $\mathbb{N}$.[^o29]
- The [club filter](wikipedia://en/1679210) of a [regular](wikipedia://en/374128) [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) [cardinal](wikipedia://en/6173) $\kappa$ is the filter of all sets containing a [club subset](wikipedia://en/1251559) of $\kappa$.
- If $(\mathcal{F}_i)_{i \in I}$ is a family of filters on $X$ and $\mathcal{J}$ is a filter on $I$ then $\bigcup_{A \in \mathcal{J}} \bigcap_{i \in A} \mathcal{F}_i$ is a filter on $X$ called *Kowalsky's filter*.[^n17]

:::

## Principal and free filters {#Principal_and_free_filters}

The *kernel* of a filter $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$ is the intersection of all the subsets of $X$ in $\mathcal{F}$.

A filter $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$ is *principal*[^n3] (or *atomic*[^n13]) when it has a particularly simple form: it contains exactly the supersets of $Y$, for some fixed subset $Y \subseteq X$. When $Y = \varnothing$, this yields the improper filter. When $Y = \{y\}$ is a singleton, this filter (which consists of all subsets that contain $y$) is called the *fundamental filter*[^n3] (or *discrete filter*[^n18]) associated with $y$.

A filter $\mathcal{F}$ is principal if and only if the kernel of $\mathcal{F}$ is an element of $\mathcal{F}$, and when this is the case, $\mathcal{F}$ consists of the supersets of its kernel.[^n19] On a finite set, every filter is principal (since the intersection defining the kernel is finite).

A filter is said to be *free* when it has empty kernel, otherwise it is *fixed* (and if $x$ is an element of the kernel, it is *fixed by $x$*).[^n20] A filter on a set $X$ is free if and only if it contains the [Fréchet filter](wikipedia://en/1586291) on $X$.[^n21]

Two filters $\mathcal{F}_1$ and $\mathcal{F}_2$ on $X$ *mesh* when every member of $\mathcal{F}_1$ intersects every member of $\mathcal{F}_2$.[^n22] For every filter $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$, there exists a unique pair of filters $\mathcal{F}_f$ (the *free part* of $\mathcal{F}$) and $\mathcal{F}_p$ (the *principal part* of $\mathcal{F}$) on $X$ such that $\mathcal{F}_f$ is free, $\mathcal{F}_p$ is principal, $\mathcal{F}_f \cap \mathcal{F}_p = \mathcal{F}$, and $\mathcal{F}_p$ does not mesh with $\mathcal{F}_f$. The principal part $\mathcal{F}_p$ is the principal filter generated by the kernel of $\mathcal{F}$, and the free part $\mathcal{F}_f$ consists of elements of $\mathcal{F}$ with any number of elements from the kernel possibly removed.[^n21]

A filter $\mathcal{F}$ is *countably deep* if the kernel of any countable subset of $\mathcal{F}$ belongs to $\mathcal{F}$.[^n14]

:::

## Correspondence with order filters {#Correspondence_with_order_filters}

The concept of a filter on a set is a special case of the more general concept of a [filter on a partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/19719). By definition, a filter on a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) $P$ is a subset $\mathcal{F}$ of $P$ which is [upwards-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwards-closed_set) (if $x \in \mathcal{F}$ and $x \leq y$ then $y \in \mathcal{F}$) and [downwards-directed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downwards-directed_set) (every finite subset of $\mathcal{F}$ has a lower bound in $\mathcal{F}$). A filter on a set $X$ is the same as a filter on the powerset $\mathcal{P}(X)$ ordered by inclusion.

## Constructions of filters {#Constructions_of_filters}

### Intersection of filters {#Intersection_of_filters}

If $(\mathcal{F}_i)_{i \in I}$ is a family of filters on $X$, its intersection $\bigcap_{i \in I} \mathcal{F}_i$ is a filter on $X$. The intersection is a [greatest lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_lower_bound) operation in the set of filters on $X$ partially ordered by inclusion, which endows the filters on $X$ with a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777) structure.[^n14][^n23]

The intersection $\bigcap_{i \in I} \mathcal{F}_i$ consists of the subsets which can be written as $\bigcup_{i \in I} A_i$ where $A_i \in \mathcal{F}_i$ for each $i \in I$.

::: {#o49 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Filter subbase"}

### Filter generated by a family of subsets {#Filter_generated_by_a_family_of_subsets}

Given a family of subsets $\mathcal{S} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$, there exists a minimum filter on $X$ (in the sense of inclusion) which contains $\mathcal{S}$. It can be constructed as the intersection (greatest lower bound) of all filters on $X$ containing $\mathcal{S}$. This filter $\langle \mathcal{S} \rangle$ is called the filter generated by $\mathcal{S}$, and $\mathcal{S}$ is said to be a *filter subbase* of $\langle \mathcal{S} \rangle$. [^n24]

The generated filter can also be described more explicitly: $\langle \mathcal{S} \rangle$ is obtained by closing $\mathcal{S}$ under finite intersections, then upwards, i.e., $\langle \mathcal{S} \rangle$ consists of the subsets $Y \subseteq X$ such that $A_0 \cap \dots \cap A_{n-1} \subseteq Y$ for some $A_0, \dots, A_{n-1} \in \mathcal{B}$.[^n11]

Since these operations preserve the kernel, it follows that $\langle \mathcal{S} \rangle$ is a proper filter if and only if $\mathcal{S}$ has the [finite intersection property](wikipedia://en/365026): the intersection of a finite subfamily of $\mathcal{S}$ is non-empty.[^n16]

In the complete lattice of filters on $X$ ordered by inclusion, the [least upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least_upper_bound) of a family of filters $(\mathcal{F}_i)_{i \in I}$ is the filter generated by $\bigcup_{i \in I} \mathcal{F}_i$.[^n19]

Two filters $\mathcal{F}_1$ and $\mathcal{F}_2$ on $X$ mesh if and only if $\langle \mathcal{F}_1 \cup \mathcal{F}_2 \rangle$ is proper.[^n22]

:::

### Filter bases {#Filter_bases}

Let $\mathcal{F}$ be a filter on $X$. A *filter base* of $\mathcal{F}$ is a family of subsets $\mathcal{B} \subseteq \mathcal{P}(X)$ such that $\mathcal{F}$ is the [upwards closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwards_closure) of $\mathcal{B}$, i.e., $\mathcal{F}$ consists of those subsets $Y \subseteq X$ for which $A \subseteq Y$ for some $A \in \mathcal{B}$.[^n6]

This upwards closure is a filter if and only if $\mathcal{B}$ is [downwards-directed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downwards-directed_set), i.e., $\mathcal{B}$ is non-empty and for all $A, B \in \mathcal{B}$ there exists $C \in \mathcal{B}$ such that $C \subseteq A \cap B$.[^n6][^n13] When this is the case, $\mathcal{B}$ is also called a *prefilter*, and the upwards closure is also equal to the generated filter $\langle \mathcal{B} \rangle$.[^n16] Hence, being a filter base of $\mathcal{F}$ is a stronger property than being a filter subbase of $\mathcal{F}$.

::: {#o61 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

- When $X$ is a topological space and $x \in X$, a filter base of the [neighborhood filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_filter) of $x$ is known as a [neighborhood base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_base) for $x$, and similarly, a filter subbase of the neighborhood filter of $x$ is known as a [neighborhood subbase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_subbase) for $x$. The [open](wikipedia://en/39358) neighborhoods of $x$ always form a neighborhood base for $x$, by definition of the neighborhood filter. In $X = \mathbb{R}^n$, the [closed balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_ball) of positive radius around $x$ also form a neighborhood base for $x$.
- Let $X$ be an infinite set and let $\mathcal{F}$ consist of the subsets of $X$ which contain all points but one. Then $\mathcal{F}$ is a filter subbase of the [Fréchet filter](wikipedia://en/1586291) on $X$, which consists of the [cofinite subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofinite_subset). Its closure under finite intersections is the entire Fréchet filter, but there are smaller bases of the Fréchet filter which contain the subbase $\mathcal{F}$, such as the one formed by the subsets of $X$ which contain all points but a finite odd number. In fact, for every base of the Fréchet filter, removing any subset yields another base of the Fréchet filter.
- If $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), the [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [open subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of $X$ form a filter base on $X$, because they are closed under finite intersection. The filter they generate consists of the complements of [nowhere dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowhere_dense) subsets. On $X = \mathbb{R}^n$, restricting to the [null](wikipedia://en/21520) dense open subsets yields another filter base for the same filter.
- Similarly, if $X$ is a topological space, the countable intersections of dense open subsets form a filter base which generates the filter of [comeager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comeager) subsets.
- Let $X$ be a set and let $(x_i)_{i \in I}$ be a [net](wikipedia://en/22170) with values in $X$, i.e., a family whose domain $I$ is a [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165). The filter base of *tails* of $(x_i)$ consists of the sets $\{x_j, j \geq i\}$ for $i \in I$; it is downwards-closed by directedness of $I$. The generated filter is called the *eventuality filter* or *filter of tails* of $(x_n)$. A *sequential filter*[^n25] or *elementary filter* is a filter which is the eventuality filter of some net. This example is fundamental in the application of [filters in topology](wikipedia://en/47516955).[^n13][^n26]
- Every [π-system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Π-system) is a filter base.

:::

### Trace of a filter on a subset {#Trace_of_a_filter_on_a_subset}

If $\mathcal{F}$ is a filter on $X$ and $Y \subseteq X$, the *trace* of $\mathcal{F}$ on $Y$ is $\{A \cap Y, A \in \mathcal{F}\}$, which is a filter.[^n15]

### Image of a filter by a function {#Image_of_a_filter_by_a_function}

Let $f : X \to Y$ be a function.

When $\mathcal{F}$ is a family of subsets of $X$, its image by $f$ is defined as

$$f(\mathcal{F}) = \{\{f(x), x \in A\}, A \in \mathcal{F}\}$$

The *image filter* by $f$ of a filter $\mathcal{F}$ on $X$ is defined as the generated filter $\langle f(\mathcal{F}) \rangle$.[^n27] If $f$ is surjective, then $f(\mathcal{F})$ is already a filter. In the general case, $f(\mathcal{F})$ is a filter base and hence $\langle f(\mathcal{F}) \rangle$ is its upwards closure.[^n28] Furthermore, if $\mathcal{B}$ is a filter base of $\mathcal{F}$ then $f(\mathcal{B})$ is a filter base of $\langle f(\mathcal{F}) \rangle$.

The kernels of $\mathcal{F}$ and $\langle f(\mathcal{F}) \rangle$ are linked by $f\left(\bigcap \mathcal{F}\right) \subseteq \bigcap \langle f(\mathcal{F}) \rangle$.

### Product of filters {#Product_of_filters}

Given a family of sets $(X_i)_{i \in I}$ and a filter $\mathcal{F}_i$ on each $X_i$, the product filter $\prod_{i \in I} \mathcal{F}_i$ on the product set $\prod_{i \in I} X_i$ is defined as the filter generated by the sets $\pi_i^{-1}(A)$ for $i \in I$ and $A \in \mathcal{F}_i$, where $\pi_i : \left(\prod_{j \in I} X_j\right) \to X_i$ is the projection from the product set onto the $i$-th component.[^n12][^n29] This construction is similar to the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910).

If each $\mathcal{B}_i$ is a filter base on $\mathcal{F}_i$, a filter base of $\prod_{i \in I} \mathcal{F}_i$ is given by the sets $\prod_{i \in I} A_i$ where $(A_i)$ is a family such that $A_i \in \mathcal{F}_i$ for all $i \in I$ and $A_i = X_i$ for all but finitely many $i \in I$.[^n12][^n30]
