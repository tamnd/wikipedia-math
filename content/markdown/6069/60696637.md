# Julena Steinheider Duncombe

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Julena Steinheider Duncombe"}

*Image: Heart Mountain Relocation Center, Heart Mountain, Wyoming. In his barracks home at Block 7 - 21 - NARA - 539206 - Restoration.jpg.* Julena Steinheider (far right) and two other staff members dining with interned newspaper editor [Bill Hosokawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Hosokawa) and his family at Heart Mountain

**Julena Steinheider Duncombe** (1911–2003) was an American mathematician and astronomer. She was known for her work as a teacher at the [Heart Mountain Relocation Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_Mountain_Relocation_Center) and as an astronomer at the [United States Naval Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Naval_Observatory), where she made pioneering observations with the 6-inch [transit circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_circle), introduced the use of [punched cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punched_card) in [cataloging stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_catalogue) and constructing tables of positions of celestial bodies, and led the production of eclipse predictions for almanacs.

:::

## Life {#Life}

Julena Steinheider was born in [Dorchester, Nebraska](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorchester,_Nebraska) on September 21, 1911, the only daughter among five children of a farming family. She graduated in 1932 from [Doane College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doane_College) with a degree in mathematics and a minor in astronomy. She became a teacher, first in a one-room schoolhouse and later as a geometry teacher at the [Heart Mountain Relocation Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heart_Mountain_Relocation_Center) in [Wyoming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wyoming), one of the [internment camps for Japanese-Americans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internment_of_Japanese_Americans) during World War II.

*Image: TransitCircle USNO.jpg.* The 6-inch [transit circle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_circle) at the USNO. Although it had been built much earlier in 1898, Duncombe become the first woman to use it.

She began working for the United States Naval Observatory in [Washington, DC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_DC) in 1944, and met her husband, astronomer Raynor L. Duncombe, there. They married in 1948, and she took her husband's name. They moved to [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University), but returned to the Naval Observatory in 1950.

Duncombe retired in 1973, and moved with her husband in 1975 to [Austin, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin,_Texas), maintaining a second home in [Highlands, North Carolina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highlands,_North_Carolina). In her retirement, she assisted her husband with the production of the journal *[Celestial Mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_Mechanics_and_Dynamical_Astronomy)*.

She died on September 13, 2003.

## Recognition {#Recognition}

Duncombe was elected as a fellow of the [American Association for the Advancement of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Association_for_the_Advancement_of_Science).
