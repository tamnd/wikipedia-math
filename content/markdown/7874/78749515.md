# Dihedral group of order 8

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dihedral group of order 8"}

*Image: Animated symmetries of square.gif.* The dihedral group of order 8 as the symmetry group of a [square](wikipedia://en/659939)

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **$D_{4}$** (sometimes alternatively denoted by **$D_{8}$**) is the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of [degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degree_%28permutation_group%29) 4 and [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) 8. It is the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of a [square](wikipedia://en/659939).[^o2][^o3] A square maintains its appearance under 90° rotations about its center (4-fold [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096)), and under reflections across four lines passing through the center – two lines parallel to the sides and its two diagonals ([reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873)).[^o4]

:::

## Symmetries of a square {#Symmetries_of_a_square}

Consider a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) of a certain thickness with the letter "F" written on it to make the different positions distinguishable. In order to describe its symmetry, one can form the set of all those rigid movements of the square that do not make a visible difference (except the "F"); each of these is a [symmetry transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_transformation). For instance, when the square is turned 90° clockwise or reflected across its vertical axis, it still looks the same, so these two movements are symmetry transformations.

Every point inside the square which does not lie on any of its four axes of symmetry will be mapped to 8 distinct points by these transformations, collectively called the point's *[orbit](wikipedia://en/12781#Orbits_and_stabilizers)*. An octant of the square (the region between one horizontal or vertical axis and one diagonal axis, an [isosceles right triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isosceles_right_triangle)) is a [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) of the square's surface under these symmetry transformations, containing one point from each possible orbit. One octant is highlighted in blue in the images above, to show how each symmetry transformation maps it onto a distinct octant.

Instead of pictorial symbols, mathematicians more commonly use letters. The [juxtaposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juxtaposition#Mathematics) of two letters $xy$ represents the composition of their respective symmetries $x \circ y$, and an integer [superscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superscript) represents the composition of the same symmetry multiple times, e.g. $x^3$ represents $x \circ x \circ x$ and $x^{-1}$ represents the inverse of $x$. The letter $e$ is commonly used for the identity element. The use of this shorthand reduces the number of distinct symbols required to represent group elements.

Any element which can be written as the composition of a particular set of elements is said to be *generated* by those elements, for example the element $x^2yx = x \circ x \circ y \circ x$ is generated by $x$ and $y$; any set of elements which generates the whole group is called a [generating set of the group](wikipedia://en/99945). Every dihedral group can be generated from two elements, so only three elements of the group (those two and the identity) need be assigned symbols, and the other elements can be expressed by combining them.

In mathematics this group is known as the **[dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267)** of order 8, and is either denoted Dih<sub>4</sub>, D<sub>4</sub> or D<sub>8</sub>, depending on the convention. This is an example of a [non-abelian group](wikipedia://en/4791400): the operation $\circ$ here is not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), which can be seen from the table; the table is not symmetrical about the main diagonal.

There are five different groups of order 8. Three of them are abelian: the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) *C*<sub>8</sub> and the [direct products](wikipedia://en/3706015) of cyclic groups *C*<sub>4</sub>×*C*<sub>2</sub> and *C*<sub>2</sub>×*C*<sub>2</sub>×*C*<sub>2</sub>. The other two, the dihedral group of order 8 and the [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130), are not.[^o10]

### Permutation representation {#Permutation_representation}

*Image: Dihedral group of order 8; Cayley table (element orders 1,2,2,4,2,2,4,2); subgroup of S4.svg.* $D_{4}$ is isomorphic to the permutation group generated by $(1234)$ and $(13)$, a subgroup of $S_{4}$. The numbers in this table come from numbering the $4! = 24$ elements of $S_{4}$ from $0$ (shown as a black circle) to $23$.

Every symmetry transformation of the square permutes (re-orders) the square's four corners, so each symmetry transformation can be associated to a particular [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of four elements; therefore $\mathrm{D}_4$ is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to a [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634), a subgroup of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $\mathrm{S}_4$ of all permutations of $4$ elements.

### Matrix representation {#Matrix_representation}

Symmetry transformations of an axis-aligned square centered at the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) are [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation), so can be [represented](wikipedia://en/12695) by [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) acting on the plane by multiplication on [column vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector) of coordinates $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}x\\y\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$. Specifically, these transformations correspond to [signed permutation matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_permutation_matrix).

The identity transformation (

*Image: D4 symmetry symbol – I.png.*

) is represented by the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$. A horizontal reflection (

*Image: D4 symmetry symbol – H.png.*

) is represented by the matrix $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}-1&0\\0&1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ which negates the $x$ coordinate, while a vertical reflection (

*Image: D4 symmetry symbol – V.png.*

) is represented by the matrix $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ which negates the $y$ coordinate. The NW–SE diagonal reflection (

*Image: D4 symmetry symbol – D1.png.*

) is represented by the matrix $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ which exchanges the two coordinates, while the NE–SW diagonal reflection (

*Image: D4 symmetry symbol – D2.png.*

) is represented by its negative $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$. A rotation anticlockwise by a quarter turn (

*Image: D4 symmetry symbol – R1.png.*

) is represented by the matrix $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&-1\\1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$, and a rotation by three quarters of a turn (

*Image: D4 symmetry symbol – R3.png.*

) by its negative $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}0&1\\-1&0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$. Finally, a rotation by a half-turn (

*Image: D4 symmetry symbol – R2.png.*

), equivalent to a [reflection across the center point](wikipedia://en/12487536), is represented by the matrix $\bigl[\begin{smallmatrix}-1&0\\0&-1\end{smallmatrix}\bigr]$ which negates both coordinates. The group composition operation is represented as [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280).

Four of these matrices, representing for example the identity transformation, a vertical reflection, a diagonal reflection, and a quarter-turn rotation, form a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for the [matrix algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_algebra) $\mathrm{M}_2(\mathbb{R})$ of $2 \times 2$ real matrices.

Larger signed permutation matrices represent in the same way the [hyperoctahedral groups](wikipedia://en/24268961), the groups of symmetries of higher dimensional cubes, [octahedra](wikipedia://en/22458), [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783), and [cross polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_polytope).[^o14]

### Split-quaternions {#Split-quaternions}

The [split-quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Split-quaternions) are a number-like algebraic structure with four dimensions, similar to the [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions). Like the quaternions, their multiplication is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) but not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative).

They can be written in terms of four basis elements $\{1, i, j, k\}$ which satisfy the basic identities:

$$\begin{aligned} i^2 &= -1, & ij &= -ji = k, \\ j^2 &= 1, & jk &= -kj = -i, \\ k^2 &= 1, & ki &= -ik = j. \end{aligned}$$

The group of these basis elements and their negatives, with multiplication as the group operation, is isomorphic to the dihedral group $\mathrm{D}_4$, where $1$ corresponds to the identity transformation (

### Normal subgroups {#Normal_subgroups}

A subgroup is called *[normal](wikipedia://en/21918)* if it contains all of the conjugates of its elements. Both of the groups of rotations, as well as the dihedral groups of order 4, are normal subgroups. The four subgroups generated by a single reflection are not normal.

For a normal subgroup, the left and right cosets coincide, and the cosets themselves form a [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526).
