# Authenticated encryption

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Authenticated encryption"}

**Authenticated encryption** (AE) is any [encryption](wikipedia://en/10294) scheme which simultaneously assures the data confidentiality (also known as privacy: the encrypted message is impossible to understand without the knowledge of a secret [key](wikipedia://en/53039)[^n1]) and [authenticity](wikipedia://en/46671693) (in other words, it is unforgeable:[^n2] the encrypted message includes an [authentication tag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authentication_tag) that the sender can calculate only while possessing the secret key[^n1]). Examples of [encryption modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encryption_mode) that provide AE are [GCM](wikipedia://en/5897139) and [CCM](wikipedia://en/2244043).[^n1]

:::

Many (but not all) AE schemes allow the message to contain "associated data" (AD) which is not made confidential, but is integrity protected (i.e., readable, but [tamperevident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamper-evident_technology)). A typical example is the [header](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Header_%28computing%29) of a [network packet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_packet) that contains its destination address. To properly [route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routing) the packet, all intermediate nodes in the message path need to know the destination, but for security reasons they cannot possess the secret key.[^n3] Schemes that allow associated data provide authenticated encryption with associated data, or **AEAD**.[^n3]

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Additional authenticated data"}

## Programming interface {#Programming_interface}

A typical [programming interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Application_programming_interface) for an AE implementation provides the following functions:

- Encryption
  - Input: *plaintext*, *key*, and optionally a *header* (also known as **additional authenticated data**, **AAD**, or *associated data*, *AD*) in plaintext that will not be encrypted, but will be covered by authenticity protection.
  - Output: *ciphertext* and *authentication tag* ([message authentication code](wikipedia://en/567523) or MAC).
- Decryption
  - Input: *ciphertext*, *key*, *authentication tag*, and optionally a *header* (if used during the encryption).
  - Output: *plaintext*, or an error if the *authentication tag* does not match the supplied *ciphertext* or *header*.

The *header* part is intended to provide authenticity and integrity protection for networking or storage metadata for which confidentiality is unnecessary, but authenticity is desired.

:::

## History {#History}

The need for authenticated encryption emerged from the observation that securely combining separate *confidentiality* and *authentication* block cipher operation modes could be error prone and difficult.[^o11][^o12] This was confirmed by a number of practical attacks introduced into production protocols and applications by incorrect implementation, or lack of authentication.[^o13]

Around the year 2000, a number of efforts evolved around the notion of standardizing modes that ensured correct implementation. In particular, strong interest in possibly secure modes was sparked by the publication of [Charanjit Jutla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charanjit_Jutla)'s integrity-aware CBC and [integrity-aware parallelizable](wikipedia://en/26805464), IAPM, modes[^o14] in 2000 (see [OCB](wikipedia://en/2233546) and chronology[^o15]). Six different authenticated encryption modes (namely [offset codebook mode 2.0](wikipedia://en/2233546), OCB 2.0; [Key Wrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_Wrap); [counter with CBC-MAC](wikipedia://en/2244043), CCM; [encrypt then authenticate then translate](wikipedia://en/4260161), EAX; [encrypt-then-MAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encrypt-then-MAC), EtM; and [Galois/counter mode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois/counter_mode), GCM) have been standardized in ISO/IEC 19772:2009.[^o16] More authenticated encryption methods were developed in response to [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST) solicitation.[^o17] [Sponge functions](wikipedia://en/37217701) can be used in duplex mode to provide authenticated encryption.[^o18]

Bellare and Namprempre (2000) analyzed three compositions of encryption and MAC primitives, and demonstrated that encrypting a message and subsequently applying a MAC to the ciphertext (the Encrypt-then-MAC approach) implies security against an [adaptive chosen ciphertext attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adaptive_chosen_ciphertext_attack), provided that both functions meet minimum required properties. Katz and Yung investigated the notion under the name "unforgeable encryption" and proved it implies security against chosen ciphertext attacks.[^o19]

In 2013, the [CAESAR competition](wikipedia://en/70035770) was announced to encourage design of authenticated encryption modes.[^o20]

In 2015, [ChaCha20-Poly1305](wikipedia://en/69611898) is added as an alternative AE construction to [GCM](wikipedia://en/5897139) in [IETF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet_Engineering_Task_Force) protocols.

## Variants {#Variants}

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="AEAD"}

### Authenticated encryption with associated data {#Authenticated_encryption_with_associated_data}

Authenticated encryption with associated data (AEAD) is a variant of AE that allows the message to include "associated data" (AD, additional non-confidential information, a.k.a. "additional authenticated data", AAD). A recipient can check the integrity of both the associated data and the confidential information in a message. AD is useful, for example, in [network packets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_packet) where the [header](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Header_%28computing%29) should be visible for [routing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Routing), but the payload needs to be confidential, and both need [integrity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_integrity) and [authenticity](wikipedia://en/46671693). The notion of AEAD was formalized by [Rogaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillip_Rogaway) (2002).[^n3]

:::

::: {#o27 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Key commitment"}

### Key-committing AEAD {#Key-committing_AEAD}

AE was originally designed primarily to provide the ciphertext integrity: successful validation of an authentication tag by [Alice](wikipedia://en/679079) using her symmetric key K<sub>A</sub> indicates that the message was not tampered with by an adversary [Mallory](wikipedia://en/679079) that does not possess the K<sub>A</sub>. The AE schemes usually do not provide the **key commitment**, a guarantee that the decryption would fail for any other key.[^n4] As of 2021, most existing AE schemes (including the very popular GCM) allow some messages to be decrypted without an error using more than just the (correct) K<sub>A</sub>; while the plaintext decrypted using a second (wrong) key K<sub>M</sub> will be incorrect, the authentication tag would still match the new plaintext.[^o29] Since crafting a message with such property requires Mallory to already possess both K<sub>A</sub> and K<sub>M</sub>, the issue might appear to be one of a purely academic interest.[^n5] However, under special circumstances, practical attacks can be mounted against vulnerable implementations. For example, if an identity authentication protocol is based on successful decryption of a message that uses a password-based key, Mallory's ability to craft a single message that would be successfully decrypted using 1000 different keys associated with [weak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_password), and thus known to her, potential passwords, can speed up her search for passwords by a factor of almost 1000. For this [dictionary attack](wikipedia://en/53785) to succeed, Mallory also needs an ability to distinguish successful decryption by Alice from an unsuccessful one, due, for example, to a poor protocol design or implementation turning Alice's side into an [oracle](wikipedia://en/40705185). Naturally, this attack cannot be mounted at all when the keys are generated randomly.[^o31]

Key commitment was originally studied in the 2010s by Abdalla et al.[^n6] and Farshim et al.[^n7] under the name "robust encryption".[^n5][^n8]

To mitigate the attack described above without removing the "oracle", a *key-committing AEAD* that does not allow this type of crafted messages to exist can be used. AEGIS is an example of fast (if the [AES instruction set](wikipedia://en/23095336) is present), key-committing AEAD.[^o36] It is possible to add key-commitment to an existing AEAD scheme.[^o37][^o38]

:::

### Misuse-resistant authenticated encryption {#Misuse-resistant_authenticated_encryption}

Misuse-resistant authenticated encryption (MRAE) has the additional property that reusing the same [nonce](wikipedia://en/6988390) for several messages does not allow an attacker to recover the plaintext. MRAE was formalized in 2006 by [Phillip Rogaway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phillip_Rogaway) and Thomas Shrimpton.[^o40] One example of a MRAE algorithm is [AES-GCM-SIV](wikipedia://en/61514054).

:::

## Approaches to authenticated encryption {#Approaches_to_authenticated_encryption}

### Encrypt-then-MAC (EtM) {#Encrypt-then-MAC_(EtM)}

*Image: Authenticated Encryption EtM.png.* EtM approach

The plaintext is first encrypted, then a MAC is produced based on the resulting ciphertext. The ciphertext and its MAC are sent together. ETM is the standard method according to ISO/IEC 19772:2009.[^o16] It is the only method which can reach the highest definition of security in AE, but this can only be achieved when the MAC used is "strongly unforgeable".[^o43]

[IPSec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPsec) adopted EtM in 2005.[^o44] In November 2014, TLS and DTLS received extensions for EtM with 7366. Various EtM ciphersuites exist for SSHv2 as well (e.g., hmac-sha1-etm@openssh.com).

### Encrypt-and-MAC (E&M) {#Encrypt-and-MAC_(E&M)}

*Image: Authenticated Encryption EaM.png.* E&M approach

A MAC is produced based on the plaintext, and the plaintext is encrypted without the MAC. The plaintext's MAC and the ciphertext are sent together. Used in, e.g., [SSH](wikipedia://en/28814).[^o46] Even though the E&M approach has not been proved to be strongly unforgeable in itself,[^o43] it is possible to apply some minor modifications to [SSH](wikipedia://en/28814) to make it strongly unforgeable despite the approach.[^o47]

### MAC-then-Encrypt (MtE) {#MAC-then-Encrypt_(MtE)}

*Image: Authenticated Encryption MtE.png.* MtE approach

A MAC is produced based on the plaintext, then the plaintext and MAC are together encrypted to produce a ciphertext based on both. The ciphertext (containing an encrypted MAC) is sent. Until TLS 1.2, all available [SSL/TLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSL/TLS) cipher suites were MtE.[^o49]

MtE has not been proven to be strongly unforgeable in itself.[^o43] The [SSL/TLS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SSL/TLS) implementation has been proven to be strongly unforgeable by [Krawczyk](wikipedia://en/69633259) who showed that SSL/TLS was, in fact, secure because of the encoding used alongside the MtE mechanism.[^o50] However, Krawczyk's proof contains flawed assumptions about the randomness of the [initialization vector](wikipedia://en/105971) (IV). The 2011 BEAST attack exploited the non-random chained IV and broke all CBC algorithms in TLS 1.0 and under.[^o51]

In addition, deeper analysis of SSL/TLS modeled the protection as MAC-then-pad-then-encrypt, i.e. the plaintext is first padded to the block size of the encryption function. Padding errors often result in the detectable errors on the recipient's side, which in turn lead to [padding oracle attacks](wikipedia://en/29157021), such as [Lucky Thirteen](wikipedia://en/38419704).
