# Debye–Hückel theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Debye–Hückel theory"}

The **Debye–Hückel theory** was proposed by [Peter Debye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Debye) and [Erich Hückel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erich_Hückel) as a theoretical explanation for departures from ideality in solutions of [electrolytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte) and [plasmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_%28physics%29).[^o2] It is a linearized [Poisson–Boltzmann model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson–Boltzmann_equation), which assumes an extremely simplified model of electrolyte solution but nevertheless gave accurate predictions of mean [activity coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) for ions in dilute solution. The Debye–Hückel equation provides a starting point for modern treatments of non-ideality of electrolyte solutions.[^o3]

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

In the [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) of [electrolyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte) solutions, an [ideal solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_solution) is a solution whose [colligative properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colligative_properties) are proportional to the [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration) of the [solute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solute). Real solutions may show departures from this kind of ideality. In order to accommodate these effects in the [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) of solutions, the concept of [activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_%28chemistry%29) was introduced: the properties are then proportional to the activities of the ions. Activity *a* is proportional to concentration *c*, with the proportionality constant known as an [activity coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) $\gamma$:[^o5]

$$a = \gamma c/c^0.$$

In an ideal electrolyte solution the activity coefficients for all the ions are equal to one. Ideality of an electrolyte solution can be achieved only in very dilute solutions. Non-ideality of more concentrated solutions arises principally (but not exclusively) because ions of unlike charges attract each other due to [electrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic) forces, while ions of the like charges repel each other. In consequence, ions are not randomly distributed throughout the solution, as they would be in an ideal solution.

Activity coefficients of single ions cannot be measured experimentally because an electrolyte solution must contain both positively charged ions and negatively charged ions. Instead, a mean activity coefficient $\gamma_{\pm}$ is defined. For example, with the electrolyte NaCl,

$$\gamma_{\pm} = {\left(\gamma_\ce{Na+} \gamma_\ce{Cl-}\right)}^{1/2}.$$

In general, the mean activity coefficient of a fully dissociated electrolyte of formula *A<sub>n</sub>B<sub>m</sub>* is given by[^o6]

$$\gamma_{\pm} = {\left({\gamma_A}^n{\gamma _B}^m\right)}^{1/(n+m)}.$$

Activity coefficients are themselves functions of concentration, since the amount of inter-ionic interaction increases as the concentration of the electrolyte increases. Debye and Hückel developed a theory with which single-ion activity coefficients could be calculated. By calculating the mean activity coefficients from them, the theory could be tested against experimental data. It was found to give excellent agreement for "dilute" solutions.

## The model {#The_model}

*Image: Ionenverteilung in Loesung.svg.* An idealized representation of a solution of a 1:1 electrolyte

A description of Debye–Hückel theory includes a very detailed discussion of the assumptions and their limitations as well as the mathematical development and applications.[^o8]

A snapshot of a 2-dimensional section of an idealized electrolyte solution is shown in the picture. The ions are shown as spheres with unit electrical charge. The solvent (pale blue) is shown as a uniform medium, without structure. On average, each ion is surrounded more closely by ions of opposite charge than by ions of like charge. These concepts were developed into a quantitative theory involving ions of charge *z*<sub>1</sub>*e*<sup>\+</sup> and *z*<sub>2</sub>*e*<sup>−</sup>, where *z* can be any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563). The principal assumption is that departure from ideality is due to electrostatic interactions between ions, mediated by [Coulomb's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law): the force of interaction between two electric charges, separated by a distance, *r* in a medium of [relative permittivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_permittivity) $\varepsilon_{r}$ is given by[^o9]

$$\text{force} = \frac {z_1 z_2 e^2}{4 \pi \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r r^2}$$

It is also assumed that

- The [solute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte) is completely dissociated; it is a [strong electrolyte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_electrolyte).
- Ions are spherical and are not [polarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarizability) by the surrounding [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092). [Solvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvation) of ions is ignored except insofar as it determines the effective sizes of the ions.
- The solvent plays no role other than providing a medium of constant relative permittivity ([dielectric constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric_constant)).
- There is no [electrostriction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostriction).
- Individual ions surrounding a "central" ion can be represented by a statistically averaged cloud of continuous charge density, with a minimum [distance of closest approach](wikipedia://en/22697098).

The last assumption means that each cation is surrounded by a spherically symmetric cloud of other ions. The cloud has a net negative charge. Similarly each anion is surrounded by a cloud with net positive charge.[^o10]

## Mathematical development {#Mathematical_development}

The deviation from ideality is taken to be a function of the potential energy resulting from the electrostatic interactions between ions and their surrounding clouds. To calculate this energy two steps are needed.

The first step is to specify the electrostatic potential for ion *j* by means of [Poisson's equation](wikipedia://en/193735)

$$\nabla^2 \psi_j(r) = - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r} \rho_j(r)$$

$\psi(r)$ is the total potential at a distance, $r$, from the central ion and $\rho(r)$ is the averaged charge density of the surrounding cloud at that distance. To apply this formula it is essential that the cloud has spherical symmetry, that is, the [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) is a function only of distance from the central ion as this allows the Poisson equation to be cast in terms of [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) with no angular dependence.[^o12]

The second step is to calculate the charge density by means of a [Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/4107).

$$n'_i = n_i \exp \left(\frac{-z_i e \psi_j(r)}{k_\text{B} T}\right)$$

where $k_{B}$ is [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant) and $T$ is the temperature. This distribution also depends on the potential $\psi(r)$ and this introduces a serious difficulty in terms of the [superposition principle](wikipedia://en/1201321). Nevertheless, the two equations can be combined to produce the [Poisson–Boltzmann equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson–Boltzmann_equation).[^o13]

$$\nabla^2 \psi_j(r) = - \frac{1}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r} \sum_i \left[n_i (z_i e) \exp \left( \frac{-z_i e \psi_j(r)}{k_\text{B} T} \right)\right]$$

Solution of this equation is far from straightforward. Debye and Hückel expanded the exponential as a truncated [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) to first order. The zeroth order term vanishes because the solution is on average [electrically neutral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrically_neutral) (so that $\sum n_i z_i = 0$), which leaves us with only the first order term. The result has the form of the [Helmholtz equation](wikipedia://en/1156215)[^o14]

$$\nabla^2\psi_j(r) = \kappa^2 \psi_j(r) \qquad \text{with} \qquad \kappa^2 = \frac{e^2}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r k_\text{B} T} \sum_i n_i z_i^2,$$

which has an [analytical solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_solution). This equation applies to electrolytes with equal numbers of ions of each charge. Nonsymmetrical electrolytes require another term with $\psi^{2}$. For symmetrical electrolytes, this reduces to the modified spherical Bessel equation

$$\left(\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2} + \frac{2}{r} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} - \kappa^2\right) \psi_j = 0,$$

with solutions

$$\psi_j(r) = A' \frac{e^{-\kappa r}}{r} + A'' \frac{e^{\kappa r}}{r}.$$

The coefficients $A'$ and $A''$ are fixed by the boundary conditions. As $r \to \infty$, $\psi$ must not diverge, so $A'' = 0$. At $r = a_0$, which is the distance of the closest approach of ions, the force exerted by the charge should be balanced by the force of other ions, imposing $\partial_r \psi_j(a_0) = -z_j e/(4\pi \varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r a_0^2)$, from which $A'$ is found, yielding

$$\psi_j(r) = \frac{z_j e}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r} \frac{e^{\kappa a_0}}{1+\kappa a_0} \frac{e^{-\kappa r}}{r}$$

The [electrostatic potential energy](wikipedia://en/1975956#Definition), $u_j$, of the ion at $r=0$ is

$$u_j = z_j e \left( \psi_j(a_0) - \frac{z_j e}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r} \frac{1}{a_0} \right) = -\frac{z_j^2 e^2}{4\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r} \frac{\kappa}{1+\kappa a_0}$$

This is the potential energy of a single ion in a solution. The multiple-charge generalization from electrostatics gives an expression for the potential energy of the entire solution.

*Image: Debye-Huckel-extended.png.* Debye–Hückel extended law compared to KCl data

The mean activity coefficient is given by the logarithm of this quantity as follows [^o15]

$$\log_{10}\gamma_\pm = -A z_j^2 \frac{\sqrt I}{1 +Ba_0\sqrt I}$$

$$A = \frac{e^2B}{2.303 \times 8\pi\varepsilon_0\varepsilon_r k_\text{B}T}$$

$$B = \left( \frac{2e^2 N}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r k_\text{B} T} \right)^{1/2}$$

where *I* is the [ionic strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_strength) and *a*<sub>0</sub> is a parameter that represents the distance of closest approach of ions. For aqueous solutions at 25 °C *A* = 0.51 mol<sup>−1/2</sup>dm<sup>3/2</sup> and *B* = 3.29 nm<sup>−1</sup>mol<sup>−1/2</sup>dm<sup>3/2</sup>[^o16]

$A$ is a constant that depends on temperature. If $I$ is expressed in terms of molality, instead of molarity (as in the equation above and in the rest of this article), then an experimental value for $A$ *of water* is $1.172\text{ mol}^{-1/2}\text{kg}^{1/2}$ at 25 °C. It is common to use a base-10 logarithm, in which case we factor $\ln 10$, so *A* is $0.509\text{ mol}^{-1/2}\text{kg}^{1/2}$. The multiplier 3 before $I/2$ in the equation is for the case when the dimensions of $I$ are $\text{mol}/\text{dm}^3$. When the dimensions of $I$ are $\text{mole}/\text{m}^3$, the multiplier 3 must be dropped from the equation [^o17]

*Image: Debye-Huckel-limiting.png.* Debye–Hückel limiting law compared to KCl data

The most significant aspect of this result is the prediction that the mean activity coefficient is a function of *ionic strength* rather than the electrolyte concentration. For very low values of the ionic strength the value of the denominator in the expression above becomes nearly equal to one. In this situation the mean activity coefficient is proportional to the square root of the ionic strength. This is known as the Debye–Hückel limiting law. In this limit the equation is given as follows[^o17]

$$\ln(\gamma_i) = -\frac{z_i^2 q^2 \kappa}{8 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T} = -\frac{z_i^2 q^3 N^{1/2}_\text{A}}{4 \pi (\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T)^{3/2}} \sqrt{10^3\frac{I}{2}} = -A z_i^2 \sqrt{I},$$

The excess [osmotic pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmotic_pressure) obtained from Debye–Hückel theory is in [cgs units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cgs_units):[^o18]

$$P^\text{ex} = -\frac{k_\text{B} T \kappa_\text{cgs}^3}{24\pi} = -\frac{k_\text{B} T }{24\pi} {\left(\frac{4\pi \sum_j c_j q_j}{\varepsilon_0 \varepsilon_r k_\text{B} T }\right)}^{3/2}.$$

Therefore, the total pressure is the sum of the excess osmotic pressure and the ideal pressure $P^\text{id} = k_\text{B} T \sum_i c_i$. The [osmotic coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Osmotic_coefficient) is then given by

$$\phi = \frac{P^\text{id} + P^\text{ex}}{P^\text{id}} = 1 + \frac{P^\text{ex}}{P^\text{id}}.$$

## Nondimensionalization {#Nondimensionalization}

Taking the [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) from [earlier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debye-Hückel_theory#Mathematical_development) (as stated above, the equation only holds for low concentrations):

$$\frac{\partial^2}{\partial r^2} \varphi(r) + \frac{2}{r} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \varphi(r) = \frac{I q \varphi(r)}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T} = \kappa^2 \varphi(r).$$

Using the [Buckingham π theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buckingham_π_theorem) on this problem results in the following dimensionless groups:

$$\begin{aligned} \pi_1 &= \frac{q \varphi(r)}{k_\text{B} T} = \Phi(R(r)), & \pi_2 &= \varepsilon_r, \\[1ex] \pi_3 &= \frac{a k_\text{B} T \varepsilon_0}{q^2}, & \pi_4 &= a^3 I, \\[1ex] \pi_5 &= z_0, & \pi_6 &= \frac{r}{a} = R(r). \end{aligned}$$

$\Phi$ is called the reduced scalar electric potential field. $R$ is called the reduced radius. The existing groups may be recombined to form two other dimensionless groups for substitution into the differential equation. The first is what could be called the square of the reduced inverse [screening length](wikipedia://en/1158235), $(\kappa a)^2$. The second could be called the reduced central ion charge, $Z_0$ (with a capital Z). Note that, though $z_0$ is already dimensionless, without the substitution given below, the differential equation would still be dimensional.

$$\frac{\pi_4}{\pi_2 \pi_3} = \frac{a^2 q^2 I}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T} = (\kappa a)^2$$

$$\frac{\pi_5}{\pi_2 \pi_3} = \frac{z_0 q^2}{4 \pi a \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T} = Z_0$$

To obtain the nondimensionalized differential equation and initial conditions, use the $\pi$ groups to eliminate $\varphi(r)$ in favor of $\Phi(R(r))$, then eliminate $R(r)$ in favor of $r$ while carrying out the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) and substituting ${R^\prime}(r) = a$, then eliminate $r$ in favor of $R$ (no chain rule needed), then eliminate $I$ in favor of $(\kappa a)^2$, then eliminate $z_0$ in favor of $Z_0$. The resulting equations are as follows:

$$\left.\frac{\partial \Phi(R) }{\partial R}\right|_{R=1} = - Z_0$$

$$\Phi(\infty) = 0$$

$$\frac{\partial^2 \Phi(R) }{\partial R^2} + \frac{2}{R} \frac{\partial \Phi(R) }{\partial R} = (\kappa a)^2 \Phi(R).$$

For table salt in 0.01 M solution at 25 °C, a typical value of $(\kappa a)^2$ is 0.0005636, while a typical value of $Z_0$ is 7.017, highlighting the fact that, in low concentrations, $(\kappa a)^2$ is a target for a zero [order of magnitude](wikipedia://en/22657) approximation such as perturbation analysis. Unfortunately, because of the boundary condition at infinity, regular perturbation does not work. The same boundary condition prevents us from finding the exact solution to the equations. [Singular perturbation](wikipedia://en/525234) may work, however.

## Limitations and extensions {#Limitations_and_extensions}

*Image: Na+H2O.svg.* The first solvation shell of a [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium) [ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion) dissolved in water. The oxygen atoms are arranged at the vertices of an [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) with the sodium ion at its centre

This equation for $\log\gamma_\pm$ gives satisfactory agreement with experimental measurements for low electrolyte concentrations, typically less than 10<sup>−3</sup> mol/L. Deviations from the theory occur at higher concentrations and with electrolytes that produce ions of higher charges, particularly unsymmetrical electrolytes. Essentially these deviations occur because the model is [oversimplified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oversimplification), so there is little to be gained making small adjustments to the model.[^o21] The individual assumptions can be challenged in turn.

- Complete dissociation. [Ion association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion_association) may take place, particularly with ions of higher charge. This was followed up in detail by [Niels Bjerrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niels_Bjerrum). The [Bjerrum length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bjerrum_length) is the separation at which the electrostatic interaction between two ions is comparable in magnitude to $k_{B}T$.
- Weak electrolytes. A weak electrolyte is one that is not fully dissociated. As such it has a [dissociation constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant). The dissociation constant can be used to calculate the extent of dissociation and hence, make the necessary correction needed to calculate activity coefficients.[^o22]
- Ions are spherical, not [point charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge) and are not [polarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polarizability). Many ions such as the [nitrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nitrate) ion, NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup>, are not spherical. Polyatomic ions are also polarizable.
- Role of the solvent. The solvent is not a structureless medium but is made up of molecules. The water molecules in [aqueous solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solution) are both [dipolar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipolar) and polarizable. Both cations and anions have a strong primary [solvation shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvation_shell) and a weaker secondary solvation shell. [Ion–solvent interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvation) are ignored in Debye–Hückel theory.

Moreover, ionic radius is assumed to be negligible, but at higher concentrations, the [ionic radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_radius) becomes comparable to the radius of the [ionic atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_atmosphere). Most extensions to Debye–Hückel theory are empirical in nature. They usually allow the Debye–Hückel equation to be followed at low concentration and add further terms in some power of the ionic strength to fit experimental observations. The main extensions are the [Davies equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davies_equation), [Pitzer equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitzer_equation) and [specific ion interaction theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_ion_interaction_theory).

One such extended Debye–Hückel equation is given by:

$$- \log_{10}(\gamma) = \frac{A|z_+z_-|\sqrt{I}}{1 + Ba\sqrt{I}}$$

where $\gamma$ as its [common logarithm](wikipedia://en/174482) is the activity coefficient, $z$ is the integer charge of the ion (1 for H<sup>\+</sup>, 2 for Mg<sup>2+</sup> etc.), $I$ is the ionic strength of the aqueous solution, and $a$ is the size or **effective diameter** of the ion in [angstrom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angstrom). The effective hydrated radius of the ion, a is the radius of the ion and its closely bound water molecules. Large ions and less highly charged ions bind water less tightly and have smaller hydrated radii than smaller, more highly charged ions. Typical values are 3Å for ions such as H<sup>\+</sup>, Cl<sup>−</sup>, CN<sup>−</sup>, and HCOO<sup>−</sup>. The effective diameter for the [hydronium ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydronium_ion) is 9Å. $A$ and $B$ are constants with values of respectively 0.5085 and 0.3281 at 25 °C in water .

The extended Debye–Hückel equation provides accurate results for μ ≤ 0.1. For solutions of greater ionic strengths, the [Pitzer equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pitzer_equations) should be used. In these solutions the activity coefficient may actually increase with ionic strength.

*Image: Debye-Hckel equation.png.* The Debye–Hückel plot with different values for ion charge Z and ion diameter a

The Debye–Hückel equation cannot be used in the solutions of [surfactants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfactant) where the presence of [micelles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micelle) influences on the electrochemical properties of the system (even rough judgement overestimates $\gamma$ for ~50%).

### Electrolytes mixtures {#Electrolytes_mixtures}

The theory can be applied also to dilute solutions of mixed electrolytes. [Freezing point depression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freezing_point_depression) measurements has been used to this purpose.[^o24]

## Conductivity {#Conductivity}

*Image: electrophoresis2.svg.* Forces affecting a charged ion or cloud moving in an electric field

The treatment given so far is for a system not subject to an external electric field. When [conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conductivity_%28electrolytic%29) is measured the system is subject to an oscillating external field due to the application of an [AC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_current) voltage to electrodes immersed in the solution. Debye and Hückel modified their theory in 1926 and their theory was further modified by [Lars Onsager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Onsager) in 1927. All the postulates of the original theory were retained. In addition it was assumed that the electric field causes the charge cloud to be distorted away from spherical symmetry.[^o27] After taking this into account, together with the specific requirements of moving ions, such as [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) and [electrophoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrophoretic) effects, Onsager was able to derive a theoretical expression to account for the empirical relation known as [Kohlrausch's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kohlrausch's_Law), for the molar conductivity, Λ<sub>m</sub>.

$$\Lambda_m = \Lambda_m^0-K\sqrt{c}$$

$\Lambda_m^0$ is known as the limiting molar conductivity, *K* is an empirical constant and *c* is the electrolyte concentration. Limiting here means "at the limit of the infinite dilution"). Onsager's expression is

$$\Lambda_m = \Lambda_m^0 - (A + B \Lambda_m^0) \sqrt{c}$$

where *A* and *B* are constants that depend only on known quantities such as temperature, the charges on the ions and the dielectric constant and viscosity of the solvent. This is known as the Debye–Hückel–Onsager equation. However, this equation only applies to very dilute solutions and has been largely superseded by other equations due to Fuoss and Onsager, 1932 and 1957 and later.[^o28]

:::

## Summary of Debye and Hückel's first article on the theory of dilute electrolytes {#Summary_of_Debye_and_Hückel's_first_article_on_the_theory_of_dilute_electrolytes}

The English title of the article is "On the Theory of Electrolytes. I. Freezing Point Depression and Related Phenomena". It was originally published in 1923 in volume 24 of a German-language journal Physikalische Zeitschrift. An English translation[^o30]p. 217–63 of the article is included in a book of collected papers presented to Debye by "his pupils, friends, and the publishers on the occasion of his seventieth birthday on March 24, 1954".[^o30]p. xv Another English translation was completed in 2019.[^o31] The article deals with the calculation of properties of electrolyte solutions that are under the influence of ion-induced electric fields, thus it deals with [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics).

In the same year they first published this article, Debye and Hückel, hereinafter D&H, also released an article that covered their initial characterization of solutions under the influence of electric fields called "On the Theory of Electrolytes. II. Limiting Law for Electric Conductivity", but that subsequent article is not (yet) covered here.

In the following summary (as yet incomplete and unchecked), modern notation and terminology are used, from both chemistry and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in order to prevent confusion. Also, with a few exceptions to improve clarity, the subsections in this summary are (very) condensed versions of the same subsections of the original article.

### Introduction {#Introduction}

D&H note that the Guldberg–Waage formula for electrolyte species in [chemical reaction equilibrium](wikipedia://en/5306) in classical form is[^o30]p. 221

$$\prod_{i=1}^s x_i^{\nu_i} = K,$$

where

- $\prod$ is a notation for [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845#Capital_Pi_notation),
- $i$ is a [dummy variable](wikipedia://en/147460) indicating the species,
- $s$ is the number of species participating in the reaction,
- $x_i$ is the [mole fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_fraction) of species $i$,
- $\nu_i$ is the [stoichiometric coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometric_coefficient) of species $i$,
- *K* is the [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant).

D&H say that, due to the "mutual electrostatic forces between the ions", it is necessary to modify the Guldberg–Waage equation by replacing $K$ with $\gamma K$, where $\gamma$ is an overall activity coefficient, not a "special" [activity coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) (a separate activity coefficient associated with each species)—which is what is used in modern chemistry .

The relationship between $\gamma$ and the special activity coefficients $\gamma_i$ is[^o30]p. 248

$$\log(\gamma) = \sum_{i=1}^s \nu_i \log(\gamma_i).$$

### Fundamentals {#Fundamentals}

D&H use the [Helmholtz and Gibbs free entropies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_entropy) $\Phi$ and $\Xi$ to express the effect of electrostatic forces in an electrolyte on its [thermodynamic state](wikipedia://en/2747182). Specifically, they split most of the [thermodynamic potentials](wikipedia://en/255446) into classical and electrostatic terms:

$$\Phi = S - \frac{U}{T} = -\frac{A}{T},$$

where

- $\Phi$ is [Helmholtz free entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_entropy),
- $S$ is [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891),
- $U$ is [internal energy](wikipedia://en/340757),
- $T$ is [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050),
- $A$ is [Helmholtz free energy](wikipedia://en/255447).

D&H give the [total differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28infinitesimal%29) of $\Phi$ as[^o30]p. 222

$$d \Phi = \frac{P}{T} \,dV + \frac{U}{T^2} \,dT,$$

where

- $P$ is [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure),
- $V$ is [volume](wikipedia://en/32498).

By the definition of the total differential, this means that

$$\frac{P}{T} = \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial V},$$

$$\frac{U}{T^2} = \frac{\partial \Phi}{\partial T},$$

which are useful further on.

As stated previously, the internal energy is divided into two parts:[^o30]p. 222

$$U = U_k + U_e$$

where

- $k$ indicates the classical part,
- $e$ indicates the electric part.

Similarly, the Helmholtz free entropy is also divided into two parts:

$$\Phi = \Phi_k + \Phi_e.$$

D&H state, without giving the logic, that[^o30]p. 222

$$\Phi_e = \int \frac{U_e}{T^2} \,dT.$$

It would seem that, without some justification,

$$\Phi_e = \int \frac{P_e}{T} \,dV + \int \frac{U_e}{T^2} \,dT.$$

Without mentioning it specifically, D&H later give what might be the required (above) justification while arguing that $\Phi_e = \Xi_e$, an assumption that the solvent is incompressible.

The definition of the [Gibbs free entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_entropy) $\Xi$ is[^o30]p. 222–3

$$\Xi = S - \frac{U + PV}{T} = \Phi - \frac{PV}{T} = -\frac{G}{T},$$

where $G$ is [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181).

D&H give the total differential of $\Xi$ as[^o30]p. 222

$$d\Xi = -\frac{V}{T} \,dP + \frac{U + PV}{T^2} \,dT.$$

At this point D&H note that, for water containing 1 mole per liter of [potassium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potassium_chloride) (nominal pressure and temperature aren't given), the electric pressure $P_e$ amounts to 20 atmospheres. Furthermore, they note that this level of pressure gives a relative volume change of 0.001. Therefore, they neglect change in volume of water due to electric pressure, writing[^o30]p. 223

$$\Xi = \Xi_k + \Xi_e,$$

and put

$$\Xi_e = \Phi_e = \int \frac{U_e}{T^2} \,dT.$$

D&H say that, according to Planck, the classical part of the Gibbs free entropy is[^o30]p. 223

$$\Xi_k = \sum_{i=0}^s N_i (\xi_i - k_\text{B} \ln(x_i)),$$

where

- $i$ is a species,
- $s$ is the number of different particle types in solution,
- $N_i$ is the number of particles of species *i*,
- $\xi_i$ is the particle specific Gibbs free entropy of species *i*,
- $k_\text{B}$ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant),
- $x_i$ is the mole fraction of species *i*.

Species zero is the solvent. The definition of $\xi_i$ is as follows, where lower-case letters indicate the particle specific versions of the corresponding extensive properties:[^o30]p. 223

$$\xi_i = s_i - \frac{u_i + P v_i}{T}.$$

D&H don't say so, but the functional form for $\Xi_k$ may be derived from the functional dependence of the [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628) of a component of an [ideal mixture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_mixture) upon its mole fraction.[^o34]

D&H note that the internal energy $U$ of a solution is lowered by the electrical interaction of its ions, but that this effect can't be determined by using the crystallographic approximation for distances between dissimilar atoms (the [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506) of the ratio of total volume to the number of particles in the volume). This is because there is more thermal motion in a liquid solution than in a crystal. The thermal motion tends to smear out the natural lattice that would otherwise be constructed by the ions. Instead, D&H introduce the concept of an [ionic atmosphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_atmosphere) or cloud. Like the crystal lattice, each ion still attempts to surround itself with oppositely charged ions, but in a more free-form manner; at small distances away from positive ions, one is more likely to find negative ions and vice versa.[^o30]p. 225

### The potential energy of an arbitrary ion solution {#The_potential_energy_of_an_arbitrary_ion_solution}

Electroneutrality of a solution requires that[^o30]p. 233

$$\sum_{i=1}^s N_i z_i = 0,$$

where

- $N_i$ is the total number of ions of species *i* in the solution,
- $z_i$ is the [charge number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_number) of species *i*.

To bring an ion of species *i*, initially far away, to a point $P$ within the ion cloud requires [interaction energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interaction_energy) in the amount of $z_i q \varphi$, where $q$ is the [elementary charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_charge), and $\varphi$ is the value of the scalar [electric potential](wikipedia://en/59615) field at $P$. If electric forces were the only factor in play, the minimal-energy configuration of all the ions would be achieved in a close-packed lattice configuration. However, the ions are in [thermal equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_equilibrium) with each other and are relatively free to move. Thus they obey [Boltzmann statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_statistics) and form a [Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/4107). All species' [number densities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_density) $n_i$ are altered from their bulk (overall average) values $n^0_i$ by the corresponding [Boltzmann factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_factor) $e^{-\frac{z_i q \varphi}{k_\text{B} T}}$, where $k_\text{B}$ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant), and $T$ is the temperature.[^o36] Thus at every point in the cloud[^o30]p. 233

$$n_i = \frac{N_i}{V} e^{-\frac{z_i q \varphi}{k_\text{B} T}} = n^0_i e^{-\frac{z_i q \varphi}{k_\text{B} T}}.$$

Note that in the infinite temperature limit, all ions are distributed uniformly, with no regard for their electrostatic interactions.[^o30]p. 227

The [charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) is related to the number density:[^o30]p. 233

$$\rho = \sum_i z_i q n_i = \sum_i z_i q n^0_i e^{-\frac{z_i q \varphi}{k_\text{B} T}}.$$

When combining this result for the charge density with the [Poisson equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_equation) from electrostatics, a form of the [Poisson–Boltzmann equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson–Boltzmann_equation) results:[^o30]p. 233

$$\nabla^2 \varphi = -\frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} = -\sum_i \frac{z_i q n^0_i}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} e^{-\frac{z_i q \varphi}{k_\text{B} T}}.$$

This equation is difficult to solve and does not follow the principle of [linear superposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_superposition) for the relationship between the number of charges and the strength of the potential field. It has been solved analyticallt by the Swedish mathematician [Thomas Hakon Gronwall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hakon_Gronwall) and his collaborators physical chemists V. K. La Mer and Karl Sandved in a 1928 article from [Physikalische Zeitschrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physikalische_Zeitschrift) dealing with extensions to Debye–Huckel theory.[^o37]

However, for sufficiently low concentrations of ions, a first-order [Taylor series expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series_expansion) approximation for the exponential function may be used ($e^x \approx 1 + x$ for $0 < x \ll 1$) to create a [linear differential equation](wikipedia://en/379868).[^o17] D&H say that this approximation holds at large distances between ions,[^o30]p. 227 which is the same as saying that the concentration is low. Lastly, they claim without proof that the addition of more terms in the expansion has little effect on the final solution.[^o30]p. 227 Thus

$$-\sum_i \frac{z_i q n^0_i}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} e^{-\frac{z_i q \varphi}{k_\text{B} T}} \approx -\sum_i \frac{z_i q n^0_i}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \left(1 - \frac{z_i q \varphi}{k_\text{B} T}\right) = -\left(\sum_i \frac{z_i q n^0_i}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} - \sum_i \frac{z_i^2 q^2 n^0_i \varphi}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T}\right).$$

The Poisson–Boltzmann equation is transformed to[^o30]p. 233

$$\nabla^2 \varphi = \sum_i \frac{z_i^2 q^2 n^0_i \varphi}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T},$$

because the first summation is zero due to electroneutrality.[^o30]p. 234

Factor out the scalar potential and assign the leftovers, which are constant, to $\kappa^2$. Also, let $I$ be the [ionic strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_strength) of the solution:[^o30]p. 234

$$\kappa^2 = \sum_i \frac{z_i^2 q^2 n^0_i}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T} = \frac{2 I q^2}{\varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0 k_\text{B} T},$$

$$I = \frac{1}{2} \sum_i z_i^2 n^0_i.$$

So, the fundamental equation is reduced to a form of the [Helmholtz equation](wikipedia://en/1156215):[^o38]

$$\nabla^2 \varphi = \kappa^2 \varphi.$$

Today, $\kappa^{-1}$ is called the [Debye screening length](wikipedia://en/1158235). D&H recognize the importance of the parameter in their article and characterize it as a measure of the thickness of the ion atmosphere, which is an [electrical double layer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_double_layer) of the Gouy–Chapman type.[^o30]p. 229

The equation may be expressed in [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinates) by taking $r = 0$ at some arbitrary ion:[^o39][^o30]p. 229

$$\nabla^2 \varphi = \frac{1}{r^2} \frac{\partial}{\partial r} \left( r^2 \frac{\partial\varphi(r)}{\partial r} \right) = \frac{\partial^2\varphi(r)}{\partial r^2} + \frac{2}{r} \frac{\partial\varphi(r)}{\partial r} = \kappa^2 \varphi(r).$$

The equation has the following general solution (keep in mind that $\kappa$ is a positive constant):[^o30]p. 229

$$\varphi(r) = A \frac{e^{-\sqrt{\kappa^2} r}}{r} + A' \frac{e^{\sqrt{\kappa^2} r}}{2 r \sqrt {\kappa^2}} = A \frac{e^{-\kappa r}}{r} + A'' \frac{e^{\kappa r}}{r} = A \frac{e^{-\kappa r}}{r},$$

where $A$, $A'$, and $A''$ are undetermined constants

The electric potential is zero at infinity by definition, so $A''$ must be zero.[^o30]p. 229

In the next step, D&H assume that there is a certain radius $a_i$, beyond which no ions in the atmosphere may approach the (charge) center of the singled out ion. This radius may be due to the physical size of the ion itself, the sizes of the ions in the cloud, and any water molecules that surround the ions. Mathematically, they treat the singled out ion as a [point charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_charge) to which one may not approach within the radius $a_i$.[^o30]p. 231

The potential of a point charge by itself is

$$\varphi_\text{pc}(r) = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \frac{z_i q}{r}.$$

D&H say that the total potential inside the sphere is[^o30]p. 232

$$\varphi_\text{sp}(r) = \varphi_\text{pc}(r) + B_i = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \frac{z_i q}{r} + B_i,$$

where $B_i$ is a constant that represents the potential added by the ionic atmosphere. No justification for $B_i$ being a constant is given. However, one can see that this is the case by considering that any spherical static charge distribution is subject to the mathematics of the [shell theorem](wikipedia://en/2025989). The shell theorem says that no force is exerted on charged particles inside a sphere (of arbitrary charge).[^o40] Since the ion atmosphere is assumed to be (time-averaged) spherically symmetric, with charge varying as a function of radius $r$, it may be represented as an infinite series of concentric charge shells. Therefore, inside the radius $a_i$, the ion atmosphere exerts no force. If the force is zero, then the potential is a constant (by definition).

In a combination of the continuously distributed model which gave the Poisson–Boltzmann equation and the model of the point charge, it is assumed that at the radius $a_i$, there is a continuity of $\varphi(r)$ and its first derivative. Thus[^o30]p. 232

$$\varphi(a_i) = A_i \frac{e^{-\kappa a_i}}{a_i} = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \frac{z_i q}{a_i} + B_i = \varphi_\text{sp}(a_i),$$

$$\varphi'(a_i) = -\frac{A_i e^{-\kappa a_i} (1 + \kappa a_i)}{a_i^2} = -\frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \frac{z_i q}{a_i^2} = \varphi_\text{sp}'(a_i),$$

$$A_i = \frac{z_i q}{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \frac{e^{\kappa a_i}}{1 + \kappa a_i},$$

$$B_i = -\frac{z_i q \kappa}{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \frac {1}{1 + \kappa a_i}.$$

By the definition of [electric potential energy](wikipedia://en/1975956), the potential energy associated with the singled out ion in the ion atmosphere is[^o30]p. 230,232

$$u_i = z_i q B_i = -\frac{z_i^2 q^2 \kappa}{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \frac {1}{1 + \kappa a_i}.$$

Notice that this only requires knowledge of the charge of the singled out ion and the potential of all the other ions.

To calculate the potential energy of the entire electrolyte solution, one must use the multiple-charge generalization for electric potential energy:[^o30]p. 230,232

$$U_e = \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i=1}^s N_i u_i = -\sum_{i=1}^s \frac {N_i z_i^2}{2} \frac{q^2 \kappa}{4 \pi \varepsilon_r \varepsilon_0} \frac {1}{1 + \kappa a_i}.$$

## Experimental verification of the theory {#Experimental_verification_of_the_theory}

To verify the validity of the Debye–Hückel theory, many experimental ways have been tried, measuring the activity coefficients: the problem is that we need to go towards very high dilutions. Typical examples are: measurements of vapour pressure, freezing point, osmotic pressure (indirect methods) and measurement of electric potential in cells (direct method). Going towards high dilutions good results have been found using liquid membrane cells, it has been possible to investigate aqueous media 10<sup>−4</sup> M and it has been found that for 1:1 electrolytes (as NaCl or KCl) the Debye–Hückel equation is totally correct, but for 2:2 or 3:2 electrolytes it is possible to find negative deviation from the Debye–Hückel limit law: this strange behavior can be observed only in the very dilute area, and in more concentrate regions the deviation becomes positive. It is possible that Debye–Hückel equation is not able to foresee this behavior because of the linearization of the Poisson–Boltzmann equation, or maybe not: studies about this have been started only during the last years of the 20th century because before it wasn't possible to investigate the 10<sup>−4</sup> M region, so it is possible that during the next years new theories will be born.
