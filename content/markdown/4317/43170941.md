# Least-squares adjustment

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Least-squares adjustment"}

**Least-squares adjustment** is a model for the solution of an [overdetermined system](wikipedia://en/10160606) of equations based on the principle of [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359) of [observation residuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation_residuals). It is used extensively in the disciplines of [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying), [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608), and [photogrammetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photogrammetry)—the field of [geomatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomatics), collectively.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

There are three forms of least squares adjustment: *parametric*, *conditional*, and *combined*:

- In **parametric adjustment**, one can find an observation equation $h(X) = Y$ relating observations $Y$ explicitly in terms of parameters $X$ (leading to the A-model below).
- In **conditional adjustment**, there exists a condition equation which is $g(Y) = 0$ involving only observations $Y$ (leading to the B-model below) — with no parameters $X$ at all.
- Finally, in a **combined adjustment**, both parameters $X$ and observations $Y$ are involved implicitly in a mixed-model equation $f(X, Y) = 0$.

Clearly, parametric and conditional adjustments correspond to the more general combined case when $f(X,Y) = h(X) - Y$ and $f(X, Y) = g(Y)$, respectively. Yet the special cases warrant simpler solutions, as detailed below. Often in the literature, $Y$ may be denoted $L$.

:::

## Solution {#Solution}

The equalities above only hold for the estimated parameters $\hat{X}$ and observations $\hat{Y}$, thus $f\left(\hat{X},\hat{Y}\right) = 0$. In contrast, measured observations $\tilde{Y}$ and approximate parameters $\tilde{X}$ produce a nonzero *misclosure*:

$$\tilde{w} = f\left(\tilde{X},\tilde{Y}\right).$$

One can proceed to [Taylor series expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_series_expansion) of the equations, which results in the [Jacobians](wikipedia://en/195351) or [design matrices](wikipedia://en/949189): the first one,

$$A = \partial{f}/\partial{X};$$

and the second one,

$$B = \partial{f}/\partial{Y}.$$

The linearized model then reads:

$$\tilde{w} + A \hat{x} + B \hat{y} = 0,$$

where $\hat{x}=\hat{X}-\tilde{X}$ are estimated *parameter corrections* to the *a priori* values, and $\hat{y} = \hat{Y} - \tilde{Y}$ are post-fit *observation [residuals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics)*.

In the parametric adjustment, the second design matrix is an identity, *B*=-*I*, and the misclosure vector can be interpreted as the pre-fit residuals, $\tilde{y} = \tilde{w} = h(\tilde{X}) - \tilde{Y}$, so the system simplifies to:

$$A \hat{x} = \hat{y} - \tilde{y},$$

which is in the form of [ordinary least squares](wikipedia://en/1651906). In the conditional adjustment, the first design matrix is null, $A = 0$. For the more general cases, [Lagrange multipliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_multipliers) are introduced to relate the two Jacobian matrices, and transform the [constrained](wikipedia://en/7418540) least squares problem into an unconstrained one (albeit a larger one). In any case, their manipulation leads to the $\hat{X}$ and $\hat{Y}$ vectors as well as the respective parameters and observations *a posteriori* covariance matrices.

::: {#o6 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Computation"}

### Computation {#Computation}

Given the matrices and vectors above, their solution is found via standard least-squares methods; e.g., forming the [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/170366) and applying [Cholesky decomposition](wikipedia://en/134433), applying the [QR factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/QR_factorization) directly to the Jacobian matrix, [iterative methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterative_methods) for very large systems, etc.

:::

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

- [Leveling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leveling), [traverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Traverse_%28surveying%29), and [control networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_networks)
- [Bundle adjustment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bundle_adjustment)
- [Triangulation](wikipedia://en/207174), [Trilateration](wikipedia://en/59675946), Triangulateration
- [GPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GPS)/[GNSS positioning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNSS_positioning)
- [Helmert transformation](wikipedia://en/15318324)

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

- Parametric adjustment is similar to most of [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997) and coincides with the [Gauss–Markov model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss–Markov_model)
- Combined adjustment, also known as the **Gauss–Helmert model** (named after German mathematicians/geodesists [C.F. Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) and [F.R. Helmert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Friedrich_Robert_Helmert)),[^o10][^o11] is related to the [errors-in-variables models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors-in-variables_models) and [total least squares](wikipedia://en/971437).[^o12][^o13]
- The use of *a priori* parameter covariance matrix is akin to [Tikhonov regularization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tikhonov_regularization)

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Extensions"}

## Extensions {#Extensions}

If [rank deficiency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rank_deficiency) is encountered, it can often be rectified by the inclusion of additional equations imposing constraints on the parameters and/or observations, leading to [constrained least squares](wikipedia://en/48526234).

:::
