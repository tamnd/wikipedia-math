# Oskar Becker

*Image: Oscar Becker.jpg.* Oskar Becker

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Oskar Becker"}

**Oskar Becker** (5 September 1889 – 13 November 1964) was a German [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher), [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician), [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), and [historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historian) of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831).

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Becker was born in [Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig), where he studied mathematics. His dissertation under [Otto Hölder](wikipedia://en/191652) and [Karl Rohn](wikipedia://en/35220701) (1914) was *On the Decomposition of Polygons in non-intersecting triangles on the Basis of the Axioms of Connection and Order.*

He served in [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I) and returned to study philosophy with [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl), writing his *[Habilitationsschrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitationsschrift)* on *Investigations of the Phenomenological Foundations of Geometry and their Physical Applications*, (1923). Becker was Husserl's assistant, informally, and then official editor of the *Yearbook for Phenomenological Research*.

## Work in phenomenology and mathematical philosophy {#Work_in_phenomenology_and_mathematical_philosophy}

Becker published his major work, *Mathematical Existence* in the *Yearbook* in 1927, the same year [Martin Heidegger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Heidegger)'s *[Being and Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Being_and_Time)* appeared there. Becker attended Heidegger's [seminars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seminar) at this period.

Becker utilized not only [Husserlian phenomenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_%28philosophy%29) but, much more controversially, Heideggerian [hermeneutics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermeneutics), discussing [arithmetical](wikipedia://en/3118) [counting](wikipedia://en/275768) as "being toward death". His work was criticized both by [neo-Kantians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neo-Kantian) and by more mainstream, rationalist logicians, to whom Becker feistily replied. This work has not had great influence on later debates in the [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358), despite its many interesting analyses of the topic of its title.

Becker debated with [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) and [Paul Bernays](wikipedia://en/2556580) over the role of the potential infinite in Hilbert's formalist [metamathematics](wikipedia://en/183508). Becker argued that Hilbert could not stick with [finitism](wikipedia://en/152900), but had to assume the potential infinite. Clearly enough, Hilbert and Bernays do implicitly accept the potential infinite, but they claim that each induction in their proofs is finite. Becker was correct that [complete induction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_induction) was needed for assertions of consistency in the form of [universally quantified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_quantified) sentences, as opposed to claiming that a predicate holds for each individual natural number.

### *Paraontologie* {#Paraontologie}

In discussing Heidegger, Becker introduced the German neologism *Paraontologie*.[^o5] Although fundamentally different this usage has provided some minor influences in the term "paraontology" in English made more recently by [Nahum Chandler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nahum_Chandler), [Fred Moten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fred_Moten), and others, in discussing [blackness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/African-American_culture).[^o6]

## Intuitionistic and modal logic {#Intuitionistic_and_modal_logic}

Becker made a start toward the formalization of [L. E. J. Brouwer](wikipedia://en/172896)'s [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262). He developed a semantics of intuitionistic logic based on Husserl's phenomenology, and this semantics was used by [Arend Heyting](wikipedia://en/1180911) in his own formalization. Becker struggled, somewhat unsuccessfully, with the formulation of the rejection of [excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excluded_middle) appropriate for intuitionistic logic. Becker failed in the end to correctly distinguish classical and [intuitionistic negation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitionistic_negation), but he made a start. In an appendix to his book on [mathematical existence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_existence), Becker set the problem of finding a formal calculus for intuitionistic logic. In a series of works in the early 1950s he surveyed modal, intuitionistic, probabilistic, and other [philosophical logics](wikipedia://en/304939).

Becker made contributions to [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365) (the logic of necessity and [possibility](wikipedia://en/394008)) and *[Becker's postulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Becker's_postulate)*, the claim that modal status is necessary (for instance that the possibility of *P* implies the necessity of the possibility of *P*, and also the [iteration of necessity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iteration_of_necessity)) is named for him. Becker's Postulate later played a role in the formalization given, by [Charles Hartshorne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Hartshorne), the American [process theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_theologian), of the [Ontological Proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ontological_Proof) of God's existence, stimulated by conversations with the [logical positivist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_positivist) and opponent of the alleged proof, [Rudolf Carnap](wikipedia://en/57987).

## History of mathematics {#History_of_mathematics}

Becker also made important contributions to the history and interpretation of [ancient Greek mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics). Becker, as did several others, emphasized the "crisis" in Greek mathematics occasioned by the discovery of [incommensurability](wikipedia://en/753944) of the side of the [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) (or in the later, simpler proofs, the triangle) by [Hippasus of Metapontum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hippasus_of_Metapontum), and the threat of (literally) ["irrational" numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689). To German theorists of the "crisis", the Pythagorean diagonal of the square was similar in its impact to [Cantor's diagonalization method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor's_diagonalization_method) of generating higher order infinities, and [Gödel's diagonalization method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel's_diagonalization_method) in [Gödel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel)'s proof of [incompleteness of formalized arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompleteness_of_formalized_arithmetic). Becker, like several earlier historians, suggests that the avoidance of arithmetic statement of geometrical magnitude in [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) is avoided for [ratios](wikipedia://en/87837) and proportions, as a consequence of recoil from the shock of incommensurability. Becker also showed that all the theorems of Euclidean proportion theory could be proved using an earlier alternative to the [Eudoxus](wikipedia://en/48388) technique which Becker found stated in *[Aristotle's Topics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotle's_Topics)*, and which Becker attributes to [Theaetetus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theaetetus_%28mathematician%29). Becker also showed how a constructive logic that denied unrestricted excluded middle could be used to reconstruct most of Euclid's proofs.

More recent revisionist commentators such as [Wilbur Knorr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilbur_Knorr) and [David Fowler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Fowler_%28historian%29) have accused historians of early Greek mathematics writing in the early twentieth century, such as Becker, of reading the crisis of their own times illegitimately into the early Greek period. (This “crisis” may include both the crisis of twentieth century set theory and foundations of mathematics, and the general crisis of World War I, the overthrow of the Kaiser, communist uprisings, and the Weimar Republic.)

## Later thought {#Later_thought}

At the end of his life Becker re-emphasized the distinction between intuition of the formal and [Platonic realm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_realm) as opposed to the concrete existential realm, moved to the terminology, at least, of [divination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divination). In his *Dasein und Dawesen* Becker advocated what he called a "mantic" divination. Hermeneutics of the Heideggerian sort is applicable to individual lived existence, but "mantic" decipherment is necessary not only in mathematics, but in [aesthetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics), and the investigation of the [unconscious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unconscious_mind). These realms deal with the eternal and structural, such as the symmetries of nature, and are properly investigated by a mantic phenomenology, not an hermeneutic one. (Becker's emphasis on the timelessness and formal nature of the unconscious has some parallels with the account of [Jacques Lacan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Lacan).)

## Contacts and correspondence {#Contacts_and_correspondence}

Becker carried on an extensive correspondence with some of the greatest mathematicians and philosophers of the day. These included [Ackermann](wikipedia://en/62727), [Adolf Fraenkel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adolf_Fraenkel) (later Abraham), [Arend Heyting](wikipedia://en/1180911), [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302), [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544), and [Ernst Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655) among mathematicians, as well as [Hans Reichenbach](wikipedia://en/1546863) and [Felix Kaufmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Kaufmann) among philosophers. The letters that Becker received from these major figures of twentieth century mathematics and leading logical positivist philosophers, as well as Becker's own copies of his letters to them, were destroyed during World War II.

Becker's correspondence with Weyl has been reconstructed (see bibliography), as Weyl's copies of Becker's letters to him are preserved, and Becker often extensively quotes or paraphrases Weyl's own letters. Perhaps the same can be done with some other parts of this valuable but lost correspondence. Weyl entered into correspondence with Becker with high hopes and expectations, given their mutual admiration for Husserl's phenomenology and Husserl's great admiration for the work of Becker. However, Weyl, whose sympathies were with constructivism and intuitionism, lost patience when he argued with Becker about a purported intuition of the infinite defended by Becker. Weyl concluded, sourly, that Becker would discredit phenomenological approaches to mathematics if he persisted in this position.

## Nazism and neglect {#Nazism_and_neglect}

It is possible that regard for Becker's earlier work suffered from his later [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazi) allegiances, leading to lack of reference or published commentary by émigré logicians and mathematicians who had fled Hitlerism. His lecture on "The Vacuity of Art and the Daring of the Artist," presents a "Nordic Metaphysics" in fairly standard Nazi style.

According to Oskar Becker, the "*rhythm of Nietzsche's [Dionysian-Dithyrambs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionysian-Dithyrambs) was identical to the [Will to power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Will_to_power) and physically in the sense of youth identical to the marching rhythm of the [SA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturmabteilung)*".[^o12]

Oskar Becker was classified from an [SS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schutzstaffel)-point of view in the following way in the "*SD-Dossiers über Philosophie-Professoren"* (i.e. SD-files concerning philosophy professors) that were set up by the [SS Security Service (SD)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sicherheitsdienst): "not a party member but loyal to National Socialism, tries to consolidate the National Socialistic ideology".[^o13]

Two able philosophers who were students of Becker, [Jürgen Habermas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jürgen_Habermas) and [Hans Sluga](wikipedia://en/1504912), later grappled with the issue of the influence of Nazism on German academia. The application of Heidegger's ideas to theoretical science (let alone mathematics) has only recently become widespread, particularly in the [English-speaking world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English-speaking_world). Furthermore, Becker's polemical replies probably alienated his critics still further.

He died, aged 75, in [Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonn).
