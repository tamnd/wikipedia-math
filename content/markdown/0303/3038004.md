# Berezinian

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Berezinian"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), the **Berezinian** or **superdeterminant** is a generalization of the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) to the case of [supermatrices](wikipedia://en/2498855). The name is for [Felix Berezin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Berezin). The Berezinian plays a role analogous to the determinant when considering coordinate changes for integration on a [supermanifold](wikipedia://en/891255).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The Berezinian is uniquely determined by two defining properties:

- $$\operatorname{Ber}(XY) = \operatorname{Ber}(X)\operatorname{Ber}(Y)$$
- $$\operatorname{Ber}(e^X) = e^{\operatorname{str(X)}}\,$$

where str(*X*) denotes the [supertrace](wikipedia://en/1249738) of *X*. Unlike the classical determinant, the Berezinian is defined only for invertible supermatrices.

The simplest case to consider is the Berezinian of a supermatrix with entries in a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K*. Such supermatrices represent [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) of a [super vector space](wikipedia://en/3710682) over *K*. A particular even supermatrix is a [block matrix](wikipedia://en/457464) of the form

$$X = \begin{bmatrix}A & 0 \\ 0 & D\end{bmatrix}$$

Such a matrix is invertible [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) both *A* and *D* are [invertible matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_matrices) over *K*. The Berezinian of *X* is given by

$$\operatorname{Ber}(X) = \det(A)\det(D)^{-1}$$

For a motivation of the negative exponent see the [substitution formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berezin_integration#Multiple_variables) in the odd case.

More generally, consider matrices with entries in a [supercommutative algebra](wikipedia://en/859686) *R*. An even supermatrix is then of the form

$$X = \begin{bmatrix}A & B \\ C & D\end{bmatrix}$$

where *A* and *D* have even entries and *B* and *C* have odd entries. Such a matrix is invertible if and only if both *A* and *D* are invertible in the [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R*<sub>0</sub> (the [even subalgebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_subalgebra) of *R*). In this case the Berezinian is given by

$$\operatorname{Ber}(X) = \det(A-BD^{-1}C)\det(D)^{-1}$$

or, equivalently, by

$$\operatorname{Ber}(X) = \det(A)\det(D-CA^{-1}B)^{-1}.$$

These formulas are well-defined since we are only taking determinants of matrices whose entries are in the commutative ring *R*<sub>0</sub>. The matrix

$$D-CA^{-1}B \,$$

is known as the [Schur complement](wikipedia://en/372620) of *A* relative to $\begin{bmatrix} A & B \\ C & D \end{bmatrix}.$

An odd matrix *X* can only be invertible if the number of even dimensions equals the number of odd dimensions. In this case, invertibility of *X* is equivalent to the invertibility of *JX*, where

$$J = \begin{bmatrix}0 & I \\ -I & 0\end{bmatrix}.$$

Then the Berezinian of *X* is defined as

$$\operatorname{Ber}(X) = \operatorname{Ber}(JX) = \det(C-DB^{-1}A)\det(-B)^{-1}.$$

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The Berezinian of $X$ is always a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) in the ring *R*<sub>0</sub>.
- $$\operatorname{Ber}(X^{-1}) = \operatorname{Ber}(X)^{-1}$$
- $\operatorname{Ber}(X^{st}) = \operatorname{Ber}(X)$ where $X^{st}$ denotes the supertranspose of $X$.
- $$\operatorname{Ber}(X\oplus Y) = \operatorname{Ber}(X)\mathrm{Ber}(Y)$$

:::

## Berezinian module {#Berezinian_module}

The determinant of an endomorphism of a free module *M* can be defined as the induced action on the 1-dimensional highest exterior power of *M*. In the supersymmetric case there is no highest exterior power, but there is a still a similar definition of the Berezinian as follows.

Suppose that *M* is a free module of dimension (*p*,*q*) over *R*. Let *A* be the (super)symmetric algebra *S*\*(*M*\*) of the dual *M*\* of *M*. Then an automorphism of *M* acts on the [ext](wikipedia://en/840758) module

$$Ext_{A}^p (R,A)$$

(which has dimension (1,0) if *q* is even and dimension (0,1) if *q* is odd)) as multiplication by the Berezinian.
