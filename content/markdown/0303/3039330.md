# Torsion (algebra)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Torsion"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), a **torsion element** is an element of a [module](wikipedia://en/276410) that yields zero when multiplied by some [non-zero-divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-zero-divisor) of the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404). The **torsion submodule** of a module is the [submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule) formed by the torsion elements (in cases when this is indeed a submodule, such as when the ring is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462)). A **torsion module** is a module consisting entirely of torsion elements. A module is [torsion-free](wikipedia://en/38400572) if its only torsion element is the zero element.

:::

This terminology is more commonly used for modules over a [domain](wikipedia://en/487627), that is, when the regular elements of the ring are all its nonzero elements.

This terminology applies to [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) (with "module" and "submodule" replaced by "[group](wikipedia://en/19447)" and "[subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011)"). This is just a special case of the more general situation, because abelian groups are modules over the ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties). (In fact, this is the origin of the terminology, which was introduced for abelian groups before being generalized to modules.)

In the case of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) that are noncommutative, a *torsion element* is an element of finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527). Contrary to the [commutative](wikipedia://en/2974) case, the torsion elements do not form a subgroup, in general.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

An element *m* of a [module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Module_%28algebra%29) *M* over a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* is called a *torsion element* of the module if there exists a [regular element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_element_%28ring_theory%29) *r* of the ring (an element that is neither a left nor a right [zero divisor](wikipedia://en/51441)) that annihilates *m*, i.e., *r* *m* = 0. In an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) (a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) without zero divisors), every non-zero element is regular, so a torsion element of a module over an integral domain is one annihilated by a non-zero element of the integral domain. Some authors use this as the definition of a torsion element, but this definition does not work well over more general rings.

A module *M* over a ring *R* is called a *torsion module* if all its elements are torsion elements, and *[torsion-free](wikipedia://en/38400572)* if zero is the only torsion element.[^o4] If the ring *R* is commutative then the set of all torsion elements forms a submodule of *M*, called the *torsion submodule* of *M*, sometimes denoted T(*M*). If *R* is not commutative, T(*M*) may or may not be a submodule. It is shown in (Lam 2007) that *R* is a right [Ore ring](wikipedia://en/3100560) if and only if T(*M*) is a submodule of *M* for all right *R*-modules. Since right [Noetherian domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_domain) are Ore, this covers the case when *R* is a right [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) [domain](wikipedia://en/487627) (which might not be commutative).

More generally, let *M* be a module over a ring *R* and *S* be a [multiplicatively closed subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicatively_closed_subset) of *R*. An element *m* of *M* is called an *S*-torsion element if there exists an element *s* in *S* such that *s* annihilates *m*, i.e., *s* *m* = 0. In particular, one can take for *S* the set of regular elements of the ring *R* and recover the definition above.

An element *g* of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* is called a *torsion element* of the group if it has finite order, i.e., if there is a positive integer *m* such that *g*<sup>*m*</sup> = *e*, where *e* denotes the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) of the group, and *g*<sup>*m*</sup> denotes the product of *m* copies of *g*. A group is called a *[torsion (or periodic) group](wikipedia://en/3568695)* if all its elements are torsion elements, and a **torsion-free group** if its only torsion element is the identity element. Any [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) may be viewed as a module over the ring **Z** of integers, and in this case the two notions of torsion coincide.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

1. Let *M* be a [free module](wikipedia://en/326454) over any ring *R*. Then it follows immediately from the definitions that *M* is torsion-free (if the ring *R* is not a domain then torsion is considered with respect to the set *S* of non-zero-divisors of *R*). In particular, any [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) is torsion-free and any [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* is torsion-free when viewed as a module over *K*.
1. By contrast with example 1, any [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) (abelian or not) is periodic and [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042). [Burnside's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burnside's_problem), conversely, asks whether a finitely generated periodic group must be finite. The answer is "no" in general, even if the period is fixed.
1. The torsion elements of the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of a field are its [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950).
1. In the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019), **Γ** obtained from the group SL(2, **Z**) of 2×2 integer [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with unit [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) by factoring out its [center](wikipedia://en/7125), any nontrivial torsion element either has order two and is [conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugation_%28group_theory%29) to the element *S* or has order three and is conjugate to the element *ST*. In this case, torsion elements do not form a subgroup, for example, *S*·*ST* = *T*, which has infinite order.
1. The abelian group **Q**/**Z**, consisting of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) modulo 1, is periodic, i.e. every element has finite order. Analogously, the module **K**(*t*)/**K**\[*t*\] over the ring *R* = **K**\[*t*\] of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in one variable is pure torsion. Both these examples can be generalized as follows: if *R* is an integral domain and *Q* is its [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271), then *Q*/*R* is a torsion *R*-module.
1. The [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) of (**R**/**Z**, +) is (**Q**/**Z**, +) while the groups (**R**, +) and (**Z**, +) are torsion-free. The quotient of a [torsion-free abelian group](wikipedia://en/11630973) by a subgroup is torsion-free exactly when the subgroup is a [pure subgroup](wikipedia://en/5739585).
1. Consider a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) *L* acting on a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) vector space *V* over the field *K*. If we view *V* as an *K*\[*L*\]-module in the natural way, then (as a result of many things, either simply by finite-dimensionality or as a consequence of the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547)), *V* is a torsion *K*\[*L*\]-module.

:::

## Case of a principal ideal domain {#Case_of_a_principal_ideal_domain}

Suppose that *R* is a (commutative) [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) and *M* is a [finitely generated *R*-module](wikipedia://en/326430). Then the [structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/9902787) gives a detailed description of the module *M* up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). In particular, it claims that

$$M \simeq F\oplus \mathrm T(M),$$

where *F* is a free *R*-module of finite [rank](wikipedia://en/326454) (depending only on *M*) and T(*M*) is the torsion submodule of *M*. As a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865), any finitely generated torsion-free module over *R* is free. This corollary *does not* hold for more general commutative domains, even for *R* = *K*\[*x*,*y*\], the [ring of polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_polynomials) in two variables. For non-finitely generated modules, the above direct decomposition is not true. The torsion subgroup of an abelian group may not be a [direct summand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_summand) of it.

## Torsion and localization {#Torsion_and_localization}

Assume that *R* is a commutative domain and *M* is an *R*-module. Let *Q* be the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of the ring *R*. Then one can consider the *Q*-module

$$M_Q = M \otimes_R Q,$$

obtained from *M* by [extension of scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_of_scalars). Since *Q* is a field, a module over *Q* is a vector space, possibly infinite-dimensional. There is a canonical [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) of abelian groups from *M* to *M*<sub>*Q*</sub>, and the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of this homomorphism is precisely the torsion submodule T(*M*). More generally, if *S* is a multiplicatively closed subset of the ring *R*, then we may consider [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_module) of the *R*-module *M*,

$$M_S = M \otimes_R R_S,$$

which is a module over the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) *R*<sub>*S*</sub>. There is a canonical map from *M* to *M*<sub>*S*</sub>, whose kernel is precisely the *S*-torsion submodule of *M*. Thus the torsion submodule of *M* can be interpreted as the set of the elements that "vanish in the localization". The same interpretation continues to hold in the non-commutative setting for rings satisfying the [Ore condition](wikipedia://en/3100560), or more generally for any [right denominator set](wikipedia://en/3100560#Multiplicative_sets) *S* and right *R*-module *M*.

## Torsion in homological algebra {#Torsion_in_homological_algebra}

The concept of torsion plays an important role in [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410). If *M* and *N* are two modules over a commutative domain *R* (for example, two abelian groups, when *R* = **Z**), [Tor functors](wikipedia://en/856347) yield a family of *R*-modules Tor<sub>*i*</sub>(*M*,*N*). The *S*-torsion of an *R*-module *M* is canonically isomorphic to Tor<sup>*R*</sup><sub>1</sub>(*M*, *R*<sub>*S*</sub>/*R*) by the [exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence) of Tor<sup>*R*</sup><sub>\*</sub>: The [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) $0\to R\to R_S \to R_S/R \to 0$ of *R*-modules yields an exact sequence $0\to\operatorname{Tor}^R_1(M, R_S/R)\to M\to M_S$, and hence $\operatorname{Tor}^R_1(M, R_S/R)$ is the kernel of the localisation map of *M*. The symbol $\operatorname{Tor}$ denoting the [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) reflects this relation with the algebraic torsion. This same result holds for non-commutative rings as well as long as the set *S* is a [right denominator set](wikipedia://en/3100560#Multiplicative_sets).

## Abelian varieties {#Abelian_varieties}

*Image: Lattice torsion points.svg.* The 4-torsion subgroup of an elliptic curve over the complex numbers.

The torsion elements of an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) are *torsion points* or, in an older terminology, *division points*. On [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) they may be computed in terms of [division polynomials](wikipedia://en/21357757).

:::
