# Chapman–Kolmogorov equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chapman–Kolmogorov equation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in the theory of Markovian [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895) in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **Chapman–Kolmogorov equation** (CKE) is an identity relating the [joint probability distributions](wikipedia://en/879637) of different sets of coordinates on a stochastic process. The equation was derived independently by both the British mathematician [Sydney Chapman](wikipedia://en/768762) and the Russian mathematician [Andrey Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161). The CKE is prominently used in recent [variational Bayesian methods](wikipedia://en/1208480).

:::

## Mathematical description {#Mathematical_description}

Suppose that { *f*<sub>*i*</sub> } is an indexed collection of [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), that is, a stochastic process. Let

$$p_{i_1,\ldots,i_n}(f_1,\ldots,f_n)$$

be the joint probability density function of the values of the random variables *f*<sub>1</sub> to *f<sub>n</sub>*. Then, the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation is

$$p_{i_1,\ldots,i_{n-1}}(f_1,\ldots,f_{n-1})=\int_{-\infty}^{\infty}p_{i_1,\ldots,i_n}(f_1,\ldots,f_n)\,df_n$$

i.e. a straightforward [marginalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginalization_%28probability%29) over the [nuisance variable](wikipedia://en/21890593).

(Note that nothing yet has been assumed about the temporal (or any other) ordering of the random variables—the above equation applies equally to the marginalization of any of them.)

### In terms of Markov kernels {#In_terms_of_Markov_kernels}

If we consider the [Markov kernels](wikipedia://en/21180014) induced by the transitions of a [Markov process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_process), the Chapman-Kolmogorov equation can be seen as giving a way of composing the kernel, generalizing the [way stochastic matrices compose](wikipedia://en/76142988). Given a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(X,\mathcal{A})$ and a Markov kernel $k:(X,\mathcal{A})\to(X,\mathcal{A})$, the *two-step transition* kernel $k^2:(X,\mathcal{A})\to(X,\mathcal{A})$ is given by

$$k^2(A|x) = \int_X k(A|x') \, k(dx'|x)$$

for all $x\in X$ and $A\in\mathcal{A}$.[^o4] One can interpret this as a sum, over all intermediate states, of pairs of independent probabilistic transitions.

More generally, given measurable spaces $(X,\mathcal{A})$, $(Y,\mathcal{B})$ and $(Z,\mathcal{C})$, and Markov kernels $k:(X,\mathcal{A})\to(Y,\mathcal{B})$ and $h:(Y,\mathcal{B})\to(Z,\mathcal{C})$, we get a composite kernel $h\circ k:(X,\mathcal{A})\to(Z,\mathcal{C})$ by

$$(h\circ k)(C|x) = \int_Y h(C|y)\,k(dy|x)$$

for all $x\in X$ and $C\in\mathcal{C}$.

Because of this, [Markov kernels](wikipedia://en/21180014), like [stochastic matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_matrices), form a [category](wikipedia://en/75969668).

## Application to time-dilated Markov chains {#Application_to_time-dilated_Markov_chains}

When the stochastic process under consideration is [Markovian](wikipedia://en/60876), the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation is equivalent to an identity on transition densities. In the Markov chain setting, one assumes that *i*<sub>1</sub> \< ... \< *i*<sub>*n*</sub>. Then, because of the [Markov property](wikipedia://en/306422),

$$p_{i_1,\ldots,i_n}(f_1,\ldots,f_n)=p_{i_1}(f_1)p_{i_2;i_1}(f_2\mid f_1)\cdots p_{i_n;i_{n-1}}(f_n\mid f_{n-1}),$$

where the conditional probability $p_{i;j}(f_i\mid f_j)$ is the [transition probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_probability) between the times $i>j$. So, the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation takes the form

$$p_{i_3;i_1}(f_3\mid f_1)=\int_{-\infty}^\infty p_{i_3;i_2}(f_3\mid f_2)p_{i_2;i_1}(f_2\mid f_1) \, df_2.$$

Informally, this says that the probability of going from state 1 to state 3 can be found from the probabilities of going from 1 to an intermediate state 2 and then from 2 to 3, by adding up over all the possible intermediate states 2.

When the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) on the state space of a Markov chain is discrete and the Markov chain is homogeneous, the Chapman–Kolmogorov equations can be expressed in terms of (possibly infinite-dimensional) [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280), thus:

$$P(t+s)=P(t)P(s)\,$$

where *P*(*t*) is the transition matrix of jump *t*, i.e., *P*(*t*) is the matrix such that entry *(i,j)* contains the probability of the chain moving from state *i* to state *j* in *t* steps.

As a corollary, it follows that to calculate the transition matrix of jump *t*, it is sufficient to raise the transition matrix of jump one to the power of *t*, that is

$$P(t)=P^t.\,$$

## Chapman-Kolmogorov in differential form {#Chapman-Kolmogorov_in_differential_form}

The [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519) of the Chapman–Kolmogorov equation is a representation of the [master equation](wikipedia://en/840106) associated with a time-continuous [Markov process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_process) on a continuous state space. It is obtained under the assumption that the transition dynamics can be decomposed into:

- continuous transitions, corresponding to infinitesimal state increments $|x-x'|\ll 1$;
- discontinuous transitions, corresponding to finite jumps $|x-x'| = O(1)$.

[^o7]

Starting from the general master equation, the contribution of infinitesimal transitions can be expanded using the [Kramers–Moyal expansion](wikipedia://en/56616299). If this expansion is truncated at second order, while finite jumps are retained explicitly, one obtains the following differential equation:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial t} P(x,t|x_0,t_0) = & \underbrace{- \sum_i \frac{\partial}{\partial x_i} [A_i(x,t) P(x,t|x_0,t_0)]}_{\text{Drift term (continuous)}} \\[4pt] & \underbrace{+ \frac{1}{2} \sum_{i,j} \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x_i \partial x_j} [B_{ij}(x,t) P(x,t|x_0,t_0)]}_{\text{Diffusion term (continuous)}} \\[4pt] & \underbrace{+ \int \mathrm{d}x'\, [W(x|x',t) P(x',t|x_0,t_0) - W(x'|x,t) P(x,t|x_0,t_0)]}_{\text{Jump term (discontinuous)}} \end{aligned}$$

The first two terms describe the continuous component of the dynamics and correspond to a generalized [Fokker–Planck equation](wikipedia://en/166896). The integral term accounts for discontinuous transitions and has the standard gain–loss structure of a master equation.

Here:

- $A_i(x,t)$ are the drift coefficients,
- $B_{ij}(x,t)$ is the diffusion matrix (symmetric and positive semi-definite),
- $W(x|x',t)$ is the transition rate density for a jump from state $x'$ to $x$.

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

### Special cases {#Special_cases}

Several well-known evolution equations arise as special cases of the Chapman–Kolmogorov differential form, depending on which continuous contributions—drift or diffusion—are present.

#### Wiener process {#Wiener_process}

The [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984) is a continuous Markov process characterized by pure diffusion, with zero drift and no jumps. Its transition probability density satisfies the diffusion equation

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} P(x,t) = \frac{D}{2}\, \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2} P(x,t),$$

which is obtained from the Chapman–Kolmogorov differential form by setting $A(x,t)=0$ and suppressing the jump term.

#### Fokker–Planck equation {#Fokker–Planck_equation}

The [Fokker–Planck equation](wikipedia://en/166896) describes a Markov process with drift and diffusion, but without jumps. It corresponds to the Chapman–Kolmogorov differential form with nonzero drift coefficient $A(x,t)=\mu(x,t)$ and diffusion coefficient $B(x,t)=2D(x,t)$, and with the jump term suppressed:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} P(x,t) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left[\mu(x,t)\,P(x,t)\right] + \frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\!\left[D(x,t)\,P(x,t)\right].$$

#### Continuity (deterministic) equation {#Continuity_(deterministic)_equation}

The [Continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793) describes a deterministic Markov process in which the probability density is transported by a drift field and no stochastic fluctuations are present. It is obtained from the Chapman–Kolmogorov differential form by retaining only the drift term:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} P(x,t) = -\frac{\partial}{\partial x}\!\left[\mu(x,t)\,P(x,t)\right].$$

This equation expresses probability conservation along deterministic trajectories.
