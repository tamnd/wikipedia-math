# Kardar–Parisi–Zhang equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kardar–Parisi–Zhang equation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Kardar–Parisi–Zhang (KPZ) equation** is a non-linear [stochastic partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/12473239), introduced by [Mehran Kardar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mehran_Kardar), [Giorgio Parisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_Parisi), and Yi-Cheng Zhang in 1986.[^o2][^o3] It describes the temporal change of a height field $h(\vec x,t)$ with spatial coordinate $\vec x$ and time coordinate $t$:

:::

$$\frac{\partial h(\vec x,t)}{\partial t} = \nu \nabla^2 h + \frac{\lambda}{2} \left(\nabla h\right)^2 + \eta(\vec x,t) \; .$$

Here, $\eta(\vec x,t)$ is [white](wikipedia://en/46182) [Gaussian noise](wikipedia://en/1387827) with average

$$\langle \eta(\vec x,t) \rangle = 0$$

and [second moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_moment)

$$\langle \eta(\vec x,t) \eta(\vec x',t') \rangle = 2D\delta^d(\vec x-\vec x')\delta(t-t'),$$

$\nu$, $\lambda$, and $D$ are parameters of the model, and $d$ is the dimension.

In one spatial dimension, the KPZ equation corresponds to a stochastic version of [Burgers' equation](wikipedia://en/1023353) with field $u(x,t)$ via the substitution $u=-\lambda\, \partial h/\partial x$.

Via the [renormalization group](wikipedia://en/291462), the KPZ equation is conjectured to be the [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28physics%29) of many [surface growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_growth) models, such as the [Eden model](wikipedia://en/5207843), ballistic deposition, and the weakly asymmetric single step solid on solid process (SOS) model. A rigorous proof has been given by Bertini and Giacomin in the case of the SOS model.[^o4]

## KPZ universality class {#KPZ_universality_class}

Many [interacting particle systems](wikipedia://en/37809826), such as the totally [asymmetric simple exclusion process](wikipedia://en/37646664), lie in the KPZ [universality class](wikipedia://en/353890). This class is characterized by the following [critical exponents](wikipedia://en/2029677) in one spatial dimension (1 + 1 dimension): the roughness exponent $\alpha=\tfrac{1}{2}$, growth exponent $\beta=\tfrac{1}{3}$, and dynamic exponent $z=\tfrac{3}{2}$. In order to check if a growth model is within the KPZ class, one can calculate the *width* of the surface:

$$W(L,t)=\left\langle\frac1L\int_0^L \big( h(x,t)-\bar{h}(t)\big)^2 \, dx\right\rangle^{1/2},$$

where $\bar{h}(t)$ is the mean surface height at time $t$ and $L$ is the size of the system. For models within the KPZ class, the main properties of the surface $h(x,t)$ can be characterized by the [Family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fereydoon_Family)–[Vicsek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamás_Vicsek) [scaling relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_limit) of the [roughness](wikipedia://en/14294)[^o6]

$$W(L,t) \approx L^{\alpha} f(t/L^z),$$

with a scaling function $f(u)$ satisfying

$$f(u) \propto \begin{cases} u^{\beta} & \ u\ll 1 \\ 1 & \ u\gg1\end{cases}$$

In 2014, Hairer and Quastel showed that more generally, the following KPZ-like equations lie within the KPZ universality class:[^o3]

$$\frac{\partial h(\vec x,t)}{\partial t} = \nu \nabla^2 h + P\left(\nabla h\right) + \eta(\vec x,t) \; ,$$

where $P$ is any even-degree [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000).

A family of processes that are conjectured to be universal limits in the (1+1) KPZ universality class and govern the long time fluctuations are the [Airy processes](wikipedia://en/73934690) and the [KPZ fixed point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KPZ_fixed_point).

## Solving the KPZ equation {#Solving_the_KPZ_equation}

Due to the [nonlinearity](wikipedia://en/146103) in the equation and the presence of space-time [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182), solutions to the KPZ equation are known to not be [smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000) or regular, but rather '[fractal](wikipedia://en/10913)' or '[rough](wikipedia://en/38309455).' Even without the nonlinear term, the equation reduces to the [stochastic heat equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_heat_equation), whose solution is not [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) in the space variable but satisfies a [Hölder condition](wikipedia://en/4005892) with exponent less than 1/2. Thus, the nonlinear term $\left(\nabla h\right)^2$ is [ill-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ill-defined) in a classical sense.

In 2013, [Martin Hairer](wikipedia://en/26920158) made a breakthrough in solving the KPZ equation by an extension of the [Cole–Hopf transformation](wikipedia://en/73078347) and constructing approximations using [Feynman diagrams](wikipedia://en/11617).[^o8] In 2014, he was awarded the [Fields Medal](wikipedia://en/10859) for this work on the KPZ equation, along with [rough paths theory](wikipedia://en/38309455) and [regularity structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regularity_structures). There were 6 different analytic [self-similar solutions](wikipedia://en/58495269) found for the (1+1) KPZ equation with different analytic noise terms.[^o9]

## Physical derivation {#Physical_derivation}

A common, non-rigorous derivation begins with an attempt to model [surface growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_growth) during [crystallization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallization). Let $h(x, t)$ represent the height of the surface at position $x$ and time $t$. The surface is expected to evolve through time according to some variant on the [diffusion equation](wikipedia://en/645335),

$$\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial t }=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2} h(x,t)}{\partial x^2 }\text{,}$$

which acts to [smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000) the initial conditions. The diffusion equation itself cannot describe the surface evolution because it is [deterministic](wikipedia://en/522958), omitting the effects of random [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation_%28chemistry%29) and dissolution.

The simplest adaptation adds a stochastic forcing:

$$\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial t }=\frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2} h(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}+\eta(x,t),$$

where $\eta$ is [Gaussian](wikipedia://en/1387827) [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182) with [mean](wikipedia://en/60167) zero and [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059)

$$E[\eta(x,t)\eta(x',t')]=\delta(x-x')\delta(t-t')\text{.}$$

That equation is the Edwards–Wilkinson (EW) equation, also known descriptively as the [stochastic heat equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_heat_equation) with [additive noise](wikipedia://en/202097). The EW equation is mathematically tractable, being [linear](wikipedia://en/17570); and solvable through [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659). It also exhibits an important symmetry retained in the final KPZ equation: if $h$ solves the EW equation when $\eta$ has mean 0, then $h+mt$ also solves the EW equation when $\eta$ has mean $m$. Thus any constant terms can be added or removed from the right-hand side without modifying physical applicability. However, the EW equation implies that fluctuations in $h$ are Gaussian, which is contrary to experiment. At least one term is missing for a physically-accurate model.[^o11]

The key observation of Kardar, Parisi, and Zhang (KPZ)[^o12] was that the missing term depends on surface's local slope, $\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial x }$ The crystal surface grows [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) to the (varying) surface, but $h$ is measured along a fixed height axis. Consequently regions with large $\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial x }$ have longer surface length, and see more deposition.

One might hope that surface growth is accurately modeled through an equation of the form

$$\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial t }=F\left(\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial x }\right)+ \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2} h(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}+\eta(x,t)$$

for some function $F$. If $F$ is solely proportional to local arclength, then $F$ should take (up to an additive constant, by the EW symmetry mentioned above) the value

$$F(s)=\lambda\sqrt{1+s^2}\text{,}$$

for some constant $\lambda$. However, that choice of $F$ gives an intractable equation.[^o13]

The working physicist is now tempted by habit to simply expand $F$ as a [Taylor series about $0$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_series). This Taylor expansion cannot be mathematically justified. The function $\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial x }$ is large, and has very large variation: there is no convenient point to Taylor-expand around.[^n1] However, as discussed above, the KPZ equation is universal, in the sense that the same functions solve the KPZ equation for any $F(s)$ that is a nonconstant polynomial in $s^2$. For example, if $F$ were polynomial, then one could assume it quadratic without loss of generality.

With less rigor, one can compute that the [renormalization group](wikipedia://en/291462) flow has a single fixpoint: namely, $F(s)=F''(0)s^2$. Thus if there is an interesting model to be found, then it must behave as though $F(s)=F''(0)s^2$.[^o15] The KPZ equation's (extensive!) mathematical analysis is devoted to showing that the resulting equation is well-defined: ordinarily, [tempered distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempered_distribution) have no square, but the divergence associated with the KPZ model can be mollified after applying the Hopf-Cole transformation.[^n2][^o17]

For the sake of intuition, we will assume that

$$F(s)=F'(0)+F'(0)s+\frac{1}{2}F''(0)s^{2}\text{.}$$

The first term can be removed from the equation by the time-shift symmetry alluded to above in the EW equation: if $h(x,t)$ solves the KPZ equation, then $\tilde{h}(x,t):=h(x,t)-\lambda F(0)t$ solves

$$\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial t }=-\lambda F(0)+ \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2} h(x,t)}{\partial x^{2} }+\eta(x,t).$$

The second can be removed from the equation by a constant velocity shift of coordinates, since if $h(x,t)$ solves the KPZ equation, then $\tilde{h}(x,t):=h(x- \lambda F'(0)t,t-\lambda F'(0)x)$ solves

$$\frac{\partial \tilde{h}(x,t)}{\partial t }=-\lambda F'(0)\frac{\partial \tilde{h}(x,t)}{\partial x }+ \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2} \tilde{h}(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}+\eta(x,t).$$

The quadratic term is the first nontrivial contribution, and it is the only one kept. We arrive at the KPZ equation

$$\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial t }=-\lambda\left(\frac{\partial h(x,t)}{\partial x }\right)^{2} + \frac{1}{2}\frac{\partial^{2} h(x,t)}{\partial x^{2}}+\eta(x,t).$$
