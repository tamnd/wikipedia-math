# Spherical astronomy

*Image: Positional astronomy.svg.* Diagram of several terms in positional astronomy

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spherical astronomy"}

**Spherical astronomy**, or **positional astronomy**, is a branch of [observational astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy) used to locate [astronomical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_object) on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239), as seen at a particular date, time, and location on [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228). It relies on the mathematical methods of [spherical trigonometry](wikipedia://en/650405) and the measurements of [astrometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrometry).

:::

This is the oldest branch of astronomy and dates back to [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history). Observations of celestial objects have been, and continue to be, important for religious and [astrological](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) purposes, as well as for [timekeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timekeeping) and [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_navigation). The science of actually measuring positions of celestial objects in the sky is known as astrometry.

The primary elements of spherical astronomy are [celestial coordinate systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_coordinate_system) and time. The coordinates of objects on the sky are listed using the [equatorial coordinate system](wikipedia://en/48384), which is based on the projection of Earth's [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) onto the celestial sphere. The position of an object in this system is given in terms of [right ascension](wikipedia://en/26073) (α) and [declination](wikipedia://en/8612) (δ). The latitude and local time can then be used to derive the position of the object in the [horizontal coordinate system](wikipedia://en/48386), consisting of the [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude) and [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487).

The coordinates of celestial objects such as [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star) and [galaxies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galaxy) are tabulated in a [star catalog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_catalog), which gives the position for a particular year. However, the combined effects of [axial precession](wikipedia://en/72576) and [nutation](wikipedia://en/22094) will cause the coordinates to change slightly over time. The effects of these changes in Earth's motion are compensated by the periodic publication of revised catalogs.

To determine the position of the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) and [planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planet), an astronomical [ephemeris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ephemeris) (a table of values that gives the positions of astronomical objects in the sky at a given time) is used, which can then be converted into suitable real-world coordinates.

The [unaided human eye](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naked_eye) can perceive about 6,000 stars, of which about half are below the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) at any one time. On modern [star charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart), the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) is divided into [88 constellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IAU_designated_constellations). Every star lies within a [constellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constellation). Constellations are useful for navigation. [Polaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris) lies nearly due north to an observer in the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere). This [pole star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_star) is always at [a position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_pole) nearly [directly above](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith) the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole).

## Positional phenomena {#Positional_phenomena}

- Planets which are in [conjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_conjunction) form a line which passes through the center of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System).
- The [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) is the [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) which contains the orbit of a planet, usually in reference to [Earth](wikipedia://en/9228).
- [Elongation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elongation_%28astronomy%29) refers to the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) formed by a planet, with respect to the system's center and a viewing [point](wikipedia://en/593693).
  - A [quadrature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrature_%28astronomy%29) occurs when the position of a body (moon or planet) is such that its elongation is 90° or 270°; i.e. the body-earth-sun angle is 90°
- [Superior planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferior_and_superior_planets) have a larger [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) than Earth's, while the [inferior planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferior_and_superior_planets) (Mercury and Venus) orbit the Sun inside Earth's orbit.
- A [transit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_%28astronomy%29) may occur when an inferior planet passes through a point of conjunction.
