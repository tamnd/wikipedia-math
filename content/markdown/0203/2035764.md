# Abstract analytic number theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Abstract analytic number theory"}

**Abstract analytic number theory** is a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) which takes the ideas and techniques of classical [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513) and applies them to a variety of different mathematical fields. The classical [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692) serves as a prototypical example, and the emphasis is on abstract [asymptotic distribution results](wikipedia://en/641995). The theory was invented and developed by mathematicians such as [John Knopfmacher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Knopfmacher) and [Arne Beurling](wikipedia://en/286530) in the twentieth century.

:::

## Arithmetic semigroups {#Arithmetic_semigroups}

The fundamental notion involved is that of an **arithmetic semigroup**, which is a [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) *G* satisfying the following properties:

- There exists a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) (finite or countably infinite) *P* of *G*, such that every element *a* ≠ 1 in *G* has a unique factorisation of the form

$$a = p_1^{\alpha_1} p_2^{\alpha_2} \cdots p_r^{\alpha_r}$$

where the *p*<sub>*i*</sub> are distinct elements of *P*, the α<sub>*i*</sub> are positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), *r* may depend on *a*, and two factorisations are considered the same if they differ only by the order of the factors indicated. The elements of *P* are called the *primes* of *G*.

- There exists a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued *norm mapping* $|\mbox{ }|$ on *G* such that
  1. $$|1| = 1$$
  1. $$|p| > 1 \mbox{ for all } p \in P$$
  1. $$|ab| = |a| |b| \mbox{ for all } a,b \in G$$
  1. The total number $N_G(x)$ of elements $a \in G$ of norm $|a| \leq x$ is finite, for each real $x > 0$.

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Additive number system"}

### Additive number systems {#Additive_number_systems}

An **additive number system** is an arithmetic semigroup in which the underlying monoid *G* is [free abelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_commutative_monoid). The norm function may be written additively.[^o5]

If the norm is integer-valued, we associate counting functions *a*(*n*) and *p*(*n*) with *G* where *p* counts the number of elements of *P* of norm *n*, and *a* counts the number of elements of *G* of norm *n*. We let *A*(*x*) and *P*(*x*) be the corresponding [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012). We have the *fundamental identity*[^o6]

$$A(x) = \sum_n a(n) x^n = \prod_n (1-x^n)^{-p(n)} \$$

which formally encodes the unique expression of each element of *G* as a product of elements of *P*. The *radius of convergence* of *G* is the [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476) of the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) *A*(*x*).[^o7]

The fundamental identity has the alternative form[^o8]

$$A(x) = \exp\left({ \sum_{m \ge 1} \frac{P(x^m)}{m} }\right) \ .$$

## Examples {#Examples}

- The prototypical example of an arithmetic semigroup is the multiplicative [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) of [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_and_positive_numbers) [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *G* = **Z**<sup>\+</sup> = {1, 2, 3, ...}, with subset of rational [primes](wikipedia://en/23666) *P* = {2, 3, 5, ...}. Here, the norm of an integer is simply $|n| = n$, so that $N_G(x) = \lfloor x \rfloor$, the [greatest integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function) not exceeding *x*.
- If *K* is an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822), i.e. a finite extension of the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) **Q**, then the set *G* of all nonzero [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) in the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of integers *O<sub>K</sub>* of *K* forms an arithmetic semigroup with identity element *O<sub>K</sub>* and the norm of an ideal *I* is given by the cardinality of the quotient ring *O<sub>K</sub>*/*I*. In this case, the appropriate generalisation of the prime number theorem is the *[Landau prime ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/3228360)*, which describes the asymptotic distribution of the ideals in *O<sub>K</sub>*.
- Various *arithmetical categories* which satisfy a theorem of Krull-Schmidt type can be considered. In all these cases, the elements of *G* are isomorphism classes in an appropriate [category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_%28category_theory%29), and *P* consists of all isomorphism classes of *indecomposable* objects, i.e. objects which cannot be decomposed as a direct product of nonzero objects. Some typical examples are the following.
  - The category of all [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) under the usual direct product operation and norm mapping $|A| = \operatorname{card}(A).$ The indecomposable objects are the [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) of prime power order.
  - The category of all [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [simply-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply-connected) globally symmetric Riemannian [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) under the Riemannian product of manifolds and norm mapping $|M| = c^{\dim M},$ where *c* > 1 is fixed, and dim *M* denotes the manifold dimension of *M*. The indecomposable objects are the compact simply-connected *irreducible* symmetric spaces.
  - The category of all [pseudometrisable](wikipedia://en/68503) finite [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) under the [topological sum](wikipedia://en/1564380) and norm mapping $|X| = 2^{\operatorname{card}(X)}.$ The indecomposable objects are the [connected spaces](wikipedia://en/6233).

## Methods and techniques {#Methods_and_techniques}

The use of [arithmetic functions](wikipedia://en/3170) and zeta functions is extensive. The idea is to extend the various arguments and techniques of arithmetic functions and zeta functions in classical analytic number theory to the context of an arbitrary arithmetic semigroup which may satisfy one or more additional axioms. Such a typical axiom is the following, usually called "Axiom A" in the literature:

- *Axiom A*. There exist positive constants *A* and $\delta$, and a constant $\nu$ with $0 \le \nu < \delta$, such that $N_G(x) = Ax^{\delta} + O(x^{\nu}) \mbox { as } x \rightarrow \infty.$[^o13]

For any arithmetic semigroup which satisfies Axiom *A*, we have the following *abstract prime number theorem*:[^o14]

$$\pi_G(x) \sim \frac{x^{\delta}}{\delta \log x} \mbox { as } x \rightarrow \infty$$

where π<sub>*G*</sub>(*x*) = total number of elements *p* in *P* of norm |*p*| ≤ *x*.

::: {#o16 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Arithmetical formation"}

### Arithmetical formation {#Arithmetical_formation}

The notion of **arithmetical formation** provides a generalisation of the [ideal class group](wikipedia://en/150907) in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705) and allows for abstract asymptotic distribution results under constraints. In the case of number fields, for example, this is [Chebotarev's density theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebotarev's_density_theorem). An arithmetical formation is an arithmetic semigroup *G* with an equivalence relation ≡ such that the quotient *G*/≡ is a finite abelian group *A*. This quotient is the *class group* of the formation and the equivalence classes are generalised arithmetic progressions or generalised ideal classes. If χ is a [character](wikipedia://en/1384568) of *A* then we can define a [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258)

$$\sum_{g \in G} \chi([g]) |g|^{-s}$$

which provides a notion of zeta function for arithmetical semigroup.[^o17]
