# Covering set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Covering set"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **covering set** for a [sequence of integers](wikipedia://en/51421) refers to a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) such that *every* term in the [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) is [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisible) by *at least one* member of the set.[^o2] The term "covering set" is used only in conjunction with sequences possessing [exponential growth](wikipedia://en/191933).

:::

## Sierpinski and Riesel numbers {#Sierpinski_and_Riesel_numbers}

The use of the term "covering set" is related to [Sierpinski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_number) and [Riesel numbers](wikipedia://en/321963). These are [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_and_odd_numbers) [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) $k$ for which the formula $k\,2^{n} +\,1$ (Sierpinski number) or $k\,2^{n} -\,1$ (Riesel number) produces no prime numbers.[^o4] Since 1960 it has been known that there exists an [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824) number of both Sierpinski and Riesel numbers (as solutions to families of [congruences](wikipedia://en/48167) based upon the set {3, 5, 17, 257, 641, 65537, 6700417} but, because there are an infinitude of numbers of the form $k\,2^{n} +\,1$ or $k\,2^{n} -\,1$ for any $k$, one can only [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) $k$ to be a Sierpinski or Riesel number through showing that *every* term in the sequence $k\,2^{n} +\,1$ or $k\,2^{n} -\,1$ is divisible by one of the prime numbers of a covering set.

These covering sets form from prime numbers that in [base 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_2) have short periods. To achieve a complete covering set, [Wacław Sierpiński](wikipedia://en/34122) showed that a sequence can repeat no more frequently than every 24 numbers. A repeat every 24 numbers give the covering set {3, 5, 7, 13, 17, 241}, while a repeat every 36 terms can give several covering sets: {3, 5, 7, 13, 19, 37, 73}; {3, 5, 7, 13, 19, 37, 109}; {3, 5, 7, 13, 19, 73, 109} and {3, 5, 7, 13, 37, 73, 109}.[^o5]

Riesel numbers have the same covering sets as Sierpinski numbers.

Covering sets are also used to prove the existence of composite [generalized Fibonacci sequences](wikipedia://en/8006956#Fibonacci_integer_sequences) with first two terms [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) ([primefree sequence](wikipedia://en/2716293)), such as the sequence starting with 20615674205555510 and 3794765361567513.

The concept of a covering set can easily be generalised to other sequences which turn out to be much simpler.

In the following examples + is used as it is in [regular expressions](wikipedia://en/25717) to mean 1 or more. For example, 91<sup>\+</sup>3 means the set {913, 9113, 91113, 911113, …}.

An example are the following eight sequences:

- (29·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 191) / 9 or 32<sup>\+</sup>01
- (37·10<sup>*n*</sup> + 359) / 9 or 41<sup>\+</sup>51
- (46·10<sup>*n*</sup> + 629) / 9 or 51<sup>\+</sup>81
- (59·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 293) / 9 or 65<sup>\+</sup>23
- (82·10<sup>*n*</sup> + 17) / 9 or 91<sup>\+</sup>3
- (85·10<sup>*n*</sup> + 41) / 9 or 94<sup>\+</sup>9
- (86·10<sup>*n*</sup> + 31) / 9 or 95<sup>\+</sup>9
- (89·10<sup>*n*</sup> + 593) / 9 or 98<sup>\+</sup>23

In each case, every term is divisible by one of the primes from the set {3, 7, 11, 13}.[^o11] These primes can be said to form a covering set exactly analogous to Sierpinski and Riesel numbers.[^o12] The covering set {3, 7, 11, 37} is found for several similar sequences,[^o12] including:

- (38·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 137) / 9 or 42<sup>\+</sup>07
- (4·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 337) / 9 or 4<sup>\+</sup>07
- (73·10<sup>*n*</sup> + 359) / 9 or 81<sup>\+</sup>51
- 9175·10<sup>*n*</sup> + 1 or 91750<sup>\+</sup>1
- 10176·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 1 or 101759<sup>\+</sup>
- (334·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 1)/9 or 371<sup>\+</sup>
- (12211·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 1)/3 or 40703<sup>\+</sup>
- (8026·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 7)/9 or 8917<sup>\+</sup>

Also for bases other than 10:

- 521·12<sup>*n*</sup> + 1 or 3750<sup>\+</sup>1 in [duodecimal](wikipedia://en/8400)
- (1288·12<sup>*n*</sup> − 1)/11 or 991<sup>\+</sup> in duodecimal
- (4517·12<sup>*n*</sup> − 7)/11 or 2X27<sup>\+</sup> in duodecimal
- 376·12<sup>*n*</sup> − 1 or 273E<sup>\+</sup> in duodecimal

The covering set of them is {5, 13, 29}

An even simpler case can be found in the sequence:

- (76·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 67) / 99 (***n* must be odd**) or (76)<sup>\+</sup>7 (Sequence: 7, 767, 76767, 7676767, 767676767 etc.)

Here, it can be shown that if:

- w is of form $3k$ ($n = 6k + 1$): (76)<sup>\+</sup>7 is divisible by 7
- w is of form $3k + 1$ ($n = 6k + 3$): (76)<sup>\+</sup>7 is divisible by 13
- w is of form $3k + 2$ ($n = 6k + 5$): (76)<sup>\+</sup>7 is divisible by 3

Thus we have a covering set with only three primes {3, 7, 13}.[^o13] This is only possible because the sequence gives integer terms *only for odd n*.

A covering set also occurs in the sequence:

- (343·10<sup>*n*</sup> − 1) / 9 or 381<sup>\+</sup>.

Here, it can be shown that:

- If $n = 3k + 1$, then $(343\cdot10^{n} -\,1)\,/\,9$ is divisible by 3.
- If $n = 3k + 2$, then $(343\cdot10^{n} -\,1)\,/\,9$ is divisible by 37.
- If $n = 3k$, then $(343\cdot10^{n} -\,1)\,/\,9$ algebraically factors as $((7\cdot10^{k} -\,1)\,/\,3)\cdot((49\cdot10^{2k} + 7\cdot10^{k} +\,1)\,/\,3)$.

Since $(7\cdot10^{k} -\,1)\,/\,3$ can be written as 23<sup>\+</sup>, for the sequence 381<sup>\+</sup>, we have a covering set of {3, 37, 23<sup>\+</sup>} – a covering set with *infinitely many* terms.[^o12]

The status for (343×10<sup>*n*</sup> − 1)/9 is like that for 3511808×63<sup>*n*</sup> + 1:

- If $n = 3k + 1$, then $3511808\cdot63^{n} +\,1$ is divisible by 37.
- If $n = 3k + 2$, then $3511808\cdot63^{n} +\,1$ is divisible by 109.
- If $n = 3k$, then $3511808\cdot63^{n} +\,1$ algebraically factors as $(152\cdot63^{k} +\,1)\cdot(23104\cdot63^{2k} - 152\cdot63^{k} +\,1)$

Thus we have a covering of {37, 109, 152×63 + 1, 152×63<sup>2</sup> + 1, 152×63<sup>3</sup> + 1, ...} or {37, 109, 2Q0<sup>\+</sup>1 in base 63} – a covering set with *infinitely many* terms.

A more simple example is 4×9<sup>*n*</sup> − 1, it is equal to (2×3<sup>*n*</sup> − 1) × (2×3<sup>*n*</sup> + 1), thus its covering sets are {5, 17, 53, 161, 485, ...} and {7, 19, 55, 163, 487, ...}, more generally, if *k* and *b* are both *r*-th powers for an odd *r* > 1, then *k*×*b*<sup>*n*</sup> + 1 cannot be prime, and if *k* and *b* are both *r*-th powers for an *r* > 1 then *k*×*b*<sup>*n*</sup> − 1 cannot be prime.

Another example is 1369×30<sup>*n*</sup> − 1, its covering is {7, 13, 19, 37×30<sup>*k*</sup> − 1 (*k* = 1, 2, 3, ...)}
