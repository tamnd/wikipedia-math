# Division algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Division algebra"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **division algebra** is, roughly speaking, an [algebra over a field](wikipedia://en/191788) in which [division](wikipedia://en/53696), except by zero, is always possible. The multiplication operation of a division algebra is not assumed to be necessarily either commutative or associative.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Formally, suppose that *D* is a [non-zero](wikipedia://en/7455889) [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603). Then *D* is a *division algebra* if, for every element *a* in *D* and every non-zero element *b* in *D*, there exists precisely one element *x* in *D* with *a* = *bx* and precisely one element *y* in *D* such that *a* = *yb*.

For [associative algebras](wikipedia://en/2112), the definition can be simplified as follows: a non-zero associative algebra over a field is a division algebra [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it has a multiplicative [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) 1 and every non-zero element *a* has a multiplicative inverse, that is, an element *x* with *ax* = *xa* = 1.

:::

## Associative division algebras {#Associative_division_algebras}

The best-known examples of associative division algebras are the finite-dimensional real ones (that is, algebras over the field **R** of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) that are finite-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over **R**). The [Frobenius theorem](wikipedia://en/3966889) states that [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) there are three such algebras: the reals themselves (dimension 1), the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) (dimension 2), and the [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions) (dimension 4).

[Wedderburn's little theorem](wikipedia://en/7132002) states that if *D* is a finite division algebra, then *D* is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615).[^n1]

Over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) *K* (such as the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) **C**), there are no finite-dimensional associative division algebras other than *K* itself.[^n2]

Associative division algebras have no nonzero [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441). A finite-dimensional [unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) (over any field) is a division algebra if and only if it has no nonzero zero divisors. Indeed, if $a$ is a member of a finite dimensional associative algebra $A$, then having no zero divisors implies that the linear operator $L_a = x \mapsto ax : A\to A$ is injective, and therefore surjective, i.e., $L_ax = 1$ has a solution.[^o7]

Whenever *A* is an associative [unital algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *F* and *S* is a [simple module](wikipedia://en/29430) over *A*, then the [endomorphism ring](wikipedia://en/59623) of *S* is a division algebra over *F*; every associative division algebra over *F* arises in this fashion.

The [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) of an associative division algebra *D* over the field *K* is a field containing *K*. The dimension of such an algebra over its center, if finite, is a [perfect square](wikipedia://en/213919): it is equal to the square of the dimension of a maximal subfield of *D* over the center. Given a field *F*, the [Brauer equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauer_equivalence) classes of simple (contains only trivial two-sided ideals) associative division algebras whose center is *F* and which are finite-dimensional over *F* can be turned into a group, the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) of the field *F*.

One way to construct finite-dimensional associative division algebras over arbitrary fields is given by the [quaternion algebras](wikipedia://en/2776890) (see also *[Quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440)*).

For infinite-dimensional associative division algebras, the most important cases are those where the space has some reasonable [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). See for example *[Normed division algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_division_algebra)* and *[Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665)*.

## Not necessarily associative division algebras {#Not_necessarily_associative_division_algebras}

If the division algebra is not assumed to be associative, usually some weaker condition (such as [alternativity](wikipedia://en/186050) or [power associativity](wikipedia://en/25054)) is imposed instead. See *[Algebra over a field](wikipedia://en/191788)* for a list of such conditions.

Over the reals there are (up to isomorphism) only two unitary [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) finite-dimensional division algebras: the reals themselves, and the complex numbers. These are of course both associative. For a non-associative example, consider the complex numbers with multiplication defined by taking the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of the usual multiplication:

*a* ∗ *b* = $\overline{ab}$.

[This](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Example_of_a_non-associative_algebra) is a commutative, non-associative division algebra of dimension 2 over the reals, and has no unit element. There are infinitely many other non-isomorphic commutative, non-associative, finite-dimensional real divisional algebras, but they all have dimension 2.

In fact, every finite-dimensional real commutative division algebra is either 1- or 2-dimensional. This is known as [Hopf's](wikipedia://en/543082) theorem, and was proved in 1940. The proof uses methods from [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). Although a later proof was found using [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), no direct algebraic proof is known. The [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414) is a corollary of Hopf's theorem.

Dropping the requirement of commutativity, Hopf generalized his result: Any finite-dimensional real division algebra must have dimension a power of 2.

Later work showed that in fact, any finite-dimensional real division algebra must be of dimension 1, 2, 4, or 8. This was independently proved by [Michel Kervaire](wikipedia://en/13644049) and [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) in 1958, again using techniques of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), in particular [K-theory](wikipedia://en/246748). [Adolf Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690) had shown in 1898 that the identity *q*$\overline{q}$ = sum of squares held only for dimensions 1, 2, 4 and 8.[^n3] (See *[Hurwitz's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurwitz's_theorem_%28normed_division_algebras%29)*.) The challenge of constructing a division algebra of three dimensions was tackled by several early mathematicians. [Kenneth O. May](wikipedia://en/719577) surveyed these attempts in 1966.[^n4]

Any real finite-dimensional division algebra over the reals must be

- isomorphic to **R** or **C** if unitary and commutative (equivalently: associative and commutative)
- isomorphic to the quaternions if noncommutative but associative
- isomorphic to the [octonions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octonions) if non-associative but [alternative](wikipedia://en/3160).

The following is known about the dimension of a finite-dimensional division algebra *A* over a field *K*:

- dim *A* = 1 if *K* is [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed),
- dim *A* = 1, 2, 4 or 8 if *K* is [real closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_closed), and
- If *K* is neither algebraically nor real closed, then there are infinitely many dimensions in which there exist division algebras over *K*.

We may say an algebra *A* **has multiplicative inverses** if for any nonzero *a* ∈ *A* there is an element *a*<sup>−1</sup> ∈ *A* with *aa*<sup>−1</sup> = *a*<sup>−1</sup>*a* = 1. An associative algebra has multiplicative inverses if and only if it is a division algebra. However, this fails for nonassociative algebras. The [sedenions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sedenions) are a nonassociative algebra over the real numbers that has multiplicative inverses, but is not a division algebra. On the other hand, we can construct a division algebra without multiplicative inverses by taking the quaternions and modifying the product, setting <sup>2</sup> = −1 + *εj* for some small nonzero real number *ε* while leaving the rest of the multiplication table unchanged. The element *i* then has both right and left inverses, but they are not equal.
