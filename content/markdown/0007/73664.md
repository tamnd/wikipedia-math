# Astrolabe

*Image: Khalili Collection Islamic Art sci 0430 back.jpg.* North African, 9th century ce, planispheric astrolabe. [Khalili Collection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Khalili_Collections).

*Image: Iranian Astrolabe 14.jpg.* A modern astrolabe made in 2013, in [Tabriz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabriz), Iran.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Astrolabe"}

An **astrolabe** (grc; ٱلأَسْطُرلاب; ستاره‌یاب) is an [astronomical instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_instrument) dating to ancient times. It serves as a [star chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart) and [physical model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Model#Physical_model) of the visible [half-dome of the sky](wikipedia://en/48239). Its various functions also make it an elaborate [inclinometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclinometer) and an [analog calculation device](wikipedia://en/2428) capable of working out several kinds of problems in astronomy. In its simplest form it is a metal disc with a pattern of wires, cutouts, and perforations that allows a user to calculate astronomical positions precisely. It is able to measure the [altitude](wikipedia://en/48386) above the horizon of a celestial body, day or night; it can be used to identify stars or planets, to determine local [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) given [local time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_time) (and vice versa), to survey, or to [triangulate](wikipedia://en/207174). It was used in [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity), the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire), the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age), the European [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) and the [Age of Discovery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Discovery) for all these purposes.

:::

The astrolabe, which is a precursor to the [sextant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextant),[^o2] is effective for determining latitude on land or calm seas. Although it is less reliable on the heaving deck of a ship in rough seas, the [mariner's astrolabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner's_astrolabe) was developed to solve that problem.

::: {#o4 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Lot-stoffler-johannes-1452-1531-elucidatio-fabricae-usuque-astrolabii-6069643.jpg.* 16th-century woodcut of the measurement of a building's height with an astrolabe

The 10th century [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) [ʿAbd al-Raḥmān al-Ṣūfī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ʿAbd_al-Raḥmān_al-Ṣūfī) wrote a massive text of 386 chapters on the astrolabe, which reportedly described more than 1,000 applications for the astrolabe's various functions.[^o5] These ranged from the astrological, the astronomical and the religious, to navigation, seasonal and daily time-keeping, and tide tables. At the time of their use, astrology was widely considered as much of a serious science as astronomy, and study of the two went hand-in-hand. The astronomical interest varied between folk astronomy (of the pre-Islamic tradition in Arabia) which was concerned with celestial and seasonal observations, and mathematical astronomy, which would inform intellectual practices and precise calculations based on astronomical observations. In regard to the astrolabe's religious function, the demands of Islamic prayer times were to be astronomically determined to ensure precise daily timings, and the [qibla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qibla), the direction of [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca) towards which Muslims must pray, could also be determined by this device. In addition to this, the [lunar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_calendar) that was informed by the calculations of the astrolabe was of great significance to the religion of Islam, given that it determines the dates of important religious observances such as [Ramadan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramadan).[^n1]

:::

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The *[Oxford English Dictionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_English_Dictionary)* gives the translation "star-taker" for the English word *astrolabe* and traces it through medieval Latin to the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) word $\text{ἀ}\sigma\tau\rho o\lambda\text{ά}\beta o\varsigma$: grc,[^o8][^o9] from $\text{ἄ}\sigma\tau\rho o\nu$: grc "star", and $\lambda\alpha\mu\beta\text{ά}\nu\varepsilon\iota\nu$: grc "to take".[^o10]

In the medieval Islamic world the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic) word ar (i.e., astrolabe) was given various etymologies. In Arabic texts, the word is translated as ar (آخِذُ ٱلنُّجُومْ, star-taker) – a direct translation of the Greek word.[^n2]

[Al-Biruni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Biruni) quotes and criticises medieval scientist [Hamza al-Isfahani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamza_al-Isfahani), who stated:[^n2]

"*asturlab* is an Arabisation of this Persian phrase" (fa, meaning "taker of the stars").[^n3]

In [medieval Islamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_Islam) sources, there is also a [folk etymology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folk_etymology) of the word as "lines of lab", where "Lab" refers to a certain son of [Idris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idris_%28prophet%29) ([Enoch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enoch)). This etymology is mentioned by a 10th century scientist named [al-Qummi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Ibn_Ibrahim_Qomi) but rejected by [al-Khwarizmi](wikipedia://en/649861).[^n4]

## History {#History}

### Ancient era {#Ancient_era}

An astrolabe is essentially a plane (two-dimensional) version of an [armillary sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillary_sphere), which had already been invented in the [Hellenistic period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_period) and had probably been used by [Hipparchus](wikipedia://en/13600) to produce his star catalogue. [Theon of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Alexandria) (c. 335–405) wrote a detailed treatise on the astrolabe.[^n5] The invention of the plane astrolabe is sometimes wrongly attributed to Theon's daughter [Hypatia](wikipedia://en/38375) (born c. 350–370; died 415 ce),[^o18][^o19][^o20][^o21] but it is known to have been used much earlier.[^o19][^o20][^o21] The misattribution comes from a misinterpretation of a statement in a letter written by Hypatia's pupil [Synesius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synesius) (c. 373–414),[^o19][^o20][^o21] which mentions that Hypatia had taught him how to construct a plane astrolabe, but does not say that she invented it.[^o19][^o20][^o21] Lewis argues that [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979) used an astrolabe to make the astronomical observations recorded in the *[Tetrabiblos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrabiblos)*.[^n5] However, [Emilie Savage-Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emilie_Savage-Smith) notes

"there is no convincing evidence that Ptolemy or any of his predecessors knew about the planispheric astrolabe".[^o23]

*Image: A reconstruction of the spherical astrolabe of Ptolemy, Kotsanas Museum of Ancient Greek Technology.jpg.* A modern reconstruction of the spherical astrolabe of [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979), 2nd C.E., in [Kotsanas Museum of Ancient Greek Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_of_Ancient_Greek_Technology), Athens.

In chapter 5.1 of the *[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)*, Ptolemy describes the construction of an [armillary sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillary_sphere), and it is usually assumed that this was the instrument he used.

Astrolabes continued to be used in the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire). Christian philosopher [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus) wrote a treatise (c. 550) on the astrolabe in Greek, which is the earliest extant treatise on the instrument.[^o24] [Mesopotamian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesopotamian) bishop [Severus Sebokht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Severus_Sebokht) also wrote a treatise on the astrolabe in the [Syriac language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syriac_language) during the mid-7th century. Sebokht refers to the astrolabe as being made of brass in the introduction of his treatise, indicating that metal astrolabes were known in the Christian East well before they were developed in the Islamic world or in the Latin West.[^o25]

::: {#o27 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Ibn al-Sarraj"}

### Medieval era {#Medieval_era}

Astrolabes were further developed in the [medieval Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age), where [Muslim astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_in_medieval_Islam) introduced angular scales to the design,[^o28] adding circles indicating [azimuths](wikipedia://en/47487) on the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon).[^o29] It was widely used throughout the Muslim world, chiefly as an [aid to navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography_in_medieval_Islam) and as a way of finding the [Qibla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qibla), the direction of [Mecca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mecca). Eighth-century [mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam) [Muhammad al-Fazari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_al-Fazari) is the first person credited with building the astrolabe in the Islamic world.[^o30] The earliest Arabic treatise on astrolabes was composed sometime around 815 CE.[^o31]

The mathematical background was established by Muslim astronomer [Albatenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Jābir_al-Harrānī_al-Battānī) in his treatise *Kitab az-Zij* (c. 920 ce), which was translated into Latin by [Plato Tiburtinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato_Tiburtinus) (*De Motu Stellarum*). The earliest surviving astrolabe is dated [AH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar) 315 (927–928 ce). In the Islamic world, astrolabes were used to find the times of sunrise and the rising of fixed stars, to help schedule morning prayers ([salat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salat)). In the 10th century, [al-Sufi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Sufi) first described over 1,000 different uses of an astrolabe, in areas as diverse as [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_astrology), [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mariner's_astrolabe), [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying), timekeeping, prayer, [Salat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salat), [Qibla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qibla), etc.[^o32][^o33]

*Image: Astrolabium.jpg.* An Arab astrolabe from 1208

The [spherical astrolabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_astrolabe) was a variation of both the astrolabe and the [armillary sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillary_sphere), invented during the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) by astronomers and [inventors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inventions_in_the_Muslim_world) in the Islamic world. The earliest description of the spherical astrolabe dates to [Al-Nayrizi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Nayrizi) ([fl.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floruit) 892–902). In the 12th century, [Sharaf al-Dīn al-Tūsī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharaf_al-Dīn_al-Tūsī) invented the *linear astrolabe*, sometimes called the "staff of al-Tusi", which was

"a simple wooden rod with graduated markings, but without sights. It was furnished with a plumb line and a double chord for making angular measurements and bore a perforated pointer". The geared mechanical astrolabe was invented by Abi Bakr of [Isfahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isfahan) in 1235.[^o35]

The first known metal astrolabe in Western Europe is the [Destombes astrolabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barcelona_astrolabe) made from brass in the eleventh century in Portugal.[^o36][^o43]p. 140 Metal astrolabes avoided the warping that large wooden ones were prone to, allowing the construction of larger and therefore more accurate instruments. Metal astrolabes were heavier than wooden instruments of the same size, making it difficult to use them in navigation.[^o37]

*Image: Spherical astrolabe.jpg.* Spherical astrolabe

*Image: Euclid and Herman - Ashmole MS 304, fol 2.jpg.* A depiction of [Hermann of Reichenau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_of_Reichenau) with an astrolabe in a 13th-century manuscript by [Matthew Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Paris)

[Herman Contractus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herman_Contractus) of [Reichenau Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reichenau_Abbey), examined the use of the astrolabe in *Mensura Astrolai* during the 11th century.[^o38]p. 72 [Peter of Maricourt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_of_Maricourt) wrote a treatise on the construction and use of a universal astrolabe in the last half of the 13th century entitled *Nova compositio astrolabii particularis*. Universal astrolabes can be found at the [History of Science Museum, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Science_Museum,_Oxford).[^o39] David A. King, historian of Islamic instrumentation, describes the universal astrolobe designed by Ibn al-Sarraj of [Aleppo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aleppo) (a.k.a. Ahmad bin Abi Bakr; fl. 1328) as "the most sophisticated astronomical instrument from the entire Medieval and Renaissance periods".[^o40]

English author [Geoffrey Chaucer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_Chaucer) (c. 1343–1400) compiled *[A Treatise on the Astrolabe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Treatise_on_the_Astrolabe)* for his son, mainly based on a work by [Messahalla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashallah_ibn_Athari) or [Ibn al-Saffar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_al-Saffar).[^o41][^o42] The same source was translated by French astronomer and astrologer Pélerin de Prusse and others. The first printed book on the astrolabe was *Composition and Use of Astrolabe* by [Christian of Prachatice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_of_Prachatice), also using Messahalla, but relatively original.

A simplified astrolabe, known as a *balesilha*, was used by sailors to get an accurate reading of latitude while at sea. The use of the *balesilha* was promoted by [Prince Henry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prince_Henry_the_Navigator) (1394–1460) while navigating for Portugal.[^o38]p. 460

The astrolabe was almost certainly first brought north of the Pyrenees by Gerbert of Aurillac (future [Pope Sylvester II](wikipedia://en/24614)), where it was integrated into the [quadrivium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrivium) at the school in Reims, France, sometime before the turn of the 11th century.[^o43]p. 143 In the 15th century, French instrument maker Jean Fusoris (c. 1365–1436) also started remaking and selling astrolabes in his shop in [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989), along with portable sundials and other popular scientific devices of the day. Thirteen of his astrolabes survive to this day.[^o44]

*Image: Detail of Astronomical Instrument Ieremias Palladas.png.* [Astronomical Instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Catherine_of_Alexandria_%28Palladas,_Sinai%29#Scientific_Element) Detail by [Ieremias Palladas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ieremias_Palladas) 1612

One more special example of craftsmanship in early 15th-century Europe is the astrolabe designed by Antonius de Pacento and made by Dominicus de Lanzano, dated 1420.[^o45]

In the 16th century, [Johannes Stöffler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Stöffler) published *Elucidatio fabricae ususque astrolabii*, a manual of the construction and use of the astrolabe. Four identical 16th century astrolabes made by [Georg Hartmann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Hartmann) provide some of the earliest evidence for [batch production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batch_production) by [division of labor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Division_of_labor).

Greek painter [Ieremias Palladas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ieremias_Palladas) incorporated a sophisticated astrolabe in his 1612 painting depicting [Catherine of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catherine_of_Alexandria). The painting, entitled [*Catherine of Alexandria*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Catherine_of_Alexandria_%28Palladas,_Sinai%29#Scientific_Element), in addition to the saint, showed a device labelled the 'system of the universe' ($\Sigma\text{ύ}\sigma\tau\eta\mu\alpha \tau o\text{ῦ} \Pi\alpha\nu\tau\text{ό}\varsigma$). The device featured the [classical planets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_planets) with their Greek names: [Helios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helios) (Sun), [Selene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Selene) (Moon), [Hermes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermes) (Mercury), [Aphrodite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aphrodite) (Venus), [Ares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ares) (Mars), [Zeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeus) (Jupiter), and [Cronos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cronus) (Saturn). The depicted device also had celestial spheres, following the [Ptolemaic model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemaic_model), and Earth was shown as a blue sphere with circles of geographic coordinates. A complicated line representing the axis of the Earth covered the entire instrument.[^o46]

:::

### Sanskrit works {#Sanskrit_works}

::: {#o49 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Indian astrolabe"}

#### Yantrarāja {#Yantrarāja}

*Image: Yantraraja Folio 3.jpg.* Image of folio 3 of Malayendu Sūri's commentary (1382) on Mahendra Sūri's Yantrarāja (1370). [(Internet Archive)](https://archive.org/details/wg1207/page/n15/mode/2up)

In 1370, the first Indian treatise on the astrolabe was written by the [Jain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) astronomer [Mahendra Suri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mahendra_Suri), titled *Yantrarāja*.[^o50][^o51] With the support and patronage of [Firuz Shah Tughlaq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Firuz_Shah_Tughlaq), Mahendra Sūri composed the first ever Sanskrit manual on astrolabes. It was Sūri who coined the Sanskrit name "Yantrarāja" ("the king of astronomical instruments") for the astrolabe and he also titled his manual on astrolabes as *Yantrarāja*. Sūri composed the manual in 1370 CE. Mahendra Sūri's student Malayendu Sūri composed a commentary on *Yantrarāja* in 1382. Two other commentaries on *Yantrarāja* are known, one by Gopirāja written in 1540 and other by Yajñeśvara in 1842.

The *Yantrarāja* manual in 128 verses is divided into five chapters. The first chapter *Gaṇitādhyāya* discusses the theory behind the astrolabe. The second chapter *Yantraghatanādhyāya* is devoted to descriptions of the various components of the astrolabe. The third chapter *Yantraracanādhyāya* describes the details of the construction of the astrolabe. The fourth chapter *Yantrasodhanādhyāya* discusses method for ascertaining whether the astrolabe has been properly constructed. It is in the fifth and final chapter *Yantravicāraṇādhyāya* one can see descriptions on how to use the instrument for observational and computational purposes. This chapter also dwells on the different types of astronomical and trigonometrical problems that can be solved using the astrolabe.

While Mahendra Sūri's manual is in 128 verses and contains no data in the form of tables, Malayendu Sūri's commentary is interspersed with neatly prepared tables.

:::

#### Other Sanskrit works on astrolabe {#Other_Sanskrit_works_on_astrolabe}

Over the centuries since the publication of Mahendra Sūri's *Yantrarāja* in 1370, several other Sanskrit manuals on the astrolabe have been composed. These include the following:[^o31]

- *Yantra-rāja-adhikāra* (Chapter 1 of *Yantrakiraṇāvalī*) by Padmanābha in 1423
- *Turya-yantra-prakāśa* by Bhūdhara in 1572
- *Yantrarāja-vicāra-vimśādhyāyī* by Nayanasukhopādhyāya in 1730
- *Yantrarāja-racanā* by Savāī Jaya Siṃha (1688 - 1743)
- *yantrarāja-kalpa* by Mathurānātha Śukla (1782)

### Astrolabes and clocks {#Astrolabes_and_clocks}

*Image: Het gebruik van het astrolabium door Amerigo Vespucci, Jan Collaert II, Museum Plantin-Moretus, PK.OPB.0186.018.jpg.* [Amerigo Vespucci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amerigo_Vespucci) observing the [Southern Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crux) by looking over the top of an [armillary sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armillary_sphere) bizarrely held from the top as if it were an astrolabe; however, an astrolabe cannot be used by looking over its top. The page inexplicably contains the word *astrolabium*. By [Jan Collaert II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Collaert_II). [Museum Plantin-Moretus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museum_Plantin-Moretus), [Antwerp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerp), Belgium.

Mechanical [astronomical clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_clock) were initially influenced by the astrolabe; they could be seen in many ways as clockwork astrolabes designed to produce a continual display of the current position of the sun, stars, and planets. For example, [Richard of Wallingford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_of_Wallingford)'s clock (c. 1330) consisted essentially of a star map rotating behind a fixed rete, similar to that of an astrolabe.[^n6]

Many astronomical clocks use an astrolabe-style display, such as the famous [clock at Prague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prague_Orloj), adopting a stereographic projection (see below) of the ecliptic plane. In recent times, astrolabe watches have become popular. For example, Swiss watchmaker [Ludwig Oechslin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwig_Oechslin) designed and built an astrolabe wristwatch in conjunction with [Ulysse Nardin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulysse_Nardin) in 1985.[^o55] Dutch watchmaker Christaan van der Klauuw also manufactures astrolabe watches today.[^o56]

## Construction {#Construction}

An astrolabe consists of a disk with a wide, raised rim, called the *mater* (mother), which is deep enough to hold one or more flat plates called *tympans*, or *[climates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clime)*. A [tympan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tympan) is made for a specific [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) and is engraved with a [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) of [circles](wikipedia://en/6220) denoting [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) and [altitude](wikipedia://en/48386) and representing the portion of the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) above the local horizon. The rim of the mater is typically graduated into [hours of time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hour), [degrees of arc](wikipedia://en/1195294), or both.[^o59]

Above the mater and tympan, the *rete*, a framework bearing a projection of the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) plane and several [pointers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointer_%28rod%29) indicating the positions of the brightest [stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star), is free to rotate. These pointers are often just simple points, but depending on the skill of the craftsman can be very elaborate and artistic. There are examples of astrolabes with artistic pointers in the shape of balls, stars, snakes, hands, dogs' heads, and leaves, among others.[^o59] The names of the indicated stars were often engraved on the pointers in Arabic or Latin.[^o60] Some astrolabes have a narrow *[rule](wikipedia://en/65593)* or *label* which rotates over the rete, and may be marked with a scale of [declinations](wikipedia://en/8612).

The rete, representing the [sky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky), functions as a [star chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart). When it is rotated, the stars and the [ecliptic](wikipedia://en/9264) move over the projection of the coordinates on the tympan. One complete rotation corresponds to the passage of a day. The astrolabe is, therefore, a predecessor of the modern [planisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planisphere).

On the back of the mater, there is often engraved a number of scales that are useful in the astrolabe's various applications. These vary from designer to designer, but might include curves for time conversions, a [calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calendar) for converting the day of the month to the sun's position on the ecliptic, trigonometric scales, and graduation of 360 degrees around the back edge. The *[alidade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alidade)* is attached to the back face. An alidade can be seen in the lower right illustration of the Persian astrolabe above. When the astrolabe is held vertically, the alidade can be rotated and the sun or a star sighted along its length, so that its altitude in degrees can be read ("taken") from the graduated edge of the astrolabe; hence the word's Greek roots: "astron" (ἄστρον) = star + "lab-" (λαβ-) = to take. The alidade had vertical and horizontal cross-hairs which plots locations on an azimuthal ring called an almucantar (altitude-distance circle).

An arm called a radius connects from the center of the astrolabe to the optical axis which is parallel with another arm also called a radius. The other radius contains graduations of altitude and distance measurements.

A shadow square also appears on the back of some astrolabes, developed by Muslim astrologists in the 9th Century, whereas devices of the Ancient Greek tradition featured only altitude scales on the back of the devices.[^o61] This was used to convert shadow lengths and the altitude of the sun, the uses of which were various from surveying to measuring inaccessible heights.[^o62]

Devices were usually signed by their maker with an inscription appearing on the back of the astrolabe, and if there was a patron of the object, their name would appear inscribed on the front, or in some cases, the name of the reigning sultan or the teacher of the astrolabist has also been found to appear inscribed in this place.[^o63] The date of the astrolabe's construction was often also signed. The inscriptions on astrolabes also allowed historians to conclude that astronomers tended to make their own astrolabes, but that many were also made to order and kept in stock to sell, suggesting there was some contemporary market for the devices.[^o63]

:::

## Mathematical basis {#Mathematical_basis}

The construction and design of astrolabes are based on the application of the [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431) of the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239). The point from which the projection is usually made is the [South Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Pole). The plane onto which the projection is made is that of the [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator).[^o65]

### Designing a tympanum through stereographic projection {#Designing_a_tympanum_through_stereographic_projection}

*Image: Parts of a tympan of an astrolabe.png.* Parts of an Astrolabe tympanum

The tympanum captures the celestial coordinate axes upon which the *rete* will rotate. It is the component that will enable the precise determination of a star's position at a specific time of [day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_rotation) and [year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_orbit).

Therefore, it should project:

1. The [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith), which will vary depending on the [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) of the astrolabe user.
1. The [horizon line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) and [almucantar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almucantar) or circles parallel to the horizon, which will allow for the determination of a celestial body's [altitude](wikipedia://en/48386) (from the horizon to the zenith).
1. The [celestial meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28astronomy%29) (north-south meridian, passing through the zenith) and secondary meridians (circles intersecting the north-south meridian at the zenith), which will enable the measurement of [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) for a celestial body.
1. The three main [circles of latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_latitude) ([Capricorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Capricorn), [Equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator), and [Cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Cancer)) to determine the exact moments of [solstices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solstice) and [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) throughout the year.

#### The tropics and the equator define the tympanum {#The_tropics_and_the_equator_define_the_tympanum}

*Image: Proyección estereográfica de trópicos y ecuador en un astrolabio.png.* Stereographic projection of Earth's tropics and equator from the South Pole.

On the right side of the image above:

1. The blue sphere represents the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239).
1. The blue arrow indicates the direction of true north (the [North Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris)).
1. The central blue point represents Earth (the observer's location).
1. The geographic south of the celestial sphere acts as the [projection pole](wikipedia://en/143431).
1. The celestial equatorial plane serves as the [projection plane](wikipedia://en/39680127).
1. Three parallel circles represent the projection on the celestial sphere of Earth's main [circles of latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_latitude):
   - In orange, the celestial [Tropic of Cancer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Cancer).
   - In purple, the celestial [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator).
   - In green, the celestial [Tropic of Capricorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Capricorn).

When projecting onto the celestial equatorial plane, three concentric circles correspond to the celestial sphere's three [circles of latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_of_latitude) (left side of the image). The largest of these, the projection on the celestial equatorial plane of the celestial [Tropic of Capricorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Capricorn), defines the size of the astrolabe's tympanum. The center of the tympanum (and the center of the three circles) is actually the north-south axis around which Earth rotates, and therefore, the *rete* of the astrolabe will rotate around this point as the hours of the day pass (due to [Earth's rotational motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_rotation)).

The three concentric circles on the tympanum are useful for determining the exact moments of [solstices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solstice) and [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) throughout the year: if the sun's altitude at noon on the *rete* is known and coincides with the outer circle of the tympanum (Tropic of Capricorn), it signifies the [winter solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winter_solstice) (the sun will be at the [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith) for an observer at the Tropic of Capricorn, meaning summer in the southern hemisphere and winter in the northern hemisphere). If, on the other hand, its altitude coincides with the inner circle (Tropic of Cancer), it indicates the [summer solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summer_solstice). If its altitude is on the middle circle (equator), it corresponds to one of the two [equinoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox).

#### The horizon and the measurement of altitude {#The_horizon_and_the_measurement_of_altitude}

*Image: Proyección estereográfica del horizonte sobre el tímpano de un astrolabio.png.* Stereographic projection of an observer's horizon at a specific latitude

On the right side of the image above:

1. The blue arrow indicates the direction of true north (the [North Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris)).
1. The central blue point represents Earth (the observer's location).
1. The black arrow represents the [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith) direction for the observer (which would vary depending on the observer's [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616)).
1. The two black circles represent the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) surrounding the observer, which is perpendicular to the zenith vector and defines the portion of the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) visible to the observer, and its projection on the celestial equatorial plane.
1. The geographic south of the celestial sphere acts as the [projection pole](wikipedia://en/143431).
1. The celestial equatorial plane serves as the [projection plane](wikipedia://en/39680127).

When projecting the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) onto the celestial equatorial plane, it transforms into an ellipse upward-shifted relatively to the center of the tympanum (both the observer and the projection of the north-south axis). This implies that a portion of the celestial sphere will fall outside the outer circle of the tympanum (the projection of the celestial [Tropic of Capricorn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tropic_of_Capricorn)) and, therefore, won't be represented.

*Image: Proyección estereográfica del horizonte y almucantar sobre el tímpano de un astrolabio.png.* Stereographic projection of the horizon and an almucantar.

Additionally, when drawing circles parallel to the horizon up to the zenith ([almucantar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almucantar)), and projecting them on the celestial equatorial plane, as in the image above, a grid of consecutive ellipses is constructed, allowing for the determination of a [star's altitude](wikipedia://en/48386) when its *rete* overlaps with the designed tympanum.

#### The meridians and the measurement of azimuth {#The_meridians_and_the_measurement_of_azimuth}

*Image: Proyección estereográfica del horizonte y azimut sobre el tímpano de un astrolabio.png.* Stereographic projection of the north-south meridian and a meridian 40° E on the tympanum of an astrolabe

On the right side of the image above:

1. The blue arrow indicates the direction of true north (the [North Star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris)).
1. The central blue point represents Earth (the observer's location).
1. The black arrow represents the [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith) direction for the observer (which would vary depending on the observer's [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616)).
1. The two black circles represent the [horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon) surrounding the observer, which is perpendicular to the zenith vector and defines the portion of the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) visible to the observer, and its projection on the celestial equatorial plane.
1. The five red dots represent the [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith), the [nadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadir_%28astronomy%29) (the point on the [celestial sphere](wikipedia://en/48239) opposite the zenith with respect to the observer), their projections on the celestial equatorial plane, and the center (with no physical meaning attached) of the circle obtained by projecting the secondary meridian (see below) on the celestial equatorial plane.
1. The orange circle represents the [celestial meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28astronomy%29) (or meridian that goes, for the observer, from the north of the horizon to the south of the horizon passing through the zenith).
1. The two red circles represent a secondary meridian with an [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) of 40° East relative to the observer's horizon (which, like all secondary meridians, intersects the principal meridian at the zenith and nadir), and its projection on the celestial equatorial plane.
1. The geographic south of the celestial sphere acts as the [projection pole](wikipedia://en/143431).
1. The celestial equatorial plane serves as the [projection plane](wikipedia://en/39680127).

When projecting the [celestial meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28astronomy%29), it results in a straight line that overlaps with the vertical axis of the tympanum, where the [zenith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenith) and [nadir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nadir_%28astronomy%29) are located. However, when projecting the 40° E meridian, another circle is obtained that passes through both the zenith and nadir projections, so its center is located on the perpendicular [bisection](wikipedia://en/152547) of the segment connecting both points. Indeed, the projection of the celestial meridian can be considered as a circle with an infinite radius (a straight line) whose center is on this bisection and at an infinite distance from these two points.

If successive meridians that divide the celestial sphere into equal sectors (like "orange slices" radiating from the zenith) are projected, a family of curves passing through the zenith projection on the tympanum is obtained. These curves, once overlaid with the *rete* containing the major stars, allow for determining the [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) of a star located on the *rete* and rotated for a specific time of day.
