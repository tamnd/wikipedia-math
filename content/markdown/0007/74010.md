# Demographics of Bulgaria

::: {#o5 .subject cue=lead name="Demographics of Bulgaria"}

The **demography of the Republic of Bulgaria** is monitored by the [National Statistical Institute of Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Statistical_Institute_of_Bulgaria). [Demographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographics) features of the population of [Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgaria) include population density, [ethnicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_group), education level, health of the populace, economic status, [religious affiliations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion_in_Bulgaria) and others.

:::

The population of Bulgaria was around 6.4 million in 2024.

In 2023, Bulgaria had a [Human Development Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_Development_Index) of 0.845, which corresponds to "very high human development", ranking 55th in the world together with neighbouring Romania,[^o6] was designated as a "[High-income economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank_high-income_economy)" by the World Bank, with a [GNI per capita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_GNI_%28nominal%29_per_capita) of \$15,320, as of 2024,[^o7] and was awarded the 38th position in *[Newsweek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newsweek)*'s rankings of the world's best countries to live in, measuring health, education, political environment and [economic dynamism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_dynamism) in 2010.[^o8]

## Demographic history {#Demographic_history}

1880 Various estimates have put Bulgaria's medieval population at 1.1 million in 700 AD and 2.6 million in 1365.[^o10] At the 2011 census, the population inhabiting Bulgaria was 7,364,570 in total, but the 2021 census calculated that the population had declined to 6.5 million.[^o11][^o12] The peak was in 1989, the year when the borders opened after a half of a century of communist regime, when the population numbered 9,009,018.

## Birth rates and fertility {#Birth_rates_and_fertility}

::: {#o51 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Ethnic and confessional birth and fertility rates in Bulgaria"}

### Historical birth and fertility rates {#Historical_birth_and_fertility_rates}

The first reliable data about fertility and natural increase rates in the Bulgarian lands date back to the mid-1800s.

According to Turkish statistician [Kemal Karpat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kemal_Karpat), Non-Muslims, Bulgarians included, in the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) received a remarkable boost in fertility in the early 1830s. Their average growth rate climbed up to 2% per year, compared to zero among Muslims, who suffered from demographic stagnation.[^o52]

The same trend continued well after Bulgaria's Liberation from Ottoman rule in 1878.[^o53] Until the early 1890s, Muslim birth rates in Bulgaria hesitated in the low 20s, dwarfed by rates of + 40‰ among Orthodox Christians, while natural increase rates hovered around zero.[^o53] The ongoing Muslim demographic crisis and the heavy migration to Turkey were the two primary reasons for the rapid decrease in Bulgaria's Muslim and Turkish population between 1880 and 1910, from 28.7% in 1880 to 13.8% in 1910 for Muslims and from 26.2% to 10.7% for Turks.

Muslim birth and natural increase rates started climbing slowly from the late 1890s but only surpassed Orthodox ones in 1924. Nevertheless, it was not the Eastern Orthodox but rather Bulgaria's [Roman Catholics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church_in_Bulgaria) that had the highest birth rate during the period, though usually offset by very high mortality rates.

In the 1930s and 1940s, ethnic Bulgarians completed stage 2 of their [demographic transition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demographic_transition), and crude birth rate among them fell to a mere 23.3‰ by 1946, or twice as low as the birth rate of Bulgaria's two largest minorities, [Turks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turks_in_Bulgaria) (40.9‰) and [Roma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romani_people_in_Bulgaria) (47.2‰).[^o56] At the same time, due to higher mortality, the rate of natural increase among Bulgarian Turks was almost identical to that among ethnic Bulgarians (12.1‰ vs. 11.1‰), while the rate among the Romani was twice as high (23.1‰).[^o56]

### Present-day {#Present-day}

A total of 64,984 live births were recorded in Bulgaria in 2016, giving the country a crude birth rate of 9.1‰.[^o58]

Bulgaria has a low [total fertility rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_fertility_rate) of 1.58 children per woman (according to the 2021 Census). This is up significantly from the late 1990s, but still below replacement and not enough to prevent further population decline, especially with emigration. Provinces with large Roma populations (for example [Sliven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sliven_Province), [Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Province) and [Yambol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yambol_Province)) tend to have higher fertility rates (and higher death rates) compared to other areas, whereas Turkish fertility is similar to the Bulgarian majority.[^o59] The average number of (live births) children in 2021 was 1.47 for all women (aged 12 or more), ranging from 0.04 children for women between the ages of 12-19 to 1.92 children per women between the ages of 70–79. The distribution by ethnicity shows that only among women who identify themselves as belonging to the Roma ethnic group, the average number of children is sufficient for simple reproduction – 2.25 children, mainly because of the significantly younger age structure and low educational level of this group, followed by the Turkish ethnic group with 1.79 children and 1.41 children for women who self-identified as belonging to the Bulgarian ethnic.[^o60] The distribution of the average number of live births by religion changes from 1.83 children for Muslim women and 1.82 children for Protestant women, followed by 'other Christian' (1.63), Eastern Orthodox (1.43), Catholic (1.36), Jewish (1.27) and Armenian Apostolic (1.26). Women without religion had an average number of 1.40 children.

## Demographic policies {#Demographic_policies}

The progressive decrease of the Bulgarian population is hindering economic growth and welfare improvement, and the management measures taken to mitigate the negative consequences do not address the essence of the problem. The Government implemented a program for the period 2017–2021, the first one aimed at reversing this trend. The program identifies the priority means for achieving this goal: measures to increase the birth rate, reduce youth emigration, and build up regulatory and institutional capacity to implement a modern immigration policy tailored to the needs of Bulgarian business.[^o171][^o172]

## Migration {#Migration}

### Historical migration {#Historical_migration}

The first censuses of the [Principality of Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principality_of_Bulgaria) and the autonomous province of [Eastern Rumelia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Rumelia) in 1880 recorded 31,786 and 17,970 Bulgarian refugees from [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_%28region%29) and [Ottoman Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adrianople_Vilayet), respectively, who accounted for 1.38% of the population of the Principality an 2.20% of the population of the autonomous province, respectively.[^o316][^o317][^o318] The census of the Principality also counted a total of 37,635 people, or 1.88% of the population, born in a country other than the [Ottoman Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire), mostly Bulgarians from [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarians_in_Romania), [Northern Dobruja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Dobruja) and [Bessarabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bessarabian_Bulgarians).[^o316] By 1887, when the first joint census of the Principality and the autonomous province was conducted following their peaceful [unification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bulgarian_unification) in 1885, the number of the refugees from the Ottoman Empire had grown to 54,462 people, or 1.73% of the population, while the rest of the foreign-born population had fallen to 31,637 people, 9,831 of whom born in the [Russian Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Empire), 11,843 in [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romania), 2,690 in [Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbia) and 7,273 elsewhere.[^o319]

According to the 1910 census, 300,000 or almost 10% of the ethnic Bulgarians were born in another Bulgarian municipality than the one they were enumerated in. The same data shows that the foreign-born ethnic Bulgarians numbered 78,000, or 2% of them, most numerous of whom were the 61,000 [Ottoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire)-born, 9,000 Romanian-born and by less than 2,000 [Austro-Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Empire), Serbian and Russian-born.[^o320] By the 1926 census, there had been 253,000 refugees with granted households and land or citizenship but with many more in towns of uncertain number. 35% came from [Eastern Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eastern_Thrace), 30% came from [Greek Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedonia_%28Greece%29), another 18% from [Western Thrace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Thrace), 8% from [Dobruja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dobruja), 4% from the [Western Outlands](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_Outlands), 3% from [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia_Minor), and 2% from [North Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Macedonia). They constituted 6% of the country's population. In 1940, 70,000 Bulgarians [were exchanged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_exchange_between_Bulgaria_and_Romania) from [Northern Dobruja](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Dobruja). The total number of refugees in 1878–1940 is estimated at between 700,000 and 1,200,000.[^o321] [In 1950–1951, around 150,000 Turks left Bulgaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exodus_of_Turks_from_Bulgaria_%281950–1951%29) for Turkey, and [again in 1989](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big_Excursion).

### Current migration {#Current_migration}

According to the 2011 census [Russian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russia) citizens are the most numerous foreigners – 11 991, followed by 8 444 EU citizens (UK – 2 605, Greece – 1 253, Germany – 848, Poland – 819 and Italy – 456), citizens of [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine) – 3 064, [North Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Macedonia) – 1 091, [Moldova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldova) – 893 and [Serbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serbia) – 569. 22.8% of them are from Asia, mostly from Turkey. Those with dual Bulgarian and other citizenship were 22 152, or 0.3% of the population. Of them persons with Bulgarian and Russian citizenship were 5 257 (23.7%), followed by persons with Bulgarian and Turkish citizenship – 4 282 (19.3%), Bulgarian and citizenship of the USA – 1 725 (7.8%). There are at least 17,527 [Refugees of the Syrian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refugees_of_the_Syrian_Civil_War) with applications in Bulgaria. In 2001–2015 185,447 people applied for Bulgarian citizenship and 116,222 were provided with. 113,647 were granted on grounds of proven Bulgarian ancestry, including 59,968 North Macedonia citizens. 29,218 were [Moldovan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moldova) citizens, 5930 Ukrainians, 5374 Serbians, 5194 Russians, 3840 Israeli, 2192 Albanians, 692 Turks and others.[^o323] In 2016, 12,880 foreigners were naturalized, including 6196 Macedonians.[^o324]

Bulgarian children constitute the majority of all children in 23 out of 28 provinces. They constitute more than ninety percent of all children in two provinces: [Sofia (city)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofia) (92%) and [Pernik Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pernik_Province) (90%).

Turkish children constitute the majority in [Kardzhali Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kardzhali_Province) (68% of self-declared) and [Razgrad Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Razgrad_Province) (50% of self-declared); they also constitute the largest group of all children in [Silistra Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silistra_Province) (43%).

Roma children constitute 12% of all children in Bulgaria and more than a quarter in three provinces: [Montana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Montana_Province) (29%), [Sliven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sliven_Province) (28%) and [Yambol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yambol_Province) (27%).

## Education {#Education}

Over 98% of the population is literate, the males being more literate than the females.

According to the 2011 census, about 112,778 people aged nine or more are [illiterate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illiterate). There are considerable differences in the share of illiterate persons amongst the three main ethnic groups. Amongst the Bulgarian ethnic group the share of illiterate is 0.5%, amongst the Turkish – 4.7% and amongst the Roma ethnic group – 11.8%.[^o371] About 81 thousand people aged seven or more never visited school.[^o372]

## Unemployment {#Unemployment}

*Image: Bulgaria socio-economic map.jpg.* Chart of unemployment and poverty rate by ethnic groups

The number of unemployed people declined from 207,000 people (or around 6.2% of the population) in 2017[^o375] to 156,657 in November 2023. The unemployment rate stood at 4.3 per cent in November 2023 according to [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat).[^o376]

Most unemployed people are aged 15 to 24 years old.

The [unemployment rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unemployment_rate) in rural areas (around 10.0%) is nearly two times higher than the unemployment rate in urban areas (approximately 5.1%).

[Vidin Province](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vidin_Province) has the highest unemployment rate with almost one fifth of its labour force being unemployed. The provinces of [Shumen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shumen_Province) (15.9%), [Silistra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silistra_Province) (12.5%) and [Targovishte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targovishte_Province) (12.4%) also have very high unemployment rates.

## Other statistics {#Other_statistics}

### Home ownership {#Home_ownership}

According to [Eurostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurostat), 82.3% per cent of the population live in privately owned and owner-occupied homes, ranking it as 12th [highest in ownership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_home_ownership_rate) globally.[^o379] It is down from a recent peak of 87.6% in 2008, and has been steadily falling since.[^o379]

### Internet penetration {#Internet_penetration}

The number of Internet users has increased rapidly since 2000—from 430,000 their number grew to 1.55 million in 2004, and 3.4 million (48 per cent penetration rate) in 2010.[^o381] Bulgaria has the third-fastest average Broadband Internet speed in the world after South Korea and Romania with an average speed of 1,611 kbit/s.[^o382][^o383]

### Mobile phone adoption {#Mobile_phone_adoption}

Currently there are three active mobile phone operators—[A1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mobiltel), [Yettel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yettel) and [Vivacom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vivacom), A1 is the largest one with 5.2 million users as of 2010,[^o385] Yettel has 3,9 million as of 2007 and Vivacom over 1 million.

### HIV {#HIV}

Bulgaria's [HIV rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_HIV/AIDS_adult_prevalence_rate) is among the lowest in the world, being 0.1% or 3,800 infected as of 2009.

## Urbanization {#Urbanization}

Most Bulgarians (72.5 per cent) reside in urban areas. Approximately one-sixth of them live in Sofia, which has a population exceeding 1,200,000 people.

Urban population: 5,338,261 or 72.5% of total population (Census 2011)[^o11]

Rural: 2,026,309 or 27.5%

Rate of urbanization: −0.3% annual rate of change (2005–10 est.)
