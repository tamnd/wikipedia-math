# Constant term

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Constant term"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **constant term** (sometimes referred to as a **free term**) is a [term](wikipedia://en/24885593) in an [algebraic expression](wikipedia://en/4283745) that does not contain any [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) and therefore is [constant](wikipedia://en/320861). For example, in the [quadratic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial) $x^2 + 2x + 3$, the number 3 is a constant term.[^o2]

:::

After [like terms](wikipedia://en/19222837) are combined, an algebraic expression will have at most one constant term. Thus, it is common to speak of the quadratic polynomial $ax^2+bx+c$, where $x$ is the variable, as having a constant term of $c$. If the constant term is 0, then it will conventionally be omitted when the quadratic is written out.

Any [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) written in standard form has a unique constant term, which can be considered a [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) of $x^0$. In particular, the constant term will always be the lowest [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) term of the polynomial. This also applies to multivariate polynomials. For example, the polynomial $x^2+2xy+y^2-2x+2y-4$ has a constant term of −4, which can be considered to be the coefficient of $x^0y^0$, where the variables are eliminated by being exponentiated to 0 (since any non-zero number exponentiated to 0 becomes 1). For any polynomial, the constant term can be obtained by substituting in 0 instead of each variable; thus, eliminating each variable. The concept of exponentiation to 0 can be applied to [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) and other types of series, for example in this power series:

$$a_0 + a_1 x + a_2 x^2 + a_3 x^3 + \cdots$$

$a_0$ is the constant term.

## Constant of integration {#Constant_of_integration}

The [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of a constant term is 0, so when a term containing a constant term is differentiated, the constant term vanishes, regardless of its value. Therefore the [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823) is only determined up to an unknown constant term, which is called "the constant of integration" and added in symbolic form (usually denoted as $C$).[^o4]

For example, the antiderivative of $\cos x$ is $\sin x$, since the derivative of $\sin x$ is equal to $\cos x$ based on the [properties of trigonometric derivatives](wikipedia://en/10308785#Derivatives_of_trigonometric_functions).

However, the *[integral](wikipedia://en/15532)* of $\cos x$ is equal to $\sin x$ (the antiderivative), plus an arbitrary constant:

$$\int \cos x \, \mathrm dx = \sin x + C,$$

because for any constant $C$, the derivative of the right-hand side of the equation is equal to the left-hand side of the equation.
