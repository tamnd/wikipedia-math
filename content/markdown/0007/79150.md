# Feigenbaum constants

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Feigenbaum constants"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [bifurcation theory](wikipedia://en/2326042), the **Feigenbaum constants** [^o3] $\delta$ and $\alpha$ are two [mathematical constants](wikipedia://en/48779551) which both express ratios in a [bifurcation diagram](wikipedia://en/79173) for a non-linear map. They are named after the physicist [Mitchell J. Feigenbaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitchell_J._Feigenbaum).

:::

## History {#History}

Feigenbaum originally related the first constant to the [period-doubling bifurcations](wikipedia://en/4457659) in the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137), but also showed it to hold for all [one-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-dimensional) [maps](wikipedia://en/516931) with a single [quadratic](wikipedia://en/187240) [maximum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima). As a consequence of this generality, every [chaotic system](wikipedia://en/6295) that corresponds to this description will bifurcate at the same rate. Feigenbaum made this discovery in 1975,[^o5][^o6] and he officially published it in 1978.[^o7]

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="4.669"}

## The first constant {#The_first_constant}

The **first Feigenbaum constant** or simply **Feigenbaum constant**[^o10] $\delta$ is the limiting ratio of each bifurcation interval to the next between every [period doubling](wikipedia://en/4457659), of a one-[parameter](wikipedia://en/25065) map

$$x_{i+1} = f(x_i),$$

where $f\,(x)$ is a function parameterized by the bifurcation parameter $\boldsymbol{a}$.

It is given by the [limit](wikipedia://en/285773):[^o11]

$$\delta = \lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{a_{n-1} - a_{n-2}}{a_n - a_{n-1}}$$

where $a_{n}$ are discrete values of $\boldsymbol{a}$ at the $n$th period doubling.

This gives its numerical value :

$$\delta = 4.669\,201\,609\,102\,990\,671\,853\,203\,820\,466\ldots$$

- A simple [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) approximation is $\tfrac{621}{133}$, which is correct to 5 significant values (when rounding). For more precision use $\tfrac{1228}{263}$, which is correct to 7 significant values.
- It is approximately equal to $\tfrac{10}{\pi - 1}$, with an error of 0.0047 %.

## The second constant {#The_second_constant}

The **second Feigenbaum constant** or **Feigenbaum reduction parameter**[^o10] $\alpha$ is given by :

$$\alpha = 2.502\,907\,875\,095\,892\,822\,283\,902\,873\,218\ldots$$

It is the ratio between the width of a [tine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tine_%28structural%29) and the width of one of its two subtines (except the tine closest to the fold). A negative sign is applied to $\alpha$ when the ratio between the lower subtine and the width of the tine is measured.[^o18]

These numbers apply to a large class of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) (for example, dripping faucets to population growth).[^o18]

A simple [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) approximation is $\tfrac{5}{2}$, which is correct to 2 significant values. For more precision, $\tfrac{13}{11}$ × $\tfrac{17}{11}$ × $\tfrac{37}{27}$ = $\tfrac{8177}{3267}$ is used, which is correct to 8 significant values.

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Both numbers are believed to be [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325), although they have not been [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) to be so.[^o21] In fact, there is no known proof that either constant is even [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689).

The first proof of the [universality](wikipedia://en/1752072) of the Feigenbaum constants was carried out by [Oscar Lanford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscar_Lanford)—with computer-assistance—in 1982[^o22] (with a small correction by [Jean-Pierre Eckmann](wikipedia://en/31446069) and Peter Wittwer of the [University of Geneva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Geneva) in 1987[^o23]). Over the years, non-numerical methods were discovered for different parts of the proof, aiding [Mikhail Lyubich](wikipedia://en/33120371) in producing the first complete non-numerical proof.[^o24]

:::

## Other values {#Other_values}

The period-3 window in the logistic map also has a period-tripling route to chaos (moving at each step to the period-3 subwindow), reaching chaos at $r = 3.854 077 963 591\dots$, and it has its own two Feigenbaum constants: $\delta = 55.26, \alpha = 9.277$. Similar results hold for the other isolated period-n windows in the chaotic region, yielding $\alpha$ and $\delta$ for each window. There is a universal relation between the two constants due to Eckmann, Epstein, and Wittwer, that $3\delta \approx \alpha^2$. [^o26][^o27]
