# Nernst equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nernst equation"}

In [electrochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemistry), the **Nernst equation** is a [chemical thermodynamical](wikipedia://en/29952#Chemical_thermodynamics) relationship that permits the calculation of the [reduction potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduction_potential) of a reaction ([half-cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-cell) or [full cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_cell) reaction) from the [standard electrode potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_electrode_potential), [absolute temperature](wikipedia://en/41789), the number of electrons involved in the [redox reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox), and [activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_activity) (often approximated by concentrations) of the [chemical species](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_species) undergoing reduction and [oxidation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation) respectively. It was named after [Walther Nernst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Nernst), a German [physical chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemist) who formulated the equation.[^o2][^o3]

:::

## Expression {#Expression}

### General form with chemical activities {#General_form_with_chemical_activities}

When an oxidized species ($\operatorname{Ox}$) accepts a number *z* of electrons () to be converted in its reduced form ($\operatorname{Red}$), the half-reaction is expressed as:

$$\ce{{Ox} + \mathit{z}\, e^{-} -> Red}$$

The [reaction quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_quotient) (*$Q_{r}$*), also often called the ion activity product (*IAP*), is the ratio between the [chemical activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_activity) (*a*) of the reduced form (the [reductant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reductant), $a_{\operatorname{Red}}$) and the oxidized form (the [oxidant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidant), $a_{\operatorname{Ox}}$). The chemical activity of a dissolved species corresponds to its true thermodynamic concentration taking into account the electrical interactions between all ions present in solution at elevated concentrations. For a given dissolved species, its chemical activity (a) is the product of its [activity coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) (γ) by its [molar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration) (mol/L solution), or [molal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molality) (mol/kg water), [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration) (C): a = γ C. So, if the concentration (*C*, also denoted here below with square brackets \[ \]) of all the dissolved species of interest are sufficiently low and that their [activity coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) are close to unity, their chemical activities can be approximated by their [concentrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentration) as commonly done when simplifying, or idealizing, a reaction for didactic purposes:

$$Q_r = \frac{a_\text{Red}}{a_\text{Ox}} = \frac{[\operatorname{Red}]}{[\operatorname{Ox}]}$$

At [chemical equilibrium](wikipedia://en/5306), the ratio *$Q_{r}$* of the activity of the reaction product (*a*<sub>Red</sub>) by the [reagent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reagent) activity (*a*<sub>Ox</sub>) is equal to the [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant) *$K$* of the half-reaction:

$$K = \frac{a_\text{Red}}{a_\text{Ox}}$$

The standard thermodynamics also says that the actual [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) $\Delta G$ is related to the free energy change under [standard state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_state) $\Delta G^{o}$ by the relationship:

$$\Delta G = \Delta G^{\ominus} + RT\ln Q_r$$

where $Q_{r}$ is the [reaction quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_quotient) and R is the [universal ideal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant). The cell potential $E$ associated with the electrochemical reaction is defined as the decrease in Gibbs free energy per coulomb of charge transferred, which leads to the relationship

$$\Delta G = -zFE.$$

The constant $F$ (the [Faraday constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_constant)) is a unit conversion factor $F = N_{A}q$, where $N_{A}$ is the [Avogadro constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_constant) and $q$ is the fundamental [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) charge. This immediately leads to the Nernst equation, which for an electrochemical half-cell is

$$E_\text{red} = E^\ominus_\text{red} - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln Q_r=E^\ominus_\text{red} - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln\frac{a_\text{Red}}{a_\text{Ox}}.$$

For a complete electrochemical reaction (full cell), the equation can be written as

$$E_\text{cell} = E^\ominus_\text{cell} - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln Q_r$$

where:

- $E_{\operatorname{red}}$ is the half-cell [reduction potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduction_potential) at the temperature of interest,
- $E^{o}_{\operatorname{red}}$ is the [*standard* half-cell reduction potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_electrode_potential),
- $E_{\operatorname{cell}}$ is the cell potential ([electromotive force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromotive_force)) at the temperature of interest,
- $E^{o}_{\operatorname{cell}}$ is the [standard cell potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_cell_potential) in volts,
- $R$ is the [universal ideal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant): $R = 8.31446261815324$,
- $T$ is the temperature in [kelvins](wikipedia://en/19593121),
- $z$ is the number of [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) transferred in the cell reaction or [half-reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-reaction),
- $F$ is [Faraday's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_constant), the magnitude of charge (in [coulombs](wikipedia://en/47719)) per [mole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29) of electrons: $F = 96485.3321233100184$,
- $Q_{r}$ is the reaction quotient of the cell reaction, and,
- $a$ is the chemical [activity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_%28chemistry%29) for the relevant species, where $a_{\operatorname{Red}}$ is the activity of the reduced form and $a_{\operatorname{Ox}}$ is the activity of the oxidized form.

### Thermal voltage {#Thermal_voltage}

At room temperature (25 °C), the [thermal voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_voltage) $V_T=\frac{RT}{F}$ is approximately 25.693 mV. The Nernst equation is frequently expressed in terms of base-10 [logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithms) (*i.e.*, [common logarithms](wikipedia://en/174482)) rather than [natural logarithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_logarithms), in which case it is written:

$$E = E^\ominus - \frac{V_T}{z} \ln\frac{a_\text{Red}}{a_\text{Ox}} = E^\ominus - \frac{\lambda V_T}{z} \log_{10}\frac{a_\text{Red}}{a_\text{Ox}}.$$

where *λ* = ln(10) ≈ 2.3026 and *λV<sub>T</sub>* ≈ 0.05916 Volt.

### Form with activity coefficients and concentrations {#Form_with_activity_coefficients_and_concentrations}

Similarly to equilibrium constants, activities are always measured with respect to the [standard state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_state) (1 mol/L for solutes, 1 atm for gases, and T = 298.15 K, *i.e.*, 25 °C or 77 °F). The chemical activity of a species $i$, $a_{i}$, is related to the measured concentration $C_{i}$ via the relationship $a_{i} = \gamma_{i} C_{i}$, where $\gamma_{i}$ is the [activity coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) of the species $i$. Because activity coefficients tend to unity at low concentrations, or are unknown or difficult to determine at medium and high concentrations, activities in the Nernst equation are frequently replaced by simple concentrations and then, formal standard reduction potentials $E^{\ominus'}_\text{red}$ used.

Taking into account the activity coefficients ($\gamma$) the Nernst equation becomes:

$$E_\text{red} = E^\ominus_\text{red} - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln\left(\frac{\gamma_\text{Red}}{\gamma_\text{Ox}}\frac{C_\text{Red}}{C_\text{Ox}}\right)$$

$$E_\text{red} = E^\ominus_\text{red} - \frac{RT}{zF} \left(\ln\frac{\gamma_\text{Red}}{\gamma_\text{Ox}} + \ln\frac{C_\text{Red}}{C_\text{Ox}}\right)$$

$$E_\text{red} = \underbrace{\left(E^\ominus_\text{red} - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln\frac{\gamma_\text{Red}}{\gamma_\text{Ox}}\right)}_{E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}} - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln\frac{C_\text{Red}}{C_\text{Ox}}$$

Where the first term including the [activity coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) ($\gamma$) is denoted $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ and called the formal standard reduction potential, so that $E_\text{red}$ can be directly expressed as a function of $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ and the concentrations in the simplest form of the Nernst equation:

$$E_\text{red}=E^{\ominus '}_\text{red} - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln\frac{C_\text{Red}}{C_\text{Ox}}$$

### Formal standard reduction potential {#Formal_standard_reduction_potential}

When wishing to use simple concentrations in place of activities, but that the activity coefficients are far from unity and can no longer be neglected and are unknown or too difficult to determine, it can be convenient to introduce the notion of the "so-called" standard formal reduction potential ($E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$) which is related to the standard reduction potential as follows:[^o10]

$$E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}=E^{\ominus}_\text{red}-\frac{RT}{zF}\ln\frac{\gamma_\text{Red}}{\gamma_\text{Ox}}$$

So that the Nernst equation for the half-cell reaction can be correctly formally written in terms of concentrations as:

$$E_\text{red}=E^{\ominus '}_\text{red} - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln\frac{C_\text{Red}}{C_\text{Ox}}$$

and likewise for the full cell expression.

According to Wenzel (2020),[^o11] a formal reduction potential $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ is the reduction potential that applies to a half reaction under a set of specified conditions such as, e.g., [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH), [ionic strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_strength), or the concentration of [complexing agents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexing_agent).

The formal reduction potential $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ is often a more convenient, but conditional, form of the standard reduction potential, taking into account activity coefficients and specific conditions characteristics of the reaction medium. Therefore, its value is a conditional value, *i.e.*, that it depends on the experimental conditions and because the ionic strength affects the activity coefficients, $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ will vary from medium to medium.[^o10] Several definitions of the formal reduction potential can be found in the literature, depending on the pursued objective and the experimental constraints imposed by the studied system. The general definition of $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ refers to its value determined when $\frac{C_\text{red}} {C_\text{ox}} = 1$. A more particular case is when $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ is also determined at pH 7, as e.g. for redox reactions important in biochemistry or biological systems.

#### Determination of the formal standard reduction potential when $\tfrac{C_{red}}{C_{ox}}$ = 1 {#Determination_of_the_formal_standard_reduction_potential_when_1}

The formal standard reduction potential $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ can be defined as the measured reduction potential $E_\text{red}$ of the half-reaction at unity concentration ratio of the oxidized and reduced species (*i.e.*, when $\tfrac{C_{red}}{C_{ox}}$ = 1) under given conditions.[^o13]

Indeed:

as, $E_\text{red} = E^{\ominus}_\text{red}$, when $\frac{a_\text{red}} {a_\text{ox}} = 1$,

$E_\text{red} = E^{\ominus'}_\text{red}$, when $\frac{C_\text{red}} {C_\text{ox}} = 1$,

because $\ln{1} = 0$, and that the term $\frac{\gamma_\text{red}} {\gamma_\text{ox}}$ is included in $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$.

The formal reduction potential makes possible to more simply work with [molar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration) (mol/L, M) or [molal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molality) (mol/kg , m) concentrations in place of [activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_activity). Because molar and molal concentrations were once referred as [formal concentrations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_concentration), it could explain the origin of the adjective *formal* in the expression *formal* potential.

The formal potential is thus the reversible potential of an electrode at equilibrium immersed in a solution where reactants and products are at unit concentration.[^o14] If any small incremental change of potential causes a change in the direction of the reaction, *i.e.* from reduction to oxidation or *vice versa*, the system is close to equilibrium, reversible and is at its formal potential. When the formal potential is measured under [standard conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_conditions) (*i.e.* the activity of each dissolved species is 1 mol/L, T = 298.15 K = 25 °C = 77 °F, $P_{gas}$ = 1 bar) it becomes *de facto* a standard potential.[^o15] According to Brown and Swift (1949):

> "A formal potential is defined as the potential of a half-cell, measured against the [standard hydrogen electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_hydrogen_electrode), when the total concentration of each [oxidation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxidation_state) is one [formal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_concentration)".[^o16]

In this case, as for the standard reduction potentials, the concentrations of dissolved species remain equal to one [molar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration) (M) or one [molal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molality) (m), and so are said to be one [formal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_concentration) (F). So, expressing the concentration $C$ in [molarity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration) $M$ (1 mol/L):

$$\frac{C_\text{red}} {C_\text{ox}} = \frac{1 \, \mathrm{M}_\text{red}} {1 \, \mathrm{M}_\text{ox}} = 1$$

The term formal concentration (F) is now largely ignored in the current literature and can be commonly assimilated to molar concentration (M), or molality (m) in case of thermodynamic calculations.[^o17]

The formal potential is also found halfway between the two peaks in a cyclic [voltammogram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltammetry), where at this point the concentration of Ox (the oxidized species) and Red (the reduced species) at the electrode surface are equal.

The [activity coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) $\gamma_{red}$ and $\gamma_{ox}$ are included in the formal potential $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$, and because they depend on experimental conditions such as temperature, [ionic strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_strength), and [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH), $E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$ cannot be referred as an immutable standard potential but needs to be systematically determined for each specific set of experimental conditions.[^o15]

Formal reduction potentials are applied to simplify calculations of a considered system under given conditions and measurements interpretation. The experimental conditions in which they are determined and their relationship to the standard reduction potentials must be clearly described to avoid to confuse them with standard reduction potentials.

#### Formal standard reduction potential at pH 7 {#Formal_standard_reduction_potential_at_pH_7}

Formal standard reduction potentials ($E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$) are also commonly used in [biochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biochemistry) and [cell biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_biology) for referring to [standard reduction potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_reduction_potential) measured at pH 7, a value closer to the [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) of most physiological and intracellular fluids than the [standard state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_state) pH of 0. The advantage is to defining a more appropriate [redox scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) better corresponding to real conditions than the standard state. Formal standard reduction potentials ($E^{\ominus '}_\text{red}$) allow to more easily estimate if a [redox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) reaction supposed to occur in a [metabolic process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_process) or to fuel microbial activity under some conditions is feasible or not.

While, standard reduction potentials always refer to the [standard hydrogen electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_hydrogen_electrode) (SHE), with \[\] = 1 M corresponding to a pH 0, and $E^{\ominus}_\text{red H+}$ fixed arbitrarily to zero by convention, it is no longer the case at a pH of 7. Then, the reduction potential $E_\text{red}$ of a hydrogen electrode operating at pH 7 is −0.413 V with respect to the [standard hydrogen electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_hydrogen_electrode) (SHE).[^o19]

### Expression of the Nernst equation as a function of pH {#Expression_of_the_Nernst_equation_as_a_function_of_pH}

The $E_h$ and [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) of a solution are related by the Nernst equation as commonly represented by a [Pourbaix diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pourbaix_diagram) ($E_h$ – [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) plot). $E_h$ explicitly denotes $E_\text{red}$ expressed versus the [standard hydrogen electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_hydrogen_electrode) (SHE). For a [half cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half_cell) equation, conventionally written as a reduction reaction (*i.e.*, electrons accepted by an oxidant on the left side):

$$a \, A + b \, B + h \, \ce{H+} + z \, e^{-} \quad \ce{<=>} \quad c \, C + d \, D$$

The half-cell [standard reduction potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_reduction_potential) $E^{\ominus}_\text{red}$ is given by

$$E^{\ominus}_\text{red} (\text{volt}) = -\frac{\Delta G^\ominus}{zF}$$

where $\Delta G^\ominus$ is the standard [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) change, $z$ is the number of electrons involved, and $F$ is the [Faraday's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday's_constant). The Nernst equation relates pH and $E_h$ as follows:

$$E_h = E_\text{red} = E^{\ominus}_\text{red} - \frac{0.05916}{z} \log\left(\frac{\{C\}^c\{D\}^d}{\{A\}^a\{B\}^b}\right) - \frac{0.05916\,h}{z} \text{pH}$$

where curly brackets indicate [activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_%28chemistry%29), and exponents are shown in the conventional manner. This equation is the equation of a straight line for $E_\text{red}$ as a function of pH with a slope of $-0.05916\,\left(\frac{h}{z}\right)$ volt (pH has no units).

This equation predicts lower $E_\text{red}$ at higher pH values. This is observed for the reduction of O<sub>2</sub> into H<sub>2</sub>O, or OH<sup>−</sup>, and for the reduction of H<sup>\+</sup> into H<sub>2</sub>. $E_\text{red}$ is then often noted as $E_h$ to indicate that it refers to the [standard hydrogen electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_hydrogen_electrode) (SHE) whose $E_\text{red}$ = 0 by convention under standard conditions (T = 298.15 K = 25 °C = 77 F, P<sub>gas</sub> = 1 atm (1.013 bar), concentrations = 1 M and thus pH = 0).

#### Main factors affecting the formal standard reduction potentials {#Main_factors_affecting_the_formal_standard_reduction_potentials}

The main factor affecting the formal reduction potentials in biochemical or biological processes is most often the pH. To determine approximate values of formal reduction potentials, neglecting in a first approach changes in activity coefficients due to ionic strength, the Nernst equation has to be applied taking care to first express the relationship as a function of pH. The second factor to be considered are the values of the concentrations taken into account in the Nernst equation. To define a formal reduction potential for a biochemical reaction, the pH value, the concentrations values and the hypotheses made on the activity coefficients must always be explicitly indicated. When using, or comparing, several formal reduction potentials they must also be internally consistent.

Problems may occur when mixing different sources of data using different conventions or approximations (*i.e.*, with different underlying hypotheses). When working at the frontier between inorganic and biological processes (e.g., when comparing abiotic and biotic processes in [geochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geochemistry) when microbial activity could also be at work in the system), care must be taken not to inadvertently directly mix [standard reduction potentials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_reduction_potential) versus SHE (pH = 0) with formal reduction potentials (pH = 7). Definitions must be clearly expressed and carefully controlled, especially if the sources of data are different and arise from different fields (e.g., picking and mixing data from classical electrochemistry and microbiology textbooks without paying attention to the different conventions on which they are based).

#### Examples with a Pourbaix diagram {#Examples_with_a_Pourbaix_diagram}

*Image: PourbaixWater.png.* [Pourbaix diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pourbaix_diagram) for water, including stability regions for water, oxygen and hydrogen at [standard temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure) (STP). The vertical scale (ordinate) is the electrode potential relative to a [SHE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_hydrogen_electrode) electrode. The horizontal scale (abscissa) is the [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH) of the electrolyte (otherwise non-interacting). Above the top line oxygen will bubble off of the electrode until water is totally consumed. Likewise, below the bottom line hydrogen will bubble off of the electrode until water is totally consumed.

To illustrate the dependency of the reduction potential on pH, one can simply consider the two [oxido-reduction equilibria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) determining the water stability domain in a [Pourbaix diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pourbaix_diagram) (E<sub>h</sub>–pH plot). When water is submitted to [electrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolysis_of_water) by applying a sufficient difference of [electrical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galvanic_cell) between two [electrodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrode) immersed in water, [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) is produced at the [cathode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode) (reduction of water protons) while [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) is formed at the [anode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anode) (oxidation of water oxygen atoms). The same may occur if a reductant stronger than hydrogen (e.g., metallic Na) or an oxidant stronger than oxygen (e.g., F<sub>2</sub>) enters in contact with water and reacts with it. In the E<sub>h</sub>–pH plot here beside (the simplest possible version of a Pourbaix diagram), the water stability domain (grey surface) is delimited in term of redox potential by two inclined red dashed lines:

- Lower stability line with hydrogen gas evolution due to the proton reduction at very low E<sub>h</sub>:

$2 H+ + 2 e- <-> H2$(cathode: reduction)

- Higher stability line with oxygen gas evolution due to water oxygen oxidation at very high E<sub>h</sub>:

$2 H2O <-> O2 + 4 H+ + 4 e-$(anode: oxidation)

When solving the Nernst equation for each corresponding reduction reaction (need to revert the water oxidation reaction producing oxygen), both equations have a similar form because the number of protons and the number of electrons involved within a reaction are the same and their ratio is one (2/2 for H<sub>2</sub> and 4/4 with respectively), so it simplifies when solving the Nernst equation expressed as a function of pH.

The result can be numerically expressed as follows:

$$E_\text{red} = E^{\ominus}_\text{red} - 0.05916 \ pH$$

Note that the slopes of the two water stability domain upper and lower lines are the same (−59.16 mV/pH unit), so they are parallel on a [Pourbaix diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pourbaix_diagram). As the slopes are negative, at high pH, both hydrogen and [oxygen evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen_evolution) requires a much lower reduction potential than at low pH.

For the reduction of H<sup>\+</sup> into H<sub>2</sub> the here above mentioned relationship becomes:

$E_\text{red} = - 0.05916 \ pH$ because by convention $E^{\ominus}_\text{red}$ = 0 V for the [standard hydrogen electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_hydrogen_electrode) (SHE: pH = 0). So, at pH = 7, $E_\text{red}$ = −0.414 V for the reduction of protons.

For the reduction of O<sub>2</sub> into 2 H<sub>2</sub>O the here above mentioned relationship becomes:

$E_\text{red} = 1.229 - 0.05916 \ pH$ because $E^{\ominus}_\text{red}$ = +1.229 V with respect to the [standard hydrogen electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_hydrogen_electrode) (SHE: pH = 0). So, at pH = 7, $E_\text{red}$ = +0.815 V for the reduction of oxygen.

The offset of −414 mV in $E_\text{red}$ is the same for both reduction reactions because they share the same linear relationship as a function of pH and the slopes of their lines are the same. This can be directly verified on a Pourbaix diagram. For other reduction reactions, the value of the formal reduction potential at a pH of 7, commonly referred for biochemical reactions, also depends on the slope of the corresponding line in a Pourbaix diagram *i.e.* on the ratio *$\tfrac{h}{z}$* of the number of to the number of involved in the reduction reaction, and thus on the [stoichiometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometry) of the half-reaction. The determination of the formal reduction potential at pH = 7 for a given biochemical half-reaction requires thus to calculate it with the corresponding Nernst equation as a function of pH. One cannot simply apply an offset of −414 mV to the E<sub>h</sub> value (SHE) when the ratio *$\tfrac{h}{z}$* differs from 1.

## Applications in biology {#Applications_in_biology}

Beside [important redox reactions in biochemistry and microbiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Table_of_standard_reduction_potentials_for_half-reactions_important_in_biochemistry), the Nernst equation is also used in [physiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physiology) for calculating the [electric potential](wikipedia://en/59615) of a [cell membrane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_membrane) with respect to one type of [ion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion). It can be linked to the [acid dissociation constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid_dissociation_constant).

### Nernst potential {#Nernst_potential}

The Nernst equation has a physiological application when used to calculate the potential of an ion of charge $z$ across a membrane. This potential is determined using the concentration of the ion both inside and outside the cell:

$$E = \frac{R T}{z F} \ln\frac{[\text{ion outside cell}]}{[\text{ion inside cell}]} = 2.3026\frac{R T}{z F} \log_{10}\frac{[\text{ion outside cell}]}{[\text{ion inside cell}]}.$$

When the membrane is in [thermodynamic equilibrium](wikipedia://en/265823) (i.e., no net flux of ions), and if the cell is permeable to only one ion, then the [membrane potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membrane_potential) must be equal to the Nernst potential for that ion.

### Goldman equation {#Goldman_equation}

When the membrane is permeable to more than one ion, as is inevitably the case, the [resting potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resting_potential) can be determined from the Goldman equation, which is a solution of [G-H-K influx equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GHK_flux_equation) under the constraints that total current density driven by electrochemical force is zero:

$$E_\mathrm{m} = \frac{RT}{F} \ln{ \left( \frac{ \displaystyle\sum_i^N P_{\mathrm{M}^+_i}\left[\mathrm{M}^+_i\right]_\mathrm{out} + \displaystyle\sum_j^M P_{\mathrm{A}^-_j}\left[\mathrm{A}^-_j\right]_\mathrm{in}}{ \displaystyle\sum_i^N P_{\mathrm{M}^+_i}\left[\mathrm{M}^+_i\right]_\mathrm{in} + \displaystyle\sum_j^M P_{\mathrm{A}^-_j}\left[\mathrm{A}^-_j\right]_\mathrm{out}} \right) },$$

where

- $E_{m}$ is the membrane potential (in [volts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volt), equivalent to [joules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule) per [coulomb](wikipedia://en/47719)),
- $P_{\operatorname{ion}}$ is the permeability for that ion (in meters per second),
- $[\operatorname{ion}]_{\operatorname{out}}$ is the extracellular concentration of that ion (in [moles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29) per cubic meter, to match the other [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) units, though the units strictly don't matter, as the ion concentration terms become a dimensionless ratio),
- $[\operatorname{ion}]_{\text{in}}$ is the intracellular concentration of that ion (in moles per cubic meter),
- $R$ is the [ideal gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_gas_constant) (joules per [kelvin](wikipedia://en/19593121) per mole),
- $T$ is the temperature in [kelvins](wikipedia://en/19593121),
- $F$ is the [Faraday's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_constant) (coulombs per mole).

The potential across the cell membrane that exactly opposes net diffusion of a particular ion through the membrane is called the Nernst potential for that ion. As seen above, the magnitude of the Nernst potential is determined by the ratio of the concentrations of that specific ion on the two sides of the membrane. The greater this ratio the greater the tendency for the ion to diffuse in one direction, and therefore the greater the Nernst potential required to prevent the diffusion. A similar expression exists that includes $r$ (the absolute value of the transport ratio). This takes transporters with unequal exchanges into account. See: [sodium-potassium pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium-potassium_pump) where the transport ratio would be 2/3, so r equals 1.5 in the formula below. The reason why we insert a factor r = 1.5 here is that current density *by electrochemical force* J<sub>e.c.</sub>(Na<sup>\+</sup>) + J<sub>e.c.</sub>(K<sup>\+</sup>) is no longer zero, but rather J<sub>e.c.</sub>(Na<sup>\+</sup>) + 1.5J<sub>e.c.</sub>(K<sup>\+</sup>) = 0 (as for both ions flux by electrochemical force is compensated by that by the pump, i.e. J<sub>e.c.</sub> = −J<sub>pump</sub>), altering the constraints for applying GHK equation. The other variables are the same as above. The following example includes two ions: potassium (K<sup>\+</sup>) and sodium (Na<sup>\+</sup>). Chloride is assumed to be in equilibrium.

$$E_{m} = \frac{RT}{F} \ln{ \left( \frac{ rP_{\mathrm{K}^+}\left[\mathrm{K}^+\right]_\mathrm{out} + P_{\mathrm{Na}^+}\left[\mathrm{Na}^+\right]_\mathrm{out}}{ rP_{\mathrm{K}^+}\left[\mathrm{K}^+\right]_\mathrm{in} + P_{\mathrm{Na}^+}\left[\mathrm{Na}^+\right]_\mathrm{in}} \right) }.$$

When chloride (Cl<sup>−</sup>) is taken into account,

$$E_{m} = \frac{RT}{F} \ln{ \left( \frac{r P_{\mathrm{K}^+}\left[\mathrm{K}^+\right]_\mathrm{out} + P_{\mathrm{Na}^+}\left[\mathrm{Na}^+\right]_\mathrm{out} + P_{\mathrm{Cl}^-}\left[\mathrm{Cl}^-\right]_\mathrm{in}}{r P_{\mathrm{K}^+}\left[\mathrm{K}^+\right]_\mathrm{in} + P_{\mathrm{Na}^+}\left[\mathrm{Na}^+\right]_\mathrm{in} + P_{\mathrm{Cl}^-}\left[\mathrm{Cl}^-\right]_\mathrm{out}} \right) }.$$

## Derivation {#Derivation}

### Using Boltzmann factor {#Using_Boltzmann_factor}

For simplicity, we will consider a solution of redox-active molecules that undergo a one-electron [reversible reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reversible_reaction)

$$\operatorname{Ox} + e^{-} \operatorname{Red}$$

and that have a standard potential of zero, and in which the activities are well represented by the concentrations (i.e. unit activity coefficient). The [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628) $\mu_{c}$ of this solution is the difference between the energy barriers for taking electrons from and for giving electrons to the [working electrode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Working_electrode) that is setting the solution's [electrochemical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_potential). The ratio of oxidized to reduced molecules, $\tfrac{[\operatorname{Ox}]}{[\operatorname{Red}]}$, is equivalent to the probability of being oxidized (giving electrons) over the probability of being reduced (taking electrons), which we can write in terms of the [Boltzmann factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_factor) for these processes:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{[\mathrm{Red}]}{[\mathrm{Ox}]} &= \frac{\exp \left(-[\text{barrier for gaining an electron}]/kT\right)}{\exp \left(-[\text{barrier for losing an electron}]/kT\right)}\\[6px] &= \exp \left(\frac{\mu_\mathrm{c}}{kT} \right). \end{aligned}$$

Taking the natural logarithm of both sides gives

$$\mu_\mathrm{c} = kT \ln \frac{[\mathrm{Red}]}{[\mathrm{Ox}]}.$$

If $\mu_{c} \ne 0$ at $\tfrac{[\operatorname{Ox}]}{[\operatorname{Red}]}$ = 1, we need to add in this additional constant:

$$\mu_\mathrm{c} = \mu_\mathrm{c}^\ominus + kT \ln \frac{[\mathrm{Red}]}{[\mathrm{Ox}]}.$$

Dividing the equation by $e$ to convert from chemical potentials to electrode potentials, and remembering that $\tfrac{k}{e} = \tfrac{R}{F}$,[^o28] we obtain the Nernst equation for the one-electron process $\operatorname{Ox} + e^{-} \operatorname{Red}$:

$$\begin{aligned} E &= E^\ominus - \frac{kT}{e} \ln \frac{[\mathrm{Red}]}{[\mathrm{Ox}]} \\ &= E^\ominus - \frac{RT}{F} \ln \frac{[\mathrm{Red}]}{[\mathrm{Ox}]}. \end{aligned}$$

### Using thermodynamics (chemical potential) {#Using_thermodynamics_(chemical_potential)}

Quantities here are given per molecule, not per [mole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29), and so [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant) $k$ and the [electron charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_charge) $e$ are used instead of the [gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant) $R$ and [Faraday's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_constant) $F$. To convert to the molar quantities given in most chemistry textbooks, it is simply necessary to multiply by the [Avogadro constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avogadro_constant): $R = kN_{A}$ and $F = eN_{A}$. The [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) of a molecule is defined as

$$S \ \stackrel{\mathrm{def}}{=}\ k \ln \Omega,$$

where $\Omega$ is the number of states available to the molecule. The number of states must vary linearly with the volume $V$ of the system (here an idealized system is considered for better understanding, so that activities are posited very close to the true concentrations). Fundamental statistical proof of the mentioned linearity goes beyond the scope of this section, but to see this is true it is simpler to consider usual [isothermal process](wikipedia://en/409951) for an [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905) where the change of entropy $\Delta S = nR \ln(\tfrac{V_{2}}{V_{1}})$ takes place. It follows from the definition of entropy and from the condition of constant temperature and quantity of gas $n$ that the change in the number of states must be proportional to the relative change in volume $\tfrac{V_{2}}{V_{1}}$. In this sense there is no difference in statistical properties of ideal gas atoms compared with the dissolved species of a solution with [activity coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) equaling one: particles freely "hang around" filling the provided volume), which is inversely proportional to the [concentration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_concentration) $c$, so we can also write the entropy as

$$S = k\ln \ (\mathrm{constant}\times V) = -k\ln \ (\mathrm{constant}\times c).$$

The change in entropy from some state 1 to another state 2 is therefore

$$\Delta S = S_2 - S_1 = - k \ln \frac{c_2}{c_1},$$

so that the entropy of state 2 is

$$S_2 = S_1 - k \ln \frac{c_2}{c_1}.$$

If state 1 is at standard conditions, in which $c_{1}$ is unity (e.g., 1 atm or 1 M), it will merely cancel the units of $c_{2}$. We can, therefore, write the entropy of an arbitrary molecule A as

$$S(\mathrm{A}) = S^\ominus(\mathrm{A}) - k \ln [\mathrm{A}],$$

where $S^\ominus$ is the entropy at [standard conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure) and \[A\] denotes the concentration of A. The change in entropy for a reaction

is then given by

$$\Delta S_\mathrm{rxn} = \big(yS(\mathrm{Y}) + zS(\mathrm{Z})\big) - \big(aS(\mathrm{A}) + bS(\mathrm{B})\big) = \Delta S^\ominus_\mathrm{rxn} - k \ln \frac{[\mathrm{Y}]^y [\mathrm{Z}]^z}{[\mathrm{A}]^a [\mathrm{B}]^b}.$$

We define the ratio in the last term as the [reaction quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_quotient):

$$Q_r = \frac{\displaystyle\prod_j a_j^{\nu_j}}{\displaystyle\prod_i a_i^{\nu_i}} \approx \frac{[\mathrm{Z}]^z [\mathrm{Y}]^y}{[\mathrm{A}]^a [\mathrm{B}]^b},$$

where the numerator is a product of reaction product [activities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_activity), $a_{j}$, each raised to the power of a [stoichiometric coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometric_coefficient), $\nu_{j}$, and the denominator is a similar product of reactant activities. All activities refer to a time $t$. Under certain circumstances (see [chemical equilibrium](wikipedia://en/5306)), each activity term, such as $a^{\nu_{j}}_{j}$ may be replaced by a concentration term, \[A\].In an electrochemical cell, the cell potential $E$ is the [chemical potential](wikipedia://en/218628) available from [redox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) reactions ($E = \tfrac{\mu_{c}}{e}$). $E$ is related to the [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) change $\Delta G$ only by a constant: $\Delta G = -zFE$, where $z$ is the number of electrons transferred and $F$ is the [Faraday constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faraday_constant). There is a negative sign because a spontaneous reaction has a negative [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) $\Delta G$ and a positive potential $E$. The Gibbs free energy is related to the entropy by $G = H - TS$, where $H$ is the [enthalpy](wikipedia://en/10274) and $T$ is the temperature of the system. Using these relations, we can now write the change in Gibbs free energy,

$$\Delta G = \Delta H - T \Delta S = \Delta G^\ominus + kT \ln Q_r,$$

and the cell potential,

$$E = E^\ominus - \frac{kT}{ze} \ln Q_r.$$

This is the more general form of the Nernst equation.

For the redox reaction Ox + $z$ e<sup>−</sup> → Red,

$$Q_r = \frac{[\mathrm{Red}]}{[\mathrm{Ox}]},$$

and we have:

$$\begin{aligned} E &= E^\ominus - \frac{kT}{ze} \ln \frac{[\mathrm{Red}]}{[\mathrm{Ox}]} \\ &= E^\ominus - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln \frac{[\mathrm{Red}]}{[\mathrm{Ox}]} \\ &= E^\ominus - \frac{RT}{zF} \ln Q_r. \end{aligned}$$

The cell potential at [standard temperature and pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_temperature_and_pressure) (STP) $E^\ominus$ is often replaced by the formal potential $E^{\ominus'}$, which includes the [activity coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Activity_coefficient) of the dissolved species under given experimental conditions (T, P, [ionic strength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_strength), [pH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PH), and complexing agents) and is the potential that is actually measured in an electrochemical cell.

## Relation to the chemical equilibrium {#Relation_to_the_chemical_equilibrium}

The standard [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) $\Delta G^\ominus$ is related to the [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant) $K$ as follows:[^o31]

$$\Delta G^\ominus = -RT \ln{K}$$

At the same time, $\Delta G^\ominus$ is also equal to the product of the total charge ($zF$) transferred during the reaction and the cell potential ($E^\ominus_{cell}$):

$$\Delta G^\ominus = -zF E^\ominus_{cell}$$

The sign is negative, because the considered system performs the work and thus releases energy.

So,

$$-zFE^\ominus_{cell} = -RT \ln{K}$$

And therefore:

$$E^\ominus_{cell} = \frac{RT} {zF} \ln{K}$$

Starting from the Nernst equation, one can also demonstrate the same relationship in the reverse way.

At [chemical equilibrium](wikipedia://en/5306), or [thermodynamic equilibrium](wikipedia://en/265823), the [electrochemical potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_potential) $(E) = 0$ and therefore the [reaction quotient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_quotient) ($Q_{r}$) attains the special value known as the [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant) ($K_{\operatorname{eq}}$):

$$Q_{r} = K_{\operatorname{eq}}$$

Therefore,

$$\begin{aligned} 0 &= E^\ominus - \frac{RT}{z F} \ln K \\ \frac{RT}{z F} \ln K & = E^\ominus \\ \ln K &= \frac{z F E^\ominus}{RT} \end{aligned}$$

Or at [standard state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_state),

$$\log_{10} K = \frac{zE^\ominus}{\lambda V_T} = \frac{zE^\ominus}{0.05916\text{ V}} \quad\text{at }T = 298.15~\text{K}$$

We have thus related the [standard electrode potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_electrode_potential) and the [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant) of a redox reaction.

## Limitations {#Limitations}

In dilute solutions, the Nernst equation can be expressed directly in the terms of concentrations (since activity coefficients are close to unity). But at higher concentrations, the true activities of the ions must be used. This complicates the use of the Nernst equation, since estimation of non-ideal activities of ions generally requires experimental measurements. The Nernst equation also only applies when there is no net current flow through the electrode. The activity of ions at the electrode surface changes [when there is current flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrochemical_kinetics), and there are additional [overpotential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Overpotential) and resistive loss terms which contribute to the measured potential.

At very low concentrations of the potential-determining ions, the potential predicted by Nernst equation approaches toward $\pm\infty$. This is physically meaningless because, under such conditions, the [exchange current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_current_density) becomes very low, and there may be no thermodynamic equilibrium necessary for Nernst equation to hold. The electrode is called unpoised in such case. Other effects tend to take control of the electrochemical behavior of the system, like the involvement of the [solvated electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solvated_electron) in electricity transfer and electrode equilibria, as analyzed by [Alexander Frumkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Frumkin) and B. Damaskin,[^o33] Sergio Trasatti, etc.

## Significance in other scientific fields {#Significance_in_other_scientific_fields}

The Nernst equation has been involved in the scientific controversy about [cold fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_fusion). Fleischmann and Pons, claiming that cold fusion could exist, calculated that a [palladium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palladium) [cathode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathode) immersed in a [heavy water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_water) electrolysis cell could achieve up to 10<sup>27</sup> atmospheres of pressure inside the [crystal lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_lattice) of the metal of the cathode, enough pressure to cause spontaneous [nuclear fusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_fusion). In reality, only 10,000–20,000 atmospheres were achieved. The American physicist [John R. Huizenga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_R._Huizenga) claimed their original calculation was affected by a misinterpretation of the Nernst equation.[^o40] He cited a paper about Pd–Zr [alloys](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alloy).[^o41]

The Nernst equation allows the calculation of the extent of reaction between two [redox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Redox) systems and can be used, for example, to assess whether a particular reaction will go to completion or not. At [chemical equilibrium](wikipedia://en/5306), the [electromotive forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromotive_force) (emf) of the two half cells are equal. This allows the [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant) $K$ of the reaction to be calculated and hence the extent of the reaction.
