# Residue theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Residue theorem"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), the **residue theorem**, sometimes called **Cauchy's residue theorem**, is a powerful tool to evaluate [line integrals](wikipedia://en/21245414) of [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) over closed curves; it can often be used to compute real integrals and [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) as well. It generalizes the [Cauchy integral theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_integral_theorem) and [Cauchy's integral formula](wikipedia://en/72827). The **residue theorem** should not be confused with special cases of the [generalized Stokes' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Stokes'_theorem); however, the latter can be used as a component of its proof.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Statement of Cauchy's residue theorem"}

## Statement of Cauchy's residue theorem {#Statement_of_Cauchy's_residue_theorem}

The statement is as follows:

> *Image: Residue theorem illustration.svg.* Illustration of the setting
>
> **Residue theorem**: Let $U$ be a [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) containing a finite list of points $a_1, \ldots, a_n$, $U_0 = U \smallsetminus \{a_1, \ldots, a_n\}$, and a function $f$ [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) on $U_0$. Letting $\gamma$ be a closed [rectifiable curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifiable_curve) in $U_0$, and denoting the [residue](wikipedia://en/73102) of $f$ at each point $a_k$ by $\operatorname{Res}(f, a_k)$ and the [winding number](wikipedia://en/86113) of $\gamma$ around $a_k$ by $\operatorname{I}(\gamma, a_k)$, the line integral of $f$ around $\gamma$ is equal to $2\pi i$ times the sum of residues, each counted as many times as $\gamma$ winds around the respective point:
>
> $$\oint_\gamma f(z)\, dz = 2\pi i \sum_{k=1}^n \operatorname{I}(\gamma, a_k) \operatorname{Res}(f, a_k).$$
>
> If $\gamma$ is a [positively oriented](wikipedia://en/3633706) [simple closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve), $\operatorname{I}(\gamma, a_k)$ is $1$ if $a_k$ is in the interior of $\gamma$ and $0$ if not, therefore
>
> $$\oint_\gamma f(z)\, dz = 2\pi i \sum \operatorname{Res}(f, a_k)$$
>
> with the sum over those $a_k$ inside $\gamma$.[^o4]

The relationship of the residue theorem to Stokes' theorem is given by the [Jordan curve theorem](wikipedia://en/267444). The general [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) $\gamma$ must first be reduced to a set of simple closed curves $\{\gamma_i\}$ whose total is equivalent to $\gamma$ for integration purposes; this reduces the problem to finding the integral of $f\, dz$ along a Jordan curve $\gamma_i$ with interior $V$. The requirement that $f$ be holomorphic on $U_0 = U \smallsetminus \{a_k\}$ is equivalent to the statement that the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) $d(f\, dz) = 0$ on $U_0$. Thus if two planar regions $V$ and $W$ of $U$ enclose the same subset $\{a_j\}$ of $\{a_k\}$, the regions $V \smallsetminus W$ and $W \smallsetminus V$ lie entirely in $U_0$, hence

$$\int_{V \smallsetminus W} d(f \, dz) - \int_{W \smallsetminus V} d(f \, dz)$$

is well-defined and equal to zero. Consequently, the contour integral of $f\, dz$ along $\gamma_j = \partial V$ is equal to the sum of a set of integrals along paths $\gamma_j$, each enclosing an arbitrarily small region around a single $a_j$ — the residues of $f$ (up to the conventional factor $2\pi i$ at $\{a_j\}$. Summing over $\{\gamma_j\}$, we recover the final expression of the contour integral in terms of the winding numbers $\{\operatorname{I}(\gamma, a_k)\}$.

In order to evaluate real integrals, the residue theorem is used in the following manner: the integrand is extended to the complex plane and its residues are computed (which is usually easy), and a part of the real axis is extended to a closed curve by attaching a half-circle in the upper or lower half-plane, forming a semicircle. The integral over this curve can then be computed using the residue theorem. Often, the half-circle part of the integral will tend towards zero as the radius of the half-circle grows, leaving only the real-axis part of the integral, the one we were originally interested in.

## Examples {#Examples}

### An integral along the real axis {#An_integral_along_the_real_axis}

The integral

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{itx}}{x^2+1}\,dx$$

*Image: Contour example.svg.* The contour $C$

arises in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) when calculating the [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/3229401) of the [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003). It resists the techniques of elementary [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) but can be evaluated by expressing it as a limit of [contour integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_integral).

Suppose $t > 0$ and define the contour $C$ that goes along the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) line from $-a$ to $a$ and then counterclockwise along a semicircle centered at 0 from $a$ to $-a$. Take $a$ to be greater than 1, so that the [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) unit $i$ is enclosed within the curve. Now consider the contour integral

$$\int_C {f(z)}\,dz = \int_C \frac{e^{itz}}{z^2+1}\,dz.$$

Since $e^{itz}$ is an [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372) (having no [singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) at any point in the complex plane), this function has singularities only where the denominator $z^{2} + 1$ is zero. Since $z^{2} + 1 = (z + i)(z - i)$, that happens only where $z = i$ or $z = -i$. Only one of those points is in the region bounded by this contour. Because $f(z)$ is

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{e^{itz}}{z^2+1} & =\frac{e^{itz}}{2i}\left(\frac{1}{z-i}-\frac{1}{z+i}\right) \\ & =\frac{e^{itz}}{2i(z-i)} -\frac{e^{itz}}{2i(z+i)} , \end{aligned}$$

the [residue](wikipedia://en/73102) of $f(z)$ at $z = i$ is

$$\operatorname{Res}_{z=i}f(z)=\frac{e^{-t}}{2i}.$$

According to the residue theorem, then, we have

$$\int_C f(z)\,dz=2\pi i\cdot\operatorname{Res}\limits_{z=i}f(z)=2\pi i \frac{e^{-t}}{2i} = \pi e^{-t}.$$

The contour $C$ may be split into a straight part and a curved arc, so that

$$\int_{\mathrm{straight}} f(z)\,dz+\int_{\mathrm{arc}} f(z)\,dz=\pi e^{-t}$$

and thus

$$\int_{-a}^a f(z)\,dz =\pi e^{-t}-\int_{\mathrm{arc}} f(z)\,dz.$$

Using some [estimations](wikipedia://en/5676427), we have

$$\left|\int_{\mathrm{arc}}\frac{e^{itz}}{z^2+1}\,dz\right| \leq \pi a \cdot \sup_{\text{arc}} \left| \frac{e^{itz}}{z^2+1} \right| \leq \pi a \cdot \sup_{\text{arc}} \frac{1}{|z^2+1|} \leq \frac{\pi a}{a^2 - 1},$$

and

$$\lim_{a \to \infty} \frac{\pi a}{a^2-1} = 0.$$

The estimate on the numerator follows since $t > 0$, and for [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $z$ along the arc (which lies in the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826)), the argument $\varphi$ of $z$ lies between 0 and $\pi$. So,

$$\left|e^{itz}\right| = \left|e^{it|z|(\cos\varphi + i\sin\varphi)}\right|=\left|e^{-t|z|\sin\varphi + it|z|\cos\varphi}\right|=e^{-t|z| \sin\varphi} \le 1.$$

Therefore,

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \frac{e^{itz}}{z^2+1}\,dz=\pi e^{-t}.$$

If $t < 0$ then a similar argument with an arc $C$ that winds around $-i$ rather than $i$ shows that

*Image: Contour example 2.svg.* The contour $C$

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{itz}}{z^2+1}\,dz=\pi e^t,$$

and finally we have

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty\frac{e^{itz}}{z^2+1}\,dz=\pi e^{-\left|t\right|}.$$

(If $t = 0$ then the integral yields immediately to elementary calculus methods and its value is $\pi$.)

### Evaluating zeta functions {#Evaluating_zeta_functions}

The fact that $\pi \cot(\pi z)$ has simple poles with residue 1 at each [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) can be used to compute the sum

$$\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty f(n).$$

Consider, for example, $f(z) = z^{-2}$. Let $\Gamma_{N}$ be the rectangle that is the boundary of $[-N - \tfrac{1}{2}, N + \tfrac{1}{2}]^{2}$ with positive orientation, with an integer $N$. By the residue formula,

$$\frac{1}{2 \pi i} \int_{\Gamma_N} f(z) \pi \cot(\pi z) \, dz = \operatorname{Res}\limits_{z = 0} + \sum_{n = -N \atop n\ne 0}^N n^{-2}.$$

The left-hand side goes to zero as $N \to \infty$ since $|\cot(\pi z)|$ is uniformly bounded on the contour, thanks to using $x = \pm \left(\frac 12 + N\right)$ on the left and right side of the contour, and so the integrand has order $O(N^{-2})$ over the entire contour. On the other hand,[^o10]

$$\frac{z}{2} \cot\left(\frac{z}{2}\right) = 1 - B_2 \frac{z^2}{2!} + \cdots$$

where the [Bernoulli number](wikipedia://en/4964) $B_2 = \frac{1}{6}$.

(In fact, $\tfrac{z}{2} \cot(\tfrac{z}{2}) = \tfrac{iz}{1 - e^{-iz}} - \tfrac{iz}{2}$.) Thus, the residue $\operatorname{Res}_{z=0}$ is $-\tfrac{\pi^{2}}{3}$. We conclude:

$$\sum_{n = 1}^\infty \frac{1}{n^2} = \frac{\pi^2}{6}$$

which is a proof of the [Basel problem](wikipedia://en/490303).

The same argument works for all $f(x) = x^{-2n}$ where $n$ is a positive integer, [giving us](wikipedia://en/2334098)

$$\zeta(2n) = \frac{(-1)^{n+1}B_{2n}(2\pi)^{2n}}{2(2n)!}.$$

The trick does not work when $f(x) = x^{-2n-1}$, since in this case, the residue at zero vanishes, and we obtain the useless identity $0 + \zeta(2n+1) - \zeta(2n+1) = 0$.

### Evaluating Eisenstein series {#Evaluating_Eisenstein_series}

The same trick can be used to establish the sum of the [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458):

$$\pi \cot(\pi z) = \lim_{N \to \infty} \sum_{n=-N}^N (z - n)^{-1}.$$

::: {#o12 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof.**

Pick an arbitrary $w \in \mathbb C\setminus \mathbb{Z}$. As above, define

$$g(z) := \frac{1}{w-z} \pi \cot(\pi z)$$

By the Cauchy residue theorem, for all $N$ large enough such that $\Gamma_N$ encircles $w$,

$$\frac{1}{2 \pi i} \oint_{\Gamma_N} g(z) dz = -\pi \cot(\pi w) + \sum_{n=-N}^N \frac{1}{w-n}$$

It remains to prove the integral converges to zero. Since $\pi\cot(\pi z) /z$ is an even function, and $\Gamma_N$ is symmetric about the origin, we have $\oint_{\Gamma_N} \pi\cot(\pi z) /z dz = 0$, and so

$$\oint_{\Gamma_N} g(z) dz = \oint_{\Gamma_N} \left(\frac 1z + \frac{1}{w-z}\right) \pi\cot(\pi z)dz = -w \oint_{\Gamma_N} \frac{1}{z(z-w)} \pi\cot(\pi z) dz = O(1/N)$$

:::
