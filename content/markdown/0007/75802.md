# Consistency

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Consistency"}

In [deductive logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_logic), a **consistent** [theory](wikipedia://en/3512103) is one that does not lead to a logical [contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807).[^o2] A theory $T$ is consistent if there is no [formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formula_%28mathematical_logic%29) $\varphi$ such that both $\varphi$ and its negation $\lnot\varphi$ are elements of the set of consequences of $T$. Let $A$ be a set of [closed sentences](wikipedia://en/585143) (informally "axioms") and $\langle A\rangle$ the set of closed sentences provable from $A$ under some (specified, possibly implicitly) formal deductive system. The set of axioms $A$ is **consistent** when there is no formula $\varphi$ such that $\varphi \in \langle A \rangle$ and $\lnot \varphi \in \langle A \rangle$. A *trivial* theory (i.e., one which proves every sentence in the language of the theory) is clearly inconsistent. Conversely, in an [explosive](wikipedia://en/591394) [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102) (e.g., classical or intuitionistic propositional or first-order logics) every inconsistent theory is trivial.[^o3] Consistency of a theory is a [syntactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syntactic) notion, whose [semantic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic) counterpart is [satisfiability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisfiable_theory). A theory is satisfiable if it has a [model](wikipedia://en/19858#First-order_logic), i.e., there exists an [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671) under which all [axioms](wikipedia://en/928) in the theory are true.[^o4] This is what *consistent* meant in traditional [Aristotelian logic](wikipedia://en/321652), although in contemporary mathematical logic the term *[satisfiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satisfiable)* is used instead.

:::

In a [sound formal system](wikipedia://en/26819), every satisfiable theory is consistent, but the converse does not hold. If there exists a deductive system for which these semantic and syntactic definitions are equivalent for any theory formulated in a particular deductive [logic](wikipedia://en/19636#Formal_logical_systems), the logic is called **[complete](wikipedia://en/31271451#Refutation-completeness)**. The completeness of the [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) was proved by [Paul Bernays](wikipedia://en/2556580) in 1918[^o5] and [Emil Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Post) in 1921,[^o6] while the completeness of (first order) [predicate calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_calculus) was proved by [Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) in 1930,[^o7] and consistency proofs for arithmetics restricted with respect to the [induction axiom schema](wikipedia://en/18881) were proved by Ackermann (1924), von Neumann (1927) and Herbrand (1931).[^o8] Stronger logics, such as [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428), are not complete.

A **consistency proof** is a [mathematical proof](wikipedia://en/82285) that a particular theory is consistent.[^o9] The early development of mathematical [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478) was driven by the desire to provide finitary consistency proofs for all of mathematics as part of [Hilbert's program](wikipedia://en/607286). Hilbert's program was strongly impacted by the [incompleteness theorems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incompleteness_theorems), which showed that sufficiently strong proof theories cannot prove their consistency (provided that they are consistent).

Although consistency can be proved using model theory, it is often done in a purely syntactical way, without any need to reference some model of the logic. The [cut-elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut-elimination) (or equivalently the [normalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalization_property) of the [underlying calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curry-Howard) if there is one) implies the consistency of the calculus: since there is no cut-free proof of falsity, there is no contradiction in general.

## Consistency and completeness in arithmetic and set theory {#Consistency_and_completeness_in_arithmetic_and_set_theory}

In theories of arithmetic, such as [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic), there is an intricate relationship between the consistency of the theory and its [completeness](wikipedia://en/31271451). A theory is complete if, for every formula φ in its language, at least one of φ or ¬φ is a logical consequence of the theory.[^o11]

[Presburger arithmetic](wikipedia://en/23756) is an axiom system for the natural numbers under addition. It is both consistent and complete.

[Gödel's incompleteness theorems](wikipedia://en/58863) show that any sufficiently strong [recursively enumerable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursively_enumerable) theory of arithmetic cannot be both complete and consistent. Gödel's theorem applies to the theories of [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic) (PA) and [primitive recursive arithmetic](wikipedia://en/6637022) (PRA), but not to [Presburger arithmetic](wikipedia://en/23756).

Moreover, Gödel's second incompleteness theorem shows that the consistency of sufficiently strong recursively enumerable theories of arithmetic can be tested in a particular way. Such a theory is consistent if and only if it does *not* prove a particular sentence, called the Gödel sentence of the theory, which is a formalized statement of the claim that the theory is indeed consistent. Thus the consistency of a sufficiently strong, recursively enumerable, consistent theory of arithmetic can never be proven in that system itself. The same result is true for recursively enumerable theories that can describe a strong enough fragment of arithmetic—including set theories such as [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) (ZF). These set theories cannot prove their own Gödel sentence—provided that they are consistent, which is generally believed.

Because consistency of ZF is not provable in ZF, the weaker notion **relative consistency** is interesting in set theory (and in other sufficiently expressive axiomatic systems). If *T* is a [theory](wikipedia://en/3512103) and *A* is an additional [axiom](wikipedia://en/928), *T* + *A* is said to be consistent relative to *T* (or simply that *A* is consistent with *T*) if it can be proved that if *T* is consistent then *T* + *A* is consistent. If both *A* and ¬*A* are consistent with *T*, then *A* is said to be [independent](wikipedia://en/1141208) of *T*.

## First-order logic {#First-order_logic}

::: {#o14 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

### Notation {#Notation}

In the following context of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), the [turnstile symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turnstile_symbol) $\vdash$ means "provable from". That is, $a\vdash b$ reads: *b* is provable from *a* (in some specified formal system).

:::

::: {#o16 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

- A set of [formulas](wikipedia://en/404582) $\Phi$ in first-order logic is **consistent** (written $\operatorname{Con} \Phi$) if there is no formula $\varphi$ such that $\Phi \vdash \varphi$ and $\Phi \vdash \lnot\varphi$. Otherwise $\Phi$ is **inconsistent** (written $\operatorname{Inc}\Phi$).
- $\Phi$ is said to be **simply consistent** if for no formula $\varphi$ of $\Phi$, both $\varphi$ and the [negation](wikipedia://en/161019) of $\varphi$ are theorems of $\Phi$.
- $\Phi$ is said to be **absolutely consistent** or **Post consistent** if at least one formula in the language of $\Phi$ is not a theorem of $\Phi$.
- $\Phi$ is said to be **maximally consistent** if $\Phi$ is consistent and for every formula $\varphi$, $\operatorname{Con} (\Phi \cup \{\varphi\})$ implies $\varphi \in \Phi$.
- $\Phi$ is said to **contain witnesses** if for every formula of the form $\exists x \,\varphi$ there exists a [term](wikipedia://en/24885593) $t$ such that $(\exists x \, \varphi \to \varphi {t \over x}) \in \Phi$, where $\varphi {t \over x}$ denotes the [substitution](wikipedia://en/5438948) of each $x$ in $\varphi$ by a $t$; see also [First-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983).

:::

### Basic results {#Basic_results}

1. The following are equivalent:
   1. $$\operatorname{Inc}\Phi$$
   1. For all $\varphi,\; \Phi \vdash \varphi.$
1. Every satisfiable set of formulas is consistent, where a set of formulas $\Phi$ is satisfiable if and only if there exists a model $\mathfrak{I}$ such that $\mathfrak{I} \vDash \Phi$.
1. For all $\Phi$ and $\varphi$:
   1. if not $\Phi \vdash \varphi$, then $\operatorname{Con}\left( \Phi \cup \{\lnot\varphi\}\right)$;
   1. if $\operatorname{Con}\Phi$ and $\Phi \vdash \varphi$, then $\operatorname{Con} \left(\Phi \cup \{\varphi\}\right)$;
   1. if $\operatorname{Con}\Phi$, then $\operatorname{Con}\left( \Phi \cup \{\varphi\}\right)$ or $\operatorname{Con}\left( \Phi \cup \{\lnot \varphi\}\right)$.
1. Let $\Phi$ be a maximally consistent set of formulas and suppose it contains [witnesses](wikipedia://en/24879634). For all $\varphi$ and $\psi$:
   1. if $\Phi \vdash \varphi$, then $\varphi \in \Phi$,
   1. either $\varphi \in \Phi$ or $\lnot \varphi \in \Phi$,
   1. $(\varphi \lor \psi) \in \Phi$ if and only if $\varphi \in \Phi$ or $\psi \in \Phi$,
   1. if $(\varphi\to\psi) \in \Phi$ and $\varphi \in \Phi$, then $\psi \in \Phi$,
   1. $\exists x \, \varphi \in \Phi$ if and only if there is a term $t$ such that $\varphi{t \over x}\in\Phi$.

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Henkin's theorem"}

### Henkin's theorem {#Henkin's_theorem}

Let $S$ be a [set of symbols](wikipedia://en/7788156). Let $\Phi$ be a maximally consistent set of $S$-formulas containing [witnesses](wikipedia://en/24879634#Henkin_witnesses).

Define an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $\sim$ on the set of $S$-terms by $t_0 \sim t_1$ if $\; t_0 \equiv t_1 \in \Phi$, where $\equiv$ denotes [equality](wikipedia://en/10983#Equality_and_its_axioms). Let $\overline t$ denote the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of terms containing $t$; and let $T_\Phi := \{ \; \overline t \mid t \in T^S \}$ where $T^S$ is the set of terms based on the set of symbols $S$.

Define the $S$-[structure](wikipedia://en/4055928) $\mathfrak T_\Phi$ over $T_\Phi$, also called the **term-structure** corresponding to $\Phi$, by:

1. for each $n$-ary relation symbol $R \in S$, define $R^{\mathfrak T_\Phi} \overline {t_0} \ldots \overline {t_{n-1}}$ if $\; R t_0 \ldots t_{n-1} \in \Phi;$[^o20]
1. for each $n$-ary function symbol $f \in S$, define $f^{\mathfrak T_\Phi} (\overline {t_0} \ldots \overline {t_{n-1}}) := \overline {f t_0 \ldots t_{n-1}};$
1. for each constant symbol $c \in S$, define $c^{\mathfrak T_\Phi}:= \overline c.$

Define a variable assignment $\beta_\Phi$ by $\beta_\Phi (x) := \bar x$ for each variable $x$. Let $\mathfrak I_\Phi := (\mathfrak T_\Phi,\beta_\Phi)$ be the **term [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671#First-order_logic)** associated with $\Phi$.

Then for each $S$-formula $\varphi$:

$\mathfrak I_\Phi \vDash \varphi$ if and only if $\; \varphi \in \Phi.$

:::

::: {#o22 .proof cue=heading name="Sketch of proof"}

### Sketch of proof {#Sketch_of_proof}

There are several things to verify. First, that $\sim$ is in fact an equivalence relation. Then, it needs to be verified that (1), (2), and (3) are well defined. This falls out of the fact that $\sim$ is an equivalence relation and also requires a proof that (1) and (2) are independent of the choice of $t_0, \ldots ,t_{n-1}$ class representatives. Finally, $\mathfrak I_\Phi \vDash \varphi$ can be verified by induction on formulas.

:::

## Model theory {#Model_theory}

In [ZFC set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) with classical [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983),[^o24] an **inconsistent** theory $T$ is one such that there exists a closed sentence $\varphi$ such that $T$ contains both $\varphi$ and its negation $\varphi'$. A **consistent** theory is one such that the following [logically equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) conditions hold

1. $\{\varphi,\varphi'\}\not\subseteq T$[^o25]
1. $$\varphi'\not\in T \lor \varphi\not\in T$$
