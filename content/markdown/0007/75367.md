# Non-Newtonian fluid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-Newtonian fluid"}

In [physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry) and [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988), a **non-Newtonian fluid** is a [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) that does not follow [Newton's law of viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_viscosity); that is, it has variable viscosity dependent on [stress](wikipedia://en/228107). In particular, the viscosity of non-Newtonian fluids can change when subjected to force. [Ketchup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketchup), for example, becomes runnier when shaken and is thus a non-Newtonian fluid. Many [salt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salt) solutions and molten polymers are non-Newtonian fluids, as are many commonly found substances such as [custard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Custard),[^o2] [toothpaste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toothpaste), [starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch) suspensions, [paint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paint), [blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood), melted [butter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butter) and [shampoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shampoo).

:::

A common demonstration of non-Newtonian fluids involves so-called "Ooblek" (), a mixture of corn or potato starch and water. It demonstrates [shear thickening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_thickening). With slow motions it is a moderately viscous fluid, increases in viscosity as disturbed, and briefly transforms into a near solid mass upon a sudden impact.[^o3]

Most commonly, the [viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754) (the gradual deformation by shear or tensile stresses) of non-Newtonian fluids is dependent on [shear rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_rate) or shear rate history. Some non-Newtonian fluids with shear-independent viscosity, however, still exhibit normal stress-differences or other non-Newtonian behavior. In a Newtonian fluid, the relation between the [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) and the shear rate is linear, passing through the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432), the constant of proportionality being the coefficient of viscosity. In a non-Newtonian fluid, the relation between the shear stress and the shear rate is different. The fluid can even exhibit [time-dependent viscosity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-dependent_viscosity). Therefore, a constant coefficient of viscosity cannot be defined.

Although the concept of viscosity is commonly used in [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988) to characterize the shear properties of a fluid, it can be inadequate to describe non-Newtonian fluids. They are best studied through several other [rheological](wikipedia://en/25453) properties that relate [stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stress_%28physics%29) and [strain rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_rate) tensors under many different flow conditions—such as [oscillatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation) shear or extensional flow—which are measured using different devices or [rheometers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheometer). The properties are better studied using [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965)-valued [constitutive equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutive_equations), which are common in the field of [continuum mechanics](wikipedia://en/5918).

For non-Newtonian fluid's viscosity, there are [pseudoplastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoplastic), [plastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_flow), and [dilatant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilatant) flows that are time-independent, and there are [thixotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thixotropic) and [rheopectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheopectic) flows that are time-dependent. Three well-known time-dependent non-newtonian fluids which can be identified by the defining authors are the Oldroyd-B model,[^o4] Walters’ Liquid B[^o5] and Williamson[^o6] fluids.

Time-dependent [self-similar](wikipedia://en/58495269) analysis of the [Ladyzenskaya](wikipedia://en/1914268)-type model with a non-linear velocity dependent stress tensor was performed.[^o7] No analytical solutions could be derived, but a rigorous mathematical existence theorem[^o8] was given for the solution.

For time-independent non-Newtonian fluids the known analytic solutions are much broader.[^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12]

### Shear thickening fluid {#Shear_thickening_fluid}

The viscosity of a shear thickening – i.e. [dilatant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilatant) – fluid appears to increase when the shear rate increases. [Corn starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corn_starch) suspended in water ("oobleck", see below) is a common example: when stirred slowly it looks milky, when stirred vigorously it feels like a very viscous liquid.

### Shear thinning fluid {#Shear_thinning_fluid}

*Image: Painting with non-newtonian fluid.jpg.* Paint is a non-Newtonian fluid. A flat surface covered with white paint is oriented vertically (before taking the picture the flat surface was horizontal, placed on a table). The fluid starts dripping down the surface but, because of its non-Newtonian nature, it is subjected to stress due to the [gravitational acceleration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_acceleration). Therefore, instead of slipping along the surface, it forms very large and very dense droplets with limited dripping.

A familiar example of the opposite, a [shear thinning fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_thinning), or pseudoplastic fluid, is wall [paint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paint): The paint should flow readily off the brush when it is being applied to a surface but not drip excessively. Note that all [thixotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thixotropy) fluids are extremely shear thinning, but they are significantly time dependent, whereas the colloidal "shear thinning" fluids respond instantaneously to changes in shear rate. Thus, to avoid confusion, the latter classification is more clearly termed pseudoplastic.

Another example of a shear thinning fluid is blood. This application is highly favoured within the body, as it allows the viscosity of blood to decrease with increased shear strain rate.

### Bingham plastic {#Bingham_plastic}

Fluids that have a linear shear stress/shear strain relationship but require a finite yield stress before they begin to flow (the plot of shear stress against shear strain does not pass through the origin) are called [Bingham plastics](wikipedia://en/482912). Several examples are clay suspensions, drilling mud, toothpaste, mayonnaise, chocolate, and mustard. The surface of a Bingham plastic can hold peaks when it is still. By contrast [Newtonian fluids](wikipedia://en/363903) have flat featureless surfaces when still.

### Rheopectic or anti-thixotropic {#Rheopectic_or_anti-thixotropic}

There are also fluids whose strain rate is a function of time. Fluids that require a gradually increasing shear stress to maintain a constant strain rate are referred to as [rheopectic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rheopectic). An opposite case of this is a fluid that thins out with time and requires a decreasing stress to maintain a constant strain rate ([thixotropic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thixotropic)).

## Examples {#Examples}

Many common substances exhibit non-Newtonian flows. These include:

- Soap solutions, [cosmetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmetics), and toothpaste[^o30]
- Food such as [butter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butter), [cheese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheese), [jam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jam), [mayonnaise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mayonnaise), [soup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soup), and [yogurt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogurt)[^o30]
- Natural substances such as [gums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gum), [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) solutions, and [extracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extract)[^o30]
- Biological fluids such as [blood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blood), [saliva](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saliva), [semen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semen), [mucus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mucus), and [synovial fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synovial_fluid)[^o30]
- agricultural waste, [magma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magma), and [lava](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lava),[^o30]
- [Slurries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slurry) such as cement slurry and paper pulp,[^o30]

### Oobleck {#Oobleck}

*Image: UniversumUNAM55 (cropped).JPG.* Demonstration of a non-Newtonian fluid at [Universum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universum_%28UNAM%29) in Mexico City

*Image: Corn speaker.jpg.* Oobleck on a subwoofer. Applying force to oobleck, by sound waves in this case, makes the non-Newtonian fluid thicken.[^o33]

An inexpensive, [non-toxic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toxicity) example of a non-Newtonian fluid is a suspension of [starch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starch) (e.g., cornstarch/cornflour) in water, sometimes called "oobleck", "ooze", or "magic mud" (1 part of water to 1.5–2 parts of corn starch).[^o34][^o35][^o36] The name "oobleck" is derived from the [Dr. Seuss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dr._Seuss) book *[Bartholomew and the Oobleck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomew_and_the_Oobleck)*.[^o34]

Because of its [dilatant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilatant) properties, oobleck is often used in demonstrations that exhibit its unusual behavior. A person may walk on a large tub of oobleck without sinking due to its [shear thickening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_thickening) properties, as long as the individual moves quickly enough to provide enough force with each step to cause the thickening. Also, if oobleck is placed on a large subwoofer driven at a sufficiently high volume, it will thicken and form [standing waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standing_wave) in response to low frequency sound waves from the speaker. If a person were to punch or hit oobleck, it would thicken and act like a solid. After the blow, the oobleck will go back to its thin liquid-like state.

:::

### Flubber (slime) {#Flubber_(slime)}

*Image: Pouring Slime.JPG.* Slime flows under low stresses but breaks under higher stresses

Flubber, also commonly known as slime, is a non-Newtonian fluid, easily made from [polyvinyl acetate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyvinyl_acetate)–based [glues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adhesive) (such as white "school" glue) and [borax](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borax). It flows under low stresses but breaks under higher stresses and pressures. This combination of fluid-like and solid-like properties makes it a [Maxwell fluid](wikipedia://en/480282). Its behaviour can also be described as being [viscoplastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscoplasticity) or [gelatinous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelatin).[^o38]

### Chilled caramel topping {#Chilled_caramel_topping}

Another example of non-Newtonian fluid flow is chilled caramel [ice cream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_cream) topping (so long as it incorporates hydrocolloids such as [carrageenan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carrageenan) and [gellan gum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gellan_gum)). The sudden application of [force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force)—by stabbing the surface with a finger, for example, or rapidly inverting the container holding it—causes the fluid to behave like a [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) rather than a liquid. This is the "[shear thickening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_thickening)" property of this non-Newtonian fluid. Gentler treatment, such as slowly inserting a spoon, will leave it in its liquid state. Trying to jerk the spoon back out again, however, will trigger the return of the temporary solid state.[^o40]

### Silly Putty {#Silly_Putty}

Silly Putty is a silicone polymer based [suspension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suspension_%28chemistry%29) that will flow, bounce, or break, depending on strain rate.

### Plant resin {#Plant_resin}

Plant resin is a [viscoelastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscoelastic) [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) [polymer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymer). When left in a container, it will flow slowly as a liquid to conform to the contours of its container. If struck with greater force, however, it will shatter as a solid.

### Quicksand {#Quicksand}

Quicksand is a [shear thinning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_thinning) non-Newtonian [colloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloid) that gains viscosity at rest. Quicksand's non-Newtonian properties can be observed when it experiences a slight shock (for example, when someone walks on it or agitates it with a stick), shifting between its [gel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gel) and [sol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sol_%28colloid%29) phase and seemingly liquefying, causing objects on the surface of the quicksand to sink.

### Ketchup {#Ketchup}

[Ketchup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ketchup) is a [shear thinning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_thinning) fluid.[^o45][^o46] Shear thinning means that the fluid viscosity decreases with increasing [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress). In other words, fluid motion is initially difficult at slow rates of deformation, but will flow more freely at high rates. Shaking an inverted bottle of ketchup can cause it to transition to a lower viscosity through shear thinning, making it easier to pour from the bottle.

### Dry granular flows {#Dry_granular_flows}

Under certain circumstances, flows of [granular materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Granular_material) can be modelled as a continuum, for example using the [*μ*(*I*) rheology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Μ%28I%29_rheology). Such continuum models tend to be non-Newtonian, since the apparent viscosity of granular flows increases with pressure and decreases with shear rate. The main difference is the shearing stress and rate of shear.
