# Disjunctive normal form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Disjunctive normal form"}

In [boolean logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_logic), a **disjunctive normal form** (**DNF**) is a [normal form](wikipedia://en/5085531) of a logical formula consisting of a disjunction of conjunctions; it can also be described as an **OR of ANDs**, a [sum of products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_of_products), or — in [philosophical logic](wikipedia://en/304939) — a *cluster concept*.[^n1] The disjunctive normal form and its counterpart, the [conjunctive normal form](wikipedia://en/73342), are the most common standardized ways of representing [boolean expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_expressions). They are widely used in various applications such as [circuit design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_design) or [automated theorem proving](wikipedia://en/2546).

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A logical formula is considered to be in DNF if it is a [disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962) of one or more [conjunctions](wikipedia://en/18152) of one or more [literals](wikipedia://en/7792761).[^n2][^n3][^n4] A DNF formula is in **full disjunctive normal form** if each of its variables appears exactly once in every conjunction and each conjunction appears at most once (up to the order of variables). As in [conjunctive normal form](wikipedia://en/73342) (CNF), the only propositional operators in DNF are [and](wikipedia://en/18152) ($\wedge$), [or](wikipedia://en/7962) ($\vee$), and [not](wikipedia://en/161019) ($\neg$). The *not* operator can only be used as part of a literal, which means that it can only precede a [propositional variable](wikipedia://en/1557562).

The following is a [context-free grammar](wikipedia://en/6759) for DNF:

*DNF* $\, \to \,$ (*Disjunct*) $\, \mid \,$ (*Disjunct*) $\, \lor \,$ *DNF*

*Disjunct* $\, \to \,$ *Literal* $\, \mid\,$ *Literal* $\, \land \,$ *Disjunct*

*Literal* $\, \to \,$ *Variable* $\, \mid \,$ $\, \neg \,$ *Variable*

Where *Variable* is any variable.

For example, all of the following formulas are in DNF:

- $$(A \land \neg B \land \neg C) \lor (\neg D \land E \land F \land D \land F)$$
- $$(A \land B) \lor (C)$$
- $$(A \land B)$$
- $$(A)$$

The formula $A \lor B$ is in DNF, but not in full DNF; an equivalent full-DNF version is $(A \land B) \lor (A \land \lnot B) \lor (\lnot A \land B)$.

The following formulas are **not** in DNF:

- $\neg(A \lor B)$, since an OR is nested within a NOT
- $\neg(A \land B) \lor C$, since an AND is nested within a NOT
- $A \lor (B \land (C \lor D))$, since an OR is nested within an AND[^o8]

:::

### ... by syntactic means {#..._by_syntactic_means}

The conversion involves using [logical equivalences](wikipedia://en/169324), such as [double negation elimination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_negation_elimination), [De Morgan's laws](wikipedia://en/64669), and the [distributive law](wikipedia://en/103118#Propositional_logic). Formulas built from the [primitive](wikipedia://en/5279259#Introduction) [connectives](wikipedia://en/18153) $\{\land,\lor,\lnot\}$[^o12] can be converted to DNF by the following [canonical term rewriting system](wikipedia://en/23947591):[^n6]

$$\begin{array}{rcl} (\lnot \lnot x) & \rightsquigarrow & x \\ (\lnot (x \lor y)) & \rightsquigarrow & ((\lnot x) \land (\lnot y)) \\ (\lnot (x \land y)) & \rightsquigarrow & ((\lnot x) \lor (\lnot y)) \\ (x \land (y \lor z)) & \rightsquigarrow & ((x \land y) \lor (x \land z)) \\ ((x \lor y) \land z) & \rightsquigarrow & ((x \land z) \lor (y \land z)) \\ \end{array}$$

$\phi = ((\lnot (p \land q)) \leftrightarrow (\lnot r \uparrow (p \oplus q)))$.[^o17]

$$( p \land \lnot q \land r) \lor (\lnot p \land q \land r) \lor (\lnot p \land \lnot q \land r) \lor (\lnot p \land \lnot q \land \lnot r)$$

- The full DNF equivalent of $\lnot \phi$ is

$$( p \land q \land r) \lor ( p \land q \land \lnot r) \lor ( p \land \lnot q \land \lnot r) \lor (\lnot p \land q \land \lnot r)$$

### Remark {#Remark}

A propositional formula can be represented by one and only one full DNF. In contrast, several *plain* DNFs may be possible. For example, by applying the rule $((a \land b) \lor (\lnot a \land b)) \rightsquigarrow b$ three times, the full DNF of the above $\phi$ can be simplified to $(\lnot p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land r) \lor (\lnot q \land r)$. However, there are also equivalent DNF formulas that cannot be transformed one into another by this rule, see the pictures for an example.

## Disjunctive Normal Form Theorem {#Disjunctive_Normal_Form_Theorem}

It is a theorem that all consistent formulas in [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) can be converted to disjunctive normal form.[^o21][^o24][^o22][^o23] This is called the **Disjunctive Normal Form Theorem**.[^o21][^o24][^o22][^o23] The formal statement is as follows:

> **Disjunctive Normal Form Theorem:** Suppose $X$ is a sentence in a propositional language $\mathcal{L}$ with $n$ sentence letters, which we shall denote by $A_1,...,A_n$. If $X$ is not a contradiction, then it is truth-functionally equivalent to a disjunction of conjunctions of the form $\pm A_1 \land ... \land \pm A_n$, where $+A_i=A_i$, and $-A_i= \neg A_i$.[^o24]

The proof follows from the procedure given above for generating DNFs from [truth tables](wikipedia://en/25512250). Formally, the proof is as follows:

> Suppose $X$ is a sentence in a propositional language whose sentence letters are $A, B, C, \ldots$. For each row of $X$'s truth table, write out a corresponding [conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) $\pm A \land \pm B \land \pm C \land \ldots$, where $\pm A$ is defined to be $A$ if $A$ takes the value $T$ at that row, and is $\neg A$ if $A$ takes the value $F$ at that row; similarly for $\pm B$, $\pm C$, etc. (the [alphabetical ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabetical_order) of $A, B, C, \ldots$ in the conjunctions is quite arbitrary; any other could be chosen instead). Now form the [disjunction](wikipedia://en/7962) of all these conjunctions which correspond to $T$ rows of $X$'s truth table. This disjunction is a sentence in $\mathcal{L}[A, B, C, \ldots; \land, \lor, \neg]$,[^o25] which by the reasoning above is truth-functionally equivalent to $X$. This construction obviously presupposes that $X$ takes the value $T$ on at least one row of its truth table; if $X$ doesn’t, i.e., if $X$ is a [contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807), then $X$ is equivalent to $A \land \neg A$, which is, of course, also a sentence in $\mathcal{L}[A, B, C, \ldots; \land, \lor, \neg]$.[^o24]

This theorem is a convenient way to derive many useful [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) results in propositional logic, such as, [trivially](wikipedia://en/193053), the result that the set of connectives $\{\land, \lor, \neg\}$ is [functionally complete](wikipedia://en/5279259).[^o24]

:::

## Maximum number of conjunctions {#Maximum_number_of_conjunctions}

Any propositional formula is built from $n$ variables, where $n \ge 1$.

There are $2n$ possible literals: $L = \{ p_1, \lnot p_1, p_2, \lnot p_2, \ldots, p_n, \lnot p_n\}$.

$L$ has $(2^{2n} -1)$ non-empty subsets.[^o27]

This is the maximum number of conjunctions a DNF can have.[^n8]

A full DNF can have up to $2^{n}$ conjunctions, one for each row of the truth table.

::: {#o28 .example cue=inline-head name="Example 1"}

**Example 1**

Consider a formula with two variables $p$ and $q$.

The longest possible DNF has $2^{(2 \times 2)} -1 = 15$ conjunctions:[^n8]

$$\begin{array}{lcl} (\lnot p) \lor (p) \lor (\lnot q) \lor (q) \lor \\ (\lnot p \land p) \lor \underline{(\lnot p \land \lnot q)} \lor \underline{(\lnot p \land q)} \lor \underline{( p \land \lnot q)} \lor \underline{( p \land q)} \lor (\lnot q \land q) \lor \\ (\lnot p \land p \land \lnot q) \lor (\lnot p \land p \land q) \lor (\lnot p \land \lnot q \land q) \lor ( p \land \lnot q \land q) \lor \\ (\lnot p \land p \land \lnot q \land q) \end{array}$$

The longest possible full DNF has 4 conjunctions: they are underlined.

This formula is a [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335). It can be simplified to $(\neg p \lor p)$ or to $(\neg q \lor q)$, which are also tautologies, as well as valid DNFs.

:::

::: {#o29 .example cue=inline-head name="Example 2"}

**Example 2**

Each DNF of the e.g. formula $(X_1 \lor Y_1) \land (X_2 \lor Y_2) \land \dots \land (X_n \lor Y_n)$ has $2^n$ conjunctions.

:::

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

The [Boolean satisfiability problem](wikipedia://en/4715) on [conjunctive normal form](wikipedia://en/73342) formulas is [NP-complete](wikipedia://en/23385892#Boolean_satisfiability_problem). By the [duality principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duality_principle_%28Boolean_algebra%29), so is the falsifiability problem on DNF formulas. Therefore, it is [co-NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Co-NP-hard) to decide if a DNF formula is a [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335).

Conversely, a DNF formula is satisfiable if, and only if, one of its conjunctions is satisfiable. This can be decided in [polynomial time](wikipedia://en/658550) simply by checking that at least one conjunction does not contain conflicting literals.

::: {#o32 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variants"}

## Variants {#Variants}

An important variation used in the study of [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/2230) is *k-DNF*. A formula is in *k-DNF* if it is in DNF and each conjunction contains at most k literals.[^n9]

:::
