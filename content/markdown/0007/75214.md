# Flatland

Flatworld|*Flatworld*

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Flatland"}

***Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions*** is a [satirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satire) novella by the English [theologian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theology), [Anglican priest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglican_priest) and [schoolmaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schoolmaster) [Edwin Abbott Abbott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Abbott_Abbott), first published in 1884 by [Seeley & Co.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seeley,_Service) of London. Written pseudonymously by "A Square",[^o3] the book used the fictional [two-dimensional world](wikipedia://en/19469852) of Flatland to satirise the class and gender hierarchies of [Victorian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victorian_era) society, but the novella's more enduring contribution is its examination of [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398).[^o14]

:::

A sequel, *[Sphereland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphereland)*, was written by [Dionys Burger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionys_Burger) in 1957. Several films have been based on *Flatland*, including the feature film *[Flatland](wikipedia://en/11716156)* (2007). Other efforts have been short or experimental films, including one narrated by [Dudley Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dudley_Moore) and the short films *[Flatland: The Movie](wikipedia://en/16280232)* (2007) and *[Flatland 2: Sphereland](wikipedia://en/16280232#Sequel)* (2012).[^o4]

## Plot {#Plot}

*Image: Houghton EC85 Ab264 884f - Flatland, men and women doors.jpg.* Illustration of a simple house in Flatland.

The story describes a [two-dimensional world](wikipedia://en/19469852) inhabited by [geometric figures](wikipedia://en/169191) (flatlanders[^o6]); women are [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860), while men are [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) with various numbers of sides. The narrator is a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29), a member of the [caste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caste) of gentlemen and professionals, who guides the readers through some of the implications of life in two dimensions. The first half of the story goes through the practicalities of existing in a two-dimensional universe, as well as a history leading up to the year 1999 on the eve of the 3rd Millennium.

On New Year's Eve, the Square dreams of a visit to a [one-dimensional world](wikipedia://en/25584215), "Lineland", inhabited by men, who are lines, while the women are "lustrous points". These [points](wikipedia://en/593693) and lines are unable to see the Square as anything other than a set of points on a line. Thus, the Square attempts to convince the realm's [monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monarch) of a second dimension but cannot do so. In the end, the monarch of Lineland tries to kill the Square rather than tolerate him any further.

Following this vision, the Square is visited by a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859). Similar to the "points" in Lineland, he is unable to see the three-dimensional object as anything other than a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) (more precisely, a [disk](wikipedia://en/329542)). The Sphere then levitates up and down through Flatland, allowing the Square to see the circle expand and contract between a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) and small circles. The Sphere then tries further to convince the Square of the [third dimension](wikipedia://en/3054853) by dimensional analogies (a point becomes a line, a line becomes a square). The Square is still unable to comprehend the third dimension, so the Sphere resorts to deeds: he gives information about the "insides" of the house, moves a tablet through the third dimension, and even goes inside the Square for a moment. Still unable to comprehend the third dimension, the Square is taken by the Sphere to the third dimension, Spaceland. This Sphere visits Flatland at the turn of each millennium to introduce a new [apostle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostle) to the idea of a third dimension in the hope of eventually educating the population of Flatland. From the safety of Spaceland, they can oversee the leaders of Flatland, acknowledging the Sphere's existence and prescribing the silencing. After this [proclamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proclamation) is made, many witnesses are massacred or imprisoned (according to caste), including the Square's brother.

After the Square's mind is opened to new dimensions, he tries to convince the Sphere of the theoretical possibility of the existence of a [fourth dimension](wikipedia://en/1364622) and higher spatial dimensions. The Sphere at first scoffs at the idea of higher dimensions, just as the Square had done, showing that his comprehension is not as broad as he had thought. Still, the Sphere returns his student to Flatland in disgrace.

The Square then has a dream in which the Sphere revisits him, this time to introduce him to a [zero-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/517682), Pointland, of whom the Point (sole inhabitant, monarch, and universe in one) perceives any communication as a thought originating in his own mind (cf. [Solipsism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solipsism)):

"You see," said my Teacher, "how little your words have done. So far as the Monarch understands them at all, he accepts them as his own – for he cannot conceive of any other except himself – and plumes himself upon the variety of *Its Thought* as an instance of creative Power. Let us leave this god of Pointland to the ignorant fruition of his omnipresence and omniscience: nothing that you or I can do can rescue him from his self-satisfaction."[^o7]

*Image: Flatland (first edition) page 100.png.* The last sketch in the book.

The Square recognises the ignorance of the monarchs of Pointland and Lineland correspond with his own (and the Sphere's) previous ignorance of the existence of higher dimensions than their own. Once returned to Flatland, the Square cannot convince anyone of Spaceland's existence, especially after official [decrees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree) are announced that anyone preaching the existence of three dimensions will be imprisoned (or executed, depending on caste). For example, he tries to convince his relative of the third dimension but cannot move a square "upward," as opposed to forward or sideways. Eventually, the Square himself is imprisoned for just this reason, with only occasional contact with his brother, who is imprisoned in the same facility. He cannot convince his brother, even after all they have both seen. Seven years after being imprisoned, "A. Square" writes out the book *Flatland* as a [memoir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoir), hoping to keep it as posterity for a future generation that can see beyond their two-dimensional existence.

## Social elements {#Social_elements}

Men are portrayed as polygons whose [social status](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_status) is determined by their [regularity](wikipedia://en/333306) and the number of their sides, with a Circle considered the "perfect" shape. Women are lines, quite fragile but also dangerous, as they can disappear from view and possibly stab someone. To prevent this, they are required by law to sound a "peace-cry" while moving about and to use separate doors from men.

In the world of Flatland, classes are distinguished by the "Art of Hearing", the "Art of Feeling", and the "Art of Sight Recognition". Classes can be distinguished by the sound of one's voice, but the lower classes have more developed vocal organs, enabling them to feign the voice of a Polygon or even a Circle. Feeling, practised by the lower classes and women, determines the configuration of a person by feeling one of its [angles](wikipedia://en/1196). The "Art of Sight Recognition", practised by the upper classes, is aided by "Fog", which allows an observer to determine the depth of an object. With this, polygons with sharp angles relative to the observer will fade more rapidly than polygons with more gradual angles. Colour of any kind [was banned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumptuary_law) in Flatland after [Isosceles](wikipedia://en/315428) workers painted themselves to impersonate [noble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobility) Polygons. The Square describes these events, and the ensuing [class war](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_conflict) at length.

The population of Flatland can "[evolve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution)" through the "Law of Nature", which states: "a male child shall have one more side than his father, so that each generation shall rise (as a rule) one step in the scale of development and nobility. Thus the son of a Square is a [Pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014), the son of a Pentagon, a [Hexagon](wikipedia://en/59733), and so on".

This rule is not the case when dealing with Isosceles Triangles (Soldiers and Workmen) with only two [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) sides. The smallest angle of an Isosceles Triangle gains 30 [arc minutes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minute_and_second_of_arc) (half a [degree](wikipedia://en/1195294)) each generation. Additionally, the rule does not seem to apply to many-sided Polygons. For example, the sons of several hundred-sided Polygons will often develop 50 or more sides more than their parents. Furthermore, the angle of an Isosceles Triangle or the number of sides of a (regular) Polygon may be altered during life by deeds or by surgery.

An [Equilateral Triangle](wikipedia://en/173285) is a member of the [craftsman class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artisan). Squares and Pentagons are the "gentlemen" class, as doctors, lawyers, and other professions. Hexagons are the lowest rank of nobility, all the way up to (near) Circles, who make up the [priest class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priestly_caste). The higher-order Polygons have much less of a chance of producing sons, preventing Flatland from being overcrowded with noblemen.

Apart from Isosceles Triangles, only regular Polygons are considered until chapter seven of the book when the issue of irregularity, or physical deformity is brought up. In a two-dimensional world, a regular polygon can be identified by a single angle and/or [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417). To maintain [social cohesion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_cohesiveness), irregularity is to be abhorred, with moral irregularity and criminality cited, "by some" (in the book), as inevitable additional deformities, a sentiment with which the Square concurs. If the error of deviation is above a stated amount, the irregular Polygon faces [euthanasia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euthanasia); if below, he becomes the lowest rank of [civil servant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_service). An irregular Polygon is not destroyed at birth, but allowed to develop to see if the irregularity can be "cured" or reduced. If the deformity remains, the irregular is "painlessly and mercifully consumed."[^o9]

## As social satire {#As_social_satire}

In *Flatland*, Abbott describes a society rigidly divided into classes. Social ascent is the main aspiration of its inhabitants, apparently granted to everyone but strictly controlled by the top of the hierarchy. Freedom is despised and the laws are cruel. Innovators are imprisoned or suppressed. Members of lower classes who are intellectually valuable, and potential leaders of [riots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riot), are either killed or promoted to the higher classes. Every attempt for change is considered dangerous and harmful. This world is not prepared to receive "revelations from another world". The satirical part is mainly concentrated in the first part of the book, "This World", which describes Flatland. The main points of interest are the Victorian concept of women's roles in the society and in the class-based hierarchy of men.[^o11] Abbott has been accused of [misogyny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misogyny) due to his portrayal of women in *Flatland*. ## Critical reception {#Critical_reception}

*Flatland* did not have much success when published, although it was not entirely ignored.[^o13] In the entry on Edwin Abbott in the *[Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography)* for persons who died in the period of 1922 to 1930, *Flatland* was not even mentioned.[^o14]

The book was discovered again after [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736)'s [general theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) was published, which brought to prominence the concept of a fourth dimension. *Flatland* was mentioned in a letter by William Garnett entitled "Euclid, Newton and Einstein" published in *[Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_%28journal%29)* on 12 February 1920. In this letter, Abbott is depicted, in a sense, as a prophet due to his intuition of the importance of *time* to explain certain phenomena:[^o15][^o16]

Some thirty or more years ago a little *jeu d'esprit* was written by Dr. Edwin Abbott entitled *Flatland*. At the time of its publication it did not attract as much attention as it deserved... If there is motion of our three-dimensional space relative to the fourth dimension, all the changes we experience and assign to the flow of time will be due simply to this movement, the whole of the future as well as the past always existing in the fourth dimension.

The *[Oxford Dictionary of National Biography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictionary_of_National_Biography#Oxford_Dictionary_of_National_Biography)* subsequently revised his biography to state that \[Abbott\] "is most remembered as the author of *Flatland: A Romance of Many Dimensions*.

Wendy Graham reviewed *Flatland* for *Adventurer* magazine and stated that "Once again a story with a message – crackpots aren't always crackpots."[^o17]

## Adaptations and parodies {#Adaptations_and_parodies}

Numerous imitations or sequels to *Flatland* have been created. Examples include:

**Films and TV**

- *[Flatland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatland_%281965_film%29)* (1965), an animated short film based on the novella, was directed by Eric Martin and based on an idea by [John Hubley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hubley).[^o20][^o21]
- *[Flatlandia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatlandia_%281982_film%29)* (1982), an Italian [stop motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stop_motion) short film, directed by Michele Emmer.[^o22]
- *[Flatland](wikipedia://en/11716156)* (2007), a 95-minute animated independent feature film version directed by [Ladd Ehlinger Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ladd_Ehlinger_Jr.), updates the satire from Victorian England to the modern-day United States.[^o23]
- *[Flatland: The Movie](wikipedia://en/16280232)* (2007), by Dano Johnson and Jeffrey Travis,[^o24] is a 34-minute animated educational film.[^o25] Its sequel was *[Flatland 2: Sphereland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flatland_2:_Sphereland)* (2012), inspired by the novel *[Sphereland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sphereland)* by [Dionys Burger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dionys_Burger).[^o26][^o28]

## In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

- Physicists and science popularizers [Carl Sagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sagan) and [Stephen Hawking](wikipedia://en/19376148) have both commented on and postulated about the effects of *Flatland*. Sagan recreates the [thought experiment](wikipedia://en/49535) as a set-up to discussing the possibilities of higher dimensions of the physical universe in both the [book](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmos_%28Sagan_book%29) and [television series *Cosmos*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmos:_A_Personal_Voyage),[^o31] whereas Hawking notes the peculiarity of life in two-dimensional space, as any inhabitants would necessarily be unable to digest their own food. (This concept is parodied in the episode of *Futurama* described below, "2-D Blacktop". The protagonists attempt to eat food while in the 2-D universe, but it falls out immediately. The native organisms in *Futurams*'s version of Flatland absorb food somewhat like [amoeba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amoeba).)[^o32]
- In the *[Star Trek: The Next Generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_Trek:_The_Next_Generation)* episode "[The Loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Loss)", the [USS *Enterprise*-D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USS_Enterprise_%28NCC-1701-D%29) becomes trapped within a field of two-dimensional lifeforms.[^o33]
- *[The League of Extraordinary Gentlemen, Volume 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_League_of_Extraordinary_Gentlemen,_Volume_2)*, issue 3, in chapter 3 of the series of writings *New Traveller's Almanac*, it is mentioned that in an unknown basement of [New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_%28state%29), Flatland was discovered by a mathematician.
- In the "[2-D Blacktop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-D_Blacktop)" episode of the animated science fiction TV comedy series *[Futurama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Futurama)* (season 10 episode 1, originally broadcast June 17, 2013), two spaceships moving at [relativistic speeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relativistic_speed) crash head on and are compressed together into a flat disk. They meet natives of the realm, who chase after them when the concept of a third dimension is brought up.[^o34]
- In [David Foster Wallace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Foster_Wallace)'s novel *[Infinite Jest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_Jest)* (1996), it is briefly mentioned that students from the Enfield Tennis Academy could be seen studying and highlighting copies of *Flatland* on the bus.[^o35]
- *Flatland* features in *[The Big Bang Theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Big_Bang_Theory)* episode "The Psychic Vortex",[^o36] when [Sheldon Cooper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheldon_Cooper) declares it one of his favourite imaginary places to visit.[^o37]
- On the series *[The Orville](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Orville)*, episode "New Dimensions", after entering a region of two-dimensional space Captain Ed Mercer references Flatland and its theme of social hierarchy.[^o38]
- In the *[Sons of Anarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sons_of_Anarchy)* episode "Straw", Clay Morrow is lounging on a cot in a private cell in county jail when he first meets retired [U.S. Marshal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Marshals_Service) Lee Toric. Morrow half-ignores Toric while keeping his eyes on a copy of *Flatland*.
- In the TV series *[Gravity Falls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_Falls),* it is implied by the main antagonist, [Bill Cipher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Cipher), that he originates from a dimension very similar to Flatland. In the *Gravity Falls* book "[The Book of Bill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Bill)" a picture of Flatland's cover is shown on a TV screen in the chapter titled "My Story".
- The novel appears in the 2014 film *[Interstellar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interstellar_%28film%29)*.[^o39]
- In "Behold Eck!" a 1964 episode of the science fiction anthology TV series *[Outer Limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Outer_Limits_%281963_TV_series%29)*, a 2-dimensional creature accidentally visits earth through a time-hole and causes some damage before it can get back home.
