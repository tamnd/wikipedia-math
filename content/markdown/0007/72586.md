# Simon Stevin

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Simon Stevin"}

the ship

**Simon Stevin** (; 1548–1620), sometimes called **Stevinus**, was a [Flemish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/County_of_Flanders) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), scientist and [music theorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theorist).[^o3] He made various contributions in many areas of [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), both theoretical and practical. He also translated various mathematical terms into [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language), making it one of the few European languages in which the word for [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), *wiskunde* (*wis* and *kunde*, i.e., "the knowledge of what is certain"), was not a [loanword](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loanword) from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language) but a [calque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calque) via [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin). He also replaced the word *chemie*, the Dutch for chemistry, by *scheikunde* ("the art of separating"), made in [analogy](wikipedia://en/103533#Linguistics) with *wiskunde*.

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Very little is known with certainty about Simon Stevin's life, and what we know is mostly inferred from other recorded facts.[^o5] The exact birth date and the date and place of his death are uncertain. It is assumed he was born in [Bruges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bruges), since he enrolled at [Leiden University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University) under the name *Simon Stevinus Brugensis* (meaning "Simon Stevin from Bruges"). His name is usually written as Stevin, but some documents regarding his father use the spelling *Stevijn* (pronunciation \[ˈste:vεɪn\]); this was a common spelling shift in 16th-century Dutch.[^o6] Simon Stevin's mother, Cathelijne (or Catelyne), was the daughter of a wealthy family from [Ypres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ypres); her father Hubert was a [poorter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poorter) of Bruges. Cathelijne would later marry Joost Sayon, who was involved in the carpet and [silk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk) trade and was a member of the [schuttersgilde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schutterij) Sint-Sebastiaan. Through her marriage, Cathelijne became a member of a family of [Calvinists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinist); it is thought that Simon Stevin was likely brought up in the Calvinist faith.[^o11]

It is believed that Stevin grew up in a relatively affluent environment and enjoyed a good education. He was likely educated at a [Latin school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_school) in his hometown.[^o7]

### Simon Stevin's travels {#Simon_Stevin's_travels}

Stevin left Bruges in 1571 apparently without a particular destination. Stevin was most likely a [Calvinist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinist) since a [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic) would likely not have risen to the position of trust he later occupied with [Maurice, Prince of Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice,_Prince_of_Orange). It is assumed that he left Bruges to escape the religious persecution of Protestants by the Spanish rulers. Based on references in his work *"Wisconstighe Ghedaechtenissen"* (Mathematical Memoirs), it has been inferred that he must have moved first to Antwerp where he began his career as a merchant's [clerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerk).[^o9] Some biographers mention that he travelled to [Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prussia), [Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poland), [Denmark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denmark), [Norway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norway) and [Sweden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sweden) and other parts of [Northern Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Europe), between 1571 and 1577. It is possible that he completed these travels over a longer period of time. In 1577 Simon Stevin returned to Bruges and was appointed [city clerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clerk) by the [aldermen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldermen) of Bruges, a function he occupied from 1577 to 1581. He worked in the office of Jan de Brune of the [Brugse Vrije](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brugse_Vrije), the [castellany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castellany) of Bruges.

Why he had returned to Bruges in 1577 is not clear. It may have been related to the political events of that period. Bruges was the scene of intense religious conflict. Catholics and Calvinists alternately controlled the government of the city. They usually opposed each other but would occasionally collaborate in order to counteract the dictates of King [Philip II of Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Spain). In 1576 a certain level of official religious tolerance was decreed. This could explain why Stevin returned to Bruges in 1577. Later the Calvinists seized power in many Flemish cities and incarcerated Catholic clerics and secular governors supportive of the Spanish rulers. Between 1578 and 1584 Bruges was ruled by Calvinists.

### Simon Stevin in the Netherlands {#Simon_Stevin_in_the_Netherlands}

In 1581 Stevin again left his native Bruges and moved to [Leiden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden) where he attended the Latin school.[^o7] On 16 February 1583 he enrolled, under the name *Simon Stevinus Brugensis* (meaning "Simon Stevin from Bruges"), at [Leiden University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leiden_University), which had been founded by [William the Silent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_the_Silent) in 1575. Here he befriended William the Silent's second son and heir Prince [Maurice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_of_Nassau), the Count of Nassau. Stevin is listed in the university's registers until 1590 and apparently never graduated.

Following William the Silent's assassination and Prince Maurice's assumption of his father's office, Stevin became the principal advisor and tutor of Prince Maurice. Prince Maurice asked his advice on many occasions, and made him a [public officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_office) – at first director of the so-called "waterstaet"[^o33] (the government authority for [public works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_works), especially water management) from 1592, and later [quartermaster-general](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quartermaster-general) of the army of the States-General.[^o12] Prince Maurice also asked Stevin to found an engineering school within the University of Leiden.

Stevin moved to [The Hague](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hague) where he bought a house in 1612. He married in 1610 or 1614 and had four children. It is known that he left a [widow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Widow) with two children at his death in Leiden or The Hague in 1620.[^o11]

## Discoveries and inventions {#Discoveries_and_inventions}

*Image: Simon Stevins zeilwagen voor Prins Maurits 1649.jpg.* Wind chariot or [land yacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_sailing) (Zeilwagen) designed by Simon Stevin for [Prince Maurice of Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_of_Nassau,_Prince_of_Orange) (engraving by Jacques de Gheyn).

Stevin is responsible for many discoveries and inventions. Stevin wrote numerous bestselling books, and he was a pioneer of the development and the practical application of (engineering related) science such as [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and applied science like [hydraulic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_engineering) and [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying). He was thought to have invented the [decimal fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_fraction) until the middle of the 20th century, when researchers discovered that decimal fractions had been previously introduced by the medieval Islamic scholar [al-Uqlidisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Uqlidisi) in a book written in 952. Moreover, a systematic development of decimal fractions was given well before Stevin in the book *Miftah al-Hisab* written in 1427 by [Al-Kashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kashi).

His contemporaries were most struck by his invention of a so-called [land yacht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_sailing), a carriage with sails, of which a model was preserved in [Scheveningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheveningen) until 1802. The carriage itself had been lost long before. Around the year 1600 Stevin, with [Prince Maurice of Orange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_of_Nassau,_Prince_of_Orange) and twenty-six others, used the carriage on the beach between [Scheveningen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheveningen) and [Petten](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petten). The carriage was propelled solely by the force of wind and acquired a speed which exceeded that of horses.[^o33]

### Management of waterways {#Management_of_waterways}

Stevin's work in the *waterstaet* involved improvements to the [sluices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sluice) and [spillways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spillway) to control [flooding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flood), exercises in [hydraulic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_engineering). [Windmills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windmill) were already in use to pump the water out but in *Van de Molens* (*On mills*), he suggested improvements including ideas that the wheels should move slowly with a better system for meshing of the [gear teeth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gear_teeth). These improved threefold the efficiency of the windmills used in pumping water out of the [polders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polder).[^o15] He received a [patent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patent) on his innovation in 1586.[^o12]

### Philosophy of science {#Philosophy_of_science}

Stevin's aim was to bring about a second age of [wisdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wisdom), in which mankind would have recovered all of its earlier knowledge. He deduced that the language spoken in this age would have to be Dutch, because, as he showed [empirically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical), in that language, more concepts could be indicated with [monosyllabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monosyllabic) words than in any of the (European) languages he had compared it with.[^o33] This was one of the reasons why he wrote all of his works in Dutch and left the translation of them for others to do. The other reason was that he wanted his works to be practically useful to people who had not mastered the common scientific language of the time, Latin. Thanks to Simon Stevin the [Dutch language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) got its proper scientific vocabulary such as "wiskunde" (*"kunst van het gewisse of zekere"* the art of what is known or what is certain) for [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), "natuurkunde" (the "art of nature") for [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), "scheikunde" (the "art of separation") for [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry), "sterrenkunde" (the "art of stars") for [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), "meetkunde" (the "art of measuring") for [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446).

### Geometry, physics and trigonometry {#Geometry,_physics_and_trigonometry}

*Image: StevinEquilibrium.svg.* Stevin's proof of the [law of equilibrium on an inclined plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclined_plane#History), known as the "Epitaph of Stevinus".

Stevin was the first to show how to model regular and semiregular [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra) by delineating their frames in a plane. He also distinguished stable from unstable equilibria.[^o33]

Stevin contributed to [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261) with his book, *De Driehouckhandel*.

In *The First Book of the Elements of the Art of Weighing, The second part: Of the propositions \[The Properties of Oblique Weights\], Page 41, Theorem XI, Proposition XIX*,[^o19] he derived the condition for the balance of forces on [inclined planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclined_plane) using a diagram with a "wreath" containing evenly spaced round masses resting on the planes of a triangular prism (see the illustration on the side). He concluded that the weights required were proportional to the lengths of the sides on which they rested assuming the third side was horizontal and that the effect of a weight was reduced in a similar manner. It is implicit that the reduction factor is the height of the triangle divided by the side (the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine#History) of the angle of the side with respect to the horizontal). The proof diagram of this concept is known as the "Epitaph of Stevinus". As noted by [E. J. Dijksterhuis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._J._Dijksterhuis), Stevin's proof of the equilibrium on an inclined plane can be faulted for using [perpetual motion](wikipedia://en/54077) to imply a [reductio ad absurdum](wikipedia://en/22456146). Dijksterhuis says Stevin "intuitively made use of the principle of [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088) ... long before it was formulated explicitly".[^o5]

He demonstrated the resolution of forces before [Pierre Varignon](wikipedia://en/1193511), which had not been remarked previously, even though it is a simple consequence of the law of their composition.[^o33]

Stevin discovered the [hydrostatic paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic_paradox), which states that the pressure in a liquid is independent of the shape of the vessel and the area of the base, but depends solely on its height.[^o33]

He also gave the measure for the pressure on any given portion of the side of a vessel.[^o33]

He was the first to explain the [tides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tides) using the [attraction of the moon](wikipedia://en/10610469).[^o33]

In 1586, he [demonstrated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delft_tower_experiment) that two objects of different weight fall with the same acceleration.[^o20][^o21]

### Music theory {#Music_theory}

*Image: De Spiegheling der signconst.jpg.* *Van de Spiegheling der singconst*.

The first mention of equal temperament related to the [twelfth root of two](wikipedia://en/1129156) in the West appeared in Simon Stevin's unfinished manuscript *Van de Spiegheling der singconst*(ca 1605) published posthumously three hundred years later in 1884;[^o24] however, due to insufficient accuracy of his calculation, many of the numbers (for string length) he obtained were off by one or two units from the correct values.[^o25] He appears to have been inspired by the writings of the Italian [lutenist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutenist) and musical theorist [Vincenzo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Galilei) (father of [Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374)), a onetime pupil of [Gioseffo Zarlino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gioseffo_Zarlino).

:::

### Bookkeeping {#Bookkeeping}

[Double-entry bookkeeping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-entry_bookkeeping) may have been known to Stevin, as he was a clerk in [Antwerp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antwerp) in his younger years, either practically or via the medium of the works of Italian authors such as [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473) and [Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145). However, Stevin was the first to recommend the use of impersonal [accounts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Account_%28accountancy%29) in the national household. He brought it into practice for Prince Maurice, and recommended it to the French statesman [Sully](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximilien_de_Béthune,_duc_de_Sully).[^o27][^o33]

### Decimal fractions {#Decimal_fractions}

Stevin wrote a 35-page [booklet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book) called *[De Thiende](wikipedia://en/36193619)* ("the art of tenths"), first published in Dutch in 1585 and translated into French as *La Disme*. The full title of the English translation was *[Decimal arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_arithmetic): Teaching how to perform all computations whatsoever by whole numbers without [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29), by the four principles of common arithmetic: namely, [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141), [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), and [division](wikipedia://en/53696).* The concepts referred to in the booklet included [unit fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_fractions) and [Egyptian fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_fractions). [Muslim mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim_mathematicians) were the first to utilize [decimals](wikipedia://en/8214) instead of fractions on a large scale. [Al-Kashi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Kashi)'s book, *Key to Arithmetic*, was written at the beginning of the 15th century and was the stimulus for the systematic application of decimals to whole numbers and fractions thereof.[^o30] But nobody established their daily use before Stevin. He felt that this innovation was so significant, that he declared the universal introduction of decimal coinage, measures and weights to be merely a question of time.[^o31][^o33]

His notation is rather unwieldy. The [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_mark) separating the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) from the decimal fractions seems to be the invention of [Bartholomaeus Pitiscus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartholomaeus_Pitiscus), in whose [trigonometrical tables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometrical_table) (1612) it occurs, and it was accepted by [John Napier](wikipedia://en/15993) in his [logarithmic](wikipedia://en/17860) papers (1614 and 1619).[^o33]

Stevin printed little circles around the exponents of the different powers of one-tenth. That Stevin intended these encircled numerals to denote mere exponents is clear from the fact that he employed the same symbol for powers of [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923) quantities. He did not avoid fractional exponents; only negative exponents do not appear in his work.[^o33]

Stevin wrote on other scientific subjects – for instance optics, geography, astronomy – and a number of his writings were translated into Latin by W. Snellius ([Willebrord Snell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willebrord_Snell)). There are two complete editions in French of his works, both printed in Leiden, one in 1608, the other in 1634.

### Mathematics {#Mathematics}

*Image: Stevin - Oeuvres mathematiques, 1634 - 4607786.tif.* *Oeuvres mathematiques*, 1634

Stevin wrote his *Arithmetic* in 1594. The work brought to the western world for the first time a general solution of the [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175), originally documented nearly a millennium previously by [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) in India.

According to [Van der Waerden](wikipedia://en/91390), Stevin eliminated "the classical restriction of 'numbers' to integers (Euclid) or to rational fractions ([Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110))...the real numbers formed a continuum. His general notion of a real number was accepted, [tacitly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tacit) or explicitly, by all later scientists".[^o35] A recent study attributes a greater role to Stevin in developing the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) than has been acknowledged by [Weierstrass's](wikipedia://en/93459) followers.[^o36] Stevin proved the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884) for polynomials, anticipating [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy)'s proof thereof. Stevin uses a [divide and conquer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divide_and_conquer_algorithm) procedure, subdividing the interval into ten equal parts.[^o37] Stevin's decimals were the inspiration for [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s work on [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series).[^o38]

### Neologisms {#Neologisms}

Stevin thought the [Dutch language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) to be excellent for scientific writing, and he translated many of the mathematical terms to Dutch. As a result, Dutch is one of the few Western European languages that have many mathematical terms that do not stem from Greek or Latin. This includes the very name *wiskunde* (mathematics).

His eye for the importance of having the scientific language be the same as the language of the craftsman may show from the dedication of his book *De Thiende* ('The Disme' or 'The Tenth'): 'Simon Stevin wishes the stargazers, surveyors, carpet measurers, body measurers in general, coin measurers and tradespeople good luck.' Further on in the same pamphlet, he writes: "\[this text\] teaches us all calculations that are needed by the people without using fractions. One can reduce all operations to adding, subtracting, multiplying and dividing with integers."

Some of the words he invented evolved: 'aftrekken' (*subtract*) and 'delen' (*divide*) stayed the same, but over time 'menigvuldigen' became 'vermenigvuldigen' (*multiply*, the added 'ver' emphasizes the fact it is an action). 'Vergaderen' (*gathering*) became 'optellen' (*add* lit. *count up*).

Another example is the Dutch word for diameter: 'middellijn', lit.: line through the middle.

The word 'zomenigmaal' (*quotient* lit. 'that many times') has been replaced by 'quotiënt' in modern-day Dutch.

Other terms did not make it into modern day mathematical Dutch, like 'teerling' (*[die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice)*, although still being used in the meaning as die), instead of cube.

## Legacy {#Legacy}

Following his life, Belgium and the city of Bruges have continued to name places, statues and other topics in honor of Stevin

- In 1839, the city of Bruges and the government of West Flanders commissioned the creation of Simon Stevinplein (Simon Stevin Square) in honor of Stevin. The square features a statue of Stevin, which was made by [Eugène Simonis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugène_Simonis) and was officially unveiled in 1847.[^o41] The statue features a scroll showcasing Stevin's proof of the [law of equilibrium on an inclined plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclined_plane#History).
- On 25 May 2012, VLOOT dab, a Belgian government-run ferry and vessel company, launched the [RV Simon Stevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RV_Simon_Stevin), a vessel created for oceanographic research off the port of Ostend, in [Southern Bight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Bight) of the [North Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Sea), and in the eastern part of the [English Channel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_Channel).[^o42]
- The [Dutch Research Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Research_Council) (NWO), established a scientific award named after Stevin in 2018, the [Stevin Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stevin_Prize), which highlights contributions that bridge the gap between scientific research and practical applications that benefit society.[^o43]
- The study association of mechanical engineering at the [Technische Universiteit Eindhoven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eindhoven_University_of_Technology), W.S.V. Simon Stevin,[^o44] is named after Simon Stevin. In Stevin's memory, the association calls its bar "De Weeghconst" and owns a self-built fleet of [land yachts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_sailing).
- A state-of-the-art High Voltage Substation was named after Stevin, connecting Belgium's offshore windmill parks to land.[^o45]

## Publications {#Publications}

*Image: Moers 1604.jpg.* The [Moers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moers) fortifications designed by Simon Stevin.

Amongst others, he published:

- *Tafelen van Interest* (Tables of interest) in 1582 with [present value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present_value) problems of [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_interest) and [compound interest](wikipedia://en/434428) and interest tables that had previously been unpublished by bankers;[^o11]
- *Problemata geometrica* in 1583;
- *[De Thiende](wikipedia://en/36193619)* (*La Disme*, The tenth) in 1585 in which decimals were introduced in Europe;
- *La pratique d'arithmétique* in 1585;
- *L'arithmétique* in 1585 in which he presented a uniform treatment for solving [algebraic equations](wikipedia://en/1997);
- *Dialectike ofte bewysconst* (Dialectics, or Art of Demonstration) in 1585 at Leyden by Christoffel Plantijn. Published again in 1621 at Rotterdam by Jan van Waesberge de Jonge.
- *[De Beghinselen Der Weeghconst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Beghinselen_Der_Weeghconst)* in 1586, accompanied by *De Weeghdaet*;
- *De Beghinselen des Waterwichts* (Principles on the weight of water) in 1586 on the subject of [hydrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatics);
- *Vita Politica*. Named *Burgherlick leven* (Civil life) in 1590;
- *De Stercktenbouwing* (The construction of [fortifications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortifications)) published in 1594;
- *De Havenvinding* ([Position finding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation)) published in 1599;
- *De Hemelloop* in 1608 in which he voiced support for the [Copernican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_Copernicus) theory.
- In *Wiskonstighe Ghedachtenissen* (Mathematical Memoirs, Hypomnemata Mathematica) from 1605 to 1608. This included Simon Stevin's earlier works like *De Driehouckhandel* ([Trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261)), *De Meetdaet* ([Practice of measuring](wikipedia://en/19022)), and De Deursichtighe ([Perspective](wikipedia://en/380405)), which he edited and published.;[^o47]
- *Castrametatio, dat is legermeting and Nieuwe Maniere van Stercktebou door Spilsluysen* (New ways of building of [sluices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sluice)) published in 1617;
- *De Spiegheling der Singconst* (Theory of the art of singing).
