# Lucas primality test

the test for Mersenne numbers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lucas primality test"}

In [computational number theory](wikipedia://en/511466), the **Lucas test** is a [primality test](wikipedia://en/183751) for a natural number *n*; it requires that the [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factors) of *n* − 1 be already known.[^o2][^o3] It is the basis of the [Pratt certificate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratt_certificate) that gives a concise verification that *n* is prime.

:::

## Concepts {#Concepts}

Let *n* be a positive integer. If there exists an integer *a*, 1 \< *a* \< *n*, such that

$$a^{n-1}\ \equiv\ 1 \pmod n \,$$

and for every prime factor *q* of *n* − 1

$$a^{({n-1})/q}\ \not\equiv\ 1 \pmod n \,$$

then *n* is prime. If no such number *a* exists, then *n* is either 1, 2, or [composite](wikipedia://en/82289).

The reason for the correctness of this claim is as follows: if the first equivalence holds for *a*, we can deduce that *a* and *n* are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime#Properties). If *a* also survives the second step, then the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of *a* in the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) (**Z**/*n***Z**)\* is equal to *n* − 1, which means that the order of that group is *n* − 1 (because the order of every element of a group divides the order of the group), implying that *n* is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). Conversely, if *n* is prime, then there exists a [primitive root modulo *n*](wikipedia://en/186864), or [generator](wikipedia://en/99945) of the group (**Z**/*n***Z**)\*. Such a generator has order |(**Z**/*n***Z**)\*| = *n* − 1 and both equivalences will hold for any such primitive root.

Note that if there exists an *a* \< *n* such that the first equivalence fails, *a* is called a [Fermat witness](wikipedia://en/157755#Concept) for the compositeness of *n*.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

For example, take *n* = 71. Then *n* − 1 = 70 and the prime factors of 70 are 2, 5 and 7. We randomly select an *a* = 17 \< *n*. Now we compute:

$$17^{70}\ \equiv\ 1 \pmod {71}.$$

For all integers *a* it is known that

$$a^{n - 1}\equiv 1 \pmod{n}\ \text{ if and only if } \text{ ord}(a)\mid(n-1).$$

Therefore, the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) of 17 (mod 71) is not necessarily 70 because some factor of 70 may also work above. So check 70 divided by its prime factors:

$$17^{35}\ \equiv\ 70\ \not\equiv\ 1 \pmod {71}$$

$$17^{14}\ \equiv\ 25\ \not\equiv\ 1 \pmod {71}$$

$$17^{10}\ \equiv\ 1\ \equiv\ 1 \pmod {71}.$$

Unfortunately, we get that 17<sup>10</sup> ≡ 1 (mod 71). So we still do not know if 71 is prime or not.

We try another random *a*, this time choosing *a* = 11. Now we compute:

$$11^{70}\ \equiv\ 1 \pmod {71}.$$

Again, this does not show that the multiplicative order of 11 (mod 71) is 70 because some factor of 70 may also work. So check 70 divided by its prime factors:

$$11^{35}\ \equiv\ 70\ \not\equiv\ 1 \pmod {71}$$

$$11^{14}\ \equiv\ 54\ \not\equiv\ 1 \pmod {71}$$

$$11^{10}\ \equiv\ 32\ \not\equiv\ 1 \pmod {71}.$$

So the multiplicative order of 11 (mod 71) is 70, and thus 71 is prime.

(To carry out these [modular exponentiations](wikipedia://en/903032), one could use a fast exponentiation algorithm like [binary](wikipedia://en/10237) or [addition-chain exponentiation](wikipedia://en/856356)).

:::

## Algorithm {#Algorithm}

The algorithm can be written in [pseudocode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudocode) as follows:

**algorithm** lucas\_primality\_test **is** **input**: *n* > 2, an odd integer to be tested for primality. *k*, a parameter that determines the accuracy of the test. **output**: *prime* if *n* is prime, otherwise *composite* or *possibly composite*.

determine the prime factors of *n*−1.

LOOP1: **repeat** *k* times: pick *a* randomly in the range \[2, *n* − 1\]

**if** $a^{n-1} \not\equiv 1 \pmod n$ **then**

**return** *composite* **else** gray $\color{Gray}{a^{n-1} \equiv 1 \pmod n}$ LOOP2: **for** all prime factors *q* of *n*−1:

**if** $a^\frac{n-1}q \not\equiv 1 \pmod n$ **then**

**if** we checked this equality for all prime factors of *n*−1 **then** **return** *prime* **else** **continue** LOOP2 **else** gray $\color{Gray}{a^\frac{n-1}q \equiv 1 \pmod n}$ **continue** LOOP1

**return** *possibly composite*.
