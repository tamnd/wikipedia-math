# Cantor space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cantor space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Cantor space**, named for [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216), is a [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) abstraction of the classical [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172): a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is a Cantor space if it is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to the Cantor set. In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), the topological space 2<sup>ω</sup> is called "the" Cantor space.

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) itself is a Cantor space. But the canonical example of a Cantor space is the [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite) [topological product](wikipedia://en/24910) of the [discrete 2-point space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_2-point_space) {0, 1}. This is usually written as $2^\mathbb{N}$ or 2<sup>ω</sup> (where 2 denotes the 2-element [set](wikipedia://en/26691) {0,1} with the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology)). A point in 2<sup>ω</sup> is an infinite binary sequence, that is a sequence that assumes only the values 0 or 1. Given such a sequence *a*<sub>0</sub>, *a*<sub>1</sub>, *a*<sub>2</sub>,..., one can map it to the [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438)

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{2 a_n}{3^{n+1}}.$$

This mapping gives a homeomorphism from 2<sup>ω</sup> onto the Cantor set, demonstrating that 2<sup>ω</sup> is indeed a Cantor space.

Cantor spaces occur abundantly in [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478). For example, they exist as [subspaces](wikipedia://en/910926) in every [perfect](wikipedia://en/5075551), [complete metric space](wikipedia://en/7832). (To see this, note that in such a space, any [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) perfect set contains two [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) non-empty perfect subsets of arbitrarily small diameter, and so one can imitate the construction of the usual [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172).) Also, every [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable), [separable](wikipedia://en/27855), [completely metrizable space](wikipedia://en/7975189) contains Cantor spaces as subspaces. This includes most of the common spaces in real analysis.

:::

## Characterization {#Characterization}

A topological characterization of Cantor spaces is given by [Brouwer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luitzen_Egbertus_Jan_Brouwer)'s theorem:[^o6]

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Brouwer's theorem"}

**Brouwer's theorem.**

Any two non-empty [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Hausdorff spaces](wikipedia://en/13637) without [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557) and having countable [bases](wikipedia://en/59220) consisting of [clopen sets](wikipedia://en/233398) are homeomorphic to each other.

:::

The topological property of having a base consisting of clopen sets is sometimes known as "[zero-dimensionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-dimensional)". Brouwer's theorem can be restated as:

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

A [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is a Cantor space [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is non-empty, [perfect](wikipedia://en/5075551), [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected), and [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable).

:::

This theorem is also equivalent (via [Stone's representation theorem for Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/604111)) to the fact that any two [countable atomless Boolean algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_atomless_Boolean_algebra) are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic).

Without metrizability, the statement fails. A [Cantor cube](wikipedia://en/4999981) $2^\alpha$, for $\alpha$ an uncountable ordinal, is not homeomorphic to the Cantor space (and thus is not metrizable).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

As can be expected from Brouwer's theorem, Cantor spaces appear in several forms. But many properties of Cantor spaces can be established using 2<sup>ω</sup>, because its construction as a product makes it amenable to analysis.

Cantor spaces have the following properties:

- The [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of any Cantor space is $2^{\aleph_0}$, that is, the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901).
- The product of two (or even any finite or countable number of) Cantor spaces is a Cantor space. Along with the [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819), this fact can be used to construct [space-filling curves](wikipedia://en/352398).
- A (non-empty) Hausdorff topological space is compact metrizable if and only if it is a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a Cantor space.[^o11][^o12][^o13]

Let *C*(*X*) denote the space of all real-valued, [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) continuous [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on a topological space *X*. Let *K* denote a compact [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), and Δ denote the Cantor set. Then the Cantor set has the following property:

- *C*(*K*) is [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) to a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) subspace of *C*(Δ).[^o14]

In general, this isometry is not unique, and thus is not properly a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) in the [categorical](wikipedia://en/5869) sense.

- The [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of all homeomorphisms of the Cantor space is [simple](wikipedia://en/47600).[^o15]

## Cantor group {#Cantor_group}

The Cantor space becomes an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) $\{0,1\}^{\mathbb Z}$ where the group operation is defined as pointwise addition [modulo](wikipedia://en/1352428) 2. Thus if $(x_i)_{i\in\mathbb Z}$ and $(y_i)_{i\in\mathbb Z}$ are two points of the Cantor space, with $x_i,y_i\in\{0,1\}$, the group law is given by

$$(x_i) + (y_i) = (x_i+y_i\bmod 2).$$

The identity element is the constant sequence $x_i=0$. Since $x+x\equiv 0\pmod{2}$ for any $x\in\{0,1\}$, every element of the group is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450), i.e., $(x_i) + (x_i) = (0)_{i\in\mathbb Z}$ or, equivalently, the inverse element of $(x_i)$ is $(x_i)$ itself, i.e., $-(x_i) = (x_i)$.

The Cantor group is a compact abelian [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315), making it an important example in [abstract harmonic analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_harmonic_analysis). Every continuous [character](wikipedia://en/293418) on the Cantor group Every continuous character of $G=\{0,1\}^{\mathbb Z}$ has the form

$$\chi_F(x)=(-1)^{\sum_{i\in F}x_i},$$

where $F\subset\mathbb Z$ is finite. Thus the [Pontryagin dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontryagin_dual) is naturally identified with the group of finite subsets of $\mathbb Z$, under [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288), or equivalently with

$$\bigoplus_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$$

under addition. This can be identified with the additive group of

$$\widehat G = \mathbb F_2[t^{-1},t]$$

of [Laurent polynomials](wikipedia://en/61351) over the [field with two elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_with_two_elements). Here $t$ corresponds to the character $\chi_1(x) = (-1)^{x_1}.$ This group is sometimes known as the *Walsh group* in this context.[^o18]

A particularly important group of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160) of the Cantor group are the [shift maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shift_map)

$$s_n(x_i) = (x_{i+n})$$

for $n$ an integer. The effect of $s_n$ is to shift the sequence to the left $n$ units (if $n > 0$) or to the right by $|n|$ units (if $n < 0$). The mapping $n\mapsto s_n$ is a representation of the additive group of integers $\mathbb Z$ into the autormorphism group of $G$. On characters, the shift action is $\chi(s_nx) = t^n\chi(x)$.

The [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) on the Cantor group is the [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) that assigns each coordinate $x_i$ an equal probability $1/2$ of coming up $0$ or $1$. Equivalently, the measure assigned to a cylinder set $X = \{(x_i) | x_{i_1}=a_1, x_{i_2}=a_2,\dots, x_{i_k}=a_k\}$, where the indices $i_j$ are distinct, is $2^{-k}$.

The Cantor space $2^{\mathbb N}$ maps to the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324), by the map $(x_i)_{i\in\mathbb N} \mapsto \sum_{n=0}^\infty\frac{x_n}{2^{n+1}}$. Under this map, the [pushforward measure](wikipedia://en/9412979) of the Haar measure is the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), and the Walsh characters define the [Rademacher functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rademacher_function). This view is useful in the [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147).

The Cantor group is a protypical example of a [profinite group](wikipedia://en/24266). It is the [inverse limit](wikipedia://en/15109) of the groups $F_2^n$ under the coordinate projection maps $F_2^{n+1}\to F_2^n$. Topologically, every profinite group is homeomorphic to the Cantor space, but need not be isomorphic to it. An example is the additive group $\mathbb Z_2$ of [2-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-adic_integers). This is an inverse limit of the groups $\mathbb Z/2^n\mathbb Z$ under the reduction maps $\mathbb Z/2^{n+1}\xrightarrow{\bmod 2^n}\mathbb Z/2^n\mathbb Z$, but is not isomorphic to it as a group. For example, $\mathbb Z_2$ contains no nontrivial involutions. More generally, the additive group of the [p-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integers) is homeomorphic to the Cantor space (as is the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) in any non-archimedean [local field](wikipedia://en/145375)).

## Topology {#Topology}

The [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) of the Cantor space can be specified by giving the [basic open sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_open_set). Given a finite sequence $a_1,\dots,a_n\in\{0,1\}$, the [cylinder set](wikipedia://en/2613658)

$$U(a_1,\dots,a_n) = \{ (x_i) | x_1 = a_1,\dots,x_n=a_n\}$$

is an open set. It is also a closed set, its complement being the union of open sets $U(a'_1,a'_k,\dots,a'_n)$ where at least one $a_i'$ is different from $a_i$. Thus it is a [clopen set](wikipedia://en/233398). Not every open subset of the Cantor space is clopen, but this basis of the topology consists entirely of clopen sets, a general feature of profinite spaces.

This basis of the topology can be described as follows.[^o20] If $F$ is a finite subset of $\mathbb N$, then there is a projection $\pi_F : \{0,1\}^{\mathbb N}\to \{0,1\}^F$ corresponding to restricting the coordinates to $F$. Now, $\{0,1\}^F$ is a finite set, and it can be given the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology). The topology on the Cantor space is the coarsest topology making all of the maps $\pi_F$ continuous; i.e., it is the [initial topology](wikipedia://en/1197123) of the family of projections. The basic open sets are thus stratified into

$$\mathcal U_F = \{\pi_F^{-1}(S) | S\subset \{0,1\}^F\}.$$

The topology on the Cantor space is generated by $\bigcup_{F \subset \mathbb N,\, |F|<\infty} \mathcal U_F$, by closing this under the unions of elements.

### Continuous functions {#Continuous_functions}

A function on the Cantor space is continuous if, for every $\epsilon > 0$ there is a finite set $F$ of integers such that $|f(x) - f(y)| < \epsilon$ for all $x$ and $y$ that agree on $F$.

### Locally constant functions and distributions {#Locally_constant_functions_and_distributions}

A particular subspace of the space of continuous functions is the [locally constant functions](wikipedia://en/295629). A function is locally constant if there is a partition of the Cantor space into clopen subsets, such that the function is constant on each set of the partition. Let $C_{lc}(\Delta)$ denote the space of locally constant functions on the Cantor space $\Delta$. The space $C_{lc}(\Delta)$ carries a finer topology than $C(\Delta)$ by declaring that a sequence $f_n\to 0$ if any only if all but finitely many $f_n$ agree outside a finite set of $F$ of coordinates, and converge on the residual finite-dimensional space of functions on $\{0,1\}^F$. This stronger topology is a [nuclear topology](wikipedia://en/2702957) defining a space of [test functions](wikipedia://en/301505) and whose [continuous dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual) defines a space of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).

## Banach algebras {#Banach_algebras}

Several natural commutative [Banach algebras](wikipedia://en/4665) are associated with the Cantor space (or group) $\Delta$. The Banach algebra $C(\Delta)$ under pointwise multiplication, with the [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm), is the most fundamental. It has the property that the [Gelfand spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelfand_spectrum) is $\Delta$ itself, and the Gelfand embedding $C(\Delta)\to C(\Delta^\vee)$ is an isomorphism (and is canonical). This is typical of [Stone spaces](wikipedia://en/380219), but not of other [compact Hausdorff spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Hausdorff_space).

On the Cantor group, another Banach algebra is the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/381010), which is the space $L^1(\Delta, \mu)$ of $L^1$-integrable classes of functions on $\Delta$ with respect to the Haar measure $\mu$, and the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) operation as multiplication:

$$(f*g)(t) = \int_\Delta f(t-x)g(x)\,dx.$$

The maximal ideal space is the Pontryan dual $\widehat\Delta=\bigoplus_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Z/2\mathbb Z$. The Gelfand transform is the Fourier transform, associating to $f\in L^1(\Delta,\mu)$ the function $\hat f(\gamma)$ of a character $\gamma$ given by $\hat f(\gamma) = \int_\Delta f(x)\gamma(x)\,dx$ (there is no [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) in the characters, because the characters are all real.)

Likewise, the algebra $\ell^1(\widehat\Delta)$ of absolutely summable functions on the countable [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) $\widehat\Delta$ is a group algebra under the group convolution. Then $\Delta$ is the space of maximal ideals in $\ell^1(\widehat\Delta)$, and the Fourier transform is a one-to-one map from $\ell^1(\widehat\Delta)$ onto a subalgebra of $C(\Delta)$, the [Wiener algebra](wikipedia://en/27489616). The Wiener algebra $A(\Delta)$ includes all of the locally constant functions, but is a proper subset of $C(\Delta)$:

$$C_{lc}(\Delta)\subset A(\Delta) \subset C(\Delta).$$
