# Dickson's lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dickson's lemma"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Dickson's lemma** states that every set of $n$-tuples of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) has finitely many [minimal elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element), for the pointwise partial order. This simple fact from [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) has become attributed to the American algebraist [L. E. Dickson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Dickson), who used it to [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) a result in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) about [perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/23670).[^o12] However, the [lemma](wikipedia://en/18634) was certainly known earlier, for example to [Paul Gordan](wikipedia://en/1059850) in his research on [invariant theory](wikipedia://en/363762).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

*Image: Dickson hyperbola9.svg.* Infinitely many minimal pairs of real numbers *x*,*y* (the black hyperbola) but only five minimal pairs of positive integers (red) have *xy* ≥ 9.

Let $K$ be a fixed natural number, and let $S = \{(x,y)\mid xy\ge K\}$ be the set of pairs of numbers whose product is at least $K$. When defined over the positive [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), $S$ has infinitely many minimal elements of the form $(x,K/x)$, one for each positive number $x$; this set of points forms one of the branches of a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052). The pairs on this hyperbola are minimal, because it is not possible for a different pair that belongs to $S$ to be less than or equal to $(x,K/x)$ in both of its coordinates. However, Dickson's lemma concerns only tuples of natural numbers, and over the natural numbers there are only finitely many minimal pairs. Every minimal pair $(x,y)$ of natural numbers has $x \le K$ and $y \le K$, for if *x* were greater than *K* then (*x* − 1, *y*) would also belong to *S*, contradicting the minimality of (*x*, *y*), and symmetrically if *y* were greater than *K* then (*x*, *y* − 1) would also belong to *S*. Therefore, over the natural numbers, $S$ has at most $K^2$ minimal elements, a finite number.[^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Formal statement"}

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

Let $\mathbb{N}$ be the set of non-negative integers ([natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474)), let *n* be any fixed constant, and let $\mathbb{N}^n$ be the set of $n$-tuples of natural numbers. These tuples may be given a [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797) [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order), the [product order](wikipedia://en/1956306), in which $(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)\le (b_1,b_2,\dots b_n)$ if and only if $a_i\le b_i$ for every $i$. The set of tuples that are greater than or equal to some particular tuple $(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)$ forms a positive [orthant](wikipedia://en/1283725) with its apex at the given tuple.

With this notation, Dickson's lemma may be stated in several equivalent forms:

- In every [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $S$ of $\mathbb{N}^n$ there is at least one but no more than a finite number of elements that are [minimal elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_element) of $S$ for the pointwise partial order.[^o8]
- For every infinite sequence $(x_i)_{i\in\mathbb{N}}$ of $n$-tuples of natural numbers, there exist two indices $i<j$ such that $x_i \leq x_j$ holds with respect to the pointwise order.[^o9]
- The partially ordered set $(\mathbb{N}^n,\le)$ does not contain infinite [antichains](wikipedia://en/348973) nor [infinite (strictly) descending sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descending_chain_condition) of $n$-tuples.[^o9]
- The partially ordered set $(\mathbb{N}^n,\le)$ is a [well partial order](wikipedia://en/739775).[^o10]
- Every subset $S$ of $\mathbb{N}^n$ may be covered by a finite set of positive orthants, whose apexes all belong to $S$.

:::

## Generalizations and applications {#Generalizations_and_applications}

Dickson used his lemma to prove that, for any given number $n$, there can exist only a finite number of [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) [perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/23670) that have at most $n$ [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) factors.[^o12] However, [it remains open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_perfect_number) whether there exist any odd perfect numbers at all.

The [divisibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisibility) [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558) among the [*P*-smooth numbers](wikipedia://en/1035039), natural numbers whose prime factors all belong to the [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) *P*, gives these numbers the structure of a partially ordered set [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/23916899) to $(\mathbb{N}^{|P|},\le)$. Thus, for any set *S* of *P*-smooth numbers, there is a finite subset of *S* such that every element of *S* is divisible by one of the numbers in this subset. This fact has been used, for instance, to show that there exists an [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for classifying the winning and losing moves from the initial position in the game of [Sylver coinage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sylver_coinage), even though the algorithm itself remains unknown.[^o13]

The tuples $(a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n)$ in $\mathbb{N}^n$ correspond one-for-one with the [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) $x_1^{a_1} x_2^{a_2} \dots x_n^{a_n}$ over a set of $n$ variables $x_1,x_2,\dots x_n$. Under this correspondence, Dickson's lemma may be seen as a special case of [Hilbert's basis theorem](wikipedia://en/13733) stating that every [polynomial](wikipedia://en/373065) [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) has a finite basis, for the ideals generated by monomials. Indeed, [Paul Gordan](wikipedia://en/1059850) used this restatement of Dickson's lemma in 1899 as part of a proof of Hilbert's basis theorem.[^o2]
