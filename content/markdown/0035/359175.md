# Serre duality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Serre duality"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Serre duality** is a [duality](wikipedia://en/609737) for the [coherent sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/23535337) of algebraic varieties, proved by [Jean-Pierre Serre](wikipedia://en/205483). The basic version applies to [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524) on a smooth projective variety, but [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) found wide generalizations, for example to singular varieties. On an *n*-dimensional variety, the theorem says that a cohomology group $H^i$ is the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of another one, $H^{n-i}$. Serre duality is the analog for coherent sheaf cohomology of [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732) in topology, with the [canonical line bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_line_bundle) replacing the [orientation sheaf](wikipedia://en/49112440).

:::

The Serre duality theorem is also true in [complex geometry](wikipedia://en/186101) more generally, for compact [complex manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_manifolds) that are not necessarily [projective](wikipedia://en/320469) [complex algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/39219687). In this setting, the Serre duality theorem is an application of [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590) for [Dolbeault cohomology](wikipedia://en/3189601), and may be seen as a result in the theory of [elliptic operators](wikipedia://en/550137).

These two different interpretations of Serre duality coincide for non-singular projective complex algebraic varieties, by an application of [Dolbeault's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolbeault's_theorem) relating sheaf cohomology to Dolbeault cohomology.

## Serre duality for vector bundles {#Serre_duality_for_vector_bundles}

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Algebraic theorem"}

### Algebraic theorem {#Algebraic_theorem}

Let *X* be a [smooth variety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_variety) of dimension *n* over a field *k*. Define the **canonical line bundle** $K_X$ to be the bundle of [*n*-forms](wikipedia://en/603780#Examples_of_vector_bundles) on *X*, the top exterior power of the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600):

$$K_X=\Omega^n_X={\bigwedge}^n(T^*X).$$

Suppose in addition that *X* is [proper](wikipedia://en/603782) (for example, [projective](wikipedia://en/320469)) over *k*. Then Serre duality says: for an [algebraic vector bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_vector_bundle) *E* on *X* and an integer *i*, there is a natural isomorphism:

$$H^i(X,E)\cong H^{n-i}(X,K_X\otimes E^{\ast})^{\ast}$$

of finite-dimensional *k*-vector spaces. Here $\otimes$ denotes the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of vector bundles. It follows that the dimensions of the two cohomology groups are equal:

$$h^i(X,E)=h^{n-i}(X,K_X\otimes E^{\ast}).$$

As in Poincaré duality, the isomorphism in Serre duality comes from the [cup product](wikipedia://en/1055357#Cup_product) in sheaf cohomology. Namely, the composition of the cup product with a natural **trace map** on $H^n(X,K_X)$ is a [perfect pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_pairing):

$$H^i(X,E)\times H^{n-i}(X,K_X\otimes E^{\ast})\to H^n(X,K_X)\to k.$$

The trace map is the analog for coherent sheaf cohomology of integration in [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530).[^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Differential-geometric theorem"}

### Differential-geometric theorem {#Differential-geometric_theorem}

Serre also proved the same duality statement for *X* a compact [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742) and *E* a [holomorphic vector bundle](wikipedia://en/4794668).[^o8] Here, the Serre duality theorem is a consequence of [Hodge theory](wikipedia://en/501590). Namely, on a compact complex manifold $X$ equipped with a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric), there is a [Hodge star operator](wikipedia://en/310914):

$$\star: \Omega^p(X) \to \Omega^{2n-p}(X),$$

where $\dim_{\mathbb{C}} X = n$. Additionally, since $X$ is complex, there is a splitting of the [complex differential forms](wikipedia://en/2497815) into forms of type $(p,q)$. The Hodge star operator (extended complex-linearly to complex-valued differential forms) interacts with this grading as:

$$\star: \Omega^{p,q}(X) \to \Omega^{n-q,n-p}(X).$$

Notice that the holomorphic and anti-holomorphic indices have switched places. There is a conjugation on complex differential forms which interchanges forms of type $(p,q)$ and $(q,p)$, and if one defines the **conjugate-linear Hodge star operator** by $\bar{\star}\omega = \star \bar{\omega}$ then we have:

$$\bar{\star} : \Omega^{p,q}(X) \to \Omega^{n-p,n-q}(X).$$

Using the conjugate-linear Hodge star, one may define a [Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian) $L^2$-inner product on complex differential forms, by:

$$\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle_{L^2} = \int_X \alpha \wedge \bar{\star}\beta,$$

where now $\alpha \wedge \bar{\star}\beta$ is an $(n,n)$-form, and in particular a complex-valued $2n$-form and can therefore be integrated on $X$ with respect to its canonical [orientation](wikipedia://en/187446). Furthermore, suppose $(E,h)$ is a Hermitian holomorphic vector bundle. Then the Hermitian metric $h$ gives a conjugate-linear isomorphism $E\cong E^*$ between $E$ and its [dual vector bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_vector_bundle), say $\tau: E\to E^*$. Defining $\bar{\star}_E (\omega \otimes s) = \bar{\star} \omega \otimes \tau(s)$, one obtains an isomorphism:

$$\bar{\star}_E : \Omega^{p,q}(X,E) \to \Omega^{n-p,n-q}(X,E^*)$$

where $\Omega^{p,q}(X,E)= \Omega^{p,q}(X) \otimes \Gamma(E)$ consists of smooth $E$-valued complex differential forms. Using the pairing between $E$ and $E^*$ given by $\tau$ and $h$, one can therefore define a Hermitian $L^2$-inner product on such $E$-valued forms by:

$$\langle \alpha, \beta \rangle_{L^2} = \int_X \alpha \wedge_h \bar{\star}_E \beta,$$

where here $\wedge_h$ means wedge product of differential forms and using the pairing between $E$ and $E^*$ given by $h$.

The **Hodge theorem for Dolbeault cohomology** asserts that if we define:

$$\Delta_{\bar{\partial}_E} = \bar{\partial}_E^* \bar{\partial}_E + \bar{\partial}_E \bar{\partial}_E^*$$

where $\bar{\partial}_E$ is the [Dolbeault operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolbeault_operator) of $E$ and $\bar{\partial}_E^*$ is its formal adjoint with respect to the inner product, then:

$$H^{p,q}(X,E) \cong \mathcal{H}^{p,q}_{\Delta_{\bar{\partial}_E}} (X).$$

On the left is Dolbeault cohomology, and on the right is the vector space of **harmonic $E$-valued differential forms** defined by:

$$\mathcal{H}^{p,q}_{\Delta_{\bar{\partial}_E}} (X) = \{\alpha \in \Omega^{p,q}(X,E) \mid \Delta_{\bar{\partial}_E} (\alpha) = 0\}.$$

Using this description, the Serre duality theorem can be stated as follows: The isomorphism $\bar{\star}_E$ induces a complex linear isomorphism:

$$H^{p,q}(X,E) \cong H^{n-p,n-q}(X,E^*)^*.$$

This can be easily proved using the Hodge theory above. Namely, if $[\alpha]$ is a cohomology class in $H^{p,q}(X,E)$ with unique harmonic representative $\alpha \in \mathcal{H}^{p,q}_{\Delta_{\bar{\partial}_E}} (X)$, then:

$$(\alpha, \bar{\star}_E \alpha) = \langle \alpha, \alpha \rangle_{L^2} \ge 0$$

with equality if and only if $\alpha = 0$. In particular, the complex linear pairing:

$$(\alpha, \beta) = \int_X \alpha \wedge_h \beta$$

between $\mathcal{H}^{p,q}_{\Delta_{\bar{\partial}_E}} (X)$ and $\mathcal{H}^{n-p,n-q}_{\Delta_{\bar{\partial}_{E^*}}} (X)$ is [non-degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-degenerate), and induces the isomorphism in the Serre duality theorem.

The statement of Serre duality in the algebraic setting may be recovered by taking $p=0$, and applying [Dolbeault's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolbeault's_theorem), which states that:

$$H^{p,q}(X,E) \cong H^q(X, \boldsymbol{\Omega}^p \otimes E)$$

where on the left is Dolbeault cohomology and on the right sheaf cohomology, where $\boldsymbol{\Omega}^p$ denotes the sheaf of holomorphic $(p,0)$-forms. In particular, we obtain:

$$H^q(X,E) \cong H^{0,q}(X,E) \cong H^{n,n-q}(X,E^*)^* \cong H^{n-q}(X, K_X \otimes E^*)^*$$

where we have used that the sheaf of holomorphic $(n,0)$-forms is just the [canonical bundle](wikipedia://en/734893) of $X$.

:::

## Algebraic curves {#Algebraic_curves}

A fundamental application of Serre duality is to [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260). (Over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), it is equivalent to consider [compact Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Riemann_surface).) For a line bundle *L* on a smooth projective curve *X* over a field *k*, the only possibly nonzero cohomology groups are $H^0(X,L)$ and $H^1(X,L)$. Serre duality describes the $H^1$ group in terms of an $H^0$ group (for a different line bundle).[^o10] That is more concrete, since $H^0$ of a line bundle is simply its space of sections.

Serre duality is especially relevant to the [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261) for curves. For a line bundle *L* of degree *d* on a curve *X* of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) *g*, the Riemann–Roch theorem says that:

$$h^0(X,L)-h^1(X,L)=d-g+1.$$

Using Serre duality, this can be restated in more elementary terms:

$$h^0(X,L)-h^0(X,K_X\otimes L^*)=d-g+1.$$

The latter statement (expressed in terms of [divisors](wikipedia://en/1693055)) is in fact the original version of the theorem from the 19th century. This is the main tool used to analyze how a given curve can be embedded into [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) and hence to classify algebraic curves.

Example: Every global section of a line bundle of negative degree is zero. Moreover, the degree of the canonical bundle is $2g-2$. Therefore, Riemann–Roch implies that for a line bundle *L* of degree $d>2g-2$, $h^0(X,L)$ is equal to $d-g+1$. When the genus *g* is at least 2, it follows by Serre duality that $h^1(X,TX)=h^0(X,K_X^{\otimes 2})=3g-3$. Here $H^1(X,TX)$ is the first-order [deformation space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) of *X*. This is the basic calculation needed to show that the [moduli space of curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_space_of_curves) of genus *g* has dimension $3g-3$.

## Serre duality for coherent sheaves {#Serre_duality_for_coherent_sheaves}

Another formulation of Serre duality holds for all [coherent sheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_sheaves), not just vector bundles. As a first step in generalizing Serre duality, Grothendieck showed that this version works for [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) with mild singularities, [Cohen–Macaulay schemes](wikipedia://en/497407), not just smooth schemes.

Namely, for a Cohen–Macaulay scheme *X* of pure dimension *n* over a field *k*, Grothendieck defined a coherent sheaf $\omega_X$ on *X* called the **[dualizing sheaf](wikipedia://en/47021614)**. (Some authors call this sheaf $K_X$.) Suppose in addition that *X* is proper over *k*. For a coherent sheaf *E* on *X* and an integer *i*, Serre duality says that there is a natural isomorphism:

$$\operatorname{Ext}^i_X(E,\omega_X)\cong H^{n-i}(X,E)^*$$

of finite-dimensional *k*-vector spaces.[^o12] Here the [Ext group](wikipedia://en/840758) is taken in the [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) of [$O_X$-modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaves_of_modules). This includes the previous statement, since $\operatorname{Ext}^i_X(E,\omega_X)$ is isomorphic to $H^i(X,E^*\otimes \omega_X)$ when *E* is a vector bundle.

In order to use this result, one has to determine the dualizing sheaf explicitly, at least in special cases. When *X* is smooth over *k*, $\omega_X$ is the canonical line bundle $K_X$ defined above. More generally, if *X* is a Cohen–Macaulay subscheme of [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) *r* in a smooth scheme *Y* over *k*, then the dualizing sheaf can be described as an [Ext sheaf](wikipedia://en/44887605#Sheaf_extension):[^o13]

$$\omega_X\cong\mathcal{Ext}^r_{O_Y}(O_X,K_Y).$$

When *X* is a [local complete intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_complete_intersection) of codimension *r* in a smooth scheme *Y*, there is a more elementary description: the normal bundle of *X* in *Y* is a vector bundle of rank *r*, and the dualizing sheaf of *X* is given by:[^o14]

$$\omega_X\cong K_Y|_X\otimes {\bigwedge}^r(N_{X/Y}).$$

In this case, *X* is a Cohen–Macaulay scheme with $\omega_X$ a line bundle, which says that *X* is [Gorenstein](wikipedia://en/48497362).

Example: Let *X* be a [complete intersection](wikipedia://en/3116835) in projective space ${\mathbf P}^n$ over a field *k*, defined by homogeneous polynomials $f_1,\ldots,f_r$ of degrees $d_1,\ldots,d_r$. (To say that this is a complete intersection means that *X* has dimension $n-r$.) There are line bundles *O*(*d*) on ${\mathbf P}^n$ for integers *d*, with the property that homogeneous polynomials of degree *d* can be viewed as sections of *O*(*d*). Then the dualizing sheaf of *X* is the line bundle:

$$\omega_X=O(d_1+\cdots+d_r-n-1)|_X,$$

by the [adjunction formula](wikipedia://en/3118117). For example, the dualizing sheaf of a plane curve *X* of degree *d* is $O(d-3)|_X$.

### Complex moduli of Calabi–Yau threefolds {#Complex_moduli_of_Calabi–Yau_threefolds}

In particular, we can compute the number of complex deformations, equal to $\dim(H^1(X,TX))$ for a [quintic threefold](wikipedia://en/23562429) in $\mathbb{P}^4$, a Calabi–Yau variety, using Serre duality. Since the Calabi–Yau property ensures $K_X \cong \mathcal{O}_X$ Serre duality shows us that $H^1(X,TX) \cong H^2(X, \mathcal{O}_X\otimes \Omega_X) \cong H^2(X, \Omega_X)$ showing the number of complex moduli is equal to $h^{2,1}$ in the [Hodge diamond](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_diamond). Of course, the last statement depends on the Bogomolev–Tian–Todorov theorem which states every deformation on a Calabi–Yau is unobstructed.

## Grothendieck duality {#Grothendieck_duality}

Grothendieck's theory of [coherent duality](wikipedia://en/1815224) is a broad generalization of Serre duality, using the language of [derived categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_categories). For any scheme *X* of finite type over a field *k*, there is an object $\omega_X^{\bullet}$ of the bounded derived category of coherent sheaves on *X*, $D^b_{\operatorname{coh}}(X)$, called the **dualizing complex** of *X* over *k*. Formally, $\omega_X^{\bullet}$ is the [exceptional inverse image](wikipedia://en/15606646) $f^!O_Y$, where *f* is the given morphism $X\to Y=\operatorname{Spec}(k)$. When *X* is Cohen–Macaulay of pure dimension *n*, $\omega_X^{\bullet}$ is $\omega_X[n]$; that is, it is the dualizing sheaf discussed above, viewed as a complex in (cohomological) degree −*n*. In particular, when *X* is smooth over *k*, $\omega_X^{\bullet}$ is the canonical line bundle placed in degree −*n*.

Using the dualizing complex, Serre duality generalizes to any proper scheme *X* over *k*. Namely, there is a natural isomorphism of finite-dimensional *k*-vector spaces:

$$\operatorname{Hom}_X(E,\omega_X^{\bullet})\cong \operatorname{Hom}_X(O_X,E)^*$$

for any object *E* in $D^b_{\operatorname{coh}}(X)$.[^o17]

More generally, for a proper scheme *X* over *k*, an object *E* in $D^b_{\operatorname{coh}}(X)$, and *F* a [perfect complex](wikipedia://en/46630707) in $D_{\operatorname{perf}}(X)$, one has the elegant statement:

$$\operatorname{Hom}_X(E,F\otimes \omega_X^{\bullet})\cong\operatorname{Hom}_X(F,E)^*.$$

Here the tensor product means the [derived tensor product](wikipedia://en/54501194), as is natural in derived categories. (To compare with previous formulations, note that $\operatorname{Ext}^i_X(E,\omega_X)$ can be viewed as $\operatorname{Hom}_X(E,\omega_X[i])$.) When *X* is also smooth over *k*, every object in $D^b_{\operatorname{coh}}(X)$ is a perfect complex, and so this duality applies to all *E* and *F* in $D^b_{\operatorname{coh}}(X)$. The statement above is then summarized by saying that $F\mapsto F\otimes \omega_X^{\bullet}$ is a **Serre functor** on $D^b_{\operatorname{coh}}(X)$ for *X* smooth and proper over *k*.[^o18]

Serre duality holds more generally for proper [algebraic spaces](wikipedia://en/2394027) over a field.[^o19]
