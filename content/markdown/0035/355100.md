# Quiver (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quiver"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), a **quiver** is another name for a [multidigraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multidigraph); that is, a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) where [loops](wikipedia://en/1449083) and multiple arrows between two [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) are allowed. Quivers are commonly used in representation theory: a representation $V$ of a quiver assigns a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V(x)$ to each vertex $x$ of the quiver and a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $V(a)$ to each arrow $a$.

:::

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a quiver can be understood to be the underlying structure of a small [category](wikipedia://en/53759), but without composition or a designation of identity morphisms. That is, there is a [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) from $\mathbf{Cat}$ (the [category of small categories](wikipedia://en/2072741)) to $\mathbf{Quiv}$ (the category of multidigraphs). Its [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) is a [free functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_functor) which, from a quiver, makes the corresponding [free category](wikipedia://en/36769284).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A quiver $\Gamma$ consists of:

- The set $V$ of vertices of $\Gamma$
- The set $E$ of edges of $\Gamma$
- Two functions: $s:E \to V$ giving the *start* or *source* of the edge, and another function, $t:E \to V$ giving the *target* of the edge.

This definition is identical to that of a [multidigraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multidigraph) that has edges with their own distinct identity.

An edge $e \in E$ is a *loop* if $s(e) = t(e).$

A [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) of quivers is a mapping from vertices to vertices which takes directed edges to directed edges. Formally, if $\Gamma=(V,E,s,t)$ and $\Gamma'=(V',E',s',t')$ are two quivers, then a morphism $m=(m_v, m_e)$ of quivers consists of two functions $m_v: V\to V'$ and $m_e: E\to E'$ such that the following [diagrams commute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commuting_diagram):

*Image: Quiver Morphism Start Diagram.svg.*

*Image: Quiver Morphism Target Diagram.svg.*

That is,

$$m_v \circ s = s' \circ m_e$$

and

$$m_v \circ t = t' \circ m_e$$

:::

## Category-theoretic definition {#Category-theoretic_definition}

The above definition is based in [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553); the category-theoretic definition generalizes this into a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from the *free quiver* to the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829).

The **free quiver** (also called the **walking quiver**, **Kronecker quiver**, **2-Kronecker quiver** or **Kronecker category**) $Q$ is a category with two objects, and four morphisms: The objects are $V$ and $E$. The four morphisms are $s: E \to V$, $t: E \to V$, and the [identity morphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_morphism) $\mathrm{id}_V: V \to V$ and $\mathrm{id}_E: E \to E$. That is, the free quiver is the category

$$E \;\begin{matrix} s \\[-6pt] \rightrightarrows \\[-4pt] t \end{matrix}\; V$$

A quiver is then a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) $\Gamma:Q \to \mathbf{Set}$. (That is to say, $\Gamma$ specifies two sets $\Gamma(V)$ and $\Gamma(E)$, and two functions $\Gamma(s),\Gamma(t)\colon \Gamma(E) \longrightarrow \Gamma(V)$; this is the full extent of what it means to be a functor from $Q$ to $\mathbf{Set}$.)

More generally, a quiver in a category $C$ is a functor $\Gamma: Q \to C.$ The category $\mathbf{Quiv}(C)$ of quivers in $C$ is the [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) where:

- objects are functors $\Gamma:Q \to C,$
- morphisms are [natural transformations](wikipedia://en/45022) between functors.

Note that $\mathbf{Quiv}$ is the [category of presheaves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_presheaves) on the [opposite category](wikipedia://en/372399) $Q^{\operatorname{op}}$.

## Path algebra {#Path_algebra}

If $\Gamma$ is a quiver, then a **path** in $\Gamma$ is a sequence of arrows

$$a_n a_{n-1} \dots a_3 a_2 a_1$$

such that the head of $a_{i+1}$ is the tail of $a_i$ for $i=1,\ldots,n-1$, using the convention of concatenating paths from right to left. Note that a [path in graph theory](wikipedia://en/638889) has a stricter definition, and that this concept instead coincides with what in graph theory is called a *walk*.

If $K$ is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) then the **quiver algebra** or **path algebra** $K\,\Gamma$ is defined as a vector space having all the paths (of length $\geq 0$) in the quiver as basis (including, for each vertex $i$ of the quiver $\Gamma$, a *trivial path* $e_i$ of length 0; these paths are *not* assumed to be equal for different $i$), and multiplication given by concatenation of paths. If two paths cannot be concatenated because the end vertex of the first is not equal to the starting vertex of the second, their product is defined to be zero. This defines an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) over $K$. This algebra has a unit element [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the quiver has only finitely many vertices. In this case, the [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over $K\,\Gamma$ are naturally identified with the representations of $\Gamma$. If the quiver has infinitely many vertices, then $K\,\Gamma$ has an [approximate identity](wikipedia://en/949628) given by $e_F:=\sum_{v\in F} 1_v$ where $F$ ranges over finite subsets of the vertex set of $\Gamma$.

If the quiver has finitely many vertices and arrows, and the end vertex and starting vertex of any path are always distinct (i.e. $Q$ has no oriented cycles), then $K\,\Gamma$ is a finite-[dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_of_a_vector_space) [hereditary algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditary_algebra) over $K$. Conversely, if $K$ is algebraically closed, then any finite-dimensional, hereditary, associative algebra over $K$ is [Morita equivalent](wikipedia://en/3296134) to the path algebra of its [Ext quiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ext_quiver) (i.e., they have equivalent module categories).

:::

## Representations of quivers {#Representations_of_quivers}

A representation of a quiver $\Gamma$ is an association of an $R$-module to each vertex of $\Gamma$, and a morphism between each module for each arrow.

A representation $V$ of a quiver $\Gamma$ is said to be *trivial* if $V(x)=0$ for all vertices $x$ in $\Gamma$.

A *morphism*, $f:V \to V',$ between representations of the quiver $\Gamma$, is a collection of linear maps $f(x):V(x) \to V'(x)$ such that for every arrow $a$ in $\Gamma$ from $x$ to $y$, $V'(a)f(x) = f(y)V(a),$ i.e. the squares that $f$ forms with the arrows of $V$ and $V'$ all commute. A morphism, $f$, is an *isomorphism*, if $f (x)$ is invertible for all vertices $x$ in the quiver. With these definitions the representations of a quiver form a [category](wikipedia://en/53759).

If $V$ and $W$ are representations of a quiver $\Gamma$, then the direct sum of these representations, $V\oplus W,$ is defined by $(V\oplus W)(x)=V(x)\oplus W(x)$ for all vertices $x$ in $\Gamma$ and $(V\oplus W)(a)$ is the direct sum of the linear mappings $V(a)$ and $W(a)$.

A representation is said to be *decomposable* if it is isomorphic to the direct sum of non-zero representations.

A [categorical](wikipedia://en/5869) definition of a quiver representation can also be given. The quiver itself can be considered a category, where the vertices are objects and paths are morphisms. Then a representation of $\Gamma$ is just a covariant [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from this category to the category of finite dimensional [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370). Morphisms of representations of $\Gamma$ are precisely [natural transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_transformations) between the corresponding functors.

For a finite quiver $\Gamma$ (a quiver with finitely many vertices and edges), let $K\,\Gamma$ be its path algebra. Let $e_{i}$ denote the trivial path at vertex $i$. Then we can associate to the vertex $i$ the [projective](wikipedia://en/364488) $K\,\Gamma$-module $K\,\Gamma e_{i}$ consisting of linear combinations of paths which have starting vertex $i$. This corresponds to the representation of $\Gamma$ obtained by putting a copy of $K$ at each vertex which lies on a path starting at $i$ and 0 on each other vertex. To each edge joining two copies of $K$ we associate the identity map.

This theory was related to [cluster algebras](wikipedia://en/27038423) by Derksen, Weyman, and Zelevinsky.[^o9]

:::

## Quiver with relations {#Quiver_with_relations}

To enforce commutativity of some squares inside a quiver a generalization is the notion of quivers with relations (also named bound quivers). A relation on a quiver $\Gamma$ is a $K$ linear combination of paths from $\Gamma$. A quiver with relation is a pair $(\Gamma, I)$ with $\Gamma$ a quiver and $I \subseteq K\Gamma$ an ideal of the path algebra. The quotient $K\,\Gamma / I$ is the path algebra of $(\Gamma, I)$.

### Quiver Variety {#Quiver_Variety}

Given the dimensions of the vector spaces assigned to every vertex, one can form a variety which characterizes all representations of that quiver with those specified dimensions, and consider stability conditions. These give quiver varieties, as constructed by (King 1994).

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Gabriel's theorem"}

## Gabriel's theorem {#Gabriel's_theorem}

A quiver is of **finite type** if it has only finitely many isomorphism classes of [indecomposable representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indecomposable_representation). (Gabriel 1972) classified all quivers of finite type, and also their indecomposable representations. More precisely, Gabriel's theorem states that:

1. A (connected) quiver is of finite type if and only if its underlying graph (when the directions of the arrows are ignored) is one of the [ADE](wikipedia://en/648042) [Dynkin diagrams](wikipedia://en/277109): $A_{n}, D_{n}, E_{6}, E_{7}, E_{8}$.
1. The indecomposable representations are in a one-to-one correspondence with the positive roots of the [root system](wikipedia://en/277087) of the Dynkin diagram.

(Dlab Ringel 1973) found a generalization of Gabriel's theorem in which all Dynkin diagrams of finite dimensional semisimple Lie algebras occur. This was generalized to all quivers and their corresponding [Kac–Moody algebras](wikipedia://en/1146292) by Victor Kac.

:::

## Reflexive quiver {#Reflexive_quiver}

*Image: Reflexive quiver.svg.*

Let a quiver $\Gamma$ have edge set $E,$ vertex set $V,$ and source and target functions $s,t:E\to V.$

A **reflexive quiver** is then a pair $(\Gamma, l: V \rightarrow E),$ where $\Gamma$ is as above, such that:

- $$s \circ l = t \circ l = \mathrm{id}_{V}.$$

So, for each vertex $x,$ the edge $l(x)$ is a loop from and to the vertex.

The loops of $\Gamma$ that form the set $\{l(x) \mid x \in V\}$ are called the **distinguished loops** of $\Gamma$.

As for quivers in general, reflexive quivers do not require edge composition to be defined. [Small categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_categories)[^o18] and [multiplicative graphs](wikipedia://en/83649526)[^o19] can both be seen as reflexive quivers with additional structure.
