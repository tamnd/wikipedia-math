# Homeomorphism (graph theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Homeomorphism"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), two [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ and $G'$ are **homeomorphic** if there is a [graph isomorphism](wikipedia://en/247577) from some subdivision of $G$ to some subdivision of $G'$. If the edges of a graph are thought of as lines drawn from one [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) to another (as they are usually depicted in diagrams), then two graphs are homeomorphic to each other in the graph-theoretic sense precisely if their diagrams are [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) in the [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) sense.[^o2]

:::

## Subdivision and smoothing {#Subdivision_and_smoothing}

In general, a **subdivision** of a graph *G* (sometimes known as an **expansion**[^o5]) is a graph resulting from the subdivision of edges in *G*. The subdivision of some edge *e* with endpoints {*u*, *v*} yields a graph containing one new vertex *w*, and with an edge set replacing *e* by two new edges, {*u*, *w*} and {*w*, *v*}. For directed edges, this operation shall preserve their propagating direction.

Determining whether for graphs *G* and *H*, *H* is homeomorphic to a subgraph of *G*, is an [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) problem.[^o6]

### Reversion {#Reversion}

The reverse operation, **smoothing out** or **smoothing** a vertex *w* with regards to the pair of edges (*e*<sub>1</sub>, *e*<sub>2</sub>) incident on *w*, removes both edges containing *w* and replaces (*e*<sub>1</sub>, *e*<sub>2</sub>) with a new edge that connects the other endpoints of the pair. Here, it is emphasized that only [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448)-2 (i.e., 2-valent) vertices can be smoothed. The limit of this operation is realized by the graph that has no more [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448)-2 vertices.

### Barycentric subdivisions {#Barycentric_subdivisions}

The [barycentric subdivision](wikipedia://en/583651) subdivides each edge of the graph. This is a special subdivision, as it always results in a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431). This procedure can be repeated, so that the *n*<sup>th</sup> barycentric subdivision is the barycentric subdivision of the *n*−1st barycentric subdivision of the graph. The second such subdivision is always a [simple graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph).

## Embedding on a surface {#Embedding_on_a_surface}

It is evident that subdividing a graph preserves [planarity](wikipedia://en/24314). [Kuratowski's theorem](wikipedia://en/54493) states that

a [finite graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_graph) is planar [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it contains no [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraph) **homeomorphic** to *K*<sub>5</sub> ([complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) on five vertices) or *K*<sub>3,3</sub> ([complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) on six vertices, three of which connect to each of the other three).

In fact, a graph homeomorphic to *K*<sub>5</sub> or *K*<sub>3,3</sub> is called a [Kuratowski subgraph](wikipedia://en/54493).

A generalization, following from the [Robertson–Seymour theorem](wikipedia://en/351769), asserts that for each [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *g*, there is a finite **obstruction set** of graphs $L(g) = \left\{G_{i}^{(g)}\right\}$ such that a graph *H* is [embeddable](wikipedia://en/8149170) on a surface of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) *g* if and only if *H* contains no homeomorphic copy of any of the $G_{i}^{(g)\!}$. For example, $L(0) = \left\{K_{5}, K_{3,3}\right\}$ consists of the Kuratowski subgraphs.

[^o6]: The more commonly studied problem in the literature, under the name of the subgraph homeomorphism problem, is whether a subdivision of *H* is isomorphic to a subgraph of *G*. The case when *H* is an *n*-vertex cycle is equivalent to the [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) problem, and is therefore NP-complete. However, this formulation is only equivalent to the question of whether *H* is homeomorphic to a subgraph of *G* when *H* has no degree-two vertices, because it does not allow smoothing in *H*. The stated problem can be shown to be NP-complete by a small modification of the Hamiltonian cycle reduction: add one vertex to each of *H* and *G*, adjacent to all the other vertices. Thus, the one-vertex augmentation of a graph *G* contains a subgraph homeomorphic to an (*n* + 1)-vertex [wheel graph](wikipedia://en/1655876), if and only if *G* is Hamiltonian. For the hardness of the subgraph homeomorphism problem, see e.g. LaPaugh, Andrea S.; Rivest, Ronald L. (1980). "The subgraph homeomorphism problem". *Journal of Computer and System Sciences*. 20 (2). p. 133–149. doi:10.1016/0022-0000(80)90057-4. mr:574589.
