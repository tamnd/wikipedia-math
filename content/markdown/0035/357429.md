# Subdivisions of Scotland

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Subdivisions of Scotland"}

For [local government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_Scotland) purposes, [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland) is divided into 32 areas designated as "council areas" (comhairlean), which are all governed by [single-tier authorities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_authority) designated as "councils".[^o3] They have the option under the Local Government (Gaelic Names) (Scotland) Act 1997[^o4] of being known (but not re-designated) as a "*comhairle*" when opting for a Gaelic name; only *[Comhairle nan Eilean Siar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comhairle_nan_Eilean_Siar)* (Council of the Western Isles) has chosen this option, whereas the [Highland Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highland_Council) (*Comhairle na Gàidhealtachd*) has adopted its Gaelic form alongside its English equivalent, informally.

:::

The council areas have been in existence since 1 April 1996, under the provisions of the [Local Government etc. (Scotland) Act 1994](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_etc._%28Scotland%29_Act_1994). Historically, Scotland was divided into 34 [counties or shires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shires_of_Scotland). Although these no longer have any administrative function, they are still used to some extent in Scotland for cultural and geographical purposes, and some of the current council areas are named after them. There are also a number of other administrative divisions, some of which are handled by joint boards of the councils.

At the most local level, Scotland is divided into [civil parishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_civil_parishes_in_Scotland), which are now used only for statistical purposes such as the census. The lowest level of administrative subdivision are the [communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_council), which may elect *community councils*.

## History of the subdivisions of Scotland {#History_of_the_subdivisions_of_Scotland}

Traditionally burghs have been the key unit of the [local government of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_government_in_Scotland), being highly [autonomous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Autonomous_entity) entities, with rights to representation in the old [Parliament of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parliament_of_Scotland). Even after the [Acts of Union 1707](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_Union_1707), burghs continued to be the principal subdivision. Until 1889, administration was on a [burgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burgh) and [parish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parish) basis.

The years following 1889 saw the introduction of a hierarchy of local government administration comprising [counties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counties_of_Scotland), [counties of cities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_Government_%28Scotland%29_Act_1889), [large burghs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_burghs) and small burghs.

With effect from 16 May 1975 and until 31 March 1996 the local government divisions of Scotland consisted of an upper tier of *[regions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_and_districts_of_Scotland)* each containing a lower tier of *[districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regions_and_districts_of_Scotland)* except for the single-tier *[island council areas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Island_council_areas_of_Scotland)*. Since 1996 there has only been a single tier of government, and the former island council areas are of equal status to the other councils.

### Lieutenancy areas {#Lieutenancy_areas}

The [Lieutenancy areas of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lieutenancy_areas_of_Scotland) are the areas used for the ceremonial [lord-lieutenants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Lieutenant), the [monarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_monarch)'s representatives. The areas are similar to the Historic Counties and the Registration Counties, but are not identical to either. Most notably, the four cities of [Aberdeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen), [Dundee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dundee), [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh), and [Glasgow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glasgow) form separate areas from the surrounding countryside, with the [Lord Provost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Provost) of each city acting *[ex officio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ex_officio)* as the lord-lieutenant.

### Former police and fire services {#Former_police_and_fire_services}

The Police and Fire Reform (Scotland) Act 2012 resulted in the merger of local [police](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police) and [fire services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_department) on 1 April 2013 to form the [Police Service of Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Police_Scotland) ([Scottish Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic): Seirbheis Phoilis na h-Alba) and the [Scottish Fire and Rescue Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Fire_and_Rescue_Service) (SFRS, Scottish Gaelic: Seirbheis Smàlaidh agus Teasairginn na h-Alba).

## Lower level subdivisions {#Lower_level_subdivisions}

Scotland is divided into [871 civil parishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_civil_parishes_in_Scotland) which often resemble same-named but legally different ecclesiastical parishes. Although they have had no administrative function since 1930, they still exist and are still used for statistical purposes such as the census. Many former civil parish areas also continued to form [registration districts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Registration_district) until 1 January 2007. Many boundary changes have occurred over the years and an area currently derived from an old parish might no longer contain a place previously within that parish. Similarly, county boundaries (as still used for land registration) have also changed over the years such that a parish mentioned historically (generally before the 1860s) as being in one county (or sometimes two due to straddling a border) might now be in a neighbouring county and consequentially in a different succeeding council area.

For most administrative purposes, the base level of sub-division in Scotland is now that of [communities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Community_council), which may elect *community councils*. The main role of these bodies is to channel and reflect local opinion to other bodies; they otherwise have very limited powers. There are around 1,200 communities in Scotland. Not all communities have councils; some have joint councils.

Scottish communities are the nearest equivalent to [civil parishes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_parishes_in_England) in England.
