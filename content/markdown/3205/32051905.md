# Silk Road (marketplace)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Silk Road"}

**Silk Road** was an online [black market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_market) and the first modern [darknet market](wikipedia://en/46716831). It was launched in 2011 by its American founder [Ross Ulbricht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Ulbricht) under the pseudonym "[Dread Pirate Roberts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dread_Pirate_Roberts)". As part of the [dark web](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dark_web),[^o8] Silk Road operated as a [hidden service](wikipedia://en/3648703) on the [Tor network](wikipedia://en/20556944), allowing users to buy and sell products and services between each other anonymously. All transactions were conducted with [bitcoin](wikipedia://en/28249265), a [cryptocurrency](wikipedia://en/36662188) which aided in protecting user identities. The website was known for its illegal drug marketplace, among other illegal and legal product listings. Between February 2011 and July 2013, the site facilitated sales amounting to 9,519,664 bitcoins.[^o105]

:::

In October 2013, the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation) (FBI) shut down the Silk Road website and arrested Ulbricht.[^o105][^o9] Silk Road 2.0 came online the next month, run by other administrators of the former site,[^o10] but was shut down the following year as part of [Operation Onymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Onymous). In 2015, Ulbricht was convicted in federal court for multiple charges related to operating Silk Road and was given two [life sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_imprisonment) without possibility of [parole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parole).[^o5][^o11][^o12] He was pardoned by [President](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/President_of_the_United_States) [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump) in 2025.[^o13]

## History {#History}

### Operations {#Operations}

The website was launched in February 2011;[^o16] development had begun six months prior.[^o17][^o18] The name "Silk Road" comes from a [historical network of trade routes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silk_Road) started during the [Han Dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Han_Dynasty) (206 [BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Era) – 220 CE) between Europe, India, China, and many other countries on the Afro-Eurasian landmass. Silk Road was operated by the [pseudonymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudonymous) "Dread Pirate Roberts" (named after [the fictional character](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dread_Pirate_Roberts) from *[The Princess Bride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Princess_Bride_%28novel%29)*), who was known for espousing [libertarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarianism) ideals and criticizing [regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation).[^o9][^o19] Two other individuals were also closely involved in the site's growth and success, known as [Variety Jones and Smedley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variety_Jones_and_Smedley).[^o20]

In June 2011, [Gawker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gawker) published an article about the site[^o85] which led to an increase in notoriety and website traffic.[^o17] U.S. Senator [Chuck Schumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chuck_Schumer) asked federal law enforcement authorities to shut it down, including the [Drug Enforcement Administration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_Enforcement_Administration) (DEA) and [Department of Justice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Department_of_Justice_%28United_States%29).[^o21]

In May 2013, Silk Road was taken down for a short period of time by a sustained [DDoS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DDoS) attack.[^o22] On 23 June 2013, it was first reported that the DEA seized 11.02 [bitcoins](wikipedia://en/28249265), then worth a total of \$814, which the media suspected was a result of a Silk Road [honeypot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeypot_%28computing%29) sting.[^o23][^o24] The FBI has claimed that the real IP address of the Silk Road server was found via data leaked directly from the site's [CAPTCHA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAPTCHA) and it was located in [Reykjavík, Iceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reykjavík). IT security experts have doubted the FBI's claims because technical evidence suggests that no misconfiguration that could cause the specific leak was present at the time.[^o25][^o26]

[Henry Farrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Farrell_%28political_scientist%29), an associate professor of political science and international affairs at George Washington University, analyzed Silk Road in an essay for [Aeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aeon_%28digital_magazine%29) in 2015.[^o27] He noted that Ulbricht created the marketplace to function without government oversight but found it difficult to verify anonymous transactions.[^o27] To sustain a steady stream of revenue, he started increasing oversight to ensure low [transaction costs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transaction_cost).[^o27] To do this, he added measures to ensure trustworthiness with implementation of an automated [escrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escrow) payment system and automated review system.[^o27]

### Arrest and trial of Ross Ulbricht {#Arrest_and_trial_of_Ross_Ulbricht}

*Image: Silk Road Seized.jpg.* Image placed on original Silk Road after seizure of property by the [FBI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation)

*Image: Bitcoin October 2013.png.* Impact of the seizure on the USD/Bitcoin exchange rate

Due, in part, to off-duty research conducted by [IRS Criminal Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IRS_Criminal_Investigation) Special Agent Gary Alford,[^o30] Ross Ulbricht was alleged by the FBI to be the founder and owner of Silk Road and the person behind the pseudonym "Dread Pirate Roberts" (DPR). Alford searched for any mentions of the .onion URL or .tor address, stating that someone might have advertised or suggested the marketplace on Google. The first mention of the website was by a user named "altoid". Further searching into this altoid profile led him to a post about an open position that told interested applicants to contact what was Ross Ulbricht's personal email.[^o31] He was arrested on 1 October 2013 in San Francisco[^o105][^o32][^o33][^o34][^o35] in [Glen Park](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glen_Park,_San_Francisco) Library, a branch of the [San Francisco Public Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Public_Library).[^o35] During the arrest, the FBI seized Ulbricht's laptop which he was using to connect to the servers and manage the marketplace.[^o36] Ulbricht was indicted on charges of engaging in a [continuing criminal enterprise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuing_Criminal_Enterprise_Statute), distributing narcotics, distributing narcotics by means of the Internet, and four conspiracy charges related to distribution of narcotics, computer hacking, money laundering, and false identity documents.[^o35][^o37][^o38][^o39] He was separately indicted for a single murder-for-hire charge.[^o40] Prosecutors alleged that Ulbricht paid \$730,000 to others to commit the murders, although none of the murders actually occurred.[^o41][^o40] Ulbricht ultimately was not prosecuted for any of the alleged murder attempts.[^o42]

The FBI initially seized 26,000 bitcoins from accounts on Silk Road, worth approximately \$3.6 million at the time. An FBI spokesperson said that the agency would hold the bitcoins until Ulbricht's trial finished, after which the bitcoins would be liquidated.[^o43] In October 2013, the FBI reported that it had seized 144,000 bitcoins, worth \$28.5 million, and that the bitcoins belonged to Ulbricht.[^o44] On 27 June 2014, the [U.S. Marshals Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Marshals_Service) sold 29,657 bitcoins in 10 blocks in an online auction, estimated to be worth \$18 million at contemporary rates and only about a quarter of the seized bitcoins. Another 144,342 bitcoins were kept which had been found on Ulbricht's computer, roughly \$87 million.[^o45] [Tim Draper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tim_Draper) bought the bitcoins at the auction with an estimated worth of \$17 million, to lend them to a bitcoin start-up called Vaurum which is working in developing economies of emerging markets.[^o46]

Ulbricht's trial began on 13 January 2015 in [federal court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_courts) in [Manhattan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan).[^o47] At the start of the trial, Ulbricht admitted to founding the Silk Road website, but claimed to have transferred control of the site to other people soon after he founded it.[^o48] Ulbricht's lawyers contended that Dread Pirate Roberts was really [Mark Karpelès](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Karpelès), and that Karpelès set up Ulbricht as a fall guy.[^o49] However, Judge [Katherine B. Forrest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katherine_B._Forrest) ruled that any speculative statements regarding whether Karpelès or anyone else ran Silk Road would not be allowed, and statements already made would be stricken from the record.[^o50]

In the second week of the trial, prosecutors presented documents and chat logs from Ulbricht's computer that, they said, demonstrated how Ulbricht had administered the site for many months, which contradicted the defense's claim that Ulbricht had relinquished control of Silk Road. Ulbricht's attorney suggested that the documents and chat logs were planted there by way of [BitTorrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BitTorrent), which was running on Ulbricht's computer at the time of his arrest.[^o50]

On 4 February 2015, the [jury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juries_in_the_United_States#Federal_jury_trial_rights) convicted Ulbricht of seven charges,[^o12] including charges of engaging in a continuing criminal enterprise, narcotics trafficking, money laundering, and computer hacking.[^o5][^o6] The continuing criminal enterprise charge has a minimum sentence of 20 years. The amount of narcotics distributed also triggered an additional 10-year minimum.[^o51] The government also accused Ulbricht of paying for the murders of at least five people, but there is no evidence that the murders were actually carried out, and the accusations never became formal charges against Ulbricht.[^o52][^o53]

During the trial, Judge Forrest received death threats. Users of an underground site called [The Hidden Wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Hidden_Wiki) posted her personal information there, including her address and Social Security number. Ulbricht's lawyer Joshua Dratel said that he and his client "obviously, and as strongly as possible, condemn" the anonymous postings against the judge. "They do not in any way have anything to do with Ross Ulbricht or anyone associated with him or reflect his views or those of anyone associated with him," Dratel said.[^o54]

In late March 2015, a criminal complaint issued by the [United States District Court for the Northern District of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_District_Court_for_the_Northern_District_of_California) led to the arrest of two former federal agents who had worked undercover in the [Baltimore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baltimore) Silk Road investigation of Ulbricht, former Drug Enforcement Administration agent Carl Mark Force IV and Secret Service agent Shaun Bridges.[^o55][^o56] The agents are alleged to have kept funds that Ulbricht transferred to them in exchange for purported information about the investigation.[^o55][^o57] The agents were charged with [wire fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mail_and_wire_fraud) and [money laundering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money_laundering);[^o58] both pled guilty.[^o59] In late November 2016, Ulbricht's lawyers brought forward a case on a third DEA agent, who they claim was leaking information about the investigation and tampered with evidence to omit chat logs showing conversations with him.[^o60]

In a letter to Judge Forrest before his sentencing, Ulbricht stated that his actions through Silk Road were committed through [libertarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libertarian) idealism and that "Silk Road was supposed to be about giving people the freedom to make their own choices" and admitted that he made a "terrible mistake" that "ruined his life."[^o61][^o62] On 29 May 2015, Ulbricht was given five [sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28law%29) to be served concurrently, including two of [life imprisonment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_imprisonment) without the possibility of [parole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parole).[^o63] He was also ordered to [forfeit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asset_forfeiture) \$183 million. Ulbricht's lawyer Joshua Dratel said that he would [appeal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellate_procedure_in_the_United_States) the sentencing and the original guilty verdict.[^o52] On 31 May 2017, the United States Court of Appeals for the Second Circuit denied Ulbricht's appeal, and affirmed the judgment of conviction and life sentence, in a written opinion authored by [Gerard E. Lynch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerard_E._Lynch), United States circuit judge.[^o64] The [Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supreme_Court_of_the_United_States) declined to review the case.[^o65] On January 21, 2025, Ulbricht received a full pardon by President [Donald Trump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_Trump), after 11 years and 3 months of incarceration.[^o13]

:::

### Other trials {#Other_trials}

In February 2013, an Australian [cocaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocaine) and [MDMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MDMA) ("ecstasy") dealer became the first person to be convicted of crimes directly related to Silk Road, after authorities intercepted drugs that he was importing through the mail, searched his premises, and discovered his Silk Road alias in an image file on his personal computer.[^o67] Australian police and the DEA have targeted Silk Road users and made arrests, albeit with limited success at reaching convictions.[^o85][^o68][^o69] In December 2013, a New Zealand man was sentenced to two years and four months in jail after being convicted of importing 15 grams of [methamphetamine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methamphetamine) that he had bought on Silk Road.[^o70]

23-year-old Dutch drug dealer Cornelis Jan Slomp[^o71] pled guilty to large-scale selling of drugs through the Silk Road website, and was sentenced in Chicago to 10 years in prison on 29 May 2015 with his attorney, Paul Petruzzi, present.[^o72][^o73] Dealer Steven Sadler was sentenced to five years in prison. There have been over 130 other arrests connected with Silk Road, although some of these arrests may not be directly related to Silk Road, and may not be public information for legal reasons.[^o74][^o75][^o76]

### Later seizures {#Later_seizures}

On 3 November 2020, after years of inactivity, observers of the bitcoin blockchain detected that two transactions totaling 69,370 [bitcoin](wikipedia://en/28249265) and [bitcoin cash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitcoin_cash),[^o79] worth approximately \$1 billion in total at the time of transfer, had been made from `1HQ3Go3ggs8pFnXuHVHRytPCq5fGG8Hbhx`, a bitcoin address associated with the Silk Road.[^o80][^o83] At the time of transfer, it was worth 58 times its value in 2015.[^o81] It was subsequently revealed that the transfer had been made by the [United States government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_government) in a [civil forfeiture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_forfeiture) action.[^o82] According to a press release by the [U.S. Attorney's Office of the Northern District of California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._Attorney's_Office_of_the_Northern_District_of_California), the bitcoin wallet belonged to an "Individual X" who had originally acquired the bitcoins by hacking the Silk Road.[^o83]

:::

## Products {#Products}

In March 2013, the site had 10,000 products for sale by vendors, 70% of which were drugs.[^o85][^o90] Drugs were grouped under the headings [stimulants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stimulants), [psychedelics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychedelic_drug), [prescription](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prescription_drug), [precursors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precursor_%28chemistry%29), other, [opioids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opioid), [ecstasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MDMA), [dissociatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissociative), and [steroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steroid)/[PEDs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Performance-enhancing_drugs).[^o17][^o16][^o86][^o87] Fake driver's licenses were also offered for sale.[^o88] The site's terms of service prohibited the sale of certain items. When the Silk Road marketplace first began, the creator and administrators instituted terms of service that prohibited the sale of anything whose purpose was to "harm or defraud".[^o16][^o89] This included [child pornography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_pornography), stolen credit cards, assassinations, and weapons of any type; other darknet markets such as [Black Market Reloaded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_Market_Reloaded) gained user notoriety because they were not as restrictive on these items as the Silk Road incarnations were.[^o90][^o91] There were also legal goods and services for sale, such as [apparel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clothing), [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art), [books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book), [cigarettes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cigarette), [erotica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotica), [jewellery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewellery), and writing services. A sister site, called "The Armoury", sold weapons (primarily firearms) during 2012, but was shut down, due to a lack of demand.[^o92]

The Silk Road offered over 24,400 products related to drugs for sale and an infrastructure that made these transactions. The official sellers guide stated the prohibition of any sale of goods that were meant for harm or fraud, but allowed for prescription drugs, pornography, and counterfeit documents. Only users of [Tor](wikipedia://en/20556944) could access the Silk Road.[^o93]

Buyers were able to leave reviews of sellers' products on the site and in an associated forum, where [crowdsourcing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crowdsourcing) provided information about the best sellers and worst scammers.[^o94] Most products were delivered through the mail, with the site's seller's guide instructing sellers how to vacuum-seal their products to escape detection.[^o95]

## Sales {#Sales}

*Image: Silk road payment.jpg.* A [flowchart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flowchart) depicting Silk Road's payment system. *Exhibit 113 A*, entered into evidence at Ulbricht's trial

Silk Road provided goods and services to over 100,000 buyers.[^o97] Over the $2\tfrac{1}{2}$ years in which the website was in operation, it generated \$183 million in sales and \$13 million in commissions, based on the value of bitcoin at the time of transactions.[^o98] Due to the significant rise in bitcoin value over that period, the revenue and commission are also sometimes reported as \$1.2 billion and \$80 million, respectively.[^o99][^o100][^o105]

Initially there were a limited number of new seller accounts available; new sellers had to purchase an account in an auction. Later, a fixed fee was charged for each new seller account.[^o101][^o102] Buyers and sellers conducted all transactions with [bitcoins](wikipedia://en/28249265) (BTC), a [cryptocurrency](wikipedia://en/36662188) that provides a certain degree of [anonymity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anonymity). Silk Road held buyers' bitcoins in [escrow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escrow) until the order had been received and a [hedging mechanism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedge_%28finance%29) allowed sellers to opt for the value of bitcoins held in escrow to be fixed to their value in US\$ at the time of the sale to mitigate against Bitcoin's volatility. Any changes in the price of bitcoins during transit were covered by Dread Pirate Roberts.[^o103]

The [complaint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_complaint) published when Ulbricht was arrested included information the FBI gained from a [system image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System_image) of the Silk Road server collected on 23 July 2013. It noted that, "From February 6, 2011 to July 23, 2013 there were approximately 1,229,465 transactions completed on the site. The total revenue generated from these sales was 9,519,664 Bitcoins, and the total commissions collected by Silk Road from the sales amounted to 614,305 Bitcoins. According to the government, total sales were equivalent to roughly \$1.2 billion and involved 146,946 buyers and 3,877 vendors.[^o104][^o105] According to information users provided upon registering, 30 percent were from the United States, 27 percent chose to be "undeclared", and beyond that, in descending order of prevalence: the United Kingdom, Australia, Germany, Canada, Sweden, France, Russia, Italy, and the Netherlands. During the 60-day period from 24 May to 23 July, there were 1,217,218 messages sent over Silk Road's private messaging system.[^o105]

## Similar sites {#Similar_sites}

[The Farmer's Market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Farmer's_Market) was a Tor site similar to Silk Road, but which did not use bitcoins.[^o107] It has been considered a 'proto-Silk Road' but the use of payment services such as PayPal and Western Union allowed law enforcement to trace payments and it was subsequently shut down by the FBI in 2012.[^o94][^o108][^o109] Other sites already existed when Silk Road was shut down and *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* predicted that these would take over the market that Silk Road previously dominated.[^o110][^o111] [Atlantis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantis_%28market%29) was founded in March 2013 and closed six months later, while *Project Black Flag* closed in October 2013; both websites stole their users' bitcoins.[^o10] In October 2013, *Black Market Reloaded* closed temporarily after its source code was leaked.[^o10] The market shares of various Silk Road successor sites were described by *The Economist* in May 2015.[^o112]

## Book club {#Book_club}

Silk Road had a Tor-based [book club](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_discussion_club) that continued to operate following the initial site's closure and even following the arrest of one of its members. Reading material included [conspiracy theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conspiracy_theories) and computer [hacking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hacker). Some of the titles included mainstream books as well as books such as *[The Anarchist Cookbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Anarchist_Cookbook)* and *Defeating Electromagnetic Door Locks*. Most of the titles on this book club were pirated. This book club still exists as a private Tor-based chatroom.[^o114][^o115]

## Direct successors {#Direct_successors}

::: {#o118 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Silk Road 2"}

### Silk Road 2.0 {#Silk_Road_2.0}

*Image: Silk Road Seized 2014.png.* Alert placed on the Silk Road's homepage following its seizure by the U.S. government and European law enforcement

On 6 November 2013, administrators from the closed Silk Road relaunched the site, led by a new pseudonymous Dread Pirate Roberts, and dubbed it "Silk Road 2.0." It recreated the original site's setup and promised improved security.[^o10] The new DPR took the precaution of distributing encrypted copies of the site's source code to allow the site to be quickly recreated in the event of another shutdown.[^o119]

On 20 December 2013, it was announced that three alleged Silk Road 2.0 administrators had been arrested;[^o120] two of these suspects, Andrew Michael Jones and Gary Davis, were named as the administrators "Inigo" and "Libertas" who had continued their work on Silk Road 2.0.[^o121] Around this time, the new Dread Pirate Roberts abruptly surrendered control of the site and froze its activity, including its escrow system. A new temporary administrator under the screenname "Defcon" took over and promised to bring the site back to working order.[^o122]

On 13 February 2014, Defcon announced that Silk Road 2.0's escrow accounts had been compromised through a vulnerability in [Bitcoin protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bitcoin_protocol) called "[transaction malleability](wikipedia://en/87027)".[^o123] While the site remained online, all the bitcoins in its escrow accounts, valued at \$2.7 million, were reported stolen.[^o123] It was later reported that the vulnerability was in the site's "Refresh Deposits" function, and that the Silk Road administrators had used their commissions on sales since 15 February to refund users who lost money, with 50 percent of the hack victims being completely repaid as of 8 April.[^o124]

On 6 November 2014, authorities with the [Federal Bureau of Investigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federal_Bureau_of_Investigation), [Europol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europol), and [Eurojust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eurojust) announced the arrest of [Blake Benthall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blake_Benthall), allegedly the owner and operator of Silk Road 2.0 under the pseudonym "Defcon", the previous day in San Francisco as part of [Operation Onymous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Onymous).[^o7][^o125] The creator of the relaunched website—an English computer programmer named [Thomas White](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cthulhu_%28developer%29)—was also arrested in the course of the shutdown, but his arrest was not made public until 2019 after he pled guilty to charges stemming from running the website and was sentenced to five years in prison. Among the charges White admitted to was creating child pornography, and chat logs recovered by police showed White discussing the possibility of launching a website to host such material.[^o126][^o127]

### Others {#Others}

Following the closure of Silk Road 2.0 in November 2014, Diabolus Market renamed itself to 'Silk Road 3 Reloaded' in order to capitalize on the brand.[^o130] In January 2015, Silk Road Reloaded launched on [I2P](wikipedia://en/538434) with multiple [cryptocurrency](wikipedia://en/36662188) support and similar listing restrictions to the original Silk Road market.[^o131] As of 2018, this website was also defunct.[^o132]

:::

## Advocates of dark web drug sales & Ulbricht {#Advocates_of_dark_web_drug_sales_&_Ulbricht}

Meghan Ralston, a former harm reduction manager for the [Drug Policy Alliance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drug_Policy_Alliance), was quoted as saying that the Silk Road was "a peaceable alternative to the often deadly violence so commonly associated with the global drug war, and street drug transactions in particular." Proponents of the Silk Road and similar sites argue that buying illegal narcotics from the safety of your home is better than buying them in person from criminals on the streets.[^o134][^o135]
