# Additive polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Additive polynomial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **additive polynomials** are an important topic in classical [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $k$ be a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) $k$. A [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $P(x)$ with [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) in $k$ is called an **additive polynomial**, or a **[Frobenius](wikipedia://en/1088270) polynomial**, if

$$P(a+b)=P(a)+P(b)$$

as polynomials in $a$ and $b$. It is equivalent to assume that this equality holds for all $a$ and $b$ in some infinite field containing $k$, such as its [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129).

Occasionally *absolutely additive* is used for the condition above, and *additive* is used for the weaker condition that $P(a+b)=P(a)+P(b)$ for all $a$ and $b$ in the field.[^o4] For infinite fields the conditions are equivalent,[^n1] but for [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) they are not, and the weaker condition is the "wrong" as it does not behave well. For example, over a field of order $q$ any multiple $P$ of $x^q-x$ will satisfy $P(a+b)=P(a)+P(b)$ for all $a$ and $b$ in the field, but will usually not be (absolutely) additive.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The polynomial $x^p$ is additive.[^o4] Indeed, for any $a$ and $b$ in the algebraic closure of $k$ one has by the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677)

$$(a+b)^p = \sum_{n=0}^p {p \choose n} a^n b^{p-n}.$$

Since $p$ is prime, for all $n=1,\dots,p-1$ the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668) $\tbinom{p}{n}$ is [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisible) by $p$, which implies that

$$(a+b)^p \equiv a^p+b^p \mod p$$

as polynomials in $a$ and $b$.[^o4]

Similarly all the polynomials of the form

$$\tau_p^n(x) = x^{p^n}$$

are additive, where $n$ is a non-negative [integer](wikipedia://en/14563).[^o4]

The definition makes sense even if $k$ is a field of characteristic zero, but in this case the only additive polynomials are those of the form $ax$ for some $a$ in $k$.

:::

## The ring of additive polynomials {#The_ring_of_additive_polynomials}

It is quite easy to prove that any [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of polynomials $\tau_p^n(x)$ with coefficients in $k$ is also an additive polynomial.[^o4] An interesting question is whether there are other additive polynomials except these linear combinations. The answer is that these are the only ones.[^o9]

One can check that if $P(x)$ and $M(x)$ are additive polynomials, then so are $P(x)+M(x)$ and $P(M(x))$. These imply that the additive polynomials form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) under polynomial addition and [composition](wikipedia://en/195947). This ring is denoted[^o10]

$$k\{ \tau_p\}.$$

This ring is not [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) unless $k$ is the field $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$ (see [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087)).[^o4] Indeed, consider the additive polynomials $ax$ and $x^p$ for a coefficient $a$ in $k$. For them to commute under composition, we must have

$$(ax)^p = ax^p,\,$$

and hence $a^p-a=0$. This is false for $a$ not a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of this equation, that is, for $a$ outside $\mathbb{F}_p.$[^o4]

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="The fundamental theorem of additive polynomials"}

## The fundamental theorem of additive polynomials {#The_fundamental_theorem_of_additive_polynomials}

Let $P(x)$ be a polynomial with coefficients in $k$, and $\{w_1,\dots,w_m\}\subset k$ be the set of its roots. Assuming that the roots of $P(x)$ are distinct (that is, $P(x)$ is [separable](wikipedia://en/362400)), then $P(x)$ is additive [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the set $\{w_1,\dots,w_m\}$ forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) with the field addition.[^n2]

:::
