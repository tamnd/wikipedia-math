# Coleman–Mandula theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coleman–Mandula theorem"}

In [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), the **Coleman–Mandula theorem** is a [no-go theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No-go_theorem) stating that [spacetime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spacetime_symmetries) and internal [symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29) can only combine in a trivial way. This means that the charges associated with internal symmetries must always transform as [Lorentz scalars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_scalar). Some notable exceptions to the no-go theorem are [conformal symmetry](wikipedia://en/671875) and [supersymmetry](wikipedia://en/224636). It is named after [Sidney Coleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidney_Coleman) and [Jeffrey Mandula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Mandula) who proved it in 1967 as the culmination of a series of increasingly generalized no-go theorems investigating how internal symmetries can be combined with spacetime symmetries.[^o2] The supersymmetric generalization is known as the [Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius theorem](wikipedia://en/4402244).

:::

## History {#History}

In the early 1960s, the [global](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_%28physics%29#Local_and_global) $\text{SU}(3)$ flavor symmetry associated with the [eightfold way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eightfold_way_%28physics%29) was shown to successfully describe the [hadron spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadron_spectroscopy) for [hadrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadron) of the same [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829). This led to efforts to expand the global $\text{SU}(3)$ symmetry to a larger $\text{SU}(6)$ symmetry mixing both [flavour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flavour_%28particle_physics%29) and spin, an idea similar to that previously considered in [nuclear physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_physics) by [Eugene Wigner](wikipedia://en/10101) in 1937 for an $\text{SU}(4)$ symmetry.[^o4] This non-relativistic $\text{SU}(6)$ model united [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_meson) and [pseudoscalar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoscalar_meson) [mesons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meson) of different spin into a 35-dimensional [multiplet](wikipedia://en/12416124) and it also united the two [baryon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baryon) decuplets into a 56-dimensional multiplet.[^o5] While this was reasonably successful in describing various aspects of the hadron spectrum, from the perspective of [quantum chromodynamics](wikipedia://en/25264) this success is merely a consequence of the flavour and spin independence of the force between [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark). There were many attempts to generalize this non-relativistic $\text{SU}(6)$ model into a fully [relativistic](wikipedia://en/30001) one, but these all failed.

At the time it was also an open question whether there existed a symmetry for which particles of different [masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) could belong to the same multiplet. Such a symmetry could then account for the mass splitting found in mesons and baryons.[^o6] It was only later understood that this is instead a consequence of the differing up-, down-, and strange-quark masses which leads to a breakdown of the $\text{SU}(3)$ internal flavor symmetry.

These two motivations led to a series of no-go theorems to show that spacetime symmetries and internal symmetries could not be combined in any but a trivial way.[^o7] The first notable theorem was proved by William McGlinn in 1964,[^o8] with a subsequent generalization by [Lochlainn O'Raifeartaigh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lochlainn_O'Raifeartaigh) in 1965.[^o9] These efforts culminated with the most general theorem by Sidney Coleman and Jeffrey Mandula in 1967.

Little notice was given to this theorem in subsequent years. As a result, the theorem played no role in the early development of supersymmetry, which instead emerged in the early 1970s from the study of [dual resonance models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_resonance_model), which are the precursor to [string theory](wikipedia://en/28305), rather than from any attempts to overcome the no-go theorem.[^o10] Similarly, the Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius theorem, a supersymmetric generalization of the Coleman–Mandula theorem, was proved in 1975 after the study of supersymmetry was already underway.[^o11]

::: {#o13 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

Consider a theory that can be described by an [S-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053) and that satisfies the following conditions[^o2]

- The symmetry [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) which includes the [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) as a subgroup,
- Below any mass, there are only a finite number of particle types,
- Any two-particle state undergoes some reaction at almost all [energies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy),
- The [amplitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_amplitude) for [elastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_scattering) two-body scattering are [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) of the scattering angle at almost all energies and angles,
- A technical assumption that the group [generators](wikipedia://en/3171371) are [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) in [momentum space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_and_momentum_space).

The Coleman–Mandula theorem states that the symmetry group of this theory is necessarily a [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of the Poincaré group and an internal symmetry group.[^o14] The last technical assumption is unnecessary if the theory is described by a [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) and is only needed to apply the theorem in a wider context.

A [kinematic](wikipedia://en/65914) argument for why the theorem should hold was provided by [Edward Witten](wikipedia://en/54894).[^o15] The argument is that Poincaré symmetry acts as a very strong constraint on elastic scattering, leaving only the scattering angle unknown. Any additional spacetime-dependent symmetry would [overdetermine](wikipedia://en/10160606) the amplitudes, making them nonzero only at discrete scattering angles. Since this conflicts with the assumption of the analyticity of the scattering angles, such additional spacetime-dependent symmetries are ruled out.

:::

## Limitations {#Limitations}

### Conformal symmetry {#Conformal_symmetry}

The theorem does not apply to a theory of [massless particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massless_particle), with these allowing for conformal symmetry as an additional spacetime-dependent symmetry.[^o14] In particular, the [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) of this group is the [conformal algebra](wikipedia://en/671875#commutation_relations), which consists of the Poincaré algebra together with the commutation relations for the [dilaton](wikipedia://en/386138) generator and the [special conformal transformations](wikipedia://en/24675202) generator.

### Supersymmetry {#Supersymmetry}

The Coleman–Mandula theorem assumes that the only symmetry algebras are [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944), but the theorem can be generalized by instead considering [Lie superalgebras](wikipedia://en/297501). Doing this allows for additional [anticommutating](wikipedia://en/7193#ring_theory) generators known as [supercharges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercharge) which transform as [spinors](wikipedia://en/29276) under [Lorentz transformations](wikipedia://en/18404). This extension gives rise to the [super-Poincaré algebra](wikipedia://en/1057955), with the associated symmetry known as supersymmetry. The Haag–Łopuszański–Sohnius theorem is the generalization of the Coleman–Mandula theorem to Lie superalgebras, with it stating that supersymmetry is the only new spacetime-dependent symmetry that is allowed. For a theory with massless particles, the theorem is again evaded by conformal symmetry which can be present in addition to supersymmetry giving a [superconformal algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconformal_algebra).

### Low dimensions {#Low_dimensions}

In a one or two dimensional theory the only possible scattering is forwards and backwards scattering so analyticity of the scattering angles is no longer possible and the theorem no longer holds. Spacetime-dependent internal symmetries are then possible, such as in the massive [Thirring model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thirring_model) which can admit an infinite tower of [conserved charges](wikipedia://en/3094621) of ever higher [tensorial](wikipedia://en/29965) [rank](wikipedia://en/26561).[^o20]

### Quantum groups {#Quantum_groups}

Models with [nonlocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_locality) symmetries whose charges do not act on multiparticle states as if they were a [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of one-particle states, evade the theorem.[^o22] Such an evasion is found more generally for [quantum group](wikipedia://en/340678) symmetries which avoid the theorem because the corresponding algebra is no longer a Lie algebra.

### Other limitations {#Other_limitations}

For other spacetime symmetries besides the Poincaré group, such as theories with a [de Sitter background](wikipedia://en/301666) or non-relativistic [field theories](wikipedia://en/1293340) with [Galilean invariance](wikipedia://en/286788), the theorem no longer applies.[^o24] It also does not hold for [discrete symmetries](wikipedia://en/1372580), since these are not Lie groups, or for [spontaneously broken symmetries](wikipedia://en/297466) since these do not act on the S-matrix level and thus do not commute with the S-matrix.[^o25]
