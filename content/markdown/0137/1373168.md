# Rooted graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rooted graph"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and, in particular, in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **rooted graph** is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) in which one [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) has been distinguished as the root.[^o2][^o3] Both [directed](wikipedia://en/19721986) and [undirected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) versions of rooted graphs have been studied, and there are also variant definitions that allow multiple roots.

:::

*Image: Rooted graphs.png.* Examples of rooted graphs with some variants. A digraph with the root placed such that each vertex has exactly one path directed to it from the root is an [arborescence](wikipedia://en/13251077).

Rooted graphs may also be known (depending on their application) as **pointed graphs** or **flow graphs**. In some of the applications of these graphs, there is an additional requirement that the whole graph be [reachable](wikipedia://en/2833097) from the root vertex.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Variations"}

## Variations {#Variations}

In [topological graph theory](wikipedia://en/1631772), the notion of a rooted graph may be extended to consider multiple vertices or multiple edges as roots. The former are sometimes called vertex-rooted graphs in order to distinguish them from edge-rooted graphs in this context.[^o6] Graphs with multiple nodes designated as roots are also of some interest in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170), in the area of [random graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_graphs).[^o7] These graphs are also called **multiply rooted graphs**.[^o8]

The terms **rooted [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986)** or **rooted digraph** also see variation in definitions. The obvious transplant is to consider a digraph rooted by identifying a particular node as root.[^o9][^o10] However, in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), these terms commonly refer to a narrower notion; namely, a rooted directed graph is a digraph with a distinguished node *r*, such that there is a directed path from *r* to any node other than *r*.[^o11][^o12][^o13][^o14] Authors who give the more general definition may refer to graphs meeting the narrower definition as *connected* rooted digraphs[^o9] or *accessible* rooted graphs (see Set theory).

*[The Art of Computer Programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Art_of_Computer_Programming)* defines rooted digraphs slightly more broadly, namely, a directed graph is called rooted if it has *at least one* node that can reach all the other nodes. Knuth notes that the notion thus defined is a sort of intermediate between the notions of [strongly connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strongly_connected) and [connected digraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digraph_%28mathematics%29#Digraph_connectivity).[^o15]

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### Flow graphs {#Flow_graphs}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), rooted graphs in which the root vertex can reach all other vertices are called **flow graphs** or **flowgraphs**.[^o19] Sometimes an additional restriction is added specifying that a flow graph must have a single exit ([sink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sink_%28graph_theory%29)) vertex.[^o20]

Flow graphs may be viewed as [abstractions](wikipedia://en/2972) of [flow charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_chart), with the non-structural elements (node contents and types) removed.[^o26][^o21] Perhaps the best known sub-class of flow graphs are [control-flow graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control-flow_graph), used in [compilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compiler) and [program analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Program_analysis). An arbitrary flow graph may be converted to a control-flow graph by performing an [edge contraction](wikipedia://en/3758115) on every edge that is the only outgoing edge from its source and the only incoming edge into its target.[^o22] Another type of flow graph commonly used is the [call graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Call_graph), in which nodes correspond to entire [subroutines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subroutine).[^o27]

The general notion of flow graph has been called **program graph**,[^o23] but the same term has also been used to denote only control-flow graphs.[^o24] Flow graphs have also been called **unlabeled flowgraphs**[^o25] and **proper flowgraphs**.[^o26] These graphs are sometimes used in [software testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_testing).[^o26][^o27]

When required to have a single exit, flow graphs have two properties not shared with directed graphs in general: flow graphs can be nested, which is the equivalent of a subroutine call (although there is no notion of passing parameters), and flow graphs can also be sequenced, which is the equivalent of sequential execution of two pieces of code.[^o28] *Prime flow graphs* are defined as flow graphs that cannot be decomposed via nesting or sequencing using a chosen pattern of subgraphs, for example the primitives of [structured programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structured_programming).[^o29] Theoretical research has been done on determining, for example, the proportion of prime flow graphs given a chosen set of graphs.[^o30]

### Set theory {#Set_theory}

[Peter Aczel](wikipedia://en/24089125) has used rooted directed graphs such that every node is reachable from the root (which he calls **accessible pointed graphs**) to formulate [Aczel's anti-foundation axiom](wikipedia://en/8933657) in [non-well-founded set theory](wikipedia://en/1091767). In this context, each vertex of an accessible pointed graph models a (non-well-founded) set within Aczel's (non-well-founded) set theory, and an arc from a vertex *v* to a vertex *w* models that *v* is an element of *w*. [Aczel's anti-foundation axiom](wikipedia://en/8933657) states that every accessible pointed graph models a family of (non-well-founded) sets in this way.[^o32]

### Combinatorial game theory {#Combinatorial_game_theory}

Any [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/292231) [game](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game), can be associated with a rooted directed graph whose vertices are game positions, whose edges are moves, and whose root is the starting position of the game. This graph is important in the study of [game complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_complexity), where the [state-space complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game_complexity#State-space_complexity) is the number of vertices in the graph.

:::

## Combinatorial enumeration {#Combinatorial_enumeration}

The number of rooted undirected graphs for 1, 2, ... nodes is 1, 2, 6, 20, 90, 544, ... .

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

A special case of interest are [rooted trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rooted_tree), the [trees](wikipedia://en/48560) with a distinguished root vertex. If the directed paths from the root in the rooted digraph are additionally restricted to be unique, then the notion obtained is that of (rooted) [arborescence](wikipedia://en/13251077)—the directed-graph equivalent of a rooted tree.[^o10] A rooted graph contains an arborescence with the same root if and only if the whole graph can be reached from the root, and computer scientists have studied algorithmic problems of finding optimal arborescences.[^o36]

Rooted graphs may be combined using the [rooted product of graphs](wikipedia://en/1373338).[^o37]
