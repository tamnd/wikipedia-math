# Rooted product of graphs

*Image: Graph-rooted-product.svg.* The rooted product of graphs.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rooted product of graphs"}

In mathematical [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **rooted product** (or **comb product**) of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ and a [rooted graph](wikipedia://en/1373168) $H$ is defined as follows: take $\lvert V(G)\rvert$ copies of $H$, and for every vertex $v_{i}$ of $G$, identify $v_{i}$ with the root node of the $i$-th copy of $H$.

:::

More formally, assuming that

$$\begin{aligned} V(G) &= \{g_1, \ldots, g_n \}, \\ V(H) &= \{h_1, \ldots, h_m \}, \end{aligned}$$

and that the root node of $H$ is $h_{1}$, define

$$G \circ H := (V, E),$$

where

$$V = \left\{(g_i, h_j): 1\leq i\leq n, 1\leq j\leq m\right\}$$

and

$$E = \Bigl\{\bigl((g_i, h_1), (g_k, h_1)\bigr): (g_i, g_k) \in E(G)\Bigr\} \cup \bigcup_{i=1}^n \Bigl\{\bigl((g_i, h_j), (g_i, h_k)\bigr): (h_j, h_k) \in E(H)\Bigr\}.$$

If $G$ is also rooted at $g_{1}$, one can view the product itself as rooted, at $(g_{1}, h_{1})$. The rooted product is a [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraphs) of the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/2856852) of the same two graphs.

::: {#o3 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The rooted product is especially relevant for [trees](wikipedia://en/48560), as the rooted product of two trees is another tree. For instance, Koh et al. (1980) used rooted products to find [graceful numberings](wikipedia://en/1821467) for a wide family of trees.

If $H$ is a two-vertex complete graph $K_{2}$, then for any graph $G$, the rooted product of $G$ and $H$ has [domination number](wikipedia://en/1747972) exactly half of its number of vertices. Every connected graph in which the domination number is half the number of vertices arises in this way, with the exception of the four-vertex [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962). These graphs can be used to generate examples in which the bound of [Vizing's conjecture](wikipedia://en/8175877), an unproven inequality between the domination number of the graphs in a different graph product, the [cartesian product of graphs](wikipedia://en/2856852), is exactly met (Fink Jacobson Kinch Roberts 1985). They are also [well-covered graphs](wikipedia://en/32696387).
