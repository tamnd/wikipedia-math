# Stability (probability)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stability"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **stability** of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) is the property that a linear combination of two [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) copies of the variable has the same [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543), up to [location](wikipedia://en/17516) and [scale](wikipedia://en/769503) parameters.[^o2] The distributions of random variables having this property are said to be "stable distributions". Results available in probability theory show that all possible distributions having this property are members of a four-parameter family of distributions. The article on the [stable distribution](wikipedia://en/1117869) describes this family together with some of the properties of these distributions.

:::

The importance in probability theory of "stability" and of the stable family of probability distributions is that they are "attractors" for properly normed sums of [independent and identically distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_and_identically_distributed) random variables.

Important special cases of stable distributions are the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462), the [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003) and the [Lévy distribution](wikipedia://en/1346871). For details see [stable distribution](wikipedia://en/1117869).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

There are several basic definitions for what is meant by stability. Some are based on summations of random variables and others on properties of [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/3229401).

### Definition via distribution functions {#Definition_via_distribution_functions}

Feller[^o6] makes the following basic definition. A random variable *X* is called stable (has a stable distribution) if, for *n* independent copies *X<sub>i</sub>* of *X*, there exist constants *c<sub>n</sub>* > 0 and *d<sub>n</sub>* such that

$$X_1+X_2+\ldots+X_n \stackrel{d}{=} c_n X+d_n ,$$

where this equality refers to equality of distributions. A conclusion drawn from this starting point is that the sequence of constants *c<sub>n</sub>* must be of the form

$c_n = n^{1/\alpha} \,$ for $0 < \alpha \leq 2 .$

A further conclusion is that it is enough for the above distributional identity to hold for *n*=2 and *n*=3 only.[^o7]

:::

## Stability in probability theory {#Stability_in_probability_theory}

There are a number of mathematical results that can be derived for distributions which have the stability property. That is, all possible families of distributions which have the property of being closed under [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) are being considered.[^o9] It is convenient here to call these stable distributions, without meaning specifically the distribution described in the article named [stable distribution](wikipedia://en/1117869), or to say that a distribution is stable if it is assumed that it has the stability property. The following results can be obtained for [univariate distributions](wikipedia://en/9253219) which are stable.

- Stable distributions are always [infinitely divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitely_divisible).[^o10]
- All stable distributions are [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous).[^o11]
- All stable distributions are [unimodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodal).[^o12]

## Other types of stability {#Other_types_of_stability}

The above concept of stability is based on the idea of a class of distributions being closed under a given set of operations on random variables, where the operation is "summation" or "averaging". Other operations that have been considered include:

- **geometric stability**: here the operation is to take the sum of a random number of random variables, where the number has a [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922).[^o14] The counterpart of the stable distribution in this case is the [geometric stable distribution](wikipedia://en/31036750)
- **Max-stability**: here the operation is to take the maximum of a number of random variables. The counterpart of the stable distribution in this case is the [generalized extreme value distribution](wikipedia://en/1494479), and the theory for this case is dealt with as [extreme value theory](wikipedia://en/50732). See also the [stability postulate](wikipedia://en/31283248). A version of this case in which the minimum is taken instead of the maximum is available by a simple extension.
