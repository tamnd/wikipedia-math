# Algebraic operation

*Image: quadratic root.svg.* Algebraic operations in the solution to the [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175). The radical sign √, denoting a [square root](wikipedia://en/29208), is equivalent to [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) to the power of $\tfrac{1}{2}$. The [± sign](wikipedia://en/367225) means the [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) can be written with either a + or a – sign.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic operation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a basic **algebraic operation** is a [mathematical operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_operation) similar to any one of the common [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245) of [elementary algebra](wikipedia://en/9710), which include [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), [subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141), [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), [division](wikipedia://en/53696), raising to a whole number [power](wikipedia://en/99491), and taking [roots](wikipedia://en/235029) ([fractional](wikipedia://en/1704824) power).[^o2] The operations of elementary algebra may be performed on [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690), in which case they are often called *[arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operations)*. They may also be performed, in a similar way, on [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), [algebraic expressions](wikipedia://en/4283745),[^o3] and more generally, on elements of [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364), such as [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) and [fields](wikipedia://en/10603).[^o4]

:::

An algebraic operation on a set $S$ may be defined more formally as a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that maps to $S$ the [tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) of a given length of elements of $S$. The length of the tuples is called the [arity](wikipedia://en/42301) of the operation, and each member of the tuple is called an [operand](wikipedia://en/22656). The most common case is the case of arity two, where the operation is called a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) and the operands form an [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362). A [unary operation](wikipedia://en/161006) is an operation of arity one that has only one operand; for example, the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208). An example of a [ternary operation](wikipedia://en/459413) (arity three) is the [triple product](wikipedia://en/978650).[^o5]

The term *algebraic operation* may also be used for operations that may be defined by compounding basic algebraic operations, such as the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093). In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), *algebraic operation* is also used for the operations that may be defined by purely [algebraic methods](wikipedia://en/18716923). For example, [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) with an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) or [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) exponent is an algebraic operation, but not the general exponentiation with a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) exponent. Also, the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) is an operation that is not algebraic.

::: {#o7 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

Multiplication symbols are usually omitted, and implied, when there is no operator between two variables or terms, or when a [coefficient](wikipedia://en/51203) is used. For example, 3 × *x*<sup>2</sup> is written as 3*x*<sup>2</sup>, and 2 × *x* × *y* is written as 2*xy*.[^o8] Sometimes, multiplication symbols are replaced with either a dot or center-dot, so that *x* × *y* is written as either *x* . *y* or *x* · *y*. [Plain text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_text), [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015), and [calculators](wikipedia://en/7593) also use a single asterisk to represent the multiplication symbol,[^o9] and it must be explicitly used; for example, 3*x* is written as 3 \* *x*.

Rather than using the ambiguous [division sign](wikipedia://en/276918) (÷), division is usually represented with a [vinculum](wikipedia://en/673457), a horizontal line, as in $\tfrac{3}{x + 1}$. In plain text and programming languages, a slash (also called a [solidus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slash_%28punctuation%29)) is used, e.g. 3 / (*x* + 1).

Exponents are usually formatted using superscripts, as in *x*<sup>2</sup>. In [plain text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plain_text), the [TeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeX) mark-up language, and some programming languages such as [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412) and [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29), the [caret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caret) symbol, ^, represents exponents, so *x*<sup>2</sup> is written as *x* ^ 2.[^o10][^o11] In programming languages such as [Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_%28programming_language%29),[^o12] [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran),[^o13] [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl),[^o14] [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29)[^o15] and [Ruby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_%28programming_language%29),[^o16] a double asterisk is used, so *x*<sup>2</sup> is written as *x* \*\* 2.

The [plus–minus sign](wikipedia://en/367225), ±, is used as a shorthand notation for two expressions written as one, representing one expression with a plus sign, the other with a minus sign. For example, *y* = *x* ± 1 represents the two equations *y* = *x* + 1 and *y* = *x* − 1. Sometimes, it is used for denoting a positive-or-negative term such as ±*x*.

:::

## Arithmetic vs algebraic operations {#Arithmetic_vs_algebraic_operations}

Algebraic operations work in the same way as [arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operations), as can be seen in the table below.

::: {#o18 .table cue=table}

| Operation | Arithmetic Example | Algebra Example | Comments ≡ means "equivalent to" ≢ means "not equivalent to" |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Addition](wikipedia://en/61338) | $(5 \times 5) + 5 + 5 + 3$ equivalent to: $5^2 + (2 \times 5) + 3$ | $(b \times b) + b + b + a$ equivalent to: $b^2 + 2b + a$ | $\begin{aligned} 2 \times b & \equiv 2b\\ b + b + b & \equiv 3b\\ b \times b & \equiv b^2 \end{aligned}$ |
| [Subtraction](wikipedia://en/74141) | $(7 \times 7) - 7 - 5$ equivalent to: $7^2 - 7 - 5$ | $(b \times b) - b - a$ equivalent to: $b^2 - b - a$ | $\begin{aligned}b^2 - b & \not\equiv b\\ 3b - b & \equiv 2b\\ b^2 - b & \equiv b(b-1)\end{aligned}$ |
| [Multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) | $3 \times 5$ or $3 \ .\ 5$ or $3 \cdot 5$ or $(3)(5)$ | $a \times b$ or $a . b$ or $a \cdot b$ or $ab$ | $a \times a \times a$ is the same as $a^3$ |
| [Division](wikipedia://en/53696) | $12 \div 4$ or $12 / 4$ or $\frac{12}{4}$ | $b \div a$ or $b / a$ or $\frac{b}{a}$ | $\frac{a+b}{3} \equiv \tfrac{1}{3} \times (a+b)$ |
| [Exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) | $3^{\frac{1}{2}}$ $2^3$ | $a^{\frac{1}{2}}$ $b^3$ | $a^{\frac{1}{2}}$ is the same as $\sqrt a$ $b^3$ is the same as $b \times b \times b$ |

:::

Note: the use of the letters $a$ and $b$ is arbitrary, and the examples would have been equally valid if $x$ and $y$ were used.

## Properties of arithmetic and algebraic operations {#Properties_of_arithmetic_and_algebraic_operations}

::: {#o25 .table cue=table}

| Property | Arithmetic Example | Algebra Example | Comments ≡ means "equivalent to" ≢ means "not equivalent to" |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Commutativity](wikipedia://en/294390) | $3 + 5 = 5 + 3$ $3 \times 5 = 5 \times 3$ | $a + b = b + a$ $a \times b = b \times a$ | Addition and multiplication are commutative and associative.[^o27] Subtraction and division are not: e.g. $a - b \not\equiv b - a$ |
| [Associativity](wikipedia://en/1335) | $(3 + 5) + 7 = 3 + (5 + 7)$ $(3 \times 5) \times 7 = 3 \times (5 \times 7)$ | $(a + b) + c = a + (b + c)$ $(a \times b) \times c = a \times (b \times c)$ |  |

:::
