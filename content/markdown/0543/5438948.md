# Substitution (logic)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Substitution"}

A **substitution** is a [syntactic](wikipedia://en/546039) transformation on [formal](wikipedia://en/10939) expressions. To *apply* a substitution to an [expression](wikipedia://en/609125) means to consistently replace its variable, or placeholder, symbols with other expressions.

:::

The resulting expression is called a *substitution instance*, or *instance* for short, of the original expression.

## Propositional logic {#Propositional_logic}

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

Where *ψ* and *φ* represent [formulas](wikipedia://en/404582) of [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154), *ψ* is a *substitution instance* of *φ* [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *ψ* may be obtained from *φ* by substituting formulas for [propositional variables](wikipedia://en/1557562) in *φ*, replacing each occurrence of the same variable by an occurrence of the same formula. For example:

*ψ:* (R → S) & (T → S)

is a substitution instance of

*φ:* P & Q

That is, *ψ* can be obtained by replacing P and Q in *φ* with (R → S) and (T → S) respectively. Similarly:

*ψ:* (A ↔ A) ↔ (A ↔ A)

is a substitution instance of:

*φ:* (A ↔ A)

since *ψ* can be obtained by replacing each A in *φ* with (A ↔ A).

In some [deduction systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deduction_system) for propositional logic, a new expression (a [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094)) may be entered on a line of a derivation if it is a substitution instance of a previous line of the derivation. This is how new lines are introduced in some [axiomatic systems](wikipedia://en/188401). In systems that use [rules of transformation](wikipedia://en/252311), a rule may include the use of a *substitution instance* for the purpose of introducing certain variables into a derivation.

:::

### Tautologies {#Tautologies}

A propositional formula is a [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335) if it is true under every [valuation](wikipedia://en/17456938) (or [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671)) of its predicate symbols. If Φ is a tautology, and Θ is a substitution instance of Φ, then Θ is again a tautology. This fact implies the soundness of the deduction rule described in the previous section.

## First-order logic {#First-order_logic}

In [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), a *substitution* is a total mapping $\sigma: V \to T$ from [variables](wikipedia://en/24885593#Formal_definition) to [terms](wikipedia://en/24885593); many,[^o12][^o13] but not all[^o9] authors additionally require *σ*(*x*) = *x* for all but finitely many variables *x*. The notation $\{x_{1} \mapsto t_{1}, \ldots, x_{k} \mapsto t_{k}\}$[^o10] refers to a substitution mapping each variable *x*<sub>*i*</sub> to the corresponding term *t*<sub>*i*</sub>, for *i*=1,…,*k*, and every other variable to itself; the *x*<sub>*i*</sub> must be pairwise distinct. Most authors additionally require each term *t*<sub>*i*</sub> to be syntactically different from *x*<sub>*i*</sub>, to avoid infinitely many distinct notations for the same substitution. *Applying* that substitution to a term *t* is written in [postfix notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postfix_notation) as *t* $\{x_{1} \mapsto t_{1}, ..., x_{k} \mapsto t_{k}\}$; it means to (simultaneously) replace every occurrence of each *x*<sub>*i*</sub> in *t* by *t*<sub>*i*</sub>.[^o11] The result *tσ* of applying a substitution *σ* to a term *t* is called an *instance* of that term *t*. For example, applying the substitution $\{x \mapsto z, z \mapsto h(a,y)\}$ to the term

{|

\|- || *f*( | style="text-align: center;" |*z* ||, *a*, *g*( || *x* || ), *y*) || yields |- || *f*( || *h*(*a*,*y*) ||, *a*, *g*( || *z* || ), *y*) || . |}

The *domain* *dom*(*σ*) of a substitution *σ* is commonly defined as the set of variables actually replaced, i.e. *dom*(*σ*) = $\{x \in V\mid x\sigma \ne x\}$. A substitution is called a *ground* substitution if it maps all variables of its domain to [ground](wikipedia://en/24885593#Ground_and_linear_terms), i.e. variable-free, terms. The substitution instance *tσ* of a ground substitution is a ground term if all of *t*'s variables are in *σ*'s domain, i.e. if *vars*(*t*) ⊆ *dom*(*σ*). A substitution *σ* is called a *linear* substitution if *tσ* is a [linear](wikipedia://en/24885593#Ground_and_linear_terms) term for some (and hence every) linear term *t* containing precisely the variables of *σ*'s domain, i.e. with *vars*(*t*) = *dom*(*σ*). A substitution *σ* is called a *flat* substitution if *xσ* is a variable for every variable *x*. A substitution *σ* is called a *renaming* substitution if it is a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027#Permutations_in_group_theory) on the set of all variables. Like every permutation, a renaming substitution σ always has an *inverse* substitution *σ*<sup>−1</sup>, such that *tσσ*<sup>−1</sup> = *t* = *tσ*<sup>−1</sup>*σ* for every term *t*. However, it is not possible to define an inverse for an arbitrary substitution.

For example, $\{x \mapsto 2, y \mapsto 3+4\}$ is a ground substitution, $\{x \mapsto x_{1}, y \mapsto y_{2}+4\}$ is non-ground and non-flat, but linear, $\{x \mapsto y_{2}, y \mapsto y_{2}+4\}$ is non-linear and non-flat, $\{x \mapsto y_{2}, y \mapsto y_{2}\}$ is flat, but non-linear, $\{x \mapsto x_{1}, y \mapsto y_{2}\}$ is both linear and flat, but not a renaming, since it maps both *y* and *y*<sub>2</sub> to *y*<sub>2</sub>; each of these substitutions has the set $\{x,y\}$ as its domain. An example for a renaming substitution is $\{x \mapsto x_{1}, x_{1} \mapsto y, y \mapsto y_{2}, y_{2} \mapsto x\}$, it has the inverse $\{x \mapsto y_{2}, y_{2} \mapsto y, y \mapsto x_{1}, x_{1} \mapsto x\}$. The flat substitution $\{x \mapsto z, y \mapsto z\}$ cannot have an inverse, since e.g. (*x*+*y*) $\{x \mapsto z, y \mapsto z\}$ = *z*+*z*, and the latter term cannot be transformed back to *x*+*y*, as the information about the origin a *z* stems from is lost. The ground substitution $\{x \mapsto 2\}$ cannot have an inverse due to a similar loss of origin information e.g. in (*x*+2) $\{x \mapsto 2\}$ = 2+2, even if replacing constants by variables was allowed by some fictitious kind of "generalized substitutions".

Two substitutions are considered *equal* if they map each variable to [syntactically equal](wikipedia://en/24885593#Structural_equality) result terms, formally: *σ* = *τ* if *xσ* = *xτ* for each variable *x* ∈ *V*. The *composition* of two substitutions *σ* = $\{x_{1} \mapsto t_{1}, \ldots, x_{k} \mapsto t_{k}\}$ and *τ* = $\{y_{1} \mapsto u_{1}, \ldots, y_{l} \mapsto u_{l}\}$ is obtained by removing from the substitution $\{x_{1} \mapsto t_{1}\tau, \ldots, x_{k} \mapsto t_{k}\tau, y_{1} \mapsto u_{1}, \ldots, y_{l} \mapsto u_{l}\}$ those pairs *y*<sub>*i*</sub> ↦ *u*<sub>*i*</sub> for which *y*<sub>*i*</sub> ∈ $\{x_{1}, \ldots, x_{k}\}$. The composition of *σ* and *τ* is denoted by *στ*. Composition is an [associative operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_operation), and is compatible with substitution application, i.e. (*ρσ*)*τ* = *ρ*(*στ*), and (*tσ*)*τ* = *t*(*στ*), respectively, for every substitutions *ρ*, *σ*, *τ*, and every term *t*. The *identity substitution*, which maps every variable to itself, is the [neutral element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutral_element) of substitution composition. A substitution *σ* is called *idempotent* if *σσ* = *σ*, and hence *tσσ* = *tσ* for every term *t*. When *x*<sub>*i*</sub>≠*t*<sub>*i*</sub> for all *i*, the substitution $\{x_{1} \mapsto t_{1}, \ldots, x_{k} \mapsto t_{k}\}$ is idempotent if and only if none of the variables *x*<sub>*i*</sub> occurs in any *t*<sub>*j*</sub>. Substitution composition is not commutative, that is, *στ* may be different from *τσ*, even if *σ* and *τ* are idempotent.[^o12][^o13]

For example, $\{x \mapsto 2, y \mapsto 3+4\}$ is equal to $\{y \mapsto 3+4, x \mapsto 2\}$, but different from $\{x \mapsto 2, y \mapsto 7\}$. The substitution $\{x \mapsto y+y\}$ is idempotent, e.g. ((*x*+*y*) $\{x\mapsto y+y\}$) $\{x\mapsto y+y\}$ = ((*y*+*y*)+*y*) $\{x\mapsto y+y\}$ = (*y*+*y*)+*y*, while the substitution $\{x \mapsto x+y\}$ is non-idempotent, e.g. ((*x*+*y*) $\{x\mapsto x+y\}$) $\{x\mapsto x+y\}$ = ((*x*+*y*)+*y*) $\{x\mapsto x+y\}$ = ((*x*+*y*)+*y*)+*y*. An example for non-commuting substitutions is $\{x \mapsto y\}$ $\{y \mapsto z\}$ = $\{x \mapsto z, y \mapsto z\}$, but $\{y \mapsto z\}$ $\{x \mapsto y\}$ = $\{x \mapsto y, y \mapsto z\}$.

:::

## Mathematics {#Mathematics}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), there are two common uses of substitution: *substitution of [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109)* for [constants](wikipedia://en/24758132) (also called [assignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assignment_%28computer_science%29) for that variable), and the *substitution property of [equality](wikipedia://en/90446)*, also called [*Leibniz's Law*](wikipedia://en/630398).[^o16]

Considering mathematics as a [formal language](wikipedia://en/10939), a variable is a [symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbol_%28mathematics%29) from an [alphabet](wikipedia://en/4292269), usually a letter like $x$, $y$, and $z$, which denotes a range of possible [values](wikipedia://en/4095147). If a variable is [free](wikipedia://en/147460) in a given [expression](wikipedia://en/609125) or [formula](wikipedia://en/164040#In_mathematics), then it can be replaced with any of the values in its range. Certain kinds of bound variables can be substituted too. For instance, [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065) of an expression (like the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000)), or the [argument](wikipedia://en/32842052) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427). Moreover, variables being [universally quantified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universally_quantified) can be replaced with any of the values in its range, and the result will a true [statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29). (This is called [Universal instantiation](wikipedia://en/1564194))

For a non-formalized language, that is, in most mathematical texts outside of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), for an individual expression it is not always possible to identify which variables are free and bound. For example, in $\sum_{i < k} a_{ik}$, depending on the context, the variable $i$ can be free and $k$ bound, or vice-versa, but they cannot both be free. Determining which value is assumed to be free depends on context and [semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantics_of_logic).

The *substitution property of equality*, or *Leibniz's Law* (though the latter term is usually reserved for [philosophical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical) contexts), generally states that, if two things are equal, then any property of one, must be a property of the other. It can be formally stated in [logical notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_notation) as:

$$(a=b) \implies \bigl[ \phi(a) \Rightarrow \phi(b) \bigr]$$

For every $a$ and $b$, and any [well-formed formula](wikipedia://en/404582) $\phi(x)$ (with a free variable x). For example: For all [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) $a$ and $b$, if $a = b$, then $a \ge 0$ implies $b \ge 0$ (here, $\phi(x)$ is $x \ge 0$). This is a property which is most often used in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), especially in solving [systems of equations](wikipedia://en/249617), but is applied in nearly every area of math that uses equality. This, taken together with the [reflexive property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_property) of equality, forms the [axioms of equality](wikipedia://en/10983#Equality_and_its_axioms) in first-order logic.[^o19]

Substitution is related to, but not identical to, [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947); it is closely related to *β*-reduction in [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203). In contrast to these notions, however, the accent in algebra is on the preservation of algebraic structure by the substitution operation, the fact that substitution gives a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) for the structure at hand (in the case of polynomials, the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) structure).

### Algebra

Substitution is a basic operation in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), in particular in [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805).[^o20][^o21]

A common case of substitution involves [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), where substitution of a numerical value (or another expression) for the indeterminate of a univariate polynomial amounts to evaluating the polynomial at that value. Indeed, this operation occurs so frequently that the notation for polynomials is often adapted to it; instead of designating a polynomial by a name like *P*, as one would do for other mathematical objects, one could define

$$P(X)=X^5-3X^2+5X-17$$

so that substitution for *X* can be designated by replacement inside "*P*(*X*)", say

$$P(2) = 13$$

or

$$P(X+1) = X^5 + 5X^4 + 10X^3 + 7X^2 + 4X - 14.$$

Substitution can also be applied to other kinds of formal objects built from symbols, for instance elements of [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735). In order for substitution to be defined, one needs an algebraic structure with an appropriate [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245), that asserts the existence of unique homomorphisms that send indeterminates to specific values; the substitution then amounts to finding the image of an element under such a homomorphism.

::: {#o23 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of substitution in ZFC"}

### Proof of substitution in ZFC {#Proof_of_substitution_in_ZFC}

The following is a proof of the substitution property of equality in ZFC (as defined in first-order logic without equality), which is adapted from *Introduction to Axiomatic Set Theory* (1982) by Gaisi Takeuti and Wilson M. Zaring.[^o24]

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

if $a = b$, then, for any well-formed formula $\phi$, $\phi(a) \Rightarrow \phi(b)$.

:::

See [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory § Formal language](wikipedia://en/152214#Formal_language) for the definition of formulas in ZFC. The [definition is recursive](wikipedia://en/1047605), so a proof by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) is used. In ZFC in first-order logic without equality, "set equality" is defined to mean that two sets have the same elements, written symbolically as "for all z, z is in x if and only if z is in y". Then, the Axiom of Extensionality asserts that if two sets have the same elements, then they belong to the same sets:

::: {#o26 .definition cue=math-theorem name="Definition"}

**Definition.**

$$(x=y) := \forall z [z \in x \Leftrightarrow z \in y]$$

:::

::: {#o27 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=math-theorem name="Axiom"}

**Axiom.**

$$(x = y) \Rightarrow \forall z (x \in z \Leftrightarrow y \in z)$$

:::

::: {#o28 .proof cue=math-proof name="Base formulas"}

**Base formulas.**

Let $X, Y, Z$, be metavariables for any variables or sets, such that $X = Y$

Case 1: $\phi(X): (Z \in X)$

> Assume $Z \in X$, then, by the definition of equality, $Z \in Y$, thus $(X=Y) \implies \bigl[ Z \in X \Rightarrow Z \in Y \bigr]$

Case 2: $\phi(X): (X \in Z )$

> Assume $X \in Z$, then by the axiom of extensionality, $Y \in Z$, thus $(X=Y) \implies \bigl[ X \in Z \Rightarrow Y \in Z \bigr]$

:::

::: {#o29 .proof cue=math-proof name="Recursive formulas"}

**Recursive formulas.**

Let $\psi, \varphi$ be meta variables for any formulas with the property that $(a=b) \implies \bigl[ \phi(a) \Rightarrow \phi(b) \bigr]$. Let $X, Y$, be metavariables for any variables or sets, such that $X = Y$, and let $z$ be a metavariable for any variable.

Case 1: $\neg (\psi)$

> Since $X = Y$, then $Y= X$ by symmetry of equality, therefore $\bigl[ \psi(Y) \Rightarrow \psi(X) \bigr]$, by the induction hypothesis, therefore $\bigl[ \neg\psi(X) \Rightarrow \neg\psi(Y) \bigr]$ by [contraposition](wikipedia://en/8556497), thus $(X=Y) \implies \bigl[ \neg\psi(X) \Rightarrow \neg\psi(Y) \bigr]$

Case 2: $\psi \land \varphi$

> Since $X=Y$, then $\bigl[ \psi(X) \Rightarrow \psi(Y) \bigr]$ and $\bigl[ \varphi(X) \Rightarrow \varphi(Y) \bigr]$, which implies $\bigl[ \bigl( \psi(X) \land \varphi(X)\bigr) \Rightarrow \psi(Y) \land \varphi(Y) \bigr) \bigr]$, thus $(X=Y) \implies \bigl[ \bigl( \psi(X) \land \varphi(X)\bigr) \Rightarrow \psi(Y) \land \varphi(Y) \bigr) \bigr]$

Case 3: $\exists z(\psi)$

> Since $X=Y$, $\psi(X, z) \Rightarrow \psi(Y, z)$ assume by [way of contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction) that the result is false, that is $\exists z(\psi(X,z))$ is true but $\exists z(\psi(Y,z))$ is false. By [existential instantiation](wikipedia://en/34632680), let $z_0$ denote the value such that $\psi(X, z_0)$ is true. Then $\psi(Y,z_0)$ is false by asumption, and therefore $\psi(X, z_0) \Rightarrow \psi(Y, z_0)$ is false, which contradicts our induction hypothesis, and the result follows.

:::
