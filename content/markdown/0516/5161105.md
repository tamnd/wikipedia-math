# Brahmagupta's identity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brahmagupta's identity"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), **Brahmagupta's identity** says that, for given $n$, the product of two numbers of the form $a^{2} + nb^{2}$ is itself a number of that form. In other words, the set of such numbers is [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under multiplication. Specifically:

:::

$$(a^2 + nb^2)(c^2 + nd^2) = (ac \pm nbd)^2 + n(ad \mp bc)^2.$$

This identity holds in both the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and the [ring of rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), and more generally in any [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346).

Also, it can be viewed as a special case of the following identity:

$$(ha^2 + kb^2)(hc^2 + kd^2) = (hac \pm kbd)^2 + hk(ad \mp bc)^2.$$

## History {#History}

The identity is a generalization of the so-called [Brahmagupta-Fibonacci identity](wikipedia://en/326483) (where $n = 1$) which is actually found in [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110)' *[Arithmetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetica)* (III, 19). That identity was rediscovered by [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) (598–668), an [Indian mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_mathematicians) and [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_astronomy), who generalized it and used it in his study of what is now called [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877). His *[Brahmasphutasiddhanta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmasphutasiddhanta)* was translated from [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) into [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) by [Mohammad al-Fazari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammad_al-Fazari), and was subsequently translated into [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) in 1126.[^o3] The identity later appeared in [Fibonacci](wikipedia://en/17949)'s *[Book of Squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Book_of_Squares)* in 1225.

## Application to Pell's equation {#Application_to_Pell's_equation}

In its original context, Brahmagupta applied his discovery to the solution of what was later called [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877), namely $x^{2} - Ny^{2} = 1$. Using the identity in the form

$$(x_1^2 - Ny_1^2)(x_2^2 - Ny_2^2) = (x_1x_2 + Ny_1y_2)^2 - N(x_1y_2 + x_2y_1)^2,$$

he was able to "compose" triples $(x_{1}, y_{1}, k_{1})$ and $(x_{2}, y_{2}, k_{2})$ that were solutions of $x^{2} - Ny^{2} = k$, to generate the new triple

$$(x_1x_2 + Ny_1y_2,\, x_1y_2 + x_2y_1,\, k_1k_2).$$

Not only did this give a way to generate infinitely many solutions to $x^{2} - Ny^{2} = 1$ starting with one solution, but also, by dividing such a composition by $k_{1}k_{2}$, integer or "nearly integer" solutions could often be obtained. The general method for solving the Pell equation given by [Bhaskara II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhaskara_II) in 1150, namely the [chakravala (cyclic) method](wikipedia://en/4050658), was also based on this identity.[^o5]
