# Langevin dynamics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Langevin dynamics"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), **Langevin dynamics** is an approach to the mathematical modeling of the [dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamics_%28physics%29) of molecular systems using the [Langevin equation](wikipedia://en/166890). It was originally developed by French physicist [Paul Langevin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Langevin). The approach is characterized by the use of simplified models while accounting for omitted [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29) by the use of [stochastic differential equations](wikipedia://en/1361454). Langevin dynamics simulations are a kind of [Monte Carlo simulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monte_Carlo_simulation).[^o2]

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Real world molecular systems occur in air or solvents, rather than in isolation, in a vacuum. Jostling of solvent or air molecules causes friction, and the occasional high velocity collision will perturb the system. Langevin dynamics attempts to extend [molecular dynamics](wikipedia://en/198608) to allow for these effects. Also, Langevin dynamics allows temperature to be controlled as with a thermostat, thus approximating the [canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1137672).

Langevin dynamics mimics the viscous aspect of a solvent. It does not fully model an [implicit solvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_solvent); specifically, the model does not account for the [electrostatic screening](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson-Boltzmann_equation) and also not for the [hydrophobic effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrophobic_effect). For denser solvents, hydrodynamic interactions are not captured via Langevin dynamics.

For a system of $N$ particles with masses $M$, with coordinates $X=X(t)$ that constitute a time-dependent [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685), the resulting [Langevin equation](wikipedia://en/166890) is[^o4][^o5]

$$M\,\ddot{\mathbf{X}} = - \mathbf{\nabla} U(\mathbf{X}) - \gamma\,M\,\dot{\mathbf{X}} + \sqrt{2\,M\,\gamma\,k_{\rm B} T}\,\mathbf{R}(t)\,,$$

where $U(\mathbf{X})$ is the particle interaction potential; $\nabla$ is the gradient operator such that $-\mathbf{\nabla} U(\mathbf{X})$ is the force calculated from the particle interaction potentials; the dot is a [time derivative](wikipedia://en/1783069) such that $\dot{\mathbf{X}}$ is the velocity and $\ddot{\mathbf{X}}$ is the acceleration; $\gamma$ is the damping constant (units of reciprocal time), also known as the [collision frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collision_frequency); $T$ is the temperature, $k_{\rm B}$ is the [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant); and $\mathbf{R}(t)$ is a delta-correlated [stationary](wikipedia://en/329898) [Gaussian process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_Process) with zero-mean, called Gaussian [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182), satisfying

$$\left\langle \mathbf{R}(t) \right\rangle = 0$$

$$\left\langle \mathbf{R}(t)\cdot\mathbf{R}(t') \right\rangle = \delta(t - t')$$

Here, $\delta$ is the [Dirac delta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta).

### Stochastic Differential Formulation {#Stochastic_Differential_Formulation}

Considering the [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) of [standard Brownian motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Brownian_motion) or [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984) $W_t$, we can find that

$$\mathbb{E}(W_tW_\tau) = \min(t,\tau)$$

Define the covariance matrix of the derivative as

$$\mathbb{E}(\dot{W_t}\dot{W_\tau}) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial}{\partial \tau}\mathbb{E}(W_tW_\tau) = \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\frac{\partial}{\partial \tau}\min(t,\tau) =\delta(t-\tau)$$

So under the sense of [covariance](wikipedia://en/157059) we can say that

$${\rm d}W_t = \mathbf{R}(t){\rm d}t$$

[Without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000), let the mass $M = 1$, $\sigma = \sqrt{M\gamma k_{\rm B}T}$, then the original SDE will become

$${\rm d}\dot{\mathbf{X}} = -\nabla U(\mathbf{X}){\rm d}t-\gamma {\rm d}{\mathbf{X}} +\sqrt{2}\sigma{\rm d} \mathbf{W}(t)$$

### Overdamped Langevin dynamics {#Overdamped_Langevin_dynamics}

If the main objective is to control temperature, care should be exercised to use a small damping constant $\gamma$. As $\gamma$ grows, it spans from the inertial all the way to the diffusive ([Brownian](wikipedia://en/4436)) regime. The Langevin dynamics limit of non-inertia is commonly described as [Brownian dynamics](wikipedia://en/6703785). Brownian dynamics can be considered as overdamped Langevin dynamics, i.e. Langevin dynamics where no average acceleration takes place. Under this limit we have ${\rm d}\dot{X}=0$, the original SDE then will becomes

$${\rm d}{\mathbf{X}} =-\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla U(\mathbf{X}){\rm d}t+\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{\gamma}{\rm d} \mathbf{W}(t)$$

The translational Langevin equation can be solved using various numerical methods [^o8][^o9] with differences in the sophistication of analytical solutions, the allowed time-steps, time-reversibility ([symplectic methods](wikipedia://en/2063558)), in the limit of zero friction, *etc.*

The Langevin equation can be generalized to [rotational](wikipedia://en/39789) dynamics of [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule), [Brownian](wikipedia://en/4436) particles, *etc.* A standard (according to [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology)[^o10]) way to do it is to leverage a [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440)-based description of the stochastic rotational motion.[^o11][^o12]

## Applications {#Applications}

### Langevin thermostat {#Langevin_thermostat}

Langevin thermostat[^o16] is a type of [Thermostat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermostat) algorithm in [molecular dynamics](wikipedia://en/198608), which is used to simulate a [canonical ensemble](wikipedia://en/1137672) (NVT) under a desired temperature. It integrates the following Langevin equation of motion:

$$M \ddot{\mathbf{X}} = -\nabla U(\mathbf{X}) - \gamma \dot{\mathbf{X}} + \sqrt{2\gamma k_BT} \textbf{R}(t)$$

$-\nabla U(\mathbf{X})$ is the deterministic force term; $\gamma$ is the friction coefficient and $\gamma \dot{X}$ is the friction or damping term; the last term is the random force term ($k_B$: [Boltzmann constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boltzmann_constant), $T$: temperature). This equation allows the system to couple with an imaginary "heat bath": the kinetic energy of the system dissipates from the friction/damping term, and gain from random force/fluctuation; the strength of coupling is controlled by $\gamma$. This equation can be simulated with [SDE](wikipedia://en/1361454) solvers such as [Euler–Maruyama method](wikipedia://en/7139118), where the random force term is replaced by a [Gaussian random number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_random_variable) in every integration step (variance $\sigma^2 = 2\gamma k_BT/ \Delta t$, $\Delta t$: time step), or Langevin [Leapfrog integration](wikipedia://en/15066189), etc. This method is also known as Langevin Integrator.[^o17]

### Langevin Monte Carlo {#Langevin_Monte_Carlo}

The [overdamped Langevin equation](wikipedia://en/6703785) gives

$${\rm d} \mathbf{x}_t = - \frac{D}{k_BT} \nabla_\mathbf{x} U(\mathbf{x}_t) {\rm d}t + \sqrt{2D} {\rm d} W_t$$

Here, $D = k_B T / \gamma$ is the [diffusion coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_coefficient) from [Einstein relation](wikipedia://en/2970168)**.** As proven with [Fokker-Planck equation](wikipedia://en/166896), under appropriate conditions, the [stationary distribution](wikipedia://en/2524727) of $\mathbf x_t$ is [Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/4107) $p(\mathbf{x}) \propto e^{-U(\mathbf{x})/k_BT}$.

Since that $\nabla \log p(\mathbf{x})=-\nabla U(\mathbf{x})/k_BT$, this equation is equivalent to the following form:

$${\rm d} \mathbf{x}_t = \epsilon \nabla_\mathbf{x}\log p(\mathbf x_t) {\rm d}t + \sqrt{2\epsilon} {\rm d} W_t$$

And the distribution of $\mathbf x_t (t\to \infty)$ follows $p(\mathbf{x})$. In other words, Langevin dynamics drives particles towards a stationary distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$ along a gradient flow, due to the $\nabla \log p(\mathbf{x})$ term, while still allowing for some random fluctuations. This provides a [Markov Chain Monte Carlo](wikipedia://en/236801) method that can be used to sample data $\mathbf x$ from a target distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$, known as [Langevin Monte Carlo](wikipedia://en/49312612).

In many applications, we have a desired distribution $p(\mathbf{x})$ from which we would like to sample $\mathbf x$, but direct sampling might be challenging or inefficient. Langevin Monte Carlo offers another way to sample $\mathbf x \sim p(\mathbf x)$ by sampling a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876) in accordance with the Langevin dynamics whose [stationary state](wikipedia://en/4930033) is $p(\mathbf{x})$. The [Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm](wikipedia://en/49312612) (MALA) is an example : Given a current state $\mathbf x_t$, the MALA method proposes a new state $\tilde{x}_{t+1}$ using the Langevin dynamics above. The proposal is then accepted or rejected based on the [Metropolis-Hastings algorithm](wikipedia://en/56107). The incorporation of the Langevin dynamics in the choice of $\tilde{x}_{t+1}$ provides greater computational efficiency, since the dynamics drive the particles into regions of higher $p(\mathbf{x})$ probability and are thus more likely to be accepted. Read more in [Metropolis-adjusted Langevin algorithm](wikipedia://en/49312612).

### Score-based generative model {#Score-based_generative_model}

Langevin dynamics is one of the bases of [score-based generative models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_model#Score-based_generative_model).[^o20][^o21] From (overdamped) Langevin dynamics,

$${\rm d} \mathbf{x}_t = \epsilon \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p(\mathbf x_t) {\rm d}t + \sqrt{2\epsilon} {\rm d} W_t$$

A [generative model](wikipedia://en/1222578) aims to generate samples that follow (unknown data distribution) $p(\mathbf{x})$. To achieve that, a score-based model learns an approximate [score function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Score_%28statistics%29) $\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \approx \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p(\mathbf{x})$ (a process called [score matching](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_model#Score_matching)). With access to a score function, samples are generated by the following iteration,[^o20][^o21]

$$\mathbf{x}_{i+1} \gets \mathbf{x}_i + \epsilon \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p(\mathbf{x}_i) + \sqrt{2\epsilon} \mathbf{z}_i, \quad i=0,1,\cdots, K$$

with $\mathbf{z}_i \sim N(0,1)$. As $\epsilon \to 0$ and $K \to \infty$, the generated $\mathbf{x}_K$ converge to the target distribution $p(\mathbf x)$. Score-based models use $\mathbf{s}_\theta(\mathbf{x}) \approx \nabla_\mathbf{x} \log p(\mathbf{x})$ as an approximation.[^o20]

## Relation to Other Theories {#Relation_to_Other_Theories}

### Klein-Kramers equation {#Klein-Kramers_equation}

As a [stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454)(SDE), **Langevin dynamics** equation, has its corresponding [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564)(PDE), [Klein-Kramers equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein-Kramers_equation), a special [Fokker–Planck equation](wikipedia://en/166896) that governs the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the particles in the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101). The original **Langevin dynamics** equation can be reformulated as the following first order SDEs:

$${\rm d}\mathbf{X} = \mathbf{P}{\rm d} t$$

$${\rm d} \mathbf{P} = -\gamma\mathbf{P}{\rm d}t-\nabla U(\mathbf{X}){\rm d} t+\sqrt{2}\sigma {\rm d}\mathbf{W}(t)$$

Now consider the following cases and their law of $(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P})$:

\1.$\mathbf{{\rm d}{X}} = \mathbf{P}{\rm d} t , \mathbf{{\rm d}{P}} = -\gamma\mathbf{P}{\rm d} t-\nabla U(\mathbf{X}){\rm d} t+\sqrt{2}\sigma {\rm d}\mathbf{W}(t)$ with $(\mathbf{X}_0,\mathbf{P}_0)\sim\rho_0$

\2. $\frac{\partial\rho}{\partial t} = -\mathbf{P}\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\rho+\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}(\gamma\mathbf{P}\rho+\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}U(\mathbf{X})\rho)+\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}^2(\sigma_{T}^2\rho)$ with $\rho(t=0,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P})=\rho_0$

Consider a general function of momentum and position

$$\Psi_t = \Psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P})$$

The expectation value of the function will be

$$\mathbb{E}[\Psi_t] = \int \rho(t,\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P})\Psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P}){\rm d} \mathbf{P}{\rm d} \mathbf{X}$$

Taking derivative with respect to time $t$, and applying [Itô's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itô's_formula), we have

$$\mathbb{E}[\frac{\rm d}{{\rm d}t}\Psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P})] =\mathbb{E}[\nabla_{\mathbf{X}} \Psi\frac{{\rm d}\mathbf{X}}{{\rm d}t} + \nabla_{\mathbf{P}}\Psi\frac{{\rm d}\mathbf{P}}{{\rm d}t} + \sigma_T^2\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}^2\Psi\frac{1}{{\rm d} t}({\rm d} \mathbf{W}(t))^2]$$

which can be simplified to

$$\int (\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho)\Psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P}){\rm d} \mathbf{X}{\rm d}\mathbf{P} =\mathbb{E}[(\nabla_{\mathbf{X}} \Psi)\mathbf{P} + \nabla_{\mathbf{P}}\Psi(-\gamma\mathbf{P}-\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}U(\mathbf{X})) + \sigma_T^2\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}^2\Psi]$$

[Integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) on right hand side, due to vanishing density for infinite momentum or velocity we have

$$(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\rho)\Psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P}){\rm d} \mathbf{X}{\rm d}\mathbf{P} = \int(-\mathbf{P}\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\rho+\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}(\gamma\mathbf{P}\rho+\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}U(\mathbf{X})\rho)+\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}^2(\sigma_{T}^2\rho) )\Psi(\mathbf{X},\mathbf{P}){\rm d}\mathbf{X}{\rm d}\mathbf{P}$$

This equation holds for arbitrary $\Psi$, so we require the density to satisfy

$$\frac{\partial \rho}{\partial t} = -\mathbf{P}\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}\rho+\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}(\gamma\mathbf{P}\rho+\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}U(\mathbf{X})\rho)+\nabla_{\mathbf{P}}^2(\sigma_{T}^2\rho)$$

This equation is called the [Klein-Kramers equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein-Kramers_equation), a special version of [Fokker Planck equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fokker_Planck_equation). It's a partial differential equation that describes the evolution of probability density of the system in the phase space.

### Fokker Planck equation {#Fokker_Planck_equation}

For the overdamped limit, we have ${\rm d}\mathbf{P} = 0$, so the evolution of system can be reduced to the position subspace. Following similar logic we can prove that the SDE for position,

$${\rm d} \mathbf{X} = -\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla U(\mathbf{X}){\rm d} t +\sqrt{2}\frac{\sigma}{\gamma}\mathbf{R}(t){\rm d} t$$

corresponds to the [Fokker Planck equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fokker_Planck_equation) for probability density

$$\frac{\partial \rho(t,\mathbf{X})}{\partial t} = \nabla_{\mathbf{X}}(\frac{1}{\gamma}\nabla_{\mathbf{X}}U(\mathbf{X})\rho(t,\mathbf{X}))+\Delta_\mathbf{X}(\frac{\sigma^2}{\gamma^2}\rho(t,\mathbf{X}))$$

### Fluctuation-dissipation theorem {#Fluctuation-dissipation_theorem}

Consider Langevin dynamics of a [free particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_particle) (i.e. $U(\mathbf{X})=0$), then the equation for momentum will become

$${\rm d}\mathbf{P} = -\frac{1}{\gamma} \mathbf{P}{\rm d}t +\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{\gamma} {\rm d}\mathbf{W}_t$$

the analytical solution to this [SDE](wikipedia://en/1361454) is

$$\mathbf{P} = \mathbf{P}_0e^{- t/\gamma}+\frac{\sqrt{2}\sigma}{\gamma}\int_0^t {\rm e}^{-(t-t')/\gamma}{\rm d}\mathbf{W}_t'$$

thus the average value of [second moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_moment) of momentum will becomes (here we apply the [Itô isometry](wikipedia://en/7206425))

$$\mathbb{E}(\mathbf{P}^2) = \mathbf{P}^2_0{\rm e}^{-2 t/\gamma}+ \frac{\sigma^2}{\gamma}(1-{\rm e}^{-2t/\gamma})\overset{t\to\infty}{\to}\frac{\sigma^2}{\gamma}$$

That is, the limiting behavior when time approaches positive infinity, the momentum fluctuation of this system is related to the energy dissipation ([friction](wikipedia://en/11062) term parameter $\gamma$) of this system. Combining this result with [Equipartition theorem](wikipedia://en/516133), which relates the average value of [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327) of particles with [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050)

$$\langle v^2\rangle = k_{\rm B}T$$

we can determines the value of the variance $\sigma$ in applications like Langevin thermostat.

$$\sigma^2/\gamma = k_B T \to \sigma = \sqrt{k_{\rm B} T\gamma}$$

This is consistent with the original definition assuming $M=1$.

:::

### Path integral {#Path_integral}

[Path integral formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_integral_formulation) comes from [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202). But for a Langevin SDE we can also induce a corresponding path integral. Considering the following overdamped Langevin equation under, where without loss of generality we take $\gamma = \sigma = 1$,

$${\rm d}{X} = -\nabla U({X}){\rm d}t +\sqrt{2}{\rm d}W_t$$

Discretize and define $t_n = n\Delta t$, we get

$${X}_{n+1} -{X}_{n} + \nabla U({X})\Delta t= \sqrt{2}(W_{t_n}-W_{t_{n-1}})\sim \mathcal{N}(0,2\sqrt{\Delta t})$$

Therefore the propagation probability will be

$$P({X}_{n+1}|{X}_{n}) = \int {\rm d}\xi \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi\Delta t}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\xi^2}{4\Delta t}} \delta({X}_{n+1} -{X}_{n} + \nabla U({X})\Delta t-\xi)$$

Applying [Fourier Transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_Transform) of [delta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_function), and we will get

$$P = \int \frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k({X}_{n+1}-{X}_n + \nabla U({X})\Delta t)}\int {\rm d}\xi \frac{1}{2\sqrt{\pi\Delta t}}{\rm e}^{-\frac{\xi^2}{4\Delta t}}{\rm e}^{-{\rm i}k\xi}$$

The second part is a [Gaussian Integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_Integral), which yields

$$P = \int \frac{{\rm d}k}{2\pi}{\rm e}^{{\rm i}k({X}_{n+1}-{X}_n + \nabla U({X})\Delta t)}{\rm e}^{-k^2\Delta t}$$

Now consider the probability from initial $X_0$ to final $X_n$.

$$P(\mathbf{X}_n|\mathbf{X}_0) = \int \frac{1}{2\pi}\prod_i^{N-1} {\rm d}k_i {\rm e}^{({\rm i}k_i(\dot{X} + \nabla U(X))-k_i^2)\Delta t}$$

take the limit of $\Delta t\to 0$,we will get

$$P(\mathbf{X}_n|\mathbf{X}_0) = \int \mathcal{D}[k] {\rm e}^{\int_0^{t_n}({\rm i}k(\dot{X} + \nabla U(X))-k^2){\rm d} t}$$
