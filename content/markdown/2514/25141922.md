# Alwyn Robbins

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alwyn Robbins"}

**Alwyn Rudolph Robbins** (5 January 1920 – 9 or 10 January 2002) was a British [geodesist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesist), former chairman of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society) subcommittee on geodesy, and a founding fellow of [St Cross College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Cross_College,_Oxford) at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford). Robbins published on [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) and [photogrammetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photogrammetry), with additional contributions to [geodetic astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodetic_astronomy) and the development of [clocks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock). He held multiple positions within the [International Association of Geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Geodesy) during his career as well.

:::

## Early life, education, and military service {#Early_life,_education,_and_military_service}

Alwyn Robbins was born in [Lydney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lydney), Gloucestershire on 5 January 1920. He was educated at [Blundell's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blundell's_School) in [Tiverton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiverton,_Devon), Devon, winning multiple [scholarships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholarship) and [exhibitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exhibition_%28scholarship%29) to attend [Hertford College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hertford_College,_Oxford) of the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford).[^o11] At Hertford, he studied mathematics,[^o12] and participated in [rowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rowing), [squash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squash_%28sport%29), and [tennis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennis).[^o13]

Robbins graduated from Oxford in 1940, and was awarded his degree with [first class honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_class_honours).[^o16][^o17] Later that year, he received a [commission](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commission_%28document%29) in the Survey Branch of the [Royal Engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Engineers) and was posted to [West Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Africa).

From his studies at Oxford, Robbins had also received [Master of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science), [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts), and [Doctor of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) degrees.[^o14]

## Career {#Career}

Following military demobilisation in 1946, Robbins returned to Oxford and soon gained a post as an academic post in surveying.[^o18] He was promoted multiple times and continued to climb the ranks, becoming a [reader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reader_%28academic_rank%29) and head of the surveying and geodesy department in 1966 upon the retirement of [Guy Bomford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guy_Bomford).[^o19][^o20] (Olliver 2002) records:

His work for his [BSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science) degree (converted to [MSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Science)) involved developing precise formulae for the geometry of long lines on the [earth ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/16112368). His [D.Phil.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) project was an investigation of the shape of the [geoid](wikipedia://en/176244) – the [equipotential surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equipotential_surface) of the Earth's gravity field that coincides with the sea surface – in England and Scotland. This involved making astronomical observations at 43 [Ordnance Survey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordnance_Survey) [triangulation points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangulation_point), extending from [Dover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dover) to [John o'Groats](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_o'Groats) – an arduous campaign over several seasons that had to be fitted in with his academic duties. His particular interest in astronomy was the definition and precise measurement of time, and he designed a highly accurate portable printing [chronometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clock) for use in geodetic astronomy, which was successfully marketed. He was also the author of a military handbook on the same subject.

Robbins published on geodesy and on timekeeping, and held several appointments within the [International Association of Geodesy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Association_of_Geodesy). He also represented the United Kingdom on the international scale as a delegate to conferences and assemblies, and was the chairman of the geodesy subcommittee of the [Royal Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Society).[^o21] He also had extensive international connections and spent in various countries, as well as a two-year secondment with the [Ministry of Defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ministry_of_Defence_%28United_Kingdom%29).[^o22] During his time at Oxford, robbins held multiple visiting professorships, including at [Ohio State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ohio_State_University), [Kwame Nkrumah University of Science and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kwame_Nkrumah_University_of_Science_and_Technology), and the [University of Otago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Otago).[^o23]

In 1980, Robbins retired, becoming an *[emeritus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emeritus)* fellow of St Cross College.[^o24] During his career, he had been elected [Fellow of the Royal Institution of Chartered Surveyors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Institution_of_Chartered_Surveyors), [Fellow of the Royal Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Astronomical_Society), and [Fellow of the Royal Geographical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_Royal_Geographical_Society).[^o25]

## Personal life and death {#Personal_life_and_death}

Following his retirement, Robbins moved to Devon and pursued recreational [fly-fishing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fly-fishing).[^o26] He died in his home at [Ottery St Mary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottery_St_Mary)[^o27] on 9 or 10 January 2002. He was survived by his wife Mary and his children: a son and a daughter, Mike and Rowena.[^o31][^o32]
