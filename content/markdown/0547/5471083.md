# Axiom A

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Axiom A"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Smale's axiom A** defines a class of [dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/9087) which have been extensively studied and whose dynamics is relatively well understood. A prominent example is the [Smale horseshoe map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smale_horseshoe_map). The term "axiom A" originates with [Stephen Smale](wikipedia://en/40294).[^o2][^o3] The importance of such systems is demonstrated by the [chaotic hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaotic_hypothesis), which states that, 'for all practical purposes', a many-body [thermostatted system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermostatted_system) is approximated by an [Anosov system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anosov_system).[^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *M* be a [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) with a [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) *f*: *M*→*M*. Then *f* is an **axiom A diffeomorphism** if the following two conditions hold:

1. The [nonwandering set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonwandering_set) of *f*, *Ω*(*f*), is a [hyperbolic set](wikipedia://en/2658073) and [compact](wikipedia://en/6042).
1. The set of [periodic points](wikipedia://en/4941621) of *f* is [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_subset) in *Ω*(*f*).

For surfaces, hyperbolicity of the nonwandering set implies the density of periodic points, but this is no longer true in higher dimensions. Nonetheless, axiom A diffeomorphisms are sometimes called **hyperbolic diffeomorphisms**, because the portion of *M* where the interesting dynamics occurs, namely, *Ω*(*f*), exhibits hyperbolic behavior.

Axiom A diffeomorphisms generalize [Morse–Smale systems](wikipedia://en/23024252), which satisfy further restrictions (finitely many periodic points and transversality of stable and unstable submanifolds). [Smale horseshoe map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smale_horseshoe_map) is an axiom A diffeomorphism with infinitely many periodic points and positive [topological entropy](wikipedia://en/5463978).

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Any [Anosov diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/2320078) satisfies axiom A. In this case, the whole manifold *M* is hyperbolic (although it is an open question whether the non-wandering set *Ω*(*f*) constitutes the whole *M*).

[Rufus Bowen](wikipedia://en/24717250) showed that the non-wandering set *Ω*(*f*) of any axiom A diffeomorphism supports a [Markov partition](wikipedia://en/11491735).[^o3][^o9] Thus the restriction of *f* to a certain generic subset of *Ω*(*f*) is conjugated to a [shift of finite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shift_of_finite_type).

The density of the periodic points in the non-wandering set implies its local maximality: there exists an open neighborhood *U* of *Ω*(*f*) such that

$$\cap_{n\in \mathbb Z} f^{n} (U)=\Omega(f).$$

:::

## Omega stability {#Omega_stability}

An important property of Axiom A systems is their structural stability against small perturbations.[^o11] That is, trajectories of the perturbed system remain in 1-1 topological correspondence with the unperturbed system. This property is important, in that it shows that Axiom A systems are not exceptional, but are in a sense 'robust'.

More precisely, for every *C*<sup>1</sup>-[perturbation](wikipedia://en/102140) *f*<sub>*ε*</sub> of *f*, its non-wandering set is formed by two compact, *f*<sub>*ε*</sub>-invariant subsets *Ω*<sub>1</sub> and *Ω*<sub>2</sub>. The first subset is homeomorphic to *Ω*(*f*) via a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) *h* which conjugates the restriction of *f* to *Ω*(*f*) with the restriction of *f*<sub>*ε*</sub> to *Ω*<sub>1</sub>:

$$f_\epsilon\circ h(x)=h\circ f(x), \quad \forall x\in \Omega(f).$$

If *Ω*<sub>2</sub> is empty then *h* is onto *Ω*(*f*<sub>*ε*</sub>). If this is the case for every perturbation *f*<sub>*ε*</sub> then *f* is called **omega stable**. A diffeomorphism *f* is omega stable if and only if it satisfies axiom A and the **no-cycle condition** (that an orbit, once having left a transitive subset of *Ω*(*f*), does not return).
