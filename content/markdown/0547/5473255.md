# Lewy's example

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lewy's example"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) study of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564), **Lewy's example** is a celebrated example, due to [Hans Lewy](wikipedia://en/1752415), of a [linear partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564#Linear_equations) with no solutions. It shows that the analog of the [Cauchy–Kovalevskaya theorem](wikipedia://en/46230112) does not hold in the smooth category.

:::

The original example is not explicit, since it employs the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860), but there since have been various explicit examples of the same nature found by [Howard Jacobowitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_Jacobowitz).[^o2]

The [Malgrange–Ehrenpreis theorem](wikipedia://en/14160015) states (roughly) that linear partial differential equations with constant [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) always have at least one solution; Lewy's example shows that this result cannot be extended to linear partial differential equations with [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) coefficients.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="The example"}

## The example {#The_example}

The statement is as follows

On $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{C}$, there exists a [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) (i.e., $C^\infty$) [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $F(t, z)$ such that the [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309)

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bar{z}} - iz\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = F(t, z)$$

admits no solution on any [open set](wikipedia://en/39358). Note that if $F$ is [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478), then the [Cauchy–Kovalevskaya theorem](wikipedia://en/46230112) implies that there exists a solution.

Lewy constructs this $F$ using the following result:

On $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{C}$, suppose that $u(t, z)$ is a function satisfying, in a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29) of the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432),

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial\bar{z}} - iz\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \varphi'(t)$$

for some *C*<sup>1</sup> function *φ*. Then *φ* must be [real-analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-analytic) in a (possibly smaller) neighborhood of the origin.

This may be construed as a non-existence theorem by taking *φ* to be merely a smooth function. Lewy's example takes this latter equation and in a sense *translates* its non-solvability to every point of $\mathbb{R} \times \mathbb{C}$. The method of [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) uses a [Baire category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baire_category) argument, so in a certain precise sense almost all equations of this form are unsolvable.

(Mizohata 1962) later found that the even simpler equation

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + ix\frac{\partial u}{\partial y} = F(x, y)$$

depending on 2 [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) variables *x* and *y* sometimes has no solutions. This is almost the simplest possible [partial differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886#Several_variables_2) with non-constant coefficients.

:::

## Significance for CR manifolds {#Significance_for_CR_manifolds}

A [CR manifold](wikipedia://en/3131794) comes equipped with a [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404) of differential operators, formally similar to the [Dolbeault complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dolbeault_complex) on a [complex manifold](wikipedia://en/509742), called the $\scriptstyle\bar{\partial}_b$-complex. The Dolbeault complex admits a version of the [Poincaré lemma](wikipedia://en/600030). In the language of [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466), this means that the Dolbeault complex is exact. The Lewy example, however, shows that the $\scriptstyle\bar{\partial}_b$-complex is almost never exact.
