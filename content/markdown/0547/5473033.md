# Inhabited set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inhabited set"}

In mathematics, a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $A$ is **inhabited** if there exists an element $a \in A$.

:::

In [classical mathematics](wikipedia://en/55868), the property of being inhabited is equivalent to being non-[empty](wikipedia://en/9566). However, this equivalence is not valid in constructive or [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), and so this separate terminology is mostly used in the [set theory](wikipedia://en/5042360) of [constructive mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_mathematics).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In the formal language of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), set $A$ has the property of being inhabited if

$$\exists z (z \in A).$$

### Related definitions {#Related_definitions}

A set $A$ has the property of being empty if $\forall z (z \notin A)$, or equivalently $\neg\exists z (z \in A)$. Here $z \notin A$ stands for the negation $\neg (z \in A)$.

A set $A$ is non-empty if it is not empty, that is, if $\neg\forall z (z \notin A)$, or equivalently $\neg\neg\exists z (z \in A)$.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Theorems"}

## Theorems {#Theorems}

The [inference rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference_rule) for $\to$ imply $P\to((P\to Q)\to Q)$, and taking any a false proposition for $Q$ establishes that $P\to\neg\neg P$ is always valid. Hence, any inhabited set is provably also non-empty.

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

In constructive mathematics, the double-negation elimination principle is not automatically valid. In particular, an [existence statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existential_quantifier) is generally stronger than its double-negated form. The latter merely expresses that the existence cannot be ruled out, in the strong sense that it cannot consistently be negated. In a constructive reading, in order for $\exists z \phi(z)$ to hold for some [formula](wikipedia://en/404582) $\phi$, it is necessary for a specific value of $z$ satisfying $\phi$ to be constructed or known. Likewise, the negation of a universal quantified statement is in general weaker than an existential quantification of a negated statement. In turn, a set may be proven to be non-empty without one being able to prove it is inhabited.

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

Sets such as $\{2,3,4,7\}$ or $\mathbb Q$ are inhabited, as e.g. witnessed by $3\in\{2,3,4,7\}$. The set $\{\}$ is empty and thus not inhabited. Naturally, the example section thus focuses on non-empty sets that are not provably inhabited.

It is easy to give such examples by using the [axiom of separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_separation), as with it logical statements can always be translated to set-theoretical ones. For example, with a subset $S\subseteq\{0\}$ defined as $S:=\{n\in\{0\}\mid P\}$, the proposition $P$ may always equivalently be stated as $0\in S$. The double-negated existence claim of an entity with a certain property can be expressed by stating that the set of entities with that property is non-empty.

#### Example relating to excluded middle {#Example_relating_to_excluded_middle}

Define a subset $A\subseteq\{0,1\}$ via

$$A:=\{n\in\{0,1\}\mid (P\land n=0)\lor (\neg P\land n=1)\}$$

Clearly $P\leftrightarrow 0\in A$ and $(\neg P)\leftrightarrow 1\in A$, and from the [principle of non-contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_non-contradiction) one concludes $\neg (0 \in A\land 1\in A )$. Further, $(P\lor\neg P) \leftrightarrow (0\in A \lor 1\in A)$ and in turn

$$(P\lor\neg P)\to\exists!(n\in\{0,1\})\ n\in A$$

Already [minimal logic](wikipedia://en/26267678) proves $\neg\neg(P\lor\neg P)$, the double-negation for any excluded middle statement, which here is equivalent to $\neg (0 \notin A\land 1\notin A )$. So by performing two contrapositions on the previous implication, one establishes $\neg\neg\exists!(n\in\{0,1\})\ n\in A$. In words: It *cannot consistently be ruled out* that exactly one of the numbers $0$ and $1$ inhabits $A$. In particular, the latter can be weakened to $\neg\neg\exists n\ n\in A$, saying $A$ is proven non-empty.

As example statements for $P$, consider the infamous provenly theory-independent statements such as the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705), [consistency](wikipedia://en/58863) of the sound theory at hand, or, informally, an unknowable claim about the past or future. By design, these are chosen to be unprovable. A variant of this is to consider mathematical propositions that are merely not yet established—see also [Brouwerian counterexamples](wikipedia://en/360113#Brouwerian_counterexamples). Knowledge of the validity of either $0\in A$ or $1\in A$ is equivalent to knowledge about $P$ as above, and cannot be obtained. Given neither $P$ nor $\neg P$ can be proven in the theory, it will also not prove $A$ to be inhabited by some particular number. Further, a constructive framework with the [disjunction property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction_property) then cannot prove $P\lor\neg P$ either. There is neither evidence $0\in A$ nor for $1\in A$, and constructive unprovability of their disjunction reflects this. Nonetheless, since ruling out excluded middle is provenly always inconsistent, it is also established that $A$ is not empty. Classical logic adopts $P\lor\neg P$ axiomatically, spoiling a constructive reading.

#### Example relating to choice {#Example_relating_to_choice}

There are various easily characterized sets the existence of which are not provable in [${\mathsf {ZF}}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo-Fraenkel_set_theory), but which are implied to exist by the full [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) ${\mathrm{AC}}$. As such, that axiom is itself [independent](wikipedia://en/691790) of ${\mathsf {ZF}}$. It in fact contradicts other potential axioms for a set theory. Further, it indeed also [contradicts constructive principles](wikipedia://en/18288107), in a set-theory context. A theory that does not permit excluded middle does also not validate the function existence principle ${\mathrm{AC}}$.

In ${\mathsf {ZF}}$, the ${\mathrm{AC}}$ [is equivalent](wikipedia://en/840#Equivalents) to the statement that every [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) has a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_space_basis). So more concretely, consider the question of existence of a [Hamel basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_basis) of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) over the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). This object is elusive in the sense that there are different ${\mathsf {ZF}}$ [models](wikipedia://en/19858) that either negate and validate its existence. So it is also consistent to just postulate that existence cannot be ruled out here, in the sense that it cannot consistently be negated. Again, that postulate may be expressed as saying that the set of such Hamel bases is non-empty. Over a constructive theory, such a postulate is weaker than the plain existence postulate, but (by design) is still strong enough to then negate all propositions that would imply the non-existence of a Hamel basis.

:::

### Model theory {#Model_theory}

Because inhabited sets are the same as nonempty sets in classical logic, it is not possible to produce a [model](wikipedia://en/4055928) in the classical sense that contains a nonempty set $X$ but does not satisfy "$X$ is inhabited".

However, it is possible to construct a first-order [Kripke model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kripke_model) $M$ that differentiates between the two notions. Since an implication is true in every Kripke model if and only if it is provable in intuitionistic logic, this indeed establishes that one cannot intuitionistically prove that "$X$ is nonempty" implies "$X$ is inhabited".
