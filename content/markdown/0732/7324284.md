# Indexed language

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Indexed language"}

**Indexed languages** are a class of [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939) discovered by [Alfred Aho](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Aho);[^o2] they are described by [indexed grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indexed_grammar) and can be recognized by [nested stack automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nested_stack_automata).[^o3]

:::

Indexed languages are a [proper subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subset) of [context-sensitive languages](wikipedia://en/6212).[^o2] They qualify as an [abstract family of languages](wikipedia://en/11148549) (furthermore a full AFL) and hence satisfy many closure properties. However, they are not closed under intersection or complement.[^o2]

The class of indexed languages has practical importance in [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing) as a computationally affordable generalization of [context-free languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Context-free_languages), since [indexed grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indexed_grammar) can describe many of the nonlocal constraints occurring in natural languages.

[Gerald Gazdar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Gazdar) (1988)[^o4] and Vijayashanker (1987)[^o5] introduced a [mildly context-sensitive language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mildly_context-sensitive_language) class now known as linear indexed grammars (LIG).[^o6] Linear indexed grammars have additional restrictions relative to indexed grammars. LIGs are [weakly equivalent](wikipedia://en/34615237) (generate the same language class) as [tree adjoining grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_adjoining_grammars).[^o7]

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The following languages are indexed, but are not context-free:

$\{a^n b^n c^n d^n| n \geq 1 \}$ [^o4]

$\{a^n b^m c^n d^m | m,n \geq 0 \}$ [^o3]

These two languages are also indexed, but are not even mildly context sensitive under Gazdar's characterization:

$\{a^{2^{n}} | n \geq 0 \}$ [^o3]

$\{www | w \in \{a,b\}^+ \}$ [^o4]

On the other hand, the following language is not indexed:[^o10]

$$\{(a b^n)^n | n \geq 0 \}$$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

[Hopcroft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Hopcroft) and [Ullman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeffrey_Ullman) tend to consider indexed languages as a "natural" class, since they are generated by several formalisms, such as:

- Aho's [indexed grammars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indexed_grammar)[^o2]
- Aho's one-way [nested stack automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nested_stack_automata)[^o14]
- [Fischer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_J._Fischer)'s macro grammars[^o15]
- [Greibach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheila_Greibach)'s automata with stacks of stacks[^o16]
- [Maibaum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Maibaum)'s algebraic characterization[^o17]

Hayashi[^o18] generalized the [pumping lemma](wikipedia://en/3193758) to indexed grammars. Conversely, Gilman[^o10] gives a "shrinking lemma" for indexed languages.
