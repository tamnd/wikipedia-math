# Alfred J. Lotka

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Alfred J. Lotka"}

**Alfred James Lotka** (March 2, 1880 – December 5, 1949) was a Polish-American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), [physical chemist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemist), and [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661), famous for his work in [population dynamics](wikipedia://en/501466) and [energetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_flow_%28ecology%29). A [biophysicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysicist), Lotka is best known for his proposal of the [predator–prey model](wikipedia://en/462534), developed simultaneously but independently of [Vito Volterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vito_Volterra). The [Lotka–Volterra model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka–Volterra_equation) is still the basis of many models used in the analysis of [population dynamics](wikipedia://en/501466) in [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology).

:::

## Life {#Life}

Lotka was born in [Lwów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lwów), Austria-Hungary (now Lviv, Ukraine)[^o4] to Polish-American parents.[^o5] His parents, Jacques and Marie (Doebely) Lotka, were [US](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) nationals.[^o6] He gained his B.Sc. in 1901 at the [University of Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Birmingham), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England),[^o4] he did graduate work in 1901–02 at [Leipzig University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leipzig_University),[^o4] received an M.A. in 1909 at [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University)[^o4] and a D.Sc. at [Birmingham University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_University) in 1912.[^o4]

**Occupations**

- Assistant chemist for [General Chemical Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Chemical_Company) (1902–1908, 1914–1919)[^o4]
- Patent examiner for [US Patent Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/US_Patent_Office) (1909)[^o4]
- Assistant physicist for [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) (1909–1911)[^o4]
- Editor of the *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American) Supplement* (1911–1914)[^o4]
- Staff member at [Johns Hopkins University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johns_Hopkins_University) (1922–1924)[^o4]
- Statistician for the [Metropolitan Life Insurance Company](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Life_Insurance_Company), [New York City](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_York_City) (1924 until his retirement in 1948)[^o4]

In 1935, he married Romola Beattie. They had no children.

He died in Red Bank, New Jersey.[^o7]

## Work {#Work}

*Image: LotkaVolterra.svg.* The [Lotka–Volterra equations](wikipedia://en/462534) predict linked oscillations in populations of [predator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predator) and prey.

Although he is today known mainly for the Lotka–Volterra equations used in [ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecology), Lotka was a bio-mathematician and a bio-statistician, who sought to apply the principles of the physical sciences to biological sciences as well. His main interest was [demography](wikipedia://en/45642), which possibly influenced his professional choice as a [statistician](wikipedia://en/48661) at [Metropolitan Life Insurance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metropolitan_Life_Insurance_Company).

One of Lotka's earliest publications, in 1912, proposed a solution to [Ronald Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_Ross)'s second [malaria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malaria) model. In 1923, he published a thorough five-part analysis and extension of both Ross's malaria models. The fourth part in the series, co-authored by [F. R. Sharpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._R._Sharpe), modeled the time lag for pathogen incubation. Lotka published *Elements of Physical Biology* in 1925, one of the first books on [mathematical biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_biology) after [D'Arcy Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Arcy_Thompson)'s *[On Growth and Form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Growth_and_Form)*. He is also known for his [energetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_flow_%28ecology%29) perspective on [evolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evolution). Lotka proposed that [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection) was, at its root, a struggle among organisms for available energy; [Lotka's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka's_principle) states that organisms that survive and prosper are those that capture and use [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy) more efficiently than their competitors. Lotka extended his energetics framework to human society. In particular, he suggested that the shift in reliance from [solar energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_energy) to nonrenewable energy would pose unique and fundamental challenges to society. These theories made Lotka an important forerunner to the development of [biophysical economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biophysical_economics) and [ecological economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecological_economics), advanced by [Frederick Soddy](wikipedia://en/11778), [Howard Odum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_T._Odum) and others.[^o9]

### Elements of physical biology {#Elements_of_physical_biology}

While at Johns Hopkins, Lotka completed his book *Elements of Physical Biology* (1925), in which he extended the work of [Pierre François Verhulst](wikipedia://en/491402). His first book summarizes his previous work and organizes his ideas of unity and universality of physical laws, making his works accessible to other scientists. Although the book covered a large number of topics, from energetics of evolution (see below) to the physical nature of consciousness, the author is primarily known today for the [Lotka–Volterra equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka–Volterra_equation) of population dynamics.

### Energetics of evolution {#Energetics_of_evolution}

His earlier work was centered on energetics and applications of [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952) in [life sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_sciences).

Lotka proposed the theory that the [Darwinian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darwinian) concept of [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection) could be quantified as a physical law. The law that he proposed was that the selective principle of evolution was one which favoured the maximum useful energy flow transformation. The general systems ecologist [Howard T. Odum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_T._Odum) later applied Lotka's proposal as a central guiding feature of his work in [ecosystem ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecosystem_ecology). Odum called Lotka's law the [maximum power principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_power_principle).

### Demography and public health {#Demography_and_public_health}

Lotka's work in mathematical demography began in 1907 with the publication of articles in the journal *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_%28journal%29)* and *[American Journal of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Journal_of_Science)*. He published several dozen articles on the subject over more than two decades, culminating with *Théorie Analytique des Associations Biologiques* (Analytical Theory of Biological Associations). The 45-page Part 1, titled *Principes*, was published in 1934; the 149-page Part 2, titled *Analyse demographique avec application particuliere a l'espece humaine*, was published in 1939; both by [Hermann & Cie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Éditions_Hermann), Paris.

### Bibliometrics {#Bibliometrics}

Within the field of bibliometrics, particularly that part devoted to studying scientific publications, Lotka is noted for contributing "[Lotka's law](wikipedia://en/1313004)". The law, which Lotka discovered, relates to the productivity of scientists. As noted by W. G. Poitier in 1981: "The Lotka distribution is based on an inverse square law where the number of authors writing *n* papers is 1/*n*<sup>2</sup> of the number of authors writing one paper. Each subject area can have associated with it an exponent representing its specific rate of author productivity." Lotka's work sparked additional inquiries, eventually seminally contributing to the field of [scientometrics](wikipedia://en/1235963)—the scientific study of scientific publications.

He teamed up with [Louis Israel Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Israel_Dublin), another statistician at Metropolitan Life, to write three books on demography and [public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health): *The Money Value of a Man* (1930), *Length of Life* (1936), and *Twenty-five Years of Health Progress* (1937).
