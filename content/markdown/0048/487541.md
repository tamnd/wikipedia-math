# Matrix ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Matrix ring"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **matrix ring** is a set of [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) with entries in a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R* that form a ring under [matrix addition](wikipedia://en/125276) and [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280).[^n1] The set of all *n* × *n* matrices with entries in *R* is a matrix ring denoted M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*)[^n2][^n3][^n4][^n5] (alternative notations: Mat<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*)[^n3] and *R*<sup>*n*×*n*</sup>[^n6]). Some sets of infinite matrices form **infinite matrix rings**. A subring of a matrix ring is again a matrix ring. Over a [rng](wikipedia://en/3405004), one can form matrix rngs.

:::

When *R* is a commutative ring, the matrix ring M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) is an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) over *R*, and may be called a **matrix algebra**. In this setting, if *M* is a matrix and *r* is in *R*, then the matrix *rM* is the matrix *M* with each of its entries multiplied by *r*.

::: {#o10 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The set of all *n* × *n* [square matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_matrices) over *R*, denoted M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*). This is sometimes called the "full ring of *n*-by-*n* matrices".
- The set of all upper [triangular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_matrices) over *R*.
- The set of all lower [triangular matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_matrices) over *R*.
- The set of all [diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_matrices) over *R*. This [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism) to the [direct product](wikipedia://en/25063) of *n* copies of *R*.
- For *G* any [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634), the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) $R[G]$
- For any index set *I*, the ring of endomorphisms of the right *R*-module $M=\bigoplus_{i\in I}R$ is isomorphic to the ring $\mathbb{CFM}_I(R)$ of **column finite matrices** whose entries are indexed by *I* × *I* and whose columns each contain only finitely many nonzero entries. The ring of endomorphisms of *M* considered as a left *R*-module is isomorphic to the ring $\mathbb{RFM}_I(R)$ of **row finite matrices**.
- If *R* is a [Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665), then the condition of row or column finiteness in the previous point can be relaxed. With the norm in place, [absolutely convergent series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_convergent_series) can be used instead of finite sums. For example, the matrices whose column sums are absolutely convergent sequences form a ring. Analogously of course, the matrices whose row sums are absolutely convergent series also form a ring. This idea can be used to represent [operators on Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932#Operators_on_Hilbert_spaces), for example.
- The intersection of the row-finite and column-finite matrix rings forms a ring $\mathbb{RCFM}_I(R)$.
- If *R* is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346), then M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) has a structure of a [\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/293426) over *R*, where the [involution](wikipedia://en/293450#Ring_theory) \* on M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) is [matrix transposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_transpose).
- If *A* is a [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184), then M<sub>*n*</sub>(*A*) is another C\*-algebra. If *A* is non-unital, then M<sub>*n*</sub>(*A*) is also non-unital. By the [Gelfand–Naimark theorem](wikipedia://en/315414), there exists a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) *H* and an isometric \*-isomorphism from *A* to a norm-closed subalgebra of the algebra *B*(*H*) of continuous operators; this identifies M<sub>*n*</sub>(*A*) with a subalgebra of *B*(*H*<sup>⊕*n*</sup>). For simplicity, if we further suppose that *H* is separable and *A* $\subseteq$ *B*(*H*) is a unital C\*-algebra, we can break up *A* into a matrix ring over a smaller C\*-algebra. One can do so by fixing a [projection](wikipedia://en/519182#Orthogonal_projections) *p* and hence its orthogonal projection 1 − *p*; one can identify *A* with $\begin{pmatrix} pAp & pA(1-p) \\ (1-p)Ap & (1-p)A(1-p) \end{pmatrix}$, where matrix multiplication works as intended because of the orthogonality of the projections. In order to identify *A* with a matrix ring over a C\*-algebra, we require that *p* and 1 − *p* have the same "rank"; more precisely, we need that *p* and 1 − *p* are Murray–von Neumann equivalent, i.e., there exists a [partial isometry](wikipedia://en/1348079) *u* such that *p* = *uu*\* and 1 − *p* = *u*\**u*. One can easily generalize this to matrices of larger sizes.
- Complex matrix algebras M<sub>*n*</sub>(**C**) are, up to isomorphism, the only finite-dimensional simple associative algebras over the field **C** of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). Prior to the invention of matrix algebras, [Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) in 1853 introduced a ring, whose elements he called [biquaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biquaternions)[^o11] and modern authors would call tensors in **C** ⊗<sub>**R**</sub> **H**, that was later shown to be isomorphic to M<sub>2</sub>(**C**). One [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of M<sub>2</sub>(**C**) consists of the four matrix units (matrices with one 1 and all other entries 0); another basis is given by the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) and the three [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868).
- A matrix ring over a field is a [Frobenius algebra](wikipedia://en/1814652), with Frobenius form given by the trace of the product: *σ*(*A*, *B*) = tr(*AB*).

:::

## Structure {#Structure}

- The matrix ring M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) can be identified with the [ring of endomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_endomorphisms) of the [free right *R*-module](wikipedia://en/326454) of rank *n*; that is, M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) ≅ End<sub>*R*</sub>(*R*<sup>*n*</sup>). [Matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) corresponds to composition of endomorphisms.
- The ring M<sub>*n*</sub>(*D*) over a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) *D* is an [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273) [simple ring](wikipedia://en/443387), a special type of [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring). The rings $\mathbb{CFM}_I(D)$ and $\mathbb{RFM}_I(D)$ are *not* simple and not Artinian if the set *I* is infinite, but they are still [full linear rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_linear_ring).
- The [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem) states that every semisimple ring is isomorphic to a finite [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) $\prod_{i=1}^r \operatorname{M}_{n_i}(D_i)$, for some nonnegative integer *r*, positive integers *n*<sub>*i*</sub>, and division rings *D*<sub>*i*</sub>.
- When we view M<sub>*n*</sub>(**C**) as the ring of linear endomorphisms of **C**<sup>*n*</sup>, those matrices which vanish on a given subspace *V* form a [left ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_ideal). Conversely, for a given left ideal *I* of M<sub>*n*</sub>(**C**) the intersection of [null spaces](wikipedia://en/1072915) of all matrices in *I* gives a subspace of **C**<sup>*n*</sup>. Under this construction, the left ideals of M<sub>*n*</sub>(**C**) are in bijection with the subspaces of **C**<sup>*n*</sup>.
- There is a bijection between the two-sided [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) and the two-sided ideals of *R*. Namely, for each ideal *I* of *R*, the set of all *n* × *n* matrices with entries in *I* is an ideal of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*), and each ideal of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) arises in this way. This implies that M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) is [simple](wikipedia://en/443387) if and only if *R* is simple. For *n* ≥ 2, not every left ideal or right ideal of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) arises by the previous construction from a left ideal or a right ideal in *R*. For example, the set of matrices whose columns with indices 2 through *n* are all zero forms a left ideal in M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*).
- The previous ideal correspondence actually arises from the fact that the rings *R* and M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) are [Morita equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morita_equivalent). Roughly speaking, this means that the category of left *R*-modules and the category of left M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*)-modules are very similar. Because of this, there is a natural bijective correspondence between the *isomorphism classes* of left *R*-modules and left M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*)-modules, and between the isomorphism classes of left ideals of *R* and left ideals of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*). Identical statements hold for right modules and right ideals. Through Morita equivalence, M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) inherits any [Morita-invariant](wikipedia://en/3296134) properties of *R*, such as being [simple](wikipedia://en/443387), [Artinian](wikipedia://en/489273), [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129), [prime](wikipedia://en/487540).

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- If *S* is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of *R*, then M<sub>*n*</sub>(*S*) is a subring of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*). For example, M<sub>*n*</sub>(**Z**) is a subring of M<sub>*n*</sub>(**Q**).
- The matrix ring M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) if and only if *n* = 0, *R* = 0, or *R* is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) and *n* = 1. In fact, this is true also for the subring of upper triangular matrices. Here is an example showing two upper triangular 2 × 2 matrices that do not commute, assuming 1 ≠ 0 in *R*:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

and

$$\begin{bmatrix} 1 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

- For *n* ≥ 2, the matrix ring M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) over a [nonzero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) has [zero divisors](wikipedia://en/51441) and [nilpotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_element); the same holds for the ring of upper triangular matrices. An example in 2 × 2 matrices would be

$$\begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 1 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & 0 \\ 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix}.$$

- The [center](wikipedia://en/53796787) of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) consists of the scalar multiples of the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718), *I*<sub>*n*</sub>, in which the scalar belongs to the center of *R*.
- The [unit group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_group) of M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*), consisting of the invertible matrices under multiplication, is denoted GL<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*).
- If *F* is a field, then for any two matrices *A* and *B* in M<sub>*n*</sub>(*F*), the equality *AB* = *I*<sub>*n*</sub> implies *BA* = *I*<sub>*n*</sub>. This is not true for every ring *R* though. A ring *R* whose matrix rings all have the mentioned property is known as a [stably finite ring](wikipedia://en/26021350) (Lam 1999, 5).

:::

## Matrix semiring {#Matrix_semiring}

In fact, *R* needs to be only a [semiring](wikipedia://en/371468) for M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) to be defined. In this case, M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) is a semiring, called the **matrix semiring**. Similarly, if *R* is a commutative semiring, then M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) is a **matrix semialgebra**.

For example, if *R* is the [Boolean semiring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_semiring) (the [two-element Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/2584449) *R* = $\{0, 1\}$ with 1 + 1 = 1),[^n7] then M<sub>*n*</sub>(*R*) is the semiring of [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931) on an *n*-element set with union as addition, [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170) as multiplication, the [empty relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_relation) ([zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888)) as the zero, and the [identity relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_relation) ([identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718)) as the [unity](wikipedia://en/14962).[^n8]
