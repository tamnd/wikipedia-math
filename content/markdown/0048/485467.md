# Hénon map

*Image: HenonMap.svg.* Hénon attractor for $a = 1.4$ and $b = 0.3$

*Image: Henon Multifractal Map movie.gif.* Hénon attractor for $a = 1.4$ and $b = 0.3$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hénon map"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hénon map** is a [discrete-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time) [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087). It is one of the most studied examples of dynamical systems that exhibit [chaotic behavior](wikipedia://en/6295). The Hénon map takes a point $(x_{n}, y_{n})$ in the plane and maps it to a new point:

$$\begin{cases}x_{n+1} = 1 - a x_n^2 + y_n\\y_{n+1} = b x_n\end{cases}$$

The map depends on two parameters, $a$ and $b$, which for the **classical Hénon map** have values of $a = 1.4$ and $b = 0.3$.[^o2] For the classical values, the Hénon map is chaotic. For other values of $a$ and $b$, the map may be chaotic, [intermittent](wikipedia://en/4662960), or converge to a [periodic orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit). An overview of the map's behavior at different parameter values can be seen in its [orbit diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_diagram).

:::

The map was introduced by [Michel Hénon](wikipedia://en/958653) as a simplified model for the [Poincaré section](wikipedia://en/876732) of the [Lorenz system](wikipedia://en/5642583).[^o2] For the classical map, an initial point in the plane will either approach a set of points known as the Hénon [strange attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_attractor), or it will diverge to infinity. The Hénon attractor is a [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913), smooth in one direction and a [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) in another.[^o2] Numerical estimates for the [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) of the strange attractor for the classical map yield a [correlation dimension](wikipedia://en/2075246) of 1.21 ± 0.01[^o3] and a [box-counting dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Box-counting_dimension) of 1.261 ± 0.003.[^o4]

## Dynamics {#Dynamics}

### The Attractor {#The_Attractor}

The Hénon map is a two-dimensional [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) with a constant [Jacobian determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_determinant). The Jacobian matrix of the map is:

$$J = \begin{bmatrix} -2ax & 1 \\ b & 0 \end{bmatrix}$$

The determinant of this matrix is $det(J) = -b$. Because the map is dissipative (i.e., volumes shrink under iteration), the determinant must be between -1 and 1. The Hénon map is dissipative for $|b| < 1$.[^o7] For the classical parameters $a = 1.4, b = 0.3$, the determinant is -0.3, so the map contracts areas at a constant rate. Every iteration shrinks areas by a factor of 0.3.

This contraction, combined with a stretching and folding action, creates the characteristic fractal structure of the Hénon attractor. For the classical parameters, most initial conditions lead to trajectories that outline this boomerang-like shape. The attractor contains an infinite number of unstable periodic orbits, which are fundamental to its structure.[^o8]

### Fixed points {#Fixed_points}

The map has two [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738), which remain unchanged by the mapping. These are found by solving $x = 1 - ax^{2} + y$ and $y = bx$. Substituting the second equation into the first gives the quadratic equation:

$$ax^2 + (1-b)x - 1 = 0$$

The solutions (the x-coordinates of the fixed points) are:

$$x = \frac{-(1-b) \pm \sqrt{(1-b)^2 + 4a}}{2a}$$

For the classical parameters $a = 1.4$ and $b = 0.3$, the two fixed points are:

$$x_1 \approx 0.631, \quad y_1 \approx 0.189$$

$$x_2 \approx -1.131, \quad y_2 \approx -0.339$$

The stability of these points is determined by the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues) of the Jacobian matrix $J$ evaluated at the fixed points. For the classical map, the first fixed point is a [saddle point](wikipedia://en/640249) (unstable), while the second fixed point is a repeller (also unstable).[^o10] The [unstable manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unstable_manifold) of the first fixed point is a key component that generates the strange attractor itself.[^o10]

*Image: Henon bifurcation map b=0.3.png.* [Orbit diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_diagram) for the Hénon map, keeping $b = 0.3$ and varying $a$. The plot shows the classic [period-doubling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period-doubling) route to chaos.

### Bifurcation diagram {#Bifurcation_diagram}

The Hénon map exhibits complex behavior as its parameters are varied. A common way to visualize this is with a [bifurcation diagram](wikipedia://en/79173). If $b$ is held constant (e.g., at 0.3) and $a$ is varied, the map transitions from regular (periodic) to chaotic behavior. This transition occurs through a [period-doubling cascade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period-doubling_cascade), similar to that of the [logistic map](wikipedia://en/18137).[^o7]

*Image: Hénon 3D Map.png.* A 3D view of the bifurcation diagram, varying parameter *b* along the new axis. This reveals the "boomerang" shape of the parameter space region where the attractor exists.

For small values of $a$, the system converges to a single stable fixed point. As $a$ increases, this point becomes unstable and splits into a stable 2-cycle. This cycle then becomes unstable and splits into a 4-cycle, then an 8-cycle, and so on, until a critical value of $a$ is reached where the system becomes fully chaotic. Within the chaotic region, there are also "windows" of periodicity where stable orbits reappear for certain ranges of $a$.[^o10]

### Koopman operator analysis {#Koopman_operator_analysis}

*Image: Koopman mode henon lambda 1.png.* An approximate Koopman mode of the Hénon map. The level sets of this mode reveal the complex geometry of the dynamics. The dark blue region outlines the [basin of attraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basin_of_attraction) for the strange attractor, while the intricate structures within correspond to the attractor's [stable manifold](wikipedia://en/5466649).

An alternative way to analyze dynamical systems like the Hénon map is through the [Koopman operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koopman_operator) method. This approach offers a linear perspective on nonlinear dynamics. Instead of studying the evolution of individual points in phase space, one considers the action of the system on a space of "observable" functions, $g(x, y)$. The Koopman operator, $U$, is a linear operator that maps an observable $g$ to its value at the next time step:

$$(Ug)(\mathbf{x}_n) = g(\mathbf{x}_{n+1}) = g(1-ax_n^2+y_n, bx_n)$$

While the operator $U$ is linear, it acts on an infinite-dimensional function space. The key to the analysis is to find the [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) $\varphi_{k}$ and [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) $\lambda_{k}$ of this operator, which satisfy $U\varphi_{k} = \lambda_{k}\varphi_{k}$. These eigenfunctions, also known as **Koopman modes**, and their corresponding eigenvalues contain significant information about the system's dynamics.[^o13]

For chaotic systems like the Hénon map, the eigenfunctions are typically complex, fractal-like functions. They cannot be found analytically and must be computed numerically, often using methods like [Dynamic Mode Decomposition (DMD)](wikipedia://en/19858814).[^o14] The level sets of the Koopman modes can reveal the invariant structures of the system, such as the stable and unstable manifolds and the basin of attraction, providing a global picture of the dynamics.[^o15]

## Decomposition {#Decomposition}

*Image: Henon map.gif.* The classical Hénon map after 15 iterations, showing the stretching and folding action described by the decomposition.

The Hénon map can be decomposed into a sequence of three simpler geometric transformations. This helps to understand how the map stretches, squeezes, and folds phase space.[^o2] The map $T(x, y) = (1 - ax^{2} + y, bx)$ can be seen as the composition $T = R \circ C \circ B$ of three functions:

1. **Bending**: An area-preserving nonlinear bend in the $y$ direction:

$$(x_1, y_1) = (x, 1 - ax^2 + y)$$

1. **Contraction**: A contraction in the $x$ direction:

$$(x_2, y_2) = (bx_1, y_1)$$

1. **Reflection**: A reflection across the line $y = x$:

$$(x_3, y_3) = (y_2, x_2)$$

The final point is $(x_{n+1}, y_{n+1}) = (x_{3}, y_{3})$. This decomposition separates the area-preserving folding action (step 1) from the dissipative contraction (step 2).[^o7]

## History {#History}

In 1976, the physicist [Yves Pomeau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yves_Pomeau) and his collaborator Jean-Luc Ibanez undertook a numerical study of the [Lorenz system](wikipedia://en/5642583). By analyzing the system using [Poincaré sections](wikipedia://en/876732), they observed the characteristic stretching and folding of the attractor, which was a hallmark of the work on strange attractors by [David Ruelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Ruelle).[^o18] Their physical, experimental approach to the Lorenz system led to two key insights. First, they identified a transition where the system switches from a strange attractor to a [limit cycle](wikipedia://en/452950) at a critical parameter value. This phenomenon would later be explained by Pomeau and Paul Manneville as the "scenario" of [intermittency](wikipedia://en/4662960).[^o19]

Second, Pomeau and Ibanez suggested that the complex dynamics of the three-dimensional, continuous Lorenz system could be understood by studying a much simpler, two-dimensional discrete map that possessed similar characteristics.[^o2] In January 1976, Pomeau presented this idea at a seminar at the [Côte d'Azur Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Côte_d'Azur_Observatory). [Michel Hénon](wikipedia://en/958653), an astronomer at the observatory, was in attendance. Intrigued by the suggestion, Hénon began a systematic search for the simplest possible map that would exhibit a strange attractor. He arrived at the now-famous quadratic map, publishing his findings in the seminal paper, "A two-dimensional mapping with a strange attractor."[^o2][^o20]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### 3D Hénon map {#3D_Hénon_map}

A 3-D generalization for the Hénon map was proposed by Hitzl and Zele:[^o24]

$$\mathbf{s}(n+1)= \begin{bmatrix} s_1(n+1) \\ s_2(n+1) \\ s_3(n+1) \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 - \alpha s_1^2(n) + s_3(n) \\ -\beta s_1(n) \\ \beta s_1(n) + s_2(n) \end{bmatrix}$$

For certain parameters (e.g., $\alpha=1.07$ and $\beta=0.3$), this map generates a chaotic attractor.[^o24]

### Four-dimensional extension {#Four-dimensional_extension}

*Image: Hénon 4D.webm.* Hénon map in 4D. The range for *b* is -1.5 to 0.5 and for *a* it is -2.3 to 1.0. The video shows 3D cross-sections where the points do not diverge to infinity.

The Hénon map can be plotted in [four-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/1364622) by treating its parameters, *a* and *b*, as additional axes. This allows for a visualization of the map's behavior across the entire parameter space. One way to visualize this 4D structure is to render a series of 3D slices, where each slice represents a fixed value of one parameter (e.g., *a*) while the other three (*x*, *y*, *b*) are displayed. The fourth parameter is then varied as a time variable, creating a video of the evolving 3D structure.

### Filtered Hénon map {#Filtered_Hénon_map}

Other generalizations involve introducing feedback loops with [digital filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_filter) to create complex, band-limited chaotic signals.[^o27][^o28]
