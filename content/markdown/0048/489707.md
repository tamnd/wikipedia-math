# Hasse principle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hasse principle"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [Helmut Hasse](wikipedia://en/727118)'s **local–global principle**, also known as the **Hasse principle**, is the idea that one can find an [integer solution to an equation](wikipedia://en/9109) by using the [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) to piece together solutions [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) powers of each different [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666). This is handled by examining the equation in the [completions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28ring_theory%29) of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024): the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) and the [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423). A more formal version of the Hasse principle states that certain types of equations have a rational solution [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) they have a solution in the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) *and* in the *p*-adic numbers for each prime *p*.

:::

## Intuition {#Intuition}

Given a polynomial equation with rational coefficients, if it has a rational solution, then this also yields a real solution and a *p*-adic solution, as the rationals embed in the reals and *p*-adics: a global solution yields local solutions at each prime. The Hasse principle asks when the reverse can be done, or rather, asks what the obstruction is: when can you patch together solutions over the reals and *p*-adics to yield a solution over the rationals: when can local solutions be joined to form a global solution?

One can ask this for other [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29) or [fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28algebra%29): integers, for instance, or [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field). For number fields, rather than reals and *p*-adics, one uses complex embeddings and $\mathfrak p$-adics, for [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) $\mathfrak p$ in the field's [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613).

## Forms representing 0 {#Forms_representing_0}

### Quadratic forms {#Quadratic_forms}

The [Hasse–Minkowski theorem](wikipedia://en/1109126) states that the local–global principle holds for the problem of [representing 0](wikipedia://en/10917170) by [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478) over the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) (which is [Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537)'s result); and more generally over any [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) (as proved by Hasse), when one uses all the appropriate [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) necessary conditions. [Hasse's theorem on cyclic extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasse's_theorem_on_cyclic_extensions) states that the local–global principle applies to the condition of being a relative norm for a [cyclic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_extension) of number fields.

### Cubic forms {#Cubic_forms}

A counterexample by [Ernst S. Selmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_S._Selmer) shows that the Hasse–Minkowski theorem cannot be extended to forms of degree 3: The cubic equation 3*x*<sup>3</sup> + 4*y*<sup>3</sup> + 5*z*<sup>3</sup> = 0 has a solution in real numbers, and in all p-adic fields, but it has no nontrivial solution in which *x*, *y*, and *z* are all rational numbers.[^o6]

[Roger Heath-Brown](wikipedia://en/2984550) showed[^o7] that every cubic form over the integers in at least 14 variables represents 0, improving on earlier results of [Davenport](wikipedia://en/991111).[^o8] Since every cubic form over the p-adic numbers with at least ten variables represents 0,[^o7] the local–global principle holds trivially for cubic forms over the rationals in at least 14 variables.

Restricting to non-singular forms, one can do better than this: Heath-Brown proved that every non-singular cubic form over the rational numbers in at least 10 variables represents 0,[^o9] thus trivially establishing the Hasse principle for this class of forms. It is known that Heath-Brown's result is best possible in the sense that there exist non-singular cubic forms over the rationals in 9 variables that do not represent zero.[^o10] However, [Hooley](wikipedia://en/5380265) showed that the Hasse principle holds for the representation of 0 by non-singular cubic forms over the rational numbers in at least nine variables.[^o11] Davenport, Heath-Brown and Hooley all used the [Hardy–Littlewood circle method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Littlewood_circle_method) in their proofs. According to an idea of [Manin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yuri_Ivanovitch_Manin), the obstructions to the Hasse principle holding for cubic forms can be tied into the theory of the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078); this is the [Brauer–Manin obstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brauer–Manin_obstruction), which accounts completely for the failure of the Hasse principle for some classes of variety. However, [Skorobogatov](wikipedia://en/25642036) has shown that the Brauer–Manin obstruction cannot explain all the failures of the Hasse principle.[^o12]

### Forms of higher degree {#Forms_of_higher_degree}

Counterexamples by [Fujiwara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masahiko_Fujiwara) and [Sudo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masaki_Sudo) show that the Hasse–Minkowski theorem is not extensible to forms of degree 10*n* + 5, where *n* is a non-negative integer.[^o14]

On the other hand, [Birch's theorem](wikipedia://en/5147723) shows that if *d* is any odd natural number, then there is a number *N*(*d*) such that any form of degree *d* in more than *N*(*d*) variables represents 0: the Hasse principle holds trivially.

## Albert–Brauer–Hasse–Noether theorem {#Albert–Brauer–Hasse–Noether_theorem}

The [Albert–Brauer–Hasse–Noether theorem](wikipedia://en/23502488) establishes a local–global principle for the splitting of a [central simple algebra](wikipedia://en/404082) *A* over an algebraic number field *K*. It states that if *A* splits over every [completion](wikipedia://en/145375) *K*<sub>*v*</sub> then it is isomorphic to a [matrix algebra](wikipedia://en/487541) over *K*.

:::

## Hasse principle for algebraic groups {#Hasse_principle_for_algebraic_groups}

The Hasse principle for [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924) states that if *G* is a [simply-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply-connected) algebraic group defined over the [global field](wikipedia://en/288358) *k* then the map

$$H^1(k,G)\rightarrow\prod_s H^1(k_s,G)$$

of [Galois cohomology](wikipedia://en/1111315) groups is injective, where the product is over all places *s* of *k*.

The Hasse principle for [orthogonal groups](wikipedia://en/173954) is closely related to the Hasse principle for the corresponding quadratic forms.

(Kneser 1966) and several others verified the Hasse principle by case-by-case proofs for each group. The last case was the group [*E*<sub>8</sub>](wikipedia://en/647994) which was only completed by (Chernousov 1989) many years after the other cases.

The Hasse principle for algebraic groups was used in the proofs of the [Weil conjecture for Tamagawa numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weil_conjecture_for_Tamagawa_numbers) and the [strong approximation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_approximation_theorem).
