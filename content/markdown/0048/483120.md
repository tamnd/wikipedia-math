# Closure operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Closure operator"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **closure operator** on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *S* is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $\operatorname{cl}: \mathcal{P}(S)\rightarrow \mathcal{P}(S)$ from the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of *S* to itself that satisfies the following conditions for all sets $X,Y\subseteq S$

:::

{| border="0"

\|- | $X \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(X)$ | (cl is *extensive*), |- | $X\subseteq Y \Rightarrow \operatorname{cl}(X) \subseteq \operatorname{cl}(Y)$ | (cl is *increasing*), |- | $\operatorname{cl}(\operatorname{cl}(X))=\operatorname{cl}(X)$ | (cl is *idempotent*). |}

Closure operators are determined by their **closed sets**, i.e., by the sets of the form cl(*X*), since the **closure** cl(*X*) of a set *X* is the smallest closed set containing *X*. Such families of "closed sets" are sometimes called **closure systems** or "**Moore families**".[^o2] A set together with a closure operator on it is sometimes called a **closure space**. Closure operators are also called "**hull operators**", which prevents confusion with the "closure operators" studied in [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-set_topology).

## History {#History}

[E. H. Moore](wikipedia://en/1532860) studied closure operators in his 1910 *Introduction to a form of general analysis*, whereas the concept of the closure of a subset originated in the work of [Frigyes Riesz](wikipedia://en/1073126) in connection with topological spaces.[^n1] Though not formalized at the time, the idea of closure originated in the late 19th century with notable contributions by [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015), [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) and [Georg Cantor](wikipedia://en/12216).[^o5]

## Closed sets {#Closed_sets}

The closed sets with respect to a closure operator on *S* form a subset *C* of the power set ***P***(*S*). Any intersection of sets in *C* is again in *C*. In other words, *C* is a complete [meet-subsemilattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meet-semilattice) of ***P***(*S*). Conversely, if *C* ⊆ ***P***(*S*) is closed under arbitrary intersections, then the function that associates to every subset *X* of *S* the smallest set *Y* ∈ *C* such that *X* ⊆ *Y* is a closure operator.

There is a simple and fast algorithm for generating all closed sets of a given closure operator.[^o7]

A closure operator on a set is topological if and only if the set of closed sets is closed under finite unions, i.e., *C* is a meet-complete [sublattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sublattice) of ***P***(*S*). Even for non-topological closure operators, *C* can be seen as having the structure of a lattice. (The join of two sets *X*,*Y* ⊆ ***P***(*S*) being cl(*X* $\cup$ *Y*).) But then *C* is not a sublattice of the lattice ***P***(*S*).

Given a finitary closure operator on a set, the closures of finite sets are exactly the [compact elements](wikipedia://en/680261) of the set *C* of closed sets. It follows that *C* is an [algebraic poset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_poset). Since *C* is also a lattice, it is often referred to as an algebraic lattice in this context. Conversely, if *C* is an algebraic poset, then the closure operator is finitary.

### Pseudo-closed sets {#Pseudo-closed_sets}

Each closure operator on a finite set *S* is uniquely determined by its images of its *pseudo-closed* sets.[^o9] These are recursively defined: A set is **pseudo-closed** if it is not closed and contains the closure of each of its pseudo-closed proper subsets. Formally: *P* ⊆ *S* is pseudo-closed if and only if

- *P* ≠ cl(*P*) and
- if *Q* ⊂ *P* is pseudo-closed, then cl(*Q*) ⊆ *P*.

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Simple concave polygon Convex Hull.svg.* Convex hull (red) of a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) (yellow)

The usual set [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) from [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) is a closure operator. Other examples include the [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353) of a subset of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) or [affine hull](wikipedia://en/3416209) of a subset of a vector space or the [lower semicontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_semicontinuous) hull $\overline{f}$ of a function $f \colon E \to \mathbb{R} \cup \{ \pm \infty \}$, where $E$ is e.g. a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space), defined implicitly $\operatorname{epi}(\overline{f}) = \overline{\operatorname{epi}(f)}$, where $\operatorname{epi}(f)$ is the [epigraph](wikipedia://en/5744061) of a function $f$.

The [relative interior](wikipedia://en/8612907) $\operatorname{ri}$ is not a closure operator: although it is idempotent, it is not increasing and if $C_1$ is a cube in $\mathbb{R}^3$ and $C_2$ is one of its faces, then $C_2 \subset C_1$, but $\operatorname{ri}(C_1) \ne \emptyset \ne \operatorname{ri}(C_2)$ and $\operatorname{ri}(C_1) \cap \operatorname{ri}(C_2) = \emptyset$, so it is not increasing.[^o12]

In topology, the closure operators are [*topological* closure operators](wikipedia://en/331597), which must satisfy

$$\operatorname{cl}(X_1 \cup\dots\cup X_n) = \operatorname{cl}(X_1)\cup\dots\cup \operatorname{cl}(X_n)$$

for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$ (Note that for $n=0$ this gives $\operatorname{cl}(\varnothing)=\varnothing$).

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), many closure operators are **finitary closure operators**, i.e. they satisfy

$$\operatorname{cl}(X) = \bigcup\left\{\operatorname{cl}(Y) : Y\subseteq X \text{ and } Y \text{ finite} \right\}.$$

In the theory of [partially ordered sets](wikipedia://en/23572), which are important in [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392), closure operators have a more general definition that replaces $\subseteq$ with $\leq$. (See .)

## Closure operators in topology {#Closure_operators_in_topology}

The [topological closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_closure) of a subset *X* of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) consists of all points *y* of the space, such that every [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of *y* contains a point of *X*. The function that associates to every subset *X* its closure is a topological closure operator. Conversely, every topological closure operator on a set gives rise to a topological space whose closed sets are exactly the closed sets with respect to the closure operator.

## Closure operators in algebra {#Closure_operators_in_algebra}

Finitary closure operators play a relatively prominent role in [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), and in this context they are traditionally called *algebraic closure operators*. Every subset of an [algebra](wikipedia://en/4055928) *generates* a [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/2057747): the smallest subalgebra containing the set. This gives rise to a finitary closure operator.

Perhaps the best known example for this is the function that associates to every subset of a given [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) its [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353). Similarly, the function that associates to every subset of a given [group](wikipedia://en/19447) the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) generated by it, and similarly for [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) and all other types of [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364).

The linear span in a vector space and the similar algebraic closure in a field both satisfy the *exchange property:* If *x* is in the closure of the union of *A* and {*y*} but not in the closure of *A*, then *y* is in the closure of the union of *A* and {*x*}. A finitary closure operator with this property is called a [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321). The [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of a vector space, or the [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) of a field (over its [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field)) is exactly the rank of the corresponding matroid.

The function that maps every subset of a given [field](wikipedia://en/10603) to its [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129) is also a finitary closure operator, and in general it is different from the operator mentioned before. Finitary closure operators that generalize these two operators are studied in [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) as dcl (for *definable closure*) and acl (for *algebraic closure*).

The [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) in *n*-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is another example of a finitary closure operator. It satisfies the *anti-exchange property:* If *x* is in the closure of the union of {*y*} and *A*, but not in the union of {*y*} and closure of *A*, then *y* is not in the closure of the union of {*x*} and *A*. Finitary closure operators with this property give rise to [antimatroids](wikipedia://en/667063).

As another example of a closure operator used in algebra, if some algebra has universe *A* and *X* is a set of pairs of *A*, then the operator assigning to *X* the smallest [congruence](wikipedia://en/48167) containing *X* is a finitary closure operator on *A x A*.[^o15]

## Closure operators in logic {#Closure_operators_in_logic}

Suppose you have some [logical formalism](wikipedia://en/19636) that contains certain rules allowing you to derive new formulas from given ones. Consider the set *F* of all possible formulas, and let *P* be the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of *F*, ordered by ⊆. For a set *X* of formulas, let cl(*X*) be the set of all formulas that can be derived from *X*. Then cl is a closure operator on *P*. More precisely, we can obtain cl as follows. Call "continuous" an operator *J* such that, for every [directed](wikipedia://en/9165) class *T*,

*J*(lim *T*) = lim *J*(*T*).

This continuity condition is on the basis of a fixed point theorem for *J*. Consider the one-step operator *J* of a monotone logic. This is the operator associating any set *X* of formulas with the set *J*(*X*) of formulas that are either logical axioms or are obtained by an inference rule from formulas in *X* or are in *X*. Then such an operator is continuous and we can define cl(*X*) as the least fixed point for *J* greater or equal to *X*. In accordance with such a point of view, Tarski, Brown, Suszko and other authors proposed a general approach to logic based on closure operator theory. Also, such an idea is proposed in programming logic (see Lloyd 1987) and in [fuzzy logic](wikipedia://en/49180) (see Gerla 2000).

### Consequence operators {#Consequence_operators}

Around 1930, [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) developed an abstract theory of logical deductions that models some properties of logical calculi. Mathematically, what he described is just a finitary closure operator on a set (the set of *sentences*). In [abstract algebraic logic](wikipedia://en/4613861), finitary closure operators are still studied under the name *consequence operator*, which was coined by Tarski. The set *S* represents a set of sentences, a subset *T* of *S* a theory, and cl(*T*) is the set of all sentences that follow from the theory. Nowadays the term can refer to closure operators that need not be finitary; finitary closure operators are then sometimes called **finite consequence operators**.

## Closure operators on partially ordered sets {#Closure_operators_on_partially_ordered_sets}

A [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) (poset) is a set together with a *partial order* ≤, i.e. a [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) that is reflexive (*a* ≤ *a*), transitive (*a* ≤ *b* ≤ *c* implies *a* ≤ *c*) and [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/1176) (*a* ≤ *b* ≤ *a* implies *a* = *b*). Every [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) **P**(*S*) together with inclusion ⊆ is a partially ordered set.

A function cl: *P* → *P* from a partial order *P* to itself is called a closure operator if it satisfies the following axioms for all elements *x*, *y* in *P*.

{| border="0"

\|- | *x* ≤ cl(*x*) | (cl is *extensive*) |- | *x* ≤ *y* implies cl(*x*) ≤ cl(*y*) | (cl is [increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Increasing)) |- | cl(cl(*x*)) = cl(*x*) | (cl is [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent)) |}

More succinct alternatives are available: the definition above is equivalent to the single axiom

*x* ≤ cl(*y*) if and only if cl(*x*) ≤ cl(*y*)

for all *x*, *y* in *P*.

Using the [pointwise order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointwise_order) on functions between posets, one may alternatively write the extensiveness property as id<sub>*P*</sub> ≤ cl, where id is the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069). A self-map *k* that is increasing and idempotent, but satisfies the [dual](wikipedia://en/600618) of the extensiveness property, i.e. *k* ≤ id<sub>*P*</sub> is called a **kernel operator**,[^o20] **interior operator**,[^o21] or **dual closure**.[^o22] As examples, if *A* is a subset of a set *B*, then the self-map on the powerset of *B* given by *μ<sub>A</sub>*(*X*) = *A* ∪ *X* is a closure operator, whereas *λ<sub>A</sub>*(*X*) = *A* ∩ *X* is a kernel operator. The [ceiling function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceiling_function) from the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) to the real numbers, which assigns to every real *x* the smallest [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) not smaller than *x*, is another example of a closure operator.

A [fixpoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixpoint) of the function cl, i.e. an element *c* of *P* that satisfies cl(*c*) = *c*, is called a **closed element**. A closure operator on a partially ordered set is determined by its closed elements. If *c* is a closed element, then *x* ≤ *c* and cl(*x*) ≤ *c* are equivalent conditions.

Every [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411) (or [residuated mapping](wikipedia://en/18272082)) gives rise to a closure operator (as is explained in that article). In fact, *every* closure operator arises in this way from a suitable Galois connection.[^o23] The Galois connection is not uniquely determined by the closure operator. One Galois connection that gives rise to the closure operator cl can be described as follows: if *A* is the set of closed elements with respect to cl, then cl: *P* → *A* is the lower adjoint of a Galois connection between *P* and *A*, with the upper adjoint being the embedding of *A* into *P*. Furthermore, every lower adjoint of an embedding of some subset into *P* is a closure operator. "Closure operators are lower adjoints of embeddings." Note however that not every embedding has a lower adjoint.

Any partially ordered set *P* can be viewed as a [category](wikipedia://en/5869), with a single morphism from *x* to *y* if and only if *x* ≤ *y*. The closure operators on the partially ordered set *P* are then nothing but the [monads](wikipedia://en/355140) on the category *P*. Equivalently, a closure operator can be viewed as an endofunctor on the category of partially ordered sets that has the additional *idempotent* and *extensive* properties.

If *P* is a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777), then a subset *A* of *P* is the set of closed elements for some closure operator on *P* if and only if *A* is a **Moore family** on *P*, i.e. the largest element of *P* is in *A*, and the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) (meet) of any non-empty subset of *A* is again in *A*. Any such set *A* is itself a complete lattice with the order inherited from *P* (but the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) (join) operation might differ from that of *P*). When *P* is the [powerset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powerset) [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844) of a set *X*, then a Moore family on *P* is called a **closure system** on *X*.

The closure operators on *P* form themselves a complete lattice; the order on closure operators is defined by cl<sub>1</sub> ≤ cl<sub>2</sub> [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) cl<sub>1</sub>(*x*) ≤ cl<sub>2</sub>(*x*) for all *x* in *P*.
