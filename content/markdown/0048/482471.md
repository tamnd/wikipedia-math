# Polylogarithm

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polylogarithm"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **polylogarithm** (also known as **Jonquière's function**, for Alfred Jonquière) is a [special function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_function) $\operatorname{Li}_{s}(z)$ of order $s$ and argument $z$. Only for special values of $s$ does the polylogarithm reduce to an [elementary function](wikipedia://en/10412) such as the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) or a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210). In [quantum statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_statistics), the polylogarithm function appears as the closed form of [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) of the [Fermi–Dirac distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi–Dirac_distribution) and the [Bose–Einstein distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose–Einstein_distribution), and is also known as the **Fermi–Dirac integral** or the **Bose–Einstein integral**. In [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268), polylogarithms of positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) order arise in the calculation of processes represented by higher-order [Feynman diagrams](wikipedia://en/11617).

:::

The polylogarithm function is equivalent to the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952) — either [function](wikipedia://en/185427) can be expressed in terms of the other — and both functions are special cases of the [Lerch transcendent](wikipedia://en/1342978). Polylogarithms should not be confused with [polylogarithmic functions](wikipedia://en/445957), nor with the [offset logarithmic integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offset_logarithmic_integral) $\operatorname{Li}(z)$, which has the same notation without the subscript.

The polylogarithm function is defined by a [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) in $z$ generalizing the [Mercator series](wikipedia://en/3995885), which is also a [Dirichlet series](wikipedia://en/393258) in $s$:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty {z^k \over k^s} = z + {z^2 \over 2^s} + {z^3 \over 3^s} + \cdots$$

This definition is valid for arbitrary [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) order $s$ and for all complex arguments $z$ with $\lvert z\rvert < 1$; it can be extended to $\lvert z\rvert \ge 1$ by the process of [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842). (Here the denominator $k^{s}$ is understood as $\exp(s \ln k)$). The special case $s = 1$ involves the ordinary [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), $\operatorname{Li}_{1}(z) = -\ln(1-z)$, while the special cases $s = 2$ and $s = 3$ are called the [dilogarithm](wikipedia://en/2398437) (also referred to as Spence's function) and trilogarithm respectively. The name of the function comes from the fact that it may also be defined as the repeated [integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integral) of itself:

$$\operatorname{Li}_{s+1}(z) = \int_0^z \frac{\operatorname{Li}_s(t)}{t} dt$$

thus the dilogarithm is an integral of a function involving the logarithm, and so on. For nonpositive integer orders $s$, the polylogarithm is a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210).

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

In the case where the order $s$ is an integer, it will be represented by $s=n$ (or $s=-n$ when negative). It is often convenient to define $\mu=\ln(z)$ where $\ln(z)$ is the [principal branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_branch) of the [complex logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290) $\operatorname{Ln}(z)$ so that $-\pi< \operatorname{Im}(\mu) \le \pi.$ Also, all exponentiation will be assumed to be single-valued: $z^s = \exp(s\ln(z)).$

Depending on the order $s$, the polylogarithm may be multi-valued. The *principal branch* of $\operatorname{Li}_s(z)$ is taken to be given for $| z | < 1$ by the above series definition and taken to be continuous except on the positive real axis, where a cut is made from $z = 1$ to $\infty$ such that the axis is placed on the lower half plane of $z$. In terms of $\mu$, this amounts to $-\pi < \arg(-\mu)\le \pi$. The discontinuity of the polylogarithm in dependence on $\mu$ can sometimes be confusing.

For real argument $z$, the polylogarithm of real order $s$ is real if $z < 1$, and its imaginary part for $z \ge 1$ is (Wood 1992, §3):

$$\operatorname{Im}\left( \operatorname{Li}_s(z) \right) = -{{\pi \mu^{s-1}}\over{\Gamma(s)}}.$$

Going across the cut, if *ε* is an infinitesimally small positive [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438), then:

$$\operatorname{Im}\left( \operatorname{Li}_s(z+i\epsilon) \right) = {{\pi \mu^{s-1}}\over{\Gamma(s)}}.$$

Both can be concluded from the series expansion (see below) of Li<sub>*s*</sub>(*e*${}^{\mu}$) about *μ* = 0.

The derivatives of the polylogarithm follow from the defining power series:

$$z \frac{\partial \operatorname{Li}_s(z) }{ \partial z} = \operatorname{Li}_{s-1}(z)$$

$$\frac{\partial \operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) }{ \partial \mu} = \operatorname{Li}_{s-1}(e^\mu).$$

The square relationship is seen from the series definition, and is related to the [duplication formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duplication_formula) (see also (Clunie 1954), (Schrödinger 1952)):

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(-z) + \operatorname{Li}_s(z) = 2^{1-s} \operatorname{Li}_s(z^2).$$

[Kummer's function](wikipedia://en/1516033) obeys a very similar duplication formula. This is a special case of the [multiplication formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_formula), for any positive integer *p*:

$$\sum_{m=0}^{p-1} \operatorname{Li}_s(z e^{2\pi i m/p}) = p^{1-s} \operatorname{Li}_s(z^p),$$

which can be proved using the series definition of the polylogarithm and the orthogonality of the exponential terms (see e.g. [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811)).

Another important property, the inversion formula, involves the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952) or the [Bernoulli polynomials](wikipedia://en/228161) and is found under relationship to other functions below.

## Particular values {#Particular_values}

*Image: Polylogarithm plot negative.svg.*

For particular cases, the polylogarithm may be expressed in terms of other functions (see below). Particular values for the polylogarithm may thus also be found as particular values of these other functions.

For integer values of the polylogarithm order, the following explicit expressions are obtained by repeated application of *z*·∂/∂*z* to Li<sub>1</sub>(*z*):

$$\operatorname{Li}_{1}(z) = -\ln(1-z)$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_{0}(z) = {z \over 1-z}$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-1}(z) = {z \over (1-z)^2}$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-2}(z) = {z (1+z) \over (1-z)^3}$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-3}(z) = {z (1+4z+z^2) \over (1-z)^4}$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-4}(z) = {z (1+z) (1+10z+z^2) \over (1-z)^5} .$$

Accordingly the polylogarithm reduces to a ratio of polynomials in *z*, and is therefore a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) of *z*, for all nonpositive integer orders. The general case may be expressed as a finite sum:

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-n}(z) = \left(z {\partial \over \partial z} \right)^n {z \over {1-z}} = \sum_{k=0}^n k! S(n+1, k+1) \left({z \over {1-z}} \right)^{k+1} \qquad (n=0,1,2,\ldots),$$

where *S*(*n*,*k*) are the [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292). Equivalent formulae applicable to negative integer orders are (Wood 1992, § 6):

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-n}(z) = (-1)^{n+1} \sum_{k=0}^n k! S(n+1, k+1) \left({{-1} \over {1-z}} \right)^{k+1} \qquad (n=1,2,3,\ldots),$$

and:

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-n}(z) = {1 \over (1-z)^{n+1}} \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \left\langle {n \atop k} \right\rangle z^{n-k} \qquad (n=1,2,3,\ldots),$$

where $\scriptstyle \left\langle {n \atop k} \right\rangle$ are the [Eulerian numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulerian_numbers). All roots of Li<sub>−*n*</sub>(*z*) are distinct and real; they include *z* = 0, while the remainder is negative and centered about *z* = −1 on a logarithmic scale. As *n* becomes large, the numerical evaluation of these rational expressions increasingly suffers from cancellation (Wood 1992, § 6); full accuracy can be obtained, however, by computing Li<sub>−*n*</sub>(*z*) via the general relation with the Hurwitz zeta function (see below).

Some particular expressions for half-integer values of the argument *z* are:

$$\operatorname{Li}_1(\tfrac12) = \ln 2$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(\tfrac12) = \tfrac1{12} \pi^2 - \tfrac12 (\ln 2)^2$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_3(\tfrac12) = \tfrac16 (\ln 2)^3 - \tfrac1{12} \pi^2 \ln 2 + \tfrac78 \zeta(3) ,$$

where *ζ* is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809). No formulae of this type are known for higher integer orders (Lewin 1991, 2), but one has for instance (Borwein Borwein Girgensohn 1995):

$$\operatorname{Li}_4(\tfrac12) = \tfrac 1{360} \pi^4 - \tfrac 1{24}(\ln 2)^4 + \tfrac1{24} \pi^2 (\ln 2)^2 - \tfrac 1 2 \zeta(\bar3, \bar1),$$

which involves the alternating double sum

$$\zeta(\bar3, \bar1)=\sum_{m>n>0} (-1)^{m+n} m^{-3} n^{-1}.$$

In general one has for integer orders *n* ≥ 2 (Broadhurst 1996, 9):

$$\operatorname{Li}_n(\tfrac12) = -\zeta(\bar1, \bar1, \left\{ 1 \right\}^{n-2}),$$

where *ζ*(*s*<sub>1</sub>, …, *s*<sub>*k*</sub>) is the [multiple zeta function](wikipedia://en/18037845); for example:

$$\operatorname{Li}_5(\tfrac12) = -\zeta(\bar1, \bar1, 1,1,1).$$

As a straightforward consequence of the series definition, values of the polylogarithm at the *p*th complex [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) are given by the [Fourier sum](wikipedia://en/8811):

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^{2 \pi i m/p}) = p^{-s} \sum_{k=1}^p e^{2 \pi i m k/p} \zeta(s, \tfrac {k}{p}) \qquad (m = 1, 2, \dots, p-1),$$

where *ζ* is the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952). For Re(*s*) > 1, where Li<sub>*s*</sub>(1) is finite, the relation also holds with *m* = 0 or *m* = *p*. While this formula is not as simple as that implied by the more general relation with the Hurwitz zeta function listed under relationship to other functions below, it has the advantage of applying to non-negative integer values of *s* as well. As usual, the relation may be inverted to express ζ(*s*, <sup>*m*</sup>⁄<sub>*p*</sub>) for any *m* = 1, …, *p* as a Fourier sum of Li<sub>*s*</sub>(exp(2*πi* <sup>*k*</sup>⁄<sub>*p*</sub>)) over *k* = 1, …, *p*.

## Relationship to other functions {#Relationship_to_other_functions}

- For $z = 1$, the polylogarithm reduces to the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) $$\operatorname{Li}_s(1) = \zeta(s) \qquad (\operatorname{Re}(s)>1).$$
- The polylogarithm is related to [Dirichlet eta function](wikipedia://en/447035) and the [Dirichlet beta function](wikipedia://en/2216061): $$\operatorname{Li}_s(-1) = -\eta(s),$$ where $\eta(s)$ is the Dirichlet eta function. For pure imaginary arguments, we have: $$\operatorname{Li}_s(\pm i) = -2^{-s} \eta(s) \pm i\beta(s),$$ where $\beta(s)$ is the Dirichlet beta function.
- The polylogarithm is related to the [complete Fermi–Dirac integral](wikipedia://en/406922) as: $$F_s(\mu) = -\operatorname{Li}_{s+1}(-e^\mu).$$
- The polylogarithm is related to the complete Bose–Einstein integral as: $$G_s(\mu) = \operatorname{Li}_{s+1}(e^\mu).$$
- The polylogarithm is a special case of the [incomplete polylogarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incomplete_polylogarithm) function $$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = \operatorname{Li}_s(0,z) .$$
- The polylogarithm is a special case of the [Lerch transcendent](wikipedia://en/1342978) (Erdélyi et al. 1981, § 1.11-14) $$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = z\Phi(z,s,1).$$
- The polylogarithm is related to the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952) by: $$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = {\Gamma(1 - s) \over (2\pi)^{1-s}} \left[i^{1-s} \zeta \left(1 - s, \frac{1}{2} + {\ln(-z) \over {2\pi i}} \right) + i^{s-1} ~\zeta \left(1 - s, \frac{1}{2} - {\ln(-z) \over {2\pi i}} \right) \right],$$ which relation, however, is invalidated at positive integer *s* by [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) $\Gamma(1 - s)$, and at $s = 0$ by a pole of both zeta functions; a derivation of this formula is given under series representations below. With a little help from a functional equation for the Hurwitz zeta function, the polylogarithm is consequently also related to that function via (Jonquière 1889): $$i^{-s} \operatorname{Li}_s(e^{2\pi i x}) + i^s \operatorname{Li}_s(e^{-2\pi i x}) = \frac{(2\pi)^s}{\Gamma(s)} \zeta(1 - s, x),$$ which relation holds for $0 \le \operatorname{Re}(x) < 1$ if $\operatorname{Im}(x) \ge 0$, and for $0 < \operatorname{Re}(x) \le 1$ if $\operatorname{Im}(x) < 0$. Equivalently, for all complex *s* and for complex $z \notin (0, 1]$, the inversion formula reads $$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) + (-1)^s \operatorname{Li}_s(1/z) = \frac{(2\pi i)^s}{\Gamma(s)} ~\zeta \left(1 - s, ~\frac{1}{2} + {\ln(-z) \over {2\pi i}} \right),$$ and for all complex *s* and for complex $z \notin (1, \infty)$ $$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) + (-1)^s \operatorname{Li}_s(1/z) = {(2\pi i)^s \over \Gamma(s)} ~\zeta \left(1 - s, ~\frac{1}{2} - {\ln(-1/z) \over {2\pi i}} \right) .$$ For $z \notin (0, \infty)$, one has $\ln(-z) = -\ln(-^{1}\text{⁄}_{z})$, and both expressions agree. These relations furnish the analytic continuation of the polylogarithm beyond the circle of convergence |*z*| = 1 of the defining power series. (The corresponding equation of (Jonquière 1889, eq. 5) and (Erdélyi et al. 1981, § 1.11-16) is not correct if one assumes that the principal branches of the polylogarithm and the logarithm are used simultaneously.) See the next item for a simplified formula when *s* is an integer.
- For positive integer polylogarithm orders *s*, the Hurwitz zeta function ζ(1−*s*, *x*) reduces to [Bernoulli polynomials](wikipedia://en/228161), $\zeta(1-n, x) = -B_{n}(x) / n$, and Jonquière's inversion formula for *n* = 1, 2, 3, … becomes: $$\operatorname{Li}_{n}(e^{2\pi i x}) + (-1)^n \operatorname{Li}_{n}(e^{-2\pi i x}) = -{(2\pi i)^n \over n!} B_n(x),$$ where again 0 ≤ Re(*x*) \< 1 if Im(*x*) ≥ 0, and 0 \< Re(*x*) ≤ 1 if Im(*x*) \< 0. Upon restriction of the polylogarithm argument to the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071), Im(*x*) = 0, the left hand side of this formula simplifies to 2 Re(Li<sub>*n*</sub>(*e*<sup>2*πix*</sup>)) if *n* is even, and to 2*i* Im(Li<sub>*n*</sub>(*e*<sup>2*πix*</sup>)) if *n* is odd. For negative integer orders, on the other hand, the divergence of Γ(*s*) implies for all *z* that (Erdélyi et al. 1981, § 1.11-17): $$\operatorname{Li}_{-n}(z) + (-1)^n \operatorname{Li}_{-n}(1/z) = 0 \qquad (n = 1,2,3,\ldots).$$ More generally, one has for $n = 0, \pm1, \pm2, \pm3, \ldots$: $$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Li}_{n}(z) + (-1)^n \operatorname{Li}_{n}(1/z) &= -\frac{(2\pi i)^n}{n!} B_n \left( \frac{1}{2} + {\ln(-z) \over {2\pi i}} \right) & (z \not\in ]0;1]), \\ \operatorname{Li}_{n}(z) + (-1)^n \operatorname{Li}_{n}(1/z) &= -\frac{(2\pi i)^n}{n!} B_n \left( \frac{1}{2} - {\ln(-1/z) \over {2\pi i}} \right) & (z \not\in ~]1;\infty[), \end{aligned}$$ where both expressions agree for $z \notin (0, \infty)$. (The corresponding equation of (Jonquière 1889, eq. 1) and (Erdélyi et al. 1981, § 1.11-18) is again not correct.)
- The polylogarithm with pure imaginary *μ* may be expressed in terms of the [Clausen functions](wikipedia://en/406899) *Ci*<sub>*s*</sub>(θ) and *Si*<sub>*s*</sub>(θ), and vice versa ((Lewin 1958, Ch. VII § 1.4); (Abramowitz Stegun 1972, § 27.8)): $$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^{\pm i \theta}) = Ci_s(\theta) \pm i Si_s(\theta).$$
- The [inverse tangent integral](wikipedia://en/3800787) $Ti_{s}(z)$ (Lewin 1958, Ch. VII § 1.2) can be expressed in terms of polylogarithms: $$\operatorname{Ti}_s(z) = {1 \over 2i} \left[ \operatorname{Li}_s(i z) - \operatorname{Li}_s(-i z) \right].$$ The relation in particular implies: $$\operatorname{Ti}_0(z) = {z \over 1+z^2}, \quad \operatorname{Ti}_1(z) = \arctan z, \quad \operatorname{Ti}_2(z) = \int_0^z {\arctan t \over t} dt, \quad \ldots~ \quad \operatorname{Ti}_{n+1}(z) = \int_0^z \frac{\operatorname{Ti}_n(t)}{t} dt,$$ which explains the function name.
- The [Legendre chi function](wikipedia://en/1269900) *χ*<sub>*s*</sub>(*z*) ((Lewin 1958, Ch. VII § 1.1); (Boersma Dempsey 1992)) can be expressed as the [odd part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_part_of_a_function) of polylogarithms: $$\chi_s(z) = \tfrac {1}{2} \left[ \operatorname{Li}_s(z) - \operatorname{Li}_s(-z) \right].$$
- The polylogarithm of integer order can be expressed as a [generalized hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/361184): $$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Li}_n(z) &= z\,\; _{n+1\!}F_{n} (1,1,\dots,1; 2,2,\dots,2; z) & (n = 0,1,2,\ldots), \\ \operatorname{Li}_{-n}(z) &= z\,\; _{n}F_{n-1} (2,2,\dots,2; 1,1,\dots,1; z) & (n = 1,2,3,\ldots) ~. \end{aligned}$$
- In terms of the [incomplete zeta functions](wikipedia://en/25809#Generalizations) or "[Debye functions](wikipedia://en/406907)" (Abramowitz Stegun 1972, § 27.1): $$Z_n(z) = {1 \over (n - 1)!} \int_z^\infty {t^{n-1} \over e^t-1} dt \qquad (n = 1,2,3,\ldots) ,$$ the polylogarithm Li<sub>*n*</sub>(*z*) for positive integer n may be expressed as the finite sum (Wood 1992, §16): $$\operatorname{Li}_{n}(e^\mu) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} Z_{n-k}(-\mu) {\mu^k \over k!} \qquad (n = 1,2,3,\ldots) .$$ A remarkably similar expression relates the "Debye functions" *Z*<sub>*n*</sub>(*z*) to the polylogarithm: $$Z_n(z) = \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \operatorname{Li}_{n-k}(e^{-z}) {z^k \over k!} \qquad (n = 1,2,3,\ldots) .$$
- Using [Lambert series](wikipedia://en/1367040), if $J_s(n)$ is [Jordan's totient function](wikipedia://en/19486387), then $$\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{z^nJ_{-s}(n)}{1-z^n}=\operatorname{Li}_{s}(z).$$

## Integral representations {#Integral_representations}

Any of the following integral representations furnishes the [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) of the polylogarithm beyond the circle of convergence |*z*| = 1 of the defining power series.

The polylogarithm can be expressed in terms of the integral of the [Bose–Einstein distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bose–Einstein_distribution):

$$\operatorname{Li}_{s}(z) = {1 \over \Gamma(s)} \int_0^\infty {t^{s-1} \over e^t/z-1} dt .$$

This converges for Re(*s*) > 0 and all *z* except for *z* real and ≥ 1. The polylogarithm in this context is sometimes referred to as a Bose integral but more commonly as a **Bose–Einstein integral** ((Dingle 1957a), (Dingle Arndt Roy 1957)).[^o32] Similarly, the polylogarithm can be expressed in terms of the integral of the [Fermi–Dirac distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi–Dirac_distribution):

$$-\operatorname{Li}_{s}(-z) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(s)} \int_0^\infty {t^{s-1} \over e^t/z+1} dt .$$

This converges for $\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0$ and all $z$ except for *z* real and ≤ −1. The polylogarithm in this context is sometimes referred to as a Fermi integral or a [Fermi–Dirac integral](wikipedia://en/406922) ((GSL 2010), (Dingle 1957b)). These representations are readily verified by [Taylor expansion](wikipedia://en/30448) of the integrand with respect to *z* and termwise integration. The papers of Dingle contain detailed investigations of both types of integrals.

The polylogarithm is also related to the integral of the [Maxwell–Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/19830):

$$\lim_{z \to 0} \frac{\operatorname{Li}_{s}(z)}{z} = {1 \over \Gamma(s)} \int_0^\infty {t^{s-1} e^{-t}} dt = 1 .$$

This also gives the [asymptotic behavior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_behavior) of polylogarithm at the vicinity of origin.

A complementary integral representation applies to Re(*s*) \< 0 and to all *z* except to *z* real and ≥ 0:

$$\operatorname{Li}_{s}(z) =\int_0^\infty {t^{-s} \sin[s \pi /2 - t \ln(-z)] \over \sinh(\pi t)} dt .$$

This integral follows from the general relation of the polylogarithm with the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952) (see above) and a familiar integral representation of the latter.

The polylogarithm may be quite generally represented by a [Hankel contour](wikipedia://en/1268560) integral (Whittaker Watson 1927, § 12.22, § 13.13), which extends the Bose–Einstein representation to negative orders *s*. As long as the *t* = *μ* [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of the integrand does not lie on the non-negative real axis, and *s* ≠ 1, 2, 3, …, we have:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) = -{{\Gamma(1 - s)} \over {2\pi i}} \oint_H {{(-t)^{s-1}} \over {e^{t-\mu}-1}} dt$$

where *H* represents the Hankel contour. The integrand has a cut along the real axis from zero to infinity, with the axis belonging to the lower half plane of *t*. The integration starts at +∞ on the upper half plane (Im(*t*) > 0), circles the origin without enclosing any of the poles *t* = *μ* + 2*kπi*, and terminates at +∞ on the lower half plane (Im(*t*) \< 0). For the case where *μ* is real and non-negative, we can simply subtract the contribution of the enclosed *t* = *μ* pole:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) = -{{\Gamma(1 - s)} \over {2\pi i}} \oint_H {{(-t)^{s-1}} \over {e^{t-\mu}}-1} dt - 2\pi i R$$

where *R* is the [residue](wikipedia://en/73102) of the pole:

$$R = {i \over 2\pi} \Gamma(1 - s) (-\mu)^{s-1} .$$

When the [Abel–Plana formula](wikipedia://en/37598131) is applied to the defining series of the polylogarithm, a [Hermite](wikipedia://en/22870422)-type integral representation results that is valid for all complex *z* and for all complex *s*:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}z + {\Gamma(1 - s, -\ln z) \over (-\ln z)^{1-s}} + 2z \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin(s\arctan t - t\ln z)} {(1+t^2)^{s/2} (e^{2\pi t}-1)} dt$$

where Γ is the [upper incomplete gamma-function](wikipedia://en/456050). All (but not part) of the ln(*z*) in this expression can be replaced by −ln(<sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>*z*</sub>). A related representation which also holds for all complex *s*,

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}z + z \int_0^\infty \frac{\sin[s \arctan t - t \ln(-z)]} {(1+t^2)^{s/2} \sinh(\pi t)} dt ,$$

avoids the use of the incomplete gamma function, but this integral fails for *z* on the positive real axis if Re(*s*) ≤ 0. This expression is found by writing 2<sup>*s*</sup> Li<sub>*s*</sub>(−*z*) / (−*z*) = Φ(*z*<sup>2</sup>, *s*, <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub>) − *z* Φ(*z*<sup>2</sup>, *s*, 1), where Φ is the [Lerch transcendent](wikipedia://en/1342978), and applying the Abel–Plana formula to the first Φ series and a complementary formula that involves 1 / (*e*<sup>2*πt*</sup> + 1) in place of 1 / (*e*<sup>2*πt*</sup> − 1) to the second Φ series.

We can express an integral for the polylogarithm by integrating the ordinary [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) termwise for $s \in \mathbb{N}$ as (Borwein Borwein Girgensohn 1995, §2, eqn. 4)

$$\operatorname{Li}_{s+1}(z) = \frac{z \cdot (-1)^s}{s!} \int_0^1 \frac{\log^s(t)}{1-tz} dt.$$

:::

## Series representations {#Series_representations}

As noted under integral representations above, the Bose–Einstein integral representation of the polylogarithm may be extended to negative orders *s* by means of [Hankel contour](wikipedia://en/1268560) integration:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) = -{\Gamma(1 - s) \over 2\pi i} \oint_H {(-t)^{s-1} \over e^{t-\mu}-1} dt,$$

where *H* is the Hankel contour, *s* ≠ 1, 2, 3, …, and the *t* = *μ* pole of the integrand does not lie on the non-negative real axis. The [contour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methods_of_contour_integration) can be modified so that it encloses the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of the integrand at *t* − *μ* = 2*kπi*, and the integral can be evaluated as the sum of the [residues](wikipedia://en/73102) ((Wood 1992, § 12, 13); (Gradshteyn Ryzhik 2015)):

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) = \Gamma(1 - s) \sum_{k=-\infty}^\infty (2k \pi i - \mu)^{s-1}.$$

This will hold for Re(*s*) \< 0 and all *μ* except where *e*<sup>*μ*</sup> = 1. For 0 \< Im(*μ*) ≤ 2*π* the sum can be split as:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) = \Gamma(1-s) \left[ (-2\pi i)^{s-1} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left(k + {\mu \over {2\pi i}} \right)^{s-1} + (2\pi i)^{s-1} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left(k+1- {\mu \over {2\pi i}} \right)^{s-1} \right],$$

where the two series can now be identified with the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952):

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) = {\Gamma(1 - s) \over (2\pi)^{1-s}} \left[i^{1-s} ~\zeta \left(1 - s, ~{\mu \over {2\pi i}} \right) + i^{s-1} ~\zeta \left(1 - s, ~1 - {\mu \over {2\pi i}} \right) \right] \qquad (0 < \operatorname{Im}(\mu) \leq 2\pi) .$$

This relation, which has already been given under relationship to other functions above, holds for all complex *s* ≠ 0, 1, 2, 3, … and was first derived in (Jonquière 1889, eq. 6).

In order to represent the polylogarithm as a power series about *μ* = 0, we write the series derived from the Hankel contour integral as:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) = \Gamma(1 - s) (-\mu)^{s-1} + \Gamma(1 - s) \sum_{h=1}^\infty \left[(-2 h \pi i - \mu)^{s-1} + (2 h \pi i - \mu)^{s-1} \right] .$$

When the binomial powers in the sum are expanded about *μ* = 0 and the order of summation is reversed, the sum over *h* can be expressed in closed form:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) = \Gamma(1 - s) (-\mu)^{s-1} + \sum_{k=0}^\infty {\zeta(s-k) \over k!} \mu^k .$$

This result holds for |*μ*| \< 2*π* and, thanks to the analytic continuation provided by the [zeta functions](wikipedia://en/25809), for all *s* ≠ 1, 2, 3, … . If the order is a positive integer, *s* = *n*, both the term with *k* = *n* − 1 and the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316) become infinite, although their sum does not. One obtains ((Wood 1992, § 9); (Gradshteyn Ryzhik 2015)):

$$\lim_{s \to k+1} \left[ {\zeta(s-k) \over k!} \mu^k + \Gamma(1 - s) (-\mu)^{s-1} \right] = {\mu^k \over k!} \left[\sum_{h=1}^k {1 \over h} - \ln(-\mu) \right],$$

where the sum over *h* vanishes if *k* = 0. So, for positive integer orders and for |*μ*| \< 2*π* we have the series:

$$\operatorname{Li}_{n}(e^\mu) = {\mu^{n-1} \over (n-1)!} \left[ H_{n-1} - \ln(-\mu) \right] + \sum_{k=0,k\ne n-1}^\infty {\zeta(n-k) \over k!} \mu^k ,$$

where *H*<sub>*n*</sub> denotes the *n*th [harmonic number](wikipedia://en/214729):

$$H_n = \sum_{h=1}^n {1 \over h}, \qquad H_0 = 0.$$

The problem terms now contain −ln(−*μ*) which, when multiplied by *μ*<sup>*n*−1</sup>, will tend to zero as *μ* → 0, except for *n* = 1. This reflects the fact that Li<sub>*s*</sub>(*z*) exhibits a true [logarithmic singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) at *s* = 1 and *z* = 1 since:

$$\lim_{\mu \to 0} \Gamma(1-s)(-\mu)^{s-1} = 0 \qquad (\operatorname{Re}(s) > 1).$$

For *s* close, but not equal, to a positive integer, the divergent terms in the expansion about *μ* = 0 can be expected to cause computational difficulties (Wood 1992, § 9). Erdélyi's corresponding expansion (Erdélyi et al. 1981, § 1.11-15) in powers of ln(*z*) is not correct if one assumes that the principal branches of the polylogarithm and the logarithm are used simultaneously, since ln(<sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>*z*</sub>) is not uniformly equal to −ln(*z*).

For nonpositive integer values of *s*, the zeta function ζ(*s* − *k*) in the expansion about *μ* = 0 reduces to [Bernoulli numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_numbers): ζ(−*n* − *k*) = −B<sub>1+*n*+*k*</sub> / (1 + *n* + *k*). Numerical evaluation of Li<sub>−*n*</sub>(*z*) by this series does not suffer from the cancellation effects that the finite rational expressions given under particular values above exhibit for large *n*.

By use of the identity

$$1 = {1 \over \Gamma(s)} \int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{s-1} dt \qquad (\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0) ,$$

the Bose–Einstein integral representation of the polylogarithm (see above) may be cast in the form:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}z + {z \over 2 \Gamma(s)} \int_0^\infty e^{-t} t^{s-1} \coth{t - \ln z \over 2} dt \qquad (\operatorname{Re}(s) > 0).$$

Replacing the hyperbolic cotangent with a bilateral series,

$$\coth{t-\ln z \over 2} = 2 \sum_{k = -\infty}^\infty {1 \over 2 k \pi i + t - \ln z} ,$$

then reversing the order of integral and sum, and finally identifying the summands with an integral representation of the [upper incomplete gamma function](wikipedia://en/456050), one obtains:

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = \tfrac{1}{2}z + \sum_{k = -\infty}^\infty {\Gamma(1-s, 2k \pi i - \ln z) \over (2k \pi i - \ln z)^{1-s}}.$$

For both the bilateral series of this result and that for the hyperbolic cotangent, symmetric partial sums from −*k*<sub>max</sub> to *k*<sub>max</sub> converge unconditionally as *k*<sub>max</sub> → ∞. Provided the summation is performed symmetrically, this series for Li<sub>*s*</sub>(*z*) thus holds for all complex *s* as well as all complex *z*.

Introducing an explicit expression for the [Stirling numbers of the second kind](wikipedia://en/2229292) into the finite sum for the polylogarithm of nonpositive integer order (see above) one may write:

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-n}(z) = \sum_{k=0}^n \left( {-z \over 1-z} \right)^{k+1} \sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^{j+1} {k \choose j} (j+1)^n \qquad (n=0,1,2,\ldots).$$

The infinite series obtained by simply extending the outer summation to ∞ (Guillera Sondow 2008, Theorem 2.1):

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \left( {-z \over 1-z} \right)^{k+1} ~\sum_{j=0}^k (-1)^{j+1} {k \choose j} (j+1)^{-s} ,$$

turns out to converge to the polylogarithm for all complex *s* and for complex *z* with Re(*z*) \< <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub>, as can be verified for |<sup>−*z*</sup>⁄<sub>(1−*z*)</sub>| \< <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub> by reversing the order of summation and using:

$$\sum_{k=j}^\infty {k \choose j} \left( {-z \over 1-z} \right)^{k+1} = \left[ \left( {-z \over 1-z} \right)^{-1} -1 \right]^{-j-1} = (-z)^{j+1}.$$

The inner coefficients of these series can be expressed by [Stirling-number-related](wikipedia://en/2229296) formulas involving the generalized [harmonic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_numbers). For example, see [generating function transformations](wikipedia://en/53098313#Derivative_transformations) to find proofs (references to proofs) of the following identities:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Li}_2(z) &= \sum_{j \geq 1} \frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{2} \left(H_j^2+H_j^{(2)}\right) \frac{z^j}{(1-z)^{j+1}} \\ \operatorname{Li}_3(z) &= \sum_{j \geq 1} \frac{(-1)^{j-1}}{6} \left(H_j^3+3H_j H_j^{(2)} + 2 H_j^{(3)}\right) \frac{z^j}{(1-z)^{j+1}}. \end{aligned}$$

For the other arguments with Re(*z*) \< <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>2</sub> the result follows by [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842). This procedure is equivalent to applying [Euler's transformation](wikipedia://en/3877474) to the series in *z* that defines the polylogarithm.

## Asymptotic expansions {#Asymptotic_expansions}

For |*z*| ≫ 1, the polylogarithm can be expanded into [asymptotic series](wikipedia://en/642090) in terms of ln(−*z*):

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = {\pm i\pi \over \Gamma(s)} [\ln(-z) \pm i\pi]^{s-1} - \sum_{k = 0}^\infty (-1)^k (2\pi)^{2k} {B_{2k} \over (2k)!} {[\ln(-z) \pm i\pi]^{s-2 k} \over \Gamma(s+1-2k)},$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty (-1)^k (1-2^{1-2k}) (2\pi)^{2k} {B_{2k} \over (2k)!} {[\ln(-z)]^{s-2 k} \over \Gamma(s+1-2k)},$$

where *B*<sub>2*k*</sub> are the [Bernoulli numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_numbers). Both versions hold for all *s* and for any arg(*z*). As usual, the summation should be terminated when the terms start growing in magnitude. For negative integer *s*, the expansions vanish entirely; for non-negative integer *s*, they break off after a finite number of terms. (Wood 1992, § 11) describes a method for obtaining these series from the Bose–Einstein integral representation (his equation 11.2 for Li<sub>*s*</sub>(*e*<sup>*μ*</sup>) requires −2*π* \< Im(*μ*) ≤ 0).

## Limiting behavior {#Limiting_behavior}

The following [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602) result from the various representations of the polylogarithm (Wood 1992, § 22):

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) \sim_{|z|\to 0} z$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) \sim_{|\mu|\to 0} \Gamma(1 - s) (-\mu)^{s-1} \qquad (\operatorname{Re}(s) < 1)$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(\pm e^\mu) \sim_{\operatorname{Re}(\mu) \to \infty} -{\mu^s \over \Gamma(s+1)}\qquad (s \ne -1, -2, -3, \ldots)$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_{-n}(e^\mu) \sim_{\operatorname{Re}(\mu) \to \infty} -(-1)^n e^{-\mu} \qquad (n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots)$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(z) \sim_{\operatorname{Re}(s) \to \infty} z$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(e^\mu) \sim_{\operatorname{Re}(s) \to -\infty} \Gamma(1-s) (-\mu)^{s-1} \qquad (-\pi < \operatorname{Im}(\mu) < \pi)$$

$$\operatorname{Li}_s(-e^\mu) \sim_{\operatorname{Re}(s) \to -\infty} \Gamma(1 - s) \left[ (-\mu - i\pi)^{s-1} + (-\mu + i\pi)^{s-1} \right] \qquad (\operatorname{Im}(\mu) = 0)$$

Wood's first limit for $\operatorname{Re}(\mu) \to \infty$ has been corrected in accordance with his equation 11.3. The limit for $\operatorname{Re}(s) \to -\infty$ follows from the general relation of the polylogarithm with the [Hurwitz zeta function](wikipedia://en/406952) (see above).

## Dilogarithm {#Dilogarithm}

The dilogarithm is the polylogarithm of order *s* = 2. An alternate integral expression of the dilogarithm for arbitrary complex argument *z* is (Abramowitz Stegun 1972, § 27.7):

$$\operatorname{Li}_2 (z) = -\int_0^z{\ln (1-t) \over t} dt = -\int_0^1{\ln (1-zt) \over t} dt.$$

A source of confusion is that some [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427) define the dilogarithm as dilog(*z*) = Li<sub>2</sub>(1−*z*).

In the case of real *z* ≥ 1 the first integral expression for the dilogarithm can be written as

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(z) = \frac{\pi^2}{6} - \int_1^z{\ln(t-1) \over t} dt - i\pi \ln z$$

from which expanding ln(*t*−1) and integrating term by term we obtain

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(z) = \frac{\pi^2}{3} - \frac{1}{2}(\ln z)^2 - \sum_{k=1}^\infty {1 \over k^2 z^k} - i\pi \ln z \qquad (z \ge 1).$$

The *[Abel](wikipedia://en/21573) identity* for the dilogarithm is given by (Abel 1881)

$$\operatorname{Li}_2 \left( \frac{x}{1-y} \right) + \operatorname{Li}_2 \left( \frac{y}{1-x} \right) - \operatorname{Li}_2 \left(\frac{xy}{(1-x)(1-y)} \right) = \operatorname{Li}_2(x) + \operatorname{Li}_2(y) + \ln(1-x) \ln(1-y)$$

$$(\operatorname{Re}(x) \le \tfrac{1}{2} \wedge \operatorname{Re}(y) \le \tfrac{1}{2} \vee \operatorname{Im}(x) > 0 \wedge \operatorname{Im}(y) > 0 \vee \operatorname{Im}(x) < 0 \wedge \operatorname{Im}(y) < 0 \vee \ldots).$$

This is immediately seen to hold for either *x* = 0 or *y* = 0, and for general arguments is then easily verified by differentiation ∂/∂*x* ∂/∂*y*. For *y* = 1−*x* the identity reduces to [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902)'s *reflection formula*

$$\operatorname{Li}_2 \left(x \right) + \operatorname{Li}_2 \left(1-x\right) = \frac{1}{6} \pi^2 - \ln(x)\ln(1-x) ,$$

where Li<sub>2</sub>(1) = ζ(2) = <sup>1</sup>⁄<sub>6</sub> *π*<sup>2</sup> has been used and *x* may take any complex value.

In terms of the new variables *u* = *x*/(1−*y*), *v* = *y*/(1−*x*) the Abel identity reads

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(u) + \operatorname{Li}_2(v) - \operatorname{Li}_2(uv) = \operatorname{Li}_2 \left( \frac{u-uv}{1-uv} \right) + \operatorname{Li}_2 \left( \frac{v-uv}{1-uv} \right) + \ln \left( \frac{1-u}{1-uv} \right) \ln\left( \frac{1-v}{1-uv} \right),$$

which corresponds to the *pentagon identity* given in (Rogers 1907).

From the Abel identity for *x* = *y* = 1−*z* and the square relationship we have [Landen](wikipedia://en/1400598)'s identity

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(1-z) + \operatorname{Li}_2 \left( 1-\frac{1}{z} \right) = - \frac{1}{2} (\ln z)^2 \qquad (z \not \in ~]-\infty; 0]) ,$$

and applying the reflection formula to each dilogarithm we find the inversion formula

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(z) + \operatorname{Li}_2(1/z) = -\tfrac{1}{6} \pi^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} [\ln(-z)]^2 \qquad (z \not \in [0; 1[) ,$$

and for real *z* ≥ 1 also

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(z) + \operatorname{Li}_2(1/z) = \tfrac{1}{3} \pi^2 - \tfrac{1}{2} (\ln z)^2 - i\pi \ln z .$$

## Polylogarithm ladders {#Polylogarithm_ladders}

[Leonard Lewin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Lewin_%28telecommunications_engineer%29) discovered a remarkable and broad generalization of a number of classical relationships on the polylogarithm for special values. These are now called *polylogarithm ladders*. Define $\rho = \tfrac{1}{2} (\sqrt{5}-1)$ as the reciprocal of the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386). Then two simple examples of dilogarithm ladders are

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(\rho^6) = 4 \operatorname{Li}_2(\rho^3) + 3 \operatorname{Li}_2(\rho^2) - 6 \operatorname{Li}_2(\rho) + \tfrac {7}{30} \pi^2$$

given by (txt) and

$$\operatorname{Li}_2(\rho) = \tfrac{1}{10} \pi^2 - \ln^2\rho$$

given by [Landen](wikipedia://en/1400598). Polylogarithm ladders occur naturally and deeply in [K-theory](wikipedia://en/246748) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). Polylogarithm ladders provide the basis for the rapid computations of various mathematical constants by means of the [BBP algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BBP_algorithm) (Bailey Borwein Plouffe 1997).

:::

## Monodromy {#Monodromy}

The polylogarithm has two [branch points](wikipedia://en/483173); one at *z* = 1 and another at *z* = 0. The second branch point, at *z* = 0, is not visible on the main sheet of the polylogarithm; it becomes visible only when the function is [analytically continued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytically_continued) to its other sheets. The [monodromy](wikipedia://en/251250) group for the polylogarithm consists of the [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) classes of loops that wind around the two branch points. Denoting these two by *m*<sub>0</sub> and *m*<sub>1</sub>, the monodromy group has the [group presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation)

$$\langle m_0, m_1 \vert w = m_0 m_1 m^{-1}_0 m^{-1}_1, w m_1 = m_1 w \rangle.$$

For the special case of the dilogarithm, one also has that *wm*<sub>0</sub> = *m*<sub>0</sub>*w*, and the monodromy group becomes the [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518) (identifying *m*<sub>0</sub>, *m*<sub>1</sub> and *w* with *x*, *y*, *z*) (Vepstas 2008).
