# Gromov–Hausdorff convergence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gromov–Hausdorff convergence"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Gromov–Hausdorff convergence**, named after [Mikhail Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29) and [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989), is a notion for convergence of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) which is a generalization of [Hausdorff distance](wikipedia://en/429296).

:::

## Gromov–Hausdorff distance {#Gromov–Hausdorff_distance}

*Image: Gromov-Hausdorff distance.png.* How far and how near are some figures under the Gromov–Hausdorff distance.

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance was introduced by David Edwards in 1975,[^o3][^o4] and it was later rediscovered and generalized by [Mikhail Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29) in 1981.[^o5][^o6] This distance measures how far two [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) metric spaces are from being [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777). If *X* and *Y* are two compact metric spaces, then *d<sub>GH</sub>* (*X*, *Y*) is defined to be the [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of all numbers *d*<sub>*H*</sub>(*f*(*X*), *g*(*Y*)) for all (compact) metric spaces *M* and all isometric embeddings *f* : *X* → *M* and *g* : *Y* → *M*. Here *d*<sub>*H*</sub> denotes [Hausdorff distance](wikipedia://en/429296) between subsets in *M* and the *isometric embedding* is understood in the global sense, i.e. it must preserve all distances, not only infinitesimally small ones; for example no compact [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) admits such an embedding into [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of the same dimension.

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance turns the set of all isometry classes of compact metric spaces into a metric space, called Gromov–Hausdorff space, and it therefore defines a notion of convergence for [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) of compact metric spaces, called Gromov–Hausdorff convergence. A metric space to which such a sequence converges is called the Gromov–Hausdorff limit of the sequence.

## Some properties of Gromov–Hausdorff space {#Some_properties_of_Gromov–Hausdorff_space}

The Gromov–Hausdorff space is [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected), [complete](wikipedia://en/7832), and [separable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_%28topology%29).[^o8] It is also [geodesic](wikipedia://en/490861), i.e., any two of its points are the endpoints of a minimizing [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096).[^o9][^o10] In the global sense, the Gromov–Hausdorff space is totally heterogeneous, i.e., its isometry group is trivial,[^o11] but locally there are many nontrivial isometries.[^o12]

## Pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence {#Pointed_Gromov–Hausdorff_convergence}

The pointed Gromov–Hausdorff convergence is an analog of Gromov–Hausdorff convergence appropriate for non-compact spaces. A pointed metric space is a pair (*X*,*p*) consisting of a metric space *X* and point *p* in *X*. A sequence (*X<sub>n</sub>, p<sub>n</sub>*) of pointed metric spaces converges to a pointed metric space (*Y*, *p*) if, for each *R* > 0, the sequence of closed *R*-balls around *p<sub>n</sub>* in *X<sub>n</sub>* converges to the closed *R*-ball around *p* in *Y* in the usual Gromov–Hausdorff sense.[^o14]

## Applications {#Applications}

The notion of Gromov–Hausdorff convergence was used by Gromov to prove that any [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) with [polynomial growth](wikipedia://en/498727) is virtually nilpotent (i.e. it contains a [nilpotent subgroup](wikipedia://en/144569) of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581)). See [Gromov's theorem on groups of polynomial growth](wikipedia://en/635483). (Also see D. Edwards for an earlier work.) The key ingredient in the proof was the observation that for the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of a group with polynomial growth a sequence of rescalings converges in the pointed Gromov–Hausdorff sense.

Another simple and very useful result in [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243) is [Gromov's compactness theorem](wikipedia://en/6657292), which states that the set of Riemannian manifolds with [Ricci curvature](wikipedia://en/276582) ≥ *c* and [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) ≤ *D* is [relatively compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_compact) in the Gromov–Hausdorff metric. The limit spaces are metric spaces. Additional properties on the length spaces have been proven by [Cheeger](wikipedia://en/7343761) and [Colding](wikipedia://en/25826908).[^o17]

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance metric has been applied in the field of computer graphics and computational geometry to find correspondences between different shapes.[^o18] It also has been applied in the problem of [motion planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_planning) in robotics.[^o19]

The Gromov–Hausdorff distance has been used by [Sormani](wikipedia://en/45628888) to prove the stability of the Friedmann model in Cosmology. This model of cosmology is not stable with respect to smooth variations of the metric.[^o20]

In a special case, the concept of Gromov–Hausdorff limits is closely related to [large-deviations theory](wikipedia://en/3772909).[^o21]
