# Schläfli symbol

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Schläfli symbol"}

*Image: Dodecahedron.png.* The [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/7149361) is a regular polyhedron with Schläfli symbol {5,3}, having 3 [pentagons](wikipedia://en/20741014) around each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417#Of_a_polytope).

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Schläfli symbol** is a notation of the form {*p*,*q*,*r*, ...} that defines [regular polytopes and tessellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_regular_polytopes_and_compounds).

:::

The Schläfli symbol is named after the 19th-century Swiss mathematician [Ludwig Schläfli](wikipedia://en/1452408),[^n1] who generalized [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9697) to more than three dimensions and discovered all their convex regular polytopes, including the six that occur in four dimensions.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The Schläfli symbol is a [recursive](wikipedia://en/1047605) description, starting with {*p*} for a *p*-sided [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) that is [convex](wikipedia://en/6292). For example, {3} is an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285), {4} is a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29), {5} a convex [regular pentagon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_pentagon), etc.[^n2]

Regular [star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303) are not convex, and their Schläfli symbols $\{\tfrac{p}{q}\}$ contain [irreducible fractions](wikipedia://en/14822) $\tfrac{p}{q}$, where *p* is the number of vertices, and *q* is their [turning number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turning_number). Equivalently, $\{\tfrac{p}{q}\}$ is created from the vertices of *p*, connected every *q*. For example, $\{\tfrac{5}{2}\}$ is a [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544); $\{\tfrac{5}{1}\}$ is a [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014).

A [regular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/333299) that has *q* regular *p*-sided [polygon faces](wikipedia://en/149926) around each [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417#Of_a_polytope) is represented by {*p*,*q*}. For example, the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) has 3 squares around each vertex and is represented by {4,3}.

A regular [4-dimensional polytope](wikipedia://en/24979), with *r* {*p*,*q*} regular [polyhedral cells](wikipedia://en/149926) around each edge is represented by {*p*,*q*,*r*}. For example, a [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112), {4,3,3}, has 3 [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285), {4,3}, around an edge.

In general, a [regular polytope](wikipedia://en/518693) {*p*,*q*,*r*,...,*y*,*z*} has *z* {*p*,*q*,*r*, ... ,*y*} [facets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facet_%28mathematics%29) around every [peak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peak_%28geometry%29), where a peak is a [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417#Of_a_polytope) in a polyhedron, an edge in a 4-polytope, a [face](wikipedia://en/149926#Polygonal_face) in a 5-polytope, and an [(*n*−3)-face](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K-face) in an *n*-polytope.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

A regular polytope has a regular [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719). The vertex figure of a regular polytope {*p*,*q*,*r*, ... ,*y*,*z*} is {*q*,*r*, ... ,*y*,*z*}.

Regular polytopes can have [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303) elements, like the [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544), with symbol $\{\tfrac{5}{2}\}$, represented by the vertices of a [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) but connected alternately.

The Schläfli symbol can represent a finite [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron), an infinite [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) of [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), or an infinite tessellation of [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742), depending on the [angle defect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_defect) of the construction. A positive angle defect allows the vertex figure to *fold* into a higher dimension and loops back into itself as a polytope. A zero-angle defect tessellates space of the same dimension as the facets. A negative angle defect cannot exist in ordinary space, but can be constructed in hyperbolic space.

Usually, a facet or a vertex figure is assumed to be a finite polytope, but can sometimes itself be considered a tessellation.

A regular polytope also has a dual polytope, represented by the *Schläfli symbol* elements in reverse order. A self-dual regular polytope will have a symmetric Schläfli symbol.

In addition to describing Euclidean polytopes, Schläfli symbols can be used to describe spherical polytopes or spherical honeycombs.[^n3]

:::

## History and variations {#History_and_variations}

Schläfli's work was almost unknown in his lifetime, and his notation for describing polytopes was rediscovered independently by several others. In particular, [Thorold Gosset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thorold_Gosset) rediscovered the Schläfli symbol which he wrote as |*p*|*q*|*r*|...|*z*| rather than with brackets and commas as Schläfli did.[^n4]

Gosset's form has greater symmetry, so the number of dimensions is the number of vertical bars, and the symbol exactly includes the sub-symbols for facet and vertex figure. Gosset regarded |*p* as an operator, which can be applied to |*q*|...|*z*| to produce a polytope with *p*-gonal faces whose vertex figure is |*q*|...|*z*|.

## Cases {#Cases}

### Symmetry groups {#Symmetry_groups}

Schläfli symbols are closely related to (finite) [reflection](wikipedia://en/1390873) [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186), which correspond precisely to the finite [Coxeter groups](wikipedia://en/297004) and are specified with the same indices, but square brackets instead \[*p*,*q*,*r*,...\]. Such groups are often named by the regular polytopes they generate. For example, \[3,3\] is the Coxeter group for reflective [tetrahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2904472), \[3,4\] is reflective [octahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2900805), and \[3,5\] is reflective [icosahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2877844).

### Regular polygons (plane) {#Regular_polygons_(plane)}

*Image: regular star polygons.svg.* Regular convex and star polygons with 3 to 12 vertices labelled with their Schläfli symbols

The Schläfli symbol of a convex [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) with *p* edges is {*p*}. For example, a regular [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) is represented by {5}.

For nonconvex [star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303), the constructive notation $\{\tfrac{p}{q}\}$ is used, where *p* is the number of vertices and *q*−1 is the number of vertices skipped when drawing each edge of the star. For example, $\{\tfrac{5}{2}\}$ represents the [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544).

### Regular polyhedra (3 dimensions) {#Regular_polyhedra_(3_dimensions)}

The Schläfli symbol of a regular [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) is {*p*,*q*} if its [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) are *p*-gons, and each vertex is surrounded by *q* faces (the [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) is a *q*-gon).

For example, {5,3} is the regular [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407). It has pentagonal (5 edges) faces, and 3 pentagons around each vertex.

See the 5 convex [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905), the 4 nonconvex [Kepler-Poinsot polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler-Poinsot_polyhedra).

Topologically, a regular 2-dimensional [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) may be regarded as similar to a (3-dimensional) polyhedron, but such that the [angular defect](wikipedia://en/673575) is zero. Thus, Schläfli symbols may also be defined for regular [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671) of [Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9417) or [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/241291) space in a similar way as for polyhedra. The analogy holds for higher dimensions.

For example, the [hexagonal tiling](wikipedia://en/2863719) is represented by {6,3}.

### Regular 4-polytopes (4 dimensions) {#Regular_4-polytopes_(4_dimensions)}

The Schläfli symbol of a regular [4-polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) is of the form {*p*,*q*,*r*}. Its (two-dimensional) faces are regular *p*-gons ({*p*}), the cells are regular polyhedra of type {*p*,*q*}, the vertex figures are regular polyhedra of type {*q*,*r*}, and the edge figures are regular *r*-gons (type {*r*}).

See the six [convex regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_regular_4-polytope) and 10 [regular star 4-polytopes](wikipedia://en/505253#Nonconvex_forms_%284D%29).

For example, the [120-cell](wikipedia://en/717358) is represented by {5,3,3}. It is made of [dodecahedral](wikipedia://en/7149361) cells {5,3}, and has 3 cells around each edge.

There is one regular tessellation of Euclidean 3-space: the [cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3733318), with a Schläfli symbol of {4,3,4}, made of cubic cells and 4 cubes around each edge.

There are also 4 regular compact hyperbolic tessellations including {5,3,4}, the [hyperbolic small dodecahedral honeycomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_small_dodecahedral_honeycomb), which fills space with [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) cells.

If a 4-polytope's symbol is palindromic (e.g. {3,3,3} or {3,4,3}), its bitruncation will only have truncated forms of the vertex figure as cells.

### Regular *n*-polytopes (higher dimensions) {#Regular_n-polytopes_(higher_dimensions)}

For higher-dimensional [regular polytopes](wikipedia://en/518693), the Schläfli symbol is defined recursively as $\{p_{1}, p_{2}, ..., p_{n - 1}\}$ if the [facets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facet_%28mathematics%29) have Schläfli symbol $\{p_{1}, p_{2}, ..., p_{n - 2}\}$ and the [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719) have Schläfli symbol $\{p_{2}, p_{3}, ..., p_{n - 1}\}$.

A vertex figure of a facet of a polytope and a facet of a vertex figure of the same polytope are the same: $\{p_{2}, p_{3}, ..., p_{n - 2}\}$.

There are only 3 regular polytopes in 5 dimensions and above: the [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781), {3, 3, 3, ..., 3}; the [cross-polytope](wikipedia://en/716401), {3, 3, ..., 3, 4}; and the [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783), {4, 3, 3, ..., 3}. There are no non-convex regular polytopes above 4 dimensions.

*Image: Schläfli symbol of regular polytopes.gif.*

### Dual polytopes {#Dual_polytopes}

If a polytope of dimension n≥2 has Schläfli symbol {{{thin space}}*p*<sub>1</sub>, *p*<sub>2</sub>, ..., *p*<sub>*n*−1</sub>} then its [dual](wikipedia://en/8815) has Schläfli symbol {{{thin space}}*p*<sub>*n*−1</sub>, ..., *p*<sub>2</sub>, *p*<sub>1</sub>}.

If the sequence is [palindromic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palindromic), i.e. the same forwards and backwards, the polytope is *self-dual*. Every regular polytope in 2 dimensions (polygon) is self-dual.

### Polyhedra and tilings {#Polyhedra_and_tilings}

[Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter) expanded his usage of the Schläfli symbol to [quasiregular polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasiregular_polyhedra) by adding a vertical dimension to the symbol. It was a starting point toward the more general [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram). [Norman Johnson](wikipedia://en/715527) simplified the notation for vertical symbols with an *r* prefix. The t-notation is the most general, and directly corresponds to the rings of the Coxeter diagram. Symbols have a corresponding [alternation](wikipedia://en/4349117), replacing *rings* with *holes* in a Coxeter diagram and *h* prefix standing for *half*, construction limited by the requirement that neighboring branches must be even-ordered and cuts the symmetry order in half. A related operator, *a* for *altered*, is shown with two nested holes, represents a compound polyhedra with both alternated halves, retaining the original full symmetry. A [snub](wikipedia://en/9428917) is a half form of a truncation, and a holosnub is both halves of an alternated truncation.
