# Periodogram

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Periodogram"}

In [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), a **periodogram** is an estimate of the [spectral density](wikipedia://en/202672) of a signal. The term was coined by [Arthur Schuster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Schuster) in 1898.[^o13] Today, the periodogram is a component of more sophisticated methods (see [spectral estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_estimation)). It is the most common tool for examining the amplitude vs frequency characteristics of [FIR filters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIR_filter) and [window functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Window_functions). [FFT spectrum analyzers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_analyzer) are also implemented as a time-sequence of periodograms.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

There are at least two different definitions in use today.[^o19] One of them involves time-averaging,[^o17] and one does not.[^o16] Time-averaging is also the purview of other articles ([Bartlett's method](wikipedia://en/17220224) and [Welch's method](wikipedia://en/549018)). This article is not about time-averaging. The definition of interest here is that the power spectral density of a continuous function, $x(t)$, is the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of its auto-correlation function (see [Cross-correlation theorem](wikipedia://en/52247#Cross-correlation_theorem), [Spectral density](wikipedia://en/202672#Power_spectral_density), and [Wiener–Khinchin theorem](wikipedia://en/3350111)):

$$\mathcal{F}\{x(t)\circledast x^*(-t)\} = X(f)\cdot X^*(f) = \left| X(f) \right|^2.$$

:::

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Computation"}

## Computation {#Computation}

*Image: Periodogram.svg.* A power spectrum (magnitude-squared) of two sinusoidal basis functions, calculated by the periodogram method.

*Image: Periodogram windows.svg.* Two power spectra (magnitude-squared) (rectangular and Hamming [window functions](wikipedia://en/244097) plus background noise), calculated by the periodogram method.

For sufficiently small values of parameter $T,$ an arbitrarily-accurate approximation for $X(f)$ can be observed in the region $-\tfrac{1}{2T} < f < \tfrac{1}{2T}$ of the function:

$$X_{1/T}(f)\ \triangleq \sum_{k=-\infty}^{\infty} X\left(f - k/T\right),$$

which is precisely determined by the samples $x(nT)$ that span the non-zero duration of $x(t)$ (see [Discrete-time Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/1216914)).

And for sufficiently large values of parameter $N$, $X_{1/T}(f)$ can be evaluated at an arbitrarily close frequency by a summation of the form:

$$X_{1/T}\left(\tfrac{k}{NT}\right) = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \underbrace{T\cdot x(nT)}_{x[n]}\cdot e^{-i 2\pi \frac{kn}{N}},$$

where $k$ is an integer. The periodicity of $e^{-i 2\pi \frac{kn}{N}}$ allows this to be written very simply in terms of a [Discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811):

$$X_{1/T}\left(\tfrac{k}{NT}\right) = \underbrace{\sum_{n} x_{_N}[n]\cdot e^{-i 2\pi \frac{kn}{N}},}_\text{DFT} \quad \scriptstyle{\text{(sum over any }n\text{-sequence of length }N)},$$

where $x_{_N}$ is a periodic summation: $x_{_N}[n]\ \triangleq \sum_{m=-\infty}^{\infty} x[n - mN].$

When evaluated for all integers, $k$, between 0 and $N$-1, the array:

$$S\left(\tfrac{k}{NT}\right) = \left| \sum_{n} x_{_N}[n]\cdot e^{-i 2\pi \frac{kn}{N}} \right|^2$$

is a *periodogram*.[^o16][^o21][^o22]

:::

::: {#o7 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Periodogram for Proxima Centauri b.jpg.* Periodogram for [Proxima Centauri b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proxima_Centauri_b) is shown at the bottom.[^o8]

When a periodogram is used to examine the detailed characteristics of an [FIR filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FIR_filter) or [window function](wikipedia://en/244097), the parameter $N$ is chosen to be several multiples of the non-zero duration of the $x[n]$ sequence, which is called *zero-padding* (see Discrete-time Fourier transform).$N$ is designated $NFFT$ in the Matlab and Octave applications. When it is used to implement a [filter bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filter_bank), $N$ is several sub-multiples of the non-zero duration of the $x[n]$ sequence (see Discrete-time Fourier transform).

One of the periodogram's deficiencies is that the variance at a given [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) does not decrease as the number of samples used in the computation increases. It does not provide the averaging needed to analyze noiselike signals or even sinusoids at low signal-to-noise ratios. Window functions and filter impulse responses are noiseless, but many other signals require more sophisticated methods of [spectral estimation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_estimation). Two of the alternatives use periodograms as part of the process:

- The *method of averaged periodograms*,[^o12] more commonly known as [Welch's method](wikipedia://en/549018),[^o14][^o15] divides a long x\[n\] sequence into multiple shorter, and possibly overlapping, subsequences. It computes a windowed periodogram of each one, and computes an array average, i.e. an array where each element is an average of the corresponding elements of all the periodograms. For [stationary processes](wikipedia://en/329898), this reduces the noise variance of each element by approximately a factor equal to the reciprocal of the number of periodograms.
- [Smoothing](wikipedia://en/4155662) is an averaging technique in frequency, instead of time. The smoothed periodogram is sometimes referred to as a *spectral plot*.[^o18][^o20]

Periodogram-based techniques introduce small biases that are unacceptable in some applications. Other techniques that do not rely on periodograms are presented in the [spectral density estimation](wikipedia://en/13070117) article.
