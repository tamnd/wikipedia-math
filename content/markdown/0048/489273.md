# Artinian ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Artinian ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), an **Artinian ring** (sometimes **Artin ring**) is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) that satisfies the [descending chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descending_chain_condition) on (one-sided) [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977); that is, there is no infinite descending sequence of ideals. Artinian rings are named after [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351), who first discovered that the descending chain condition for ideals simultaneously generalizes [finite rings](wikipedia://en/23706953) and rings that are [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) over [fields](wikipedia://en/10603). The definition of Artinian rings may be restated by interchanging the descending chain condition with an equivalent notion: the [minimum condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_condition).

:::

Precisely, a ring is **left Artinian** if it satisfies the descending chain condition on left ideals, **right Artinian** if it satisfies the descending chain condition on right ideals, and **Artinian** or **two-sided Artinian** if it is both left and right Artinian.[^n1] For [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) the left and right definitions coincide, but in general they are distinct from each other.

The [Wedderburn–Artin theorem](wikipedia://en/397264) characterizes every [simple](wikipedia://en/443387) Artinian ring as a [ring of matrices](wikipedia://en/487541) over a [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067). This implies that a simple ring is left Artinian [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is right Artinian.

The same definition and terminology can be applied to [modules](wikipedia://en/276410), with ideals replaced by [submodules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule).

Although the descending chain condition appears dual to the [ascending chain condition](wikipedia://en/3189), in rings it is in fact the stronger condition. Specifically, a consequence of the [Akizuki–Hopkins–Levitzki theorem](wikipedia://en/29435296) is that a left (resp. right) Artinian ring is automatically a left (resp. right) [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129). This is not true for general modules; that is, an [Artinian module](wikipedia://en/542336) need not be a [Noetherian module](wikipedia://en/542431).

## Examples and counterexamples {#Examples_and_counterexamples}

- An [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) is Artinian if and only if it is a field.
- A ring with finitely many, say left, ideals is left Artinian. In particular, a [finite ring](wikipedia://en/23706953) (e.g., $\mathbb{Z}/n \mathbb{Z}$) is left and right Artinian.
- Let $k$ be a field. Then $k[t]/(t^n)$ is Artinian for every positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *n*.
- Similarly, $k[x,y]/(x^2, y^3, xy^2) = k \oplus k\cdot x \oplus k \cdot y \oplus k\cdot xy \oplus k \cdot y^2$ is an Artinian ring with [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) $(x,y)$.
- Let $x$ be an endomorphism between a finite-dimensional vector space *V*. Then the subalgebra $A \subset \operatorname{End}(V)$ generated by $x$ is a commutative Artinian ring.
- If *I* is a [nonzero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) ideal of a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) *A*, then $A/I$ is a [principal](wikipedia://en/6961771) Artinian ring.[^n2]
- For each $n \ge 1$, the full [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) $M_n(R)$ over a left Artinian (resp. left Noetherian) ring *R* is left Artinian (resp. left Noetherian).[^n3]

The following two are examples of non-Artinian rings.

- If *R* is any ring, then the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) *R*\[*x*\] is not Artinian, since the ideal generated by $x^{n+1}$ is (properly) contained in the ideal generated by $x^n$ for all [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) *n*. In contrast, if *R* is Noetherian so is *R*\[*x*\] by the [Hilbert basis theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_basis_theorem).
- The ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ is a Noetherian ring but is not Artinian.

:::

## Modules over Artinian rings {#Modules_over_Artinian_rings}

Let *M* be a left module over a left Artinian ring. Then the following are equivalent ([Hopkins' theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopkins'_theorem)): (i) *M* is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430), (ii) *M* has [finite length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_length) (i.e., has [composition series](wikipedia://en/297493)), (iii) *M* is Noetherian, (iv) *M* is Artinian.[^n4]

## Commutative Artinian rings {#Commutative_Artinian_rings}

Let *A* be a commutative Noetherian ring with unity. Then the following are equivalent.

- *A* is Artinian.
- *A* is a finite [product](wikipedia://en/25063) of commutative Artinian [local rings](wikipedia://en/153391).[^n5]
- *A* / nil(*A*) is a [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring), where nil(*A*) is the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636) of *A*.
- Every finitely generated module over *A* has finite length. (see above)
- *A* has [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) zero.[^n6] (In particular, the nilradical is the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656) since [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) are maximal.)
- $\operatorname{Spec}A$ is finite and discrete.
- $\operatorname{Spec}A$ is discrete.[^n7]

Let *k* be a field and *A* a finitely generated *k*-[algebra](wikipedia://en/191788). Then *A* is Artinian if and only if *A* is finitely generated as a *k*-module.

An Artinian local ring is complete. A [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635) and [localization](wikipedia://en/252216) of an Artinian ring is Artinian.

## Simple Artinian ring {#Simple_Artinian_ring}

One version of the [Wedderburn–Artin theorem](wikipedia://en/397264) states that a [simple](wikipedia://en/443387) Artinian ring *A* is a matrix ring over a division ring. Indeed,[^n8] let *I* be a minimal (nonzero) right ideal of *A*, which exists since *A* is Artinian (and the rest of the proof does not use the fact that *A* is Artinian). Then, since $AI$ is a two-sided ideal, $AI = A$ since *A* is simple. Thus, we can choose $a_i \in A$ so that $1 \in a_1 I + \cdots + a_k I$. Assume *k* is minimal with respect to that property. Now consider the map of right *A*-modules:

$$\begin{cases} I^{\oplus k} \to A, \\ (y_1, \dots, y_k) \mapsto a_1y_1 + \cdots + a_k y_k \end{cases}$$

This map is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), since the image is a right ideal and contains *1*. If it is not [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective), then, say, $a_1y_1 = a_2y_2 + \cdots + a_k y_k$ with nonzero $y_1$. Then, by the minimality of *I*, we have $y_1 A = I$. It follows:

$$a_1 I = a_1 y_1 A \subset a_2 I + \cdots + a_k I,$$

which contradicts the minimality of *k*. Hence, $I^{\oplus k} \simeq A$ and thus $A \simeq \operatorname{End}_A(A) \simeq M_k(\operatorname{End}_A(I))$.
