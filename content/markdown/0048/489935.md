# Isomorphism of categories

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isomorphism of categories"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), two categories *C* and *D* are **isomorphic** if there exist [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) *F* : *C* → *D* and *G* : *D* → *C* that are mutually inverse to each other, i.e. *FG* = 1<sub>*D*</sub> (the identity functor on *D*) and *GF* = 1<sub>*C*</sub>.[^o2] This means that both the [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) and the [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) of *C* and *D* stand in a [one-to-one correspondence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_correspondence) with each other. Two isomorphic categories share all properties defined solely in category theory; for all practical purposes, they are identical and differ only in the notation of their objects and morphisms.

:::

Isomorphism of categories is a strong condition and is rarely satisfied in practice. Much more important is the notion of [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051); roughly speaking, for an equivalence of categories, we don't require that $FG$ be *equal* to $1_D$, but only *[naturally isomorphic](wikipedia://en/45022)* to $1_D$, and likewise that $GF$ be naturally isomorphic to $1_C$.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

As is true for any notion of [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), we have the following general properties formally similar to an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259):

- any category *C* is isomorphic to itself
- if *C* is isomorphic to *D*, then *D* is isomorphic to *C*
- if *C* is isomorphic to *D* and *D* is isomorphic to *E*, then *C* is isomorphic to *E*.

A functor *F* : *C* → *D* yields an isomorphism of categories if and only if it is [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) on objects and [morphism sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom_set).[^o2] This criterion can be convenient as it avoids constructing the inverse functor *G*.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- Consider a finite [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G*, a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *k* and the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223#Group_algebra_over_a_finite_group) *kG*. The category of *k*-linear [group representations](wikipedia://en/12695) of *G* is isomorphic to the category of [left modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over *kG*. The isomorphism can be described as follows: given a group representation ρ : *G* → GL(*V*), where *V* is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over *k*, GL(*V*) is the group of its *k*-linear [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160), and ρ is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396), we turn *V* into a left *kG* module by defining $$\left(\sum_{g\in G} a_g g\right) v = \sum_{g\in G} a_g \rho(g)(v)$$ for every *v* in *V* and every element Σ *a<sub>g</sub>* *g* in *kG*. Conversely, given a left *kG* module *M*, then *M* is a *k* vector space, and multiplication with an element *g* of *G* yields a *k*-linear automorphism of *M* (since *g* is invertible in *kG*), which describes a group homomorphism *G* → GL(*M*). (There are still several things to check: both these assignments are functors, i.e. they can be applied to maps between group representations resp. *kG* modules, and they are inverse to each other, both on objects and on morphisms.) See also Representation theory of finite groups#Representations, modules and the convolution algebra.
- Every [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) can be viewed as a [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521) with a single object. The [functor category](wikipedia://en/407039) of all [additive functors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_functor) from this category to the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748) is isomorphic to the category of left modules over the ring.
- Another isomorphism of categories arises in the [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959) theory: Boolean algebras is isomorphic to the category of [Boolean rings](wikipedia://en/54356). Given a Boolean algebra *B*, we turn *B* into a Boolean ring by using the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) as addition and the meet operation $\land$ as multiplication. Conversely, given a Boolean ring *R*, we define the join operation by *a*$\lor$*b* = *a* + *b* + *ab*, and the meet operation as multiplication. Again, both of these assignments can be extended to morphisms to yield functors, which are inverse to each other.
- If *C* is a category with an initial object s, then the [slice category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slice_category) (*s*↓*C*) is isomorphic to *C*. [Dually](wikipedia://en/310885), if *t* is a terminal object in *C*, the functor category (*C*↓*t*) is isomorphic to *C*. Similarly, if **1** is the category with one object and only its identity morphism (in fact, **1** is the [terminal category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object)), and *C* is any category, then the functor category *C*<sup>**1**</sup>, with objects functors *c*: **1** → *C*, selecting an object *c*∈Ob(*C*), and arrows natural transformations *f*: *c* → *d* between these functors, selecting a morphism *f*: *c* → *d* in *C*, is again isomorphic to *C*.

:::
