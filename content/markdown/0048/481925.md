# Anecdotal evidence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Anecdotal evidence"}

**Anecdotal evidence** (or **anecdata**[^o2]) is [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence) based on descriptions and reports of individual, personal experiences, or observations,[^o3][^o4] collected in a non-[systematic](wikipedia://en/2994579) manner.[^o5]

:::

The term *anecdotal* encompasses a variety of forms of evidence, including personal experiences, self-reported claims,[^o4] eyewitness accounts of others,[^o6] and those from fictional sources, making it a broad category that can lead to confusion due to its varied interpretations. Anecdotal evidence can be true or false but is not usually subjected to [scholarly methods](wikipedia://en/772062), [scientific methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method), or rules of legal, historical, academic, or [intellectual rigor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intellectual_rigor), meaning there are few or no safeguards against [fabrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fabrication_%28lie%29) or inaccuracy.[^o3] However, the use of anecdotal reports in [advertising](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advertising) or promotion of a product, service, or idea may be considered a [testimonial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testimonial), which is highly regulated in certain jurisdictions.[^o7]

The persuasiveness of anecdotal evidence compared to statistical evidence has been debated; some studies argue that people tend to overvalue anecdotal evidence, while others argue the opposite.[^o8][^o9][^o10][^o11][^o12]

## Scientific context {#Scientific_context}

In science, definitions of anecdotal evidence include:

- "casual observations/indications rather than rigorous or [scientific analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_analysis)"[^o14]
- "information passed along by word-of-mouth but not documented scientifically"[^o15]
- "evidence that comes from an individual experience. This may be the experience of a person with an illness or the experience of a practitioner based on one or more patients outside a formal research study"[^o16]
- "the report of an experience by one or more persons that is not objectively documented or an experience or outcome that occurred outside of a controlled environment"[^o17]

Anecdotal evidence may be considered within the scope of [scientific methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method). Some anecdotal evidence can be both empirical and verifiable, e.g., [case studies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_studies) in medicine. Other anecdotal evidence does not qualify as scientific evidence because its nature prevents it from being investigated by the scientific method, such as [folklore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folklore) or intentionally fictional anecdotes. Anecdotal evidence is considered the least certain type of [scientific literature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_literature).[^o18] Researchers may use anecdotal evidence for suggesting new [hypotheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis) but never as validating evidence.[^o19][^o20]

Anecdotal evidence varies in formality. For instance, in medicine, published anecdotal evidence by a doctor, such as a [case report](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_report), undergoes formal [peer review](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peer_review).[^o21] Although such evidence is seen as inconclusive, researchers sometimes regard it as an invitation to more rigorous scientific study.[^o22] For instance, one study found that 35 of 47 anecdotal reports of drug side effects were later sustained as "clearly correct".[^o23]

Where only one or a few anecdotes are presented, they risk being unreliable due to [cherry-picking](wikipedia://en/329277) or otherwise [non-representative](wikipedia://en/160361) sampling.[^o24][^o25] Similarly, psychologists have found that due to [cognitive bias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_bias), people are more likely to remember notable or unusual examples.[^o26] Thus, anecdotal evidence is not necessarily representative of a typical experience even when accurate. Determination of whether an anecdote is typical requires [statistical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical) evidence.[^o27] Misuse of anecdotal evidence in the form of **argument from anecdote** is an [informal fallacy](wikipedia://en/1655450)[^o28] and is sometimes referred to as the "person who" or **anecdotal fallacy**, with statements like "I know a person who..." or "I know of a case where...". This places undue weight on possibly atypical experiences of close peers. If an anecdote illustrates a desired conclusion rather than a logical conclusion, it is considered a [faulty generalization](wikipedia://en/228851).[^o29]

In medicine, anecdotal evidence may be subject to [the placebo effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placebo_effect).[^o30]

## Legal {#Legal}

In the legal sphere, anecdotal evidence, if it meets certain legal requirements and is admitted as [testimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testimony), is a common form of evidence in court. In many cases, anecdotal evidence is the only evidence presented at trial.[^o32]

For testimony to be considered evidence, it must be given [under oath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Under_oath), where the individual swears they will only testify to their own words and actions. Someone intentionally lying under oath is subject to [perjury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perjury). However, these legal rigors do not make testimony in a court of law equal to [scientific evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_evidence). Testimony about another person's experiences or words is termed [hearsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearsay) and is usually not admissible. However, hearsay that is not objected to by a judge is considered evidence for a [jury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jury). This means trials contain a considerable amount of anecdotal evidence that juries find relevant. [Eyewitness testimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eyewitness_testimony), a type of anecdotal evidence, is considered the most compelling form of evidence by a jury.[^o33]
