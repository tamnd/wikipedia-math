# Paley graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paley graph"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Paley graphs** are [undirected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) constructed from the members of a suitable [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) by connecting pairs of elements that differ by a [quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091). The Paley graphs form an infinite family of [conference graphs](wikipedia://en/7769842), which yield an infinite family of symmetric [conference matrices](wikipedia://en/7770047). Paley graphs allow [graph-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph-theoretic) tools to be applied to the [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) of quadratic residues, and have interesting properties that make them useful in graph theory more generally.

:::

Paley graphs are named after [Raymond Paley](wikipedia://en/2788568). They are closely related to the [Paley construction](wikipedia://en/6899907) for constructing [Hadamard matrices](wikipedia://en/507553) from quadratic residues. They were introduced as graphs independently by (Sachs 1962) and (Erdős Rényi 1963). [Sachs](wikipedia://en/15095266) was interested in them for their self-complementarity properties, while [Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) and [Rényi](wikipedia://en/614988) studied their symmetries.

**Paley digraphs** are [directed](wikipedia://en/19721986) analogs of Paley graphs that yield antisymmetric [conference matrices](wikipedia://en/7770047). They were introduced by (Graham Spencer 1971) (independently of Sachs, Erdős, and Rényi) as a way of constructing [tournaments](wikipedia://en/1427502) with a property previously known to be held only by random tournaments: in a Paley digraph, every small [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of vertices is dominated by some other vertex.

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *q* be a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) such that $q \equiv 1 \pmod 4$. That is, *q* should either be an arbitrary power of a prime congruent to 1 mod 4 (a [Pythagorean prime](wikipedia://en/2669448)) or an even power of an odd non-Pythagorean prime. This choice of *q* implies that in the unique finite field **F**<sub>*q*</sub> of order *q*, the element −1 has a square root.

Now let *V* = **F**<sub>*q*</sub> and let

$$E= \left \{\{a,b\} \ : \ a-b\in (\mathbf{F}_q^{\times})^2 \right \}.$$

If a pair {*a*,*b*} is included in *E*, it is included under either ordering of its two elements. For, *a* − *b* = −(*b* − *a*), and −1 is a square, from which it follows that *a* − *b* is a square [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) *b* − *a* is a square.

By definition *G* = (*V*, *E*) is the Paley graph of order *q*.

The sequence of orders of the Paley graphs begins

1, 5, 9, 13, 17, 25, 29, 37, 41, 49, 53, 61, 73, ...

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

For *q* = 13, the field **F**<sub>*q*</sub> is just integer arithmetic modulo 13. The numbers with square roots mod 13 are:

- ±1 (square roots ±1 for +1, ±5 for −1)
- ±3 (square roots ±4 for +3, ±6 for −3)
- ±4 (square roots ±2 for +4, ±3 for −4).

Thus, in the Paley graph, we form a vertex for each of the integers in the range \[0,12\], and connect each such integer *x* to six neighbors: *x* ± 1 (mod 13), *x* ± 3 (mod 13), and *x* ± 4 (mod 13).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The Paley graphs are [self-complementary](wikipedia://en/15093884): the complement of any Paley graph is isomorphic to it. One isomorphism is via the mapping that takes a vertex $x$ to $xk\pmod{q}$, where $k$ is any quadratic nonresidue $\bmod q$.

Paley graphs are [strongly regular graphs](wikipedia://en/1399856), with parameters

$$srg \left (q, \tfrac{1}{2}(q-1),\tfrac{1}{4}(q-5),\tfrac{1}{4}(q-1) \right ).$$

This in fact follows from the fact that the graph is [arc-transitive](wikipedia://en/1577896) and self-complementary. The strongly regular graphs with parameters of this form (for an arbitrary $q$) are called [conference graphs](wikipedia://en/7769842), so the Paley graphs form an infinite family of conference graphs. The [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) of a conference graph, such as a Paley graph, can be used to construct a [conference matrix](wikipedia://en/7770047), and vice versa. These are matrices whose coefficients are $\pm1$, with zero on the diagaonal, that give a scalar multiple of the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) when multiplied by their transpose.

The eigenvalues of Paley graphs are $\tfrac{1}{2}(q-1)$ (with multiplicity 1) and $\tfrac{1}{2} (-1 \pm \sqrt{q})$ (both with multiplicity $\tfrac{1}{2}(q-1)$). They can be calculated using the [quadratic Gauss sum](wikipedia://en/595898) or by using the theory of strongly regular graphs.

When $q$ is prime, the associated Paley graph is a [Hamiltonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) [circulant graph](wikipedia://en/3498787).

Paley graphs are *quasi-random*: the number of times each possible constant-order graph occurs as a subgraph of a Paley graph is (in the limit for large $q$) the same as for random graphs, and large sets of vertices have approximately the same number of edges as they would in random graphs.

- The Paley graph of order 9 is a [locally linear graph](wikipedia://en/59938063), a [rook's graph](wikipedia://en/7324959), and the graph of the [3-3 duoprism](wikipedia://en/41745711).
- The Paley graph of order 13 has [book thickness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_thickness) 4 and [queue number](wikipedia://en/46183215) 3.
- The Paley graph of order 17 is the unique largest graph *G* such that neither *G* nor its complement contains a complete 4-vertex subgraph. It follows that the [Ramsey number](wikipedia://en/157175) *R*(4, 4) = 18.
- The Paley graph of order 101 is currently the largest known graph *G* such that neither *G* nor its complement contains a complete 6-vertex subgraph.
- Sasukara et al. (1993) use Paley graphs to generalize the construction of the [Horrocks–Mumford bundle](wikipedia://en/3102767).

## Paley digraphs {#Paley_digraphs}

Let *q* be a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) such that *q* = 3 (mod 4). Thus, the finite field of order *q*, **F**<sub>*q*</sub>, has no square root of −1. Consequently, for each pair (*a*,*b*) of distinct elements of **F**<sub>*q*</sub>, either *a* − *b* or *b* − *a*, but not both, is a square. The **Paley digraph** is the [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) with vertex set *V* = **F**<sub>*q*</sub> and arc set

$$A = \left \{(a,b)\in \mathbf{F}_q\times\mathbf{F}_q \ : \ b-a\in (\mathbf{F}_q^{\times})^2 \right \}.$$

The Paley digraph is a [tournament](wikipedia://en/1427502) because each pair of distinct vertices is linked by an arc in one and only one direction.

The Paley digraph leads to the construction of some antisymmetric [conference matrices](wikipedia://en/7770047) and [biplane geometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biplane_geometries).

:::

## Genus {#Genus}

*Image: Paley-13 torus.svg.* Torus embedding of the order-13 Paley graph, obtained by gluing each pair of parallel sides of a hexagon

The six neighbors of each vertex in the Paley graph of order 13 are connected in a cycle; that is, the graph is [locally cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28graph_theory%29). Therefore, this graph can be embedded as a [Whitney triangulation](wikipedia://en/1440976) of a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), in which every face is a triangle and every triangle is a face. More generally, if any Paley graph of order *q* could be embedded so that all its faces are triangles, we could calculate the genus of the resulting surface via the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) as $\tfrac{1}{24}(q^2 - 13q + 24)$. [Bojan Mohar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bojan_Mohar) conjectures that the minimum genus of a surface into which a Paley graph can be embedded is near this bound in the case that *q* is a square, and questions whether such a bound might hold more generally. Specifically, Mohar conjectures that the Paley graphs of square order can be embedded into surfaces with genus

$$(q^2 - 13q + 24)\left(\tfrac{1}{24} + o(1)\right),$$

where the o(1) term can be any function of *q* that goes to zero in the limit as *q* goes to infinity.

(White 2001) finds embeddings of the Paley graphs of order *q* ≡ 1 (mod 8) that are highly symmetric and self-dual, generalizing a natural embedding of the Paley graph of order 9 as a 3×3 square grid on a torus. However the genus of White's embeddings is higher by approximately a factor of three than Mohar's conjectured bound.
