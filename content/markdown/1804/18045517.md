# Proof without words

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proof without words"}

*Image: Nicomachus\_theorem\_3D.svg.* Proof without words of the [Nicomachus theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus_theorem) ((Gulley 2010)) that the sum of the first $n$ [cubes](wikipedia://en/451445) is the [square](wikipedia://en/213919) of the $n$th [triangular number](wikipedia://en/213915)

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a ***proof without words*** (or **visual proof**) is an illustration of an [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) or mathematical statement which can be demonstrated as [self-evident](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-evident) by a diagram without any accompanying explanatory text. Such proofs can be considered more [elegant](wikipedia://en/18974136) than formal or [mathematically rigorous](wikipedia://en/655974) proofs due to their self-evident nature.[^o3] When the diagram demonstrates a particular case of a general statement, to be a proof, it must be generalisable.[^o4]

:::

A proof without words is not the same as a [mathematical proof](wikipedia://en/82285), because it omits the details of the logical argument it illustrates. However, it can provide valuable intuitions to the viewer that can help them formulate or better understand a true proof.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Sum of odd numbers {#Sum_of_odd_numbers}

*Image: Proofwithoutwords.svg.* A proof without words for the sum of odd numbers theorem

The statement that the sum of all positive [odd numbers](wikipedia://en/143135) up to 2*n* − 1 is a [perfect square](wikipedia://en/213919)—more specifically, the perfect square *n*<sup>2</sup>—can be demonstrated by a proof without words.[^o8]

In one corner of a grid, a single block represents 1, the first square. That can be wrapped on two sides by a strip of three blocks (the next odd number) to make a 2 × 2 block: 4, the second square. Adding a further five blocks makes a 3 × 3 block: 9, the third square. This process can be continued indefinitely.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Pythagorean theorem"}

### Pythagorean theorem {#Pythagorean_theorem}

Pythagorean theorem#Rearrangement proofs

*Image: Diagram of Pythagoras Theorem.png.* Rearrangement proof of the Pythagorean theorem. The uncovered area of gray space remains constant before and after the rearrangement of the triangles: on the left it is shown to equal ***c²***, and on the right ***a²+b²***.

The [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) that $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$ can be proven without words.[^o11]

One method of doing so is to visualise a larger square of sides $a+b$, with four right-angled triangles of sides $a$, $b$ and $c$ in its corners, such that the space in the middle is a diagonal square with an area of $c^2$. The four triangles can be rearranged within the larger square to split its unused space into two squares of $a^2$ and $b^2$.[^o12]

### Jensen's inequality {#Jensen's_inequality}

*Image: Jensen graph.svg.* A graphical proof of Jensen's inequality

[Jensen's inequality](wikipedia://en/297811) can also be proven graphically. A dashed curve along the *X* axis is the hypothetical distribution of *X*, while a dashed curve along the *Y* axis is the corresponding distribution of *Y* values. The convex mapping *Y*(*X*) increasingly "stretches" the distribution for increasing values of *X*.[^o15]

## Usage {#Usage}

*[Mathematics Magazine](wikipedia://en/4230329)* and *[The College Mathematics Journal](wikipedia://en/21340776)* run a regular feature titled "Proof without words" containing, as the title suggests, proofs without words.[^o8] The Art of Problem Solving and [USAMTS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/USAMTS) websites run [Java applets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_applet) illustrating proofs without words.[^o17][^o18]

## Compared to formal proofs {#Compared_to_formal_proofs}

For a proof to be accepted by the mathematical community, it must [logically](wikipedia://en/19636) show how the statement it aims to prove follows totally and inevitably from a set of [assumptions](wikipedia://en/928).[^o20] A proof without words might imply such an argument, but it does not make one directly, so it cannot take the place of a formal proof where one is required.[^o21][^o22] Rather, [mathematicians](wikipedia://en/18902) use proofs without words as illustrations and teaching aids for ideas that have already been proven formally.[^o23][^o24]
