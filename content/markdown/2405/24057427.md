# Samuelson's inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Samuelson's inequality"}

*Image: Paul Samuelson.jpg.*

In [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **Samuelson's inequality**, named after the economist [Paul Samuelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Samuelson),[^o2] also called the **Laguerre–Samuelson inequality**,[^o3][^o4] after the mathematician [Edmond Laguerre](wikipedia://en/1011080), states that every one of any collection *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>, is within $\sqrt{n - 1}$ uncorrected sample [standard deviations](wikipedia://en/27590) of their sample mean.

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Statement of the inequality"}

## Statement of the inequality {#Statement_of_the_inequality}

If we let

$$\overline{x} = \frac{x_1+\cdots+x_n}{n}$$

be the sample [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) and

$$s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \overline{x})^2 }$$

be the standard deviation of the sample, then

$\overline{x} - s\sqrt{n-1} \le x_j \le \overline{x} + s\sqrt{n-1}\qquad \text{for } j = 1,\dots,n.$[^o7]

Equality holds on the left (or right) for $x_j$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) all the *n* − 1 $x_i$s other than $x_j$ are equal to each other and greater (smaller) than $x_j.$[^o3]

If you instead define $s = \sqrt{\frac{1}{n-1} \sum_{i=1}^n (x_i - \overline{x})^2 }$ then the inequality $\overline{x} - s\sqrt{n-1} \le x_j \le \overline{x} + s\sqrt{n-1}$ still applies and can be slightly tightened to $\overline{x} - s\tfrac{n-1}{\sqrt{n}} \le x_j \le \overline{x} + s\tfrac{n-1}{\sqrt{n}}.$

:::

::: {#o9 .statement cue=named-heading name="Comparison to Chebyshev's inequality"}

## Comparison to Chebyshev's inequality {#Comparison_to_Chebyshev's_inequality}

[Chebyshev's inequality](wikipedia://en/156533) locates a certain fraction of the data within certain bounds, while Samuelson's inequality locates *all* the data points within certain bounds.

The bounds given by Chebyshev's inequality are unaffected by the number of data points, while for Samuelson's inequality the bounds loosen as the sample size increases. Thus for large enough data sets, Chebyshev's inequality is more useful.

:::

::: {#o11 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Samuelson’s inequality has several applications in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831). It is useful in the [studentization of residuals](wikipedia://en/609197) which shows a rationale for why this process should be done externally to better understand the spread of residuals in [regression analysis](wikipedia://en/826997).

In [matrix theory](wikipedia://en/20556859), Samuelson’s inequality is used to locate the [eigenvalues](wikipedia://en/2161429) of certain matrices and tensors.

Furthermore, generalizations of this inequality apply to complex data and random variables in a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325).[^o12][^o13]

:::

## Relationship to polynomials {#Relationship_to_polynomials}

Samuelson was not the first to describe this relationship: the first was probably [Laguerre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laguerre) in 1880 while investigating the [roots](wikipedia://en/537048) (zeros) of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000).[^o3] [^o15]

Consider a polynomial with all roots real:

$$a_0x^n + a_1x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_{n-1}x + a_n = 0$$

[Without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) let $a_0 = 1$ and let

$t_1 = \sum x_i$ and $t_2 = \sum x_i^2$

Then

$$a_1 = - \sum x_i = -t_1$$

and

$$a_2 = \sum x_ix_j = \frac{t_1^2 - t_2}{2} \qquad \text{ where } i < j$$

In terms of the coefficients

$$t_2 = a_1^2 - 2a_2$$

Laguerre showed that the roots of this polynomial were bounded by

$$-a_1 / n \pm b \sqrt{n - 1}$$

where

$$b = \frac{\sqrt{nt_2 - t_1^2}}{n} = \frac{\sqrt{na_1^2 - a_1^2 - 2na_2}}{n}$$

Inspection shows that $-\tfrac{a_1}{n}$ is the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) of the roots and that *b* is the standard deviation of the roots.

Laguerre failed to notice this relationship with the means and standard deviations of the roots, being more interested in the bounds themselves. This relationship permits a rapid estimate of the bounds of the roots and may be of use in their location.

When the coefficients $a_1$ and $a_2$ are both zero no information can be obtained about the location of the roots, because not all roots are real (as can be seen from [Descartes' rule of signs](wikipedia://en/2641493)) unless the constant term is also zero.
