# State of affairs (philosophy)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="State of affairs"}

In [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), a **state of affairs** (Sachverhalt),[^o2] also known as a **situation**, is a way the [actual world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actual_world) must be in order to make some given *[proposition](wikipedia://en/81094)* about the actual world true; in other words, a state of affairs is a *truth-maker*, whereas a proposition is a *truth-bearer*. Whereas states of affairs either *obtain* or *fail-to-obtain*, propositions are either *true* or *false*.[^o8] Some philosophers understand the term "states of affairs" in a more restricted sense as a synonym for "fact". In this sense, there are no states of affairs that do not obtain.[^o8]

:::

The early [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741) and [David Malet Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Malet_Armstrong) are well known for their defence of a **factualism**, a position according to which the world is a world of facts and not a world of things.[^o3]

## Overview {#Overview}

States of affairs are complex entities: they are built up from or constituted by other entities.[^o5][^o6] Atomic states of affairs are constituted by one [particular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particular) and one [property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_%28philosophy%29) exemplified by this particular.[^o7][^o8] For example, the state of affairs that Socrates is wise is constituted by the particular "Socrates" and the property "wise". Relational states of affairs involve several particulars and a relation connecting them. States of affairs that *obtain* are also referred to as [facts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact#In_philosophy).[^o8] It is controversial which ontological status should be ascribed to states of affairs that do not obtain.[^o7] States of affairs have been prominent in 20th-century ontology as various theories were proposed to describe the world as composed of states of affairs.[^o5][^o9][^o10]

In a sense of "state of affairs" favored by [Ernest Sosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernest_Sosa), states of affairs are situational [conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condition_%28philosophy%29). In fact, in the *[Cambridge Dictionary of Philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_Dictionary_of_Philosophy)*,[^o11] Sosa defines a condition to be a state of affairs, "way things are" or situation—most commonly referred to by a [nominalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nominalization) of a [sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28linguistics%29). The expression "Snow's being white", which refers to the condition snow's being white, is a nominalization of the sentence "Snow is white".[^o11] The [truth](wikipedia://en/29920) of the proposition that "snow is white" is a nominalization of the sentence "the proposition that snow is white is true". Snow's being white is a necessary and sufficient condition for the truth of the proposition that snow is white. Conditions in this sense may be called situational.

Usually, [necessity and sufficiency](wikipedia://en/169319) relate conditions of the same kind. Being an animal is a necessary attributive condition for being a dog. Fido's being an animal is a necessary situational condition for Fido's being a dog.
