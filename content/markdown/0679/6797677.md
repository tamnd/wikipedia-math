# Regular semigroup

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular semigroup"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **regular semigroup** is a [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) *S* in which every element is **regular**, i.e., for each element *a* in *S* there exists an element *x* in *S* such that *axa* = *a*.[^o2] Regular semigroups are one of the most-studied classes of semigroups, and their structure is particularly amenable to study via [Green's relations](wikipedia://en/1192012).[^o3]

:::

## History {#History}

Regular semigroups were introduced by [J. A. Green](wikipedia://en/10670093) in his influential 1951 paper "On the structure of semigroups"; this was also the paper in which [Green's relations](wikipedia://en/1192012) were introduced. The concept of *regularity* in a semigroup was adapted from an analogous condition for [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), already considered by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942).[^o5] It was Green's study of regular semigroups which led him to define his celebrated [relations](wikipedia://en/1192012). According to a footnote in Green 1951, the suggestion that the notion of regularity be applied to [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799) was first made by [David Rees](wikipedia://en/15398568).

The term **inversive semigroup** (French: demi-groupe inversif) was historically used as synonym in the papers of [Gabriel Thierrin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Thierrin) (a student of [Paul Dubreil](wikipedia://en/1389591)) in the 1950s,[^o6][^o7] and it is still used occasionally.[^o8]

## The basics {#The_basics}

There are two equivalent ways in which to define a regular semigroup *S*:

(1) for each *a* in *S*, there is an *x* in *S*, which is called a **pseudoinverse**,[^o10] with *axa* = *a*;

(2) every element *a* has at least one **inverse** *b*, in the sense that *aba* = *a* and *bab* = *b*.

To see the equivalence of these definitions, first suppose that *S* is defined by (2). Then *b* serves as the required *x* in (1). Conversely, if *S* is defined by (1), then *xax* is an inverse for *a*, since *a*(*xax*)*a* = *axa*(*xa*) = *axa* = *a* and (*xax*)*a*(*xax*) = *x*(*axa*)(*xax*) = *xa*(*xax*) = *x*(*axa*)*x* = *xax*.[^o11]

The set of inverses (in the above sense) of an element *a* in an arbitrary [semigroup](wikipedia://en/27799) *S* is denoted by *V*(*a*).[^o12] Thus, another way of expressing definition (2) above is to say that in a regular semigroup, *V*(*a*) is nonempty, for every *a* in *S*. The product of any element *a* with any *b* in *V*(*a*) is always [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent): *abab* = *ab*, since *aba* = *a*.[^o13]

### Examples of regular semigroups {#Examples_of_regular_semigroups}

- Every [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is a regular semigroup.
- Every [band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_%28mathematics%29) (idempotent semigroup) is regular in the sense of this article, though this is not what is meant by a [regular band](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Band_%28mathematics%29#Regular_bands).
- The [bicyclic semigroup](wikipedia://en/1209346) is regular.
- Any [full transformation semigroup](wikipedia://en/19313303) is regular.
- A [Rees matrix semigroup](wikipedia://en/41983973) is regular.
- The [homomorphic image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homomorphic_image) of a regular semigroup is regular.[^o15]

### Unique inverses and unique pseudoinverses {#Unique_inverses_and_unique_pseudoinverses}

A regular semigroup in which idempotents commute (with idempotents) is an [inverse semigroup](wikipedia://en/1757814), or equivalently, every element has a *unique* inverse. To see this, let *S* be a regular semigroup in which idempotents commute. Then every element of *S* has at least one inverse. Suppose that *a* in *S* has two inverses *b* and *c*, i.e.,

*aba* = *a*, *bab* = *b*, *aca* = *a* and *cac* = *c*. Also *ab*, *ba*, *ac* and *ca* are idempotents as above.

Then

*b* = *bab* = *b*(*aca*)*b* = *bac*(*a*)*b* = *bac*(*aca*)*b* = *bac*(*ac*)(*ab*) = *bac*(*ab*)(*ac*) = *ba*(*ca*)*bac* = *ca*(*ba*)*bac* = *c*(*aba*)*bac* = *cabac* = *cac* = *c*.

So, by commuting the pairs of idempotents *ab* & *ac* and *ba* & *ca*, the inverse of *a* is shown to be unique. Conversely, it can be shown that any [inverse semigroup](wikipedia://en/1757814) is a regular semigroup in which idempotents commute.[^o17]

The existence of a unique pseudoinverse implies the existence of a unique inverse, but the opposite is not true. For example, in the [symmetric inverse semigroup](wikipedia://en/18442482), the empty transformation Ø does not have a unique pseudoinverse, because Ø = Ø*f*Ø for any transformation *f*. The inverse of Ø is unique however, because only one *f* satisfies the additional constraint that *f* = *f*Ø*f*, namely *f* = Ø. This remark holds more generally in any semigroup with zero. Furthermore, if every element has a unique pseudoinverse, then the semigroup is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), and the unique pseudoinverse of an element coincides with the group inverse.

## Green's relations {#Green's_relations}

Recall that the [principal ideals](wikipedia://en/145126) of a semigroup *S* are defined in terms of *S*<sup>1</sup>, the *semigroup with identity adjoined*; this is to ensure that an element *a* belongs to the principal right, left and two-sided [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) which it generates. In a regular semigroup *S*, however, an element *a* = *axa* automatically belongs to these ideals, without recourse to adjoining an identity. [Green's relations](wikipedia://en/1192012) can therefore be redefined for regular semigroups as follows:

$a\,\mathcal{L}\,b$ [if, and only if](wikipedia://en/14922), *Sa* = *Sb*;

$a\,\mathcal{R}\,b$ if, and only if, *aS* = *bS*;

$a\,\mathcal{J}\,b$ if, and only if, *SaS* = *SbS*.[^o19]

In a regular semigroup *S*, every $\mathcal{L}$- and $\mathcal{R}$-class contains at least one [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent). If *a* is any element of *S* and *a* is any inverse for *a*, then *a* is $\mathcal{L}$-related to *aa* and $\mathcal{R}$-related to *aa*.[^o20]

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.** Let *S* be a regular semigroup; let *a* and *b* be elements of *S*, and let *V(x)* denote the set of inverses of *x* in *S*. Then

- $a\,\mathcal{L}\,b$ iff there exist *a* in *V*(*a*) and *b* in *V*(*b*) such that *aa* = *bb*;
- $a\,\mathcal{R}\,b$ iff there exist *a* in *V*(*a*) and *b* in *V*(*b*) such that *aa* = *bb*,
- $a\,\mathcal{H}\,b$ iff there exist *a* in *V*(*a*) and *b* in *V*(*b*) such that *aa* = *bb* and *aa* = *bb*.[^o22]

If *S* is an [inverse semigroup](wikipedia://en/1757814), then the idempotent in each $\mathcal{L}$- and $\mathcal{R}$-class is unique.[^o17]

:::

## Special classes of regular semigroups {#Special_classes_of_regular_semigroups}

Some special classes of regular semigroups are:[^o24]

- *Locally inverse semigroups*: a regular semigroup *S* is **locally inverse** if *eSe* is an inverse semigroup, for each [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) *e*.
- *[Orthodox semigroups](wikipedia://en/43280816)*: a regular semigroup *S* is **orthodox** if its subset of [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) forms a subsemigroup.
- *Generalised inverse semigroups*: a regular semigroup *S* is called a **generalised inverse semigroup** if its [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) form a normal band, i.e., *xyzx* = *xzyx* for all [idempotents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) *x*, *y*, *z*.

The [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of generalised inverse semigroups is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of the class of locally inverse semigroups and the class of orthodox semigroups.[^o25]

All inverse semigroups are orthodox and locally inverse. The converse statements do not hold.
