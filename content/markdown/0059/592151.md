# Even and odd functions

*Image: Sintay SVG.svg.* The [sine function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_function) and all of its [Taylor polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_polynomial) are odd functions.

*Image: Développement limité du cosinus.svg.* The [cosine function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine_function) and all of its [Taylor polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taylor_polynomials) are even functions.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Even and odd functions"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **even function** is a [real function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_function) such that $f(-x)=f(x)$ for every $x$ in its [domain](wikipedia://en/50263). Similarly, an **odd function** is a function such that $f(-x)=-f(x)$ for every $x$ in its domain.

:::

They are named for the [parity](wikipedia://en/143135) of the powers of the [power functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_Function) which satisfy each condition: the function $f(x) = x^n$ is even if n is an [even integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_integer), and it is odd if n is an odd integer.

Even functions are those real functions whose [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) is [self-symmetric](wikipedia://en/44362809) with respect to the $y$-axis, and odd functions are those whose graph is self-symmetric with respect to the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432).

If the domain of a real function is self-symmetric with respect to the origin, then the function can be uniquely decomposed as the sum of an even function and an odd function.

## Early history {#Early_history}

The concept of even and odd functions appears to date back to the early 18th century, with [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) playing a significant role in their formalization. Euler introduced the concepts of even and odd functions (using Latin terms *pares* and *impares*) in his work *Traiectoriarum Reciprocarum Solutio* from 1727. Before Euler, however, [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) had already developed geometric means of deriving coefficients of power series when writing the *Principia* (1687), and included algebraic techniques in an early draft of his *Quadrature of Curves,* though he removed it before publication in 1706. It is also noteworthy that Newton didn't explicitly name or focus on the even-odd decomposition, his work with power series would have involved understanding properties related to even and odd powers.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and examples"}

## Definition and examples {#Definition_and_examples}

Evenness and oddness are generally considered for [real functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_function), that is real-valued functions of a real variable. However, the concepts may be more generally defined for functions whose [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) and [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) both have a notion of [additive inverse](wikipedia://en/228312). This includes [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), all [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29), all [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), and all [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370). Thus, for example, a real function could be odd or even (or neither), as could a [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-valued function of a vector variable, and so on.

The given examples are real functions, to illustrate the [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) of their [graphs](wikipedia://en/87352).

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Even function"}

### Even functions {#Even_functions}

*Image: Function x^2.svg.* $f(x)=x^2$ is an example of an even function.

A [real function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_function) $f$ is **even** if, for every $x$ in its domain, $-x$ is also in its domain and[^o7]

$$f(-x) = f(x)$$

or equivalently

$$f(x) - f(-x) = 0.$$

Geometrically, the graph of an even function is [symmetric](wikipedia://en/53741) with respect to the *y*-axis, meaning that its graph remains unchanged after [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) about the *y*-axis.

Examples of even functions are:

- The [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) $x \mapsto |x|,$
- $$x \mapsto x^2,$$
- $x \mapsto x^n$ for any even integer $n,$
- [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) $\cos,$
- [hyperbolic cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) $\cosh,$
- [Gaussian function](wikipedia://en/245552) $x \mapsto \exp (-x^2).$

:::

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Odd function"}

### Odd functions {#Odd_functions}

*Image: Function-x3.svg.* $f(x)=x^3$ is an example of an odd function.

A real function $f$ is **odd** if, for every $x$ in its domain, $-x$ is also in its domain and[^o7]

$$f(-x) = -f(x)$$

or equivalently

$$f(x) + f(-x) = 0.$$

Geometrically, the graph of an odd function has rotational symmetry with respect to the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432), meaning that its graph remains unchanged after [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) of 180 [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294) about the origin.

If $x=0$ is in the domain of an odd function $f(x)$, then $f(0)=0$.

Examples of odd functions are:

- The [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449) $x \mapsto \operatorname{sgn}(x),$
- The identity function $x \mapsto x,$
- $x \mapsto x^n$ for any odd integer $n,$
- $x \mapsto \sqrt[n]{x}$ for any odd positive integer $n,$
- [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) $\sin,$
- [hyperbolic sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function) $\sinh,$
- The [error function](wikipedia://en/177111) $\operatorname{erf}.$

*Image: Function-x3plus1.svg.* $f(x)=x^3+1$ is neither even nor odd.

:::

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

### Uniqueness {#Uniqueness}

- If a function is both even and odd, it is equal to 0 everywhere it is defined.
- If a function is odd, the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of that function is an even function.

### Addition and subtraction {#Addition_and_subtraction}

- The [sum](wikipedia://en/61338) of two even functions is even.
- The sum of two odd functions is odd.
- The [difference](wikipedia://en/74141) between two odd functions is odd.
- The difference between two even functions is even.
- The sum of an even and odd function is not even or odd, unless one of the functions is equal to zero over the given [domain](wikipedia://en/50263).

### Multiplication and division {#Multiplication_and_division}

- The [product](wikipedia://en/20845) and [quotient](wikipedia://en/53696) of two even functions is an even function.
  - This implies that the product of any number of even functions is also even.
  - This implies that the [reciprocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_function) of an even function is also even.
- The product and quotient of two odd functions is an even function.
- The product and both quotients of an even function and an odd function is an odd function.
  - This implies that the reciprocal of an odd function is odd.

### Composition {#Composition}

- The [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of two even functions is even.
- The composition of two odd functions is odd.
- The composition of an even function and an odd function is even.
- The composition of any function with an even function is even (but not vice versa).

### Inverse function {#Inverse_function}

- If an odd function is [invertible](wikipedia://en/14907), then its inverse is also odd.

## Even–odd decomposition {#Even–odd_decomposition}

If a real function has a domain that is self-symmetric with respect to the origin, it may be uniquely decomposed as the sum of an even and an odd function, which are called respectively the **even part** (or the **even component**) and the **odd part** (or the **odd component**) of the function, and are defined by

$$f_\text{even}(x) = \frac {f(x)+f(-x)}{2},$$

and

$$f_\text{odd}(x) = \frac {f(x)-f(-x)}{2}.$$

It is straightforward to verify that $f_\text{even}$ is even, $f_\text{odd}$ is odd, and $f=f_\text{even}+f_\text{odd}.$

This decomposition is unique since, if

$$f(x)=g(x)+h(x),$$

where $g$ is even and $h$ is odd, then $g=f_\text{even}$ and $h=f_\text{odd},$ since

$$\begin{aligned} 2f_\text{e}(x) &=f(x)+f(-x)= g(x) + g(-x) +h(x) +h(-x) = 2g(x),\\ 2f_\text{o}(x) &=f(x)-f(-x)= g(x) - g(-x) +h(x) -h(-x) = 2h(x). \end{aligned}$$

For example, the [hyperbolic cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_cosine) and the [hyperbolic sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_sine) may be regarded as the even and odd parts of the exponential function, as the first one is an even function, the second one is odd, and

$$e^x=\underbrace{\cosh (x)}_{f_\text{even}(x)} + \underbrace{\sinh (x)}_{f_\text{odd}(x)}.$$

[Fourier](wikipedia://en/25767401)'s [sine and cosine transforms](wikipedia://en/2660978) also perform even–odd decomposition by representing a function's odd part with [sine waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine_waves) (an odd function) and the function's even part with cosine waves (an even function).

:::

## Further algebraic properties {#Further_algebraic_properties}

- Any [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of even functions is even, and the even functions form a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438). Similarly, any linear combination of odd functions is odd, and the odd functions also form a vector space over the reals. In fact, the vector space of *all* real functions is the [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_vector_spaces) of the [subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357) of even and odd functions. This is a more abstract way of expressing the property in the preceding section.
  - The space of functions can be considered a [graded algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra) over the real numbers by this property, as well as some of those above.
- The even functions form a [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) over the reals. However, the odd functions do *not* form an algebra over the reals, as they are not [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) under multiplication.

## Analytic properties {#Analytic_properties}

A function's being odd or even does not imply [differentiability](wikipedia://en/330206), or [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122). For example, the [Dirichlet function](wikipedia://en/630005) is even, but is nowhere continuous.

In the following, properties involving [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921), [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038), [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) are considered, and these concepts are thus supposed to be defined for the considered functions.

### Basic analytic properties {#Basic_analytic_properties}

- The [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of an even function is odd.
- The derivative of an odd function is even.
- If an odd function is [integrable](wikipedia://en/15532) over a [bounded symmetric interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[-A,A]$, the integral over that interval is zero; that is[^o22]

$$\int_{-A}^{A} f(x)\,dx = 0.$$

  - This implies that the [Cauchy principal value](wikipedia://en/408195) of an odd function over the entire real line is zero.
- If an even function is integrable over a bounded symmetric interval $[-A,A]$, the integral over that interval is twice the integral from 0 to *A*; that is[^o23]

$$\int_{-A}^{A} f(x)\,dx = 2\int_{0}^{A} f(x)\,dx.$$

  - This property is also true for the [improper integral](wikipedia://en/346030) when $A = \infty$, provided the integral from 0 to $\infty$ converges.

### Series {#Series}

- The [Maclaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_series) of an even function includes only even powers.
- The Maclaurin series of an odd function includes only odd powers.
- The [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of a [periodic](wikipedia://en/76848) even function includes only [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) terms.
- The Fourier series of a periodic odd function includes only [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) terms.
- The [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of a purely real-valued even function is real and even. (see Fourier\_analysis)
- The Fourier transform of a purely real-valued odd function is imaginary and odd. (see Fourier\_analysis)

## Harmonics {#Harmonics}

In [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), [harmonic distortion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic_distortion) occurs when a [sine wave](wikipedia://en/324749) signal is sent through a memory-less [nonlinear system](wikipedia://en/146103), that is, a system whose output at time *t* only depends on the input at time *t* and does not depend on the input at any previous times. Such a system is described by a response function $V_\text{out}(t) = f(V_\text{in}(t))$. The type of [harmonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic) produced depend on the response function *f*:[^o26]

- When the response function is even, the resulting signal will consist of only even harmonics of the input sine wave; $0f, 2f, 4f, 6f, \dots$
  - The [fundamental](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_frequency) is also an odd harmonic, so will not be present.
  - A simple example is a [full-wave rectifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full-wave_rectifier).
  - The $0f$ component represents the DC offset, due to the one-sided nature of even-symmetric transfer functions.
- When it is odd, the resulting signal will consist of only odd harmonics of the input sine wave; $1f, 3f, 5f, \dots$
  - The output signal will be half-wave [symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric).
  - A simple example is [clipping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clipping_%28audio%29) in a symmetric [push-pull amplifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_amplifier).
- When it is asymmetric, the resulting signal may contain either even or odd harmonics; $1f, 2f, 3f, \dots$
  - Simple examples are a half-wave rectifier, and clipping in an asymmetrical [class-A amplifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class-A_amplifier).

This does not hold true for more complex waveforms. A [sawtooth wave](wikipedia://en/52147) contains both even and odd harmonics, for instance. After even-symmetric full-wave rectification, it becomes a [triangle wave](wikipedia://en/52151), which, other than the DC offset, contains only odd harmonics.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

### Multivariate functions {#Multivariate_functions}

**Even symmetry:**

A function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is called *even symmetric* if:

$$f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)=f(-x_1,-x_2,\ldots,-x_n) \quad \text{for all } x_1,\ldots,x_n \in \mathbb{R}$$

**Odd symmetry:**

A function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is called *odd symmetric* if:

$$f(x_1,x_2,\ldots,x_n)=-f(-x_1,-x_2,\ldots,-x_n) \quad \text{for all } x_1,\ldots,x_n \in \mathbb{R}$$

### Complex-valued functions {#Complex-valued_functions}

The definitions for even and odd symmetry for [complex-valued](wikipedia://en/5826) functions of a real argument are similar to the real case. In [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), a similar symmetry is sometimes considered, which involves [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation).[^o31][^o33]

**Conjugate symmetry:**

A complex-valued function of a real argument $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$ is called *conjugate symmetric* if

$$f(x)=\overline{f(-x)} \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}$$

A complex valued function is conjugate symmetric if and only if its [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) is an even function and its [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) is an odd function.

A typical example of a conjugate symmetric function is the [cis function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cis_function)

$$x \to e^{ix}=\cos x + i\sin x$$

**Conjugate antisymmetry:**

A complex-valued function of a real argument $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{C}$ is called *conjugate antisymmetric* if:

$$f(x)=-\overline{f(-x)} \quad \text{for all } x \in \mathbb{R}$$

A complex valued function is conjugate antisymmetric if and only if its [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) is an odd function and its [imaginary part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) is an even function.

### Finite length sequences {#Finite_length_sequences}

The definitions of odd and even symmetry are extended to *N*-point sequences (i.e. functions of the form $f: \left\{0,1,\ldots,N-1\right\} \to \mathbb{R}$) as follows:[^o33]

**Even symmetry:**

A *N*-point sequence is called *conjugate symmetric* if

$$f(n) = f(N-n) \quad \text{for all } n \in \left\{ 1,\ldots,N-1 \right\}.$$

Such a sequence is often called a **palindromic sequence**; see also [Palindromic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palindromic_polynomial).

**Odd symmetry:**

A *N*-point sequence is called *conjugate antisymmetric* if

$$f(n) = -f(N-n) \quad \text{for all } n \in \left\{1,\ldots,N-1\right\}.$$

Such a sequence is sometimes called an **anti-palindromic sequence**; see also [Antipalindromic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Palindromic_polynomial).
