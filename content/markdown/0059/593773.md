# Unimodular matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unimodular matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **unimodular matrix** *M* is a [square](wikipedia://en/166022) [integer matrix](wikipedia://en/4500787) having [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) +1 or −1. Equivalently, it is an integer matrix that is [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) over the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563): there is an integer matrix *N* that is its inverse (these are equivalent under [Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008)). Thus every equation *Mx* = *b*, where *M* and *b* both have integer components and *M* is unimodular, has an integer solution. The *n* × *n* unimodular matrices form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) called the *n* × *n* [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) over $\mathbb{Z}$, which is denoted $\operatorname{GL}_n(\mathbb{Z})$.

:::

## Examples of unimodular matrices {#Examples_of_unimodular_matrices}

Unimodular matrices form a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) under [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280), i.e. the following matrices are unimodular:

- [Identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718)
- The [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_inverse) of a unimodular matrix
- The [product](wikipedia://en/125280) of two unimodular matrices

Other examples include:

- [Pascal matrices](wikipedia://en/7833211)
- [Permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/238849)
- the three transformation matrices in the ternary [tree of primitive Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/33358028)
- Certain transformation matrices for [rotation](wikipedia://en/856005), [shearing](wikipedia://en/799405) (both with determinant 1) and [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_matrix) (determinant −1).
- The unimodular matrix used (possibly implicitly) in [lattice reduction](wikipedia://en/4786593) and in the [Hermite normal form](wikipedia://en/9025098) of matrices.
- The [Kronecker product](wikipedia://en/712430) of two unimodular matrices is also unimodular. This follows since $\det(A \otimes B) = (\det A)^q (\det B)^p,$ where *p* and *q* are the dimensions of *A* and *B*, respectively.

## Total unimodularity {#Total_unimodularity}

A **totally unimodular matrix**[^o5] (TU matrix) is a matrix for which every square submatrix has determinant 0, +1 or −1. A totally unimodular matrix need not be square itself. From the definition it follows that any submatrix of a totally unimodular matrix is itself totally unimodular (TU). Furthermore it follows that any TU matrix has only 0, +1 or −1 entries. The [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) is not true, i.e., a matrix with only 0, +1 or −1 entries is not necessarily unimodular. A matrix is TU if and only if its [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) is TU.

Totally unimodular matrices are extremely important in [polyhedral combinatorics](wikipedia://en/19696519) and [combinatorial optimization](wikipedia://en/420555) since they give a quick way to verify that a [linear program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_program) is [integral](wikipedia://en/43730#Integral_linear_programs) (has an integral optimum, when any optimum exists). Specifically, if *A* is TU and *b* is integral, then linear programs of forms like $\{\min c^\top x \mid Ax \ge b, x \ge 0\}$ or $\{\max c^\top x \mid Ax \le b\}$ have integral optima, for any *c*. Hence if *A* is totally unimodular and *b* is integral, every extreme point of the feasible region (e.g. $\{x \mid Ax \ge b\}$) is integral and thus the feasible region is an [integral](wikipedia://en/43730#Integral_linear_programs) polyhedron.

### Common totally unimodular matrices {#Common_totally_unimodular_matrices}

\1. The unoriented incidence matrix of a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431), which is the coefficient matrix for bipartite [matching](wikipedia://en/581797), is totally unimodular (TU). (The unoriented incidence matrix of a non-bipartite graph is not TU.) More generally, in the appendix to a paper by Heller and Tompkins,[^o7] A.J. Hoffman and D. Gale prove the following. Let $A$ be an *m* by *n* matrix whose rows can be partitioned into two [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359) $B$ and $C$. Then the following four conditions together are [sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_conditions) for *A* to be totally unimodular:

- Every entry in $A$ is 0, +1, or −1;
- Every column of $A$ contains at most two non-zero (i.e., +1 or −1) entries;
- If two non-zero entries in a column of $A$ have the same sign, then the row of one is in $B$, and the other in $C$;
- If two non-zero entries in a column of $A$ have opposite signs, then the rows of both are in $B$, or both in $C$.

It was realized later that these conditions define an incidence matrix of a balanced [signed graph](wikipedia://en/2472618#Incidence_matrix); thus, this example says that the incidence matrix of a signed graph is totally unimodular if the signed graph is balanced. The converse is valid for signed graphs without half edges (this generalizes the property of the unoriented incidence matrix of a graph).[^o8]

\2. The [constraints](wikipedia://en/7418540) of [maximum flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_flow) and [minimum cost flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_cost_flow) problems yield a coefficient matrix with these properties (and with empty *C*). Thus, such network flow problems with bounded integer capacities have an integral optimal value. Note that this does not apply to [multi-commodity flow problems](wikipedia://en/6809151), in which it is possible to have fractional optimal value even with bounded integer capacities.

\3. The consecutive-ones property: if *A* is (or can be permuted into) a 0-1 matrix in which for every row, the 1s appear consecutively, then *A* is TU. (The same holds for columns since the transpose of a TU matrix is also TU.) [^o9]

\4. Every **network matrix** is TU. The rows of a network matrix correspond to a tree *T* = (*V*, *R*), each of whose arcs has an arbitrary orientation (it is not necessary that there exist a root vertex *r* such that the tree is "rooted into *r*" or "out of *r*").The columns correspond to another set *C* of arcs on the same vertex set *V*. To compute the entry at row *R* and column *C* = *st*, look at the *s*-to-*t* path *P* in *T*; then the entry is:

- \+1 if arc *R* appears forward in *P*,
- −1 if arc *R* appears backwards in *P*,
- 0 if arc *R* does not appear in *P*.

See more in Schrijver (2003).

\5. Ghouila-Houri showed that a matrix is TU if for every subset *R* of rows, there is an assignment $s:R \to \pm1$ of signs to rows so that the signed sum $\sum_{r \in R} s(r)r$ (which is a row vector of the same width as the matrix) has all its entries in $\{0,\pm1\}$ (i.e. the row-submatrix has [discrepancy](wikipedia://en/11508286) at most one). This and several other if-and-only-if characterizations are proven in Schrijver (1998).

\6. Hoffman and [Kruskal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Kruskal)[^o10] proved the following theorem. Suppose $G$ is a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986) without 2-dicycles, $P$ is the set of all [dipaths](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dipath) in $G$, and $A$ is the 0-1 incidence matrix of $V(G)$ versus $P$. Then $A$ is totally unimodular if and only if every simple arbitrarily-oriented cycle in $G$ consists of alternating forwards and backwards arcs.

\7. Suppose a matrix has 0-($\pm$1) entries and in each column, the entries are non-decreasing from top to bottom (so all −1s are on top, then 0s, then 1s are on the bottom). Fujishige showed[^o11] that the matrix is TU iff every 2-by-2 submatrix has determinant in $0, \pm1$.

\8. [Seymour](wikipedia://en/1575708) (1980)[^o12] proved a full characterization of all TU matrices, which we describe here only informally. Seymour's theorem is that a matrix is TU if and only if it is a certain natural combination of some **network matrices** and some copies of a particular 5-by-5 TU matrix.

## Abstract linear algebra {#Abstract_linear_algebra}

[Abstract linear algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) considers matrices with entries from any [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$, not limited to the integers. In this context, a unimodular matrix is one that is invertible over the ring; equivalently, whose determinant is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666). This [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is denoted $\operatorname{GL}_n(R)$.[^o15] A rectangular $k$-by-$m$ matrix is said to be unimodular if it can be extended with $m-k$ rows in $R^m$ to a unimodular square matrix.[^o16][^o17][^o18]

Over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), *unimodular* has the same meaning as *[non-singular](wikipedia://en/217122)*. *Unimodular* here refers to matrices with coefficients in some ring (often the integers) which are invertible over that ring, and one uses *non-singular* to mean matrices that are invertible over the field.
