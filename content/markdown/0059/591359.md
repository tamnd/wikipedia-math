# Dialetheism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dialetheism"}

**Dialetheism** (; from [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) δι- grc 'twice' and ἀλήθεια grc 'truth') is the view that there are [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29) that are both true and false. More precisely, it is the belief that there can be a true statement whose [negation](wikipedia://en/161019) is also true. Such statements are called "true [contradictions](wikipedia://en/75807)", *dialetheia*, or [nondualisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonduality_%28spirituality%29).

:::

Dialetheism is not a [system of formal logic](wikipedia://en/396102); instead, it is a thesis about [truth](wikipedia://en/29920) that influences the construction of a formal logic, often based on pre-existing systems. Introducing dialetheism has various consequences, depending on the theory into which it is introduced. A common mistake resulting from this is to reject dialetheism on the basis that, in traditional systems of logic (e.g., [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) and [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262)), every statement becomes a [theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theorem_%28logic%29) if a contradiction is true, [trivialising](wikipedia://en/10886610) such systems when dialetheism is included as an [axiom](wikipedia://en/928).[^o2] Other logical systems, however, do not [explode](wikipedia://en/591394) in this manner when contradictions are introduced; such contradiction-tolerant systems are known as [paraconsistent logics](wikipedia://en/421085). Dialetheists who do not want to allow that every statement is true are free to favour these over traditional, explosive logics.

[Graham Priest](wikipedia://en/3659382) defines dialetheism as the view that there are true contradictions.[^o3] [Jc Beall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jc_Beall) is another advocate; his position differs from Priest's in advocating constructive (methodological) [deflationism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deflationism) regarding the truth predicate.[^o4]

The term was coined by Graham Priest and [Richard Sylvan (then Routley)](wikipedia://en/433347).

## Motivations {#Motivations}

### Dialetheism resolves certain paradoxes {#Dialetheism_resolves_certain_paradoxes}

The [liar paradox](wikipedia://en/17967) and [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095) deal with self-contradictory statements in classical logic and [naïve set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naïve_set_theory), respectively. Contradictions are problematic in these theories because they cause the theories to [explode](wikipedia://en/591394)—if a contradiction is true, then every proposition is true. The classical way to solve this problem is to ban contradictory statements: to revise the axioms of the logic so that self-contradictory statements do not appear (just as with [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095)). Dialetheists, on the other hand, respond to this problem by accepting the contradictions as true. Dialetheism allows for the unrestricted [axiom of comprehension](wikipedia://en/52386#Unrestricted_comprehension) in [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), claiming that any resulting contradiction is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977).[^o7]

However, self-referential [paradoxes](wikipedia://en/24390), such as the Strengthened Liar can be avoided without revising the axioms by abandoning [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868) and accepting more than two [truth values](wikipedia://en/161711) with the help of [many-valued logic](wikipedia://en/38024), such as [fuzzy logic](wikipedia://en/49180) or [Łukasiewicz logic](wikipedia://en/11001950).

### Human reasoning {#Human_reasoning}

Ambiguous situations may cause humans to affirm both a proposition and its negation. For example, if John stands in the doorway to a room, it may seem reasonable both to affirm that *John is in the room* and to affirm that *John is not in the room*.

Critics argue that this merely reflects an ambiguity in our language rather than a dialetheic quality in our thoughts; if we replace the given statement with one that is less ambiguous (such as "John is halfway in the room" or "John is in the doorway"), the contradiction disappears. The statements appeared contradictory only because of a syntactic play; here, the actual meaning of "being in the room" is not the same in both instances, and thus each sentence is not the exact logical negation of the other: therefore, they are not necessarily contradictory.

Moreover, John appears to be standing in a [conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) of two concepts. He is in ***x*** and ***not******x*** at the same time, but not in **x** and *not in* **x** at the same time (that would result in a [contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807)). He is on his [logical connective](wikipedia://en/18153) truth-functional operator, which shows the recurrent ambiguity of human language that often fails to capture the nature of some [logical statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_sentence).

### Apparent dialetheism in other philosophical doctrines {#Apparent_dialetheism_in_other_philosophical_doctrines}

The [Jain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain) philosophical doctrine of [anekantavada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anekantavada)—non-one-sidedness—states that all statements are true in some sense and false in another.[^o10] Some interpret this as saying that dialetheia not only exist but are ubiquitous. Technically, however, a *logical contradiction* is a proposition that is true and false in the *same* sense; a proposition which is true in one sense and false in another does not constitute a logical contradiction. (For example, although in one sense a man cannot both be a "father" and "celibate"—leaving aside such cases as either a celibate man adopting a child or a man fathering a child and only later adopting celibacy—there is no contradiction for a man to be a *spiritual* father and also celibate; the sense of the word father is different here. In another example, although at the same time George W. Bush cannot both be president and not be president, he was president from 2001-2009, but was not president before 2001 or after 2009, so in different times he was both president and not president.)

The [Buddhist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhist) logic system, named "[Catuṣkoṭi](wikipedia://en/21738956)", similarly implies that a statement and its negation may possibly co-exist.[^o11][^o12]

[Graham Priest](wikipedia://en/3659382) argues in *Beyond the Limits of Thought* that dialetheia arise at the borders of expressibility, in a number of philosophical contexts other than formal semantics.

## Formal consequences {#Formal_consequences}

In classical logics, taking a contradiction $p \wedge \neg p$ (see [List of logic symbols](wikipedia://en/2498388)) as a premise (that is, taking as a premise the truth of both $p$ and $\neg p$), allows us to prove any statement $q$. Indeed, since $p$ is true, the statement $p \vee q$ is true (by generalization). Taking $p \vee q$ together with $\neg p$ is a [disjunctive syllogism](wikipedia://en/7963) from which we can conclude $q$. (This is often called the *[principle of explosion](wikipedia://en/591394)*, since the truth of a contradiction is imagined to make the number of theorems in a system "explode".)[^o2]

## Advantages {#Advantages}

The proponents of dialetheism mainly advocate its ability to avoid problems faced by other more orthodox resolutions as a consequence of their appeals to hierarchies. According to Graham Priest, "the whole point of the dialetheic solution to the semantic paradoxes is to get rid of the distinction between object language and meta-language".[^o3] Another possibility is to utilize dialetheism along with a [paraconsistent logic](wikipedia://en/421085) to resurrect the program of [logicism](wikipedia://en/350672) advocated for by [Frege](wikipedia://en/48416) and [Russell](wikipedia://en/4163).[^o15] This even allows one to prove the truth of otherwise unprovable theorems such as the [well-ordering theorem](wikipedia://en/33458) and the falsity of others such as the [continuum hypothesis](wikipedia://en/5705).

There are also dialetheic solutions to the [sorites paradox](wikipedia://en/231687).

## Criticisms {#Criticisms}

One criticism of dialetheism is that it fails to capture a crucial feature about [negation](wikipedia://en/161019), known as absoluteness of disagreement.[^o17]

Imagine John's utterance of *P*. Sally's typical way of disagreeing with John is a consequent utterance of ¬*P*. Yet, if we accept dialetheism, Sally's so uttering does not prevent her from also accepting *P*; after all, *P* may be a dialetheia and therefore it and its negation are both true. The absoluteness of disagreement is lost.

A response is that disagreement can be displayed by uttering "¬*P* and, furthermore, *P* is not a dialetheia". However, the most obvious codification of "*P* is not a dialetheia" is ¬(*P* $\wedge$ ¬*P*). But *this itself* could be a dialetheia as well. One dialetheist response is to offer a distinction between [assertion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_assertion) and rejection. This distinction might be hashed out in terms of the traditional distinction between [logical qualities](wikipedia://en/6767623), or as a distinction between two [illocutionary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Illocutionary_force) [speech acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_acts): [assertion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_assertion) and rejection. Another criticism is that dialetheism cannot describe [logical consequences](wikipedia://en/37531624), once we believe in the relevance of logical consequences, because of its inability to describe hierarchies.[^o3]
