# Principle of explosion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Principle of explosion"}

In [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), and similar [logical systems](wikipedia://en/396102), the **principle of explosion** is the [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) according to which any [statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29) can be proven from a [contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807).[^o2][^o3][^o4] That is, from a contradiction, any [proposition](wikipedia://en/81094) (including its [negation](wikipedia://en/161019)) can be inferred; this is known as **deductive explosion**.[^o5][^o6]

:::

The proof of this principle was first given by 12th-century French philosopher [William of Soissons](wikipedia://en/56058699).[^o7] Due to the principle of explosion, the existence of a contradiction ([inconsistency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inconsistency)) in a [formal axiomatic system](wikipedia://en/396102) is disastrous; since any statement—true or not—can be proven, it trivializes the concepts of truth and falsity.[^o8] Around the turn of the 20th century, the discovery of contradictions such as [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095) at the foundations of mathematics thus threatened the entire structure of mathematics. Mathematicians such as [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416), [Ernst Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655), [Abraham Fraenkel](wikipedia://en/528451), and [Thoralf Skolem](wikipedia://en/453765) put much effort into revising [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) to eliminate these contradictions, resulting in the modern [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214).

As a demonstration of the principle, consider two contradictory statements—"All [lemons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lemon) are yellow" and "Not all lemons are yellow"—and suppose that both are true. If that is the case, anything can be proven, e.g., the assertion that "[unicorns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicorn) exist", by using the following argument:

1. We know that "Not all lemons are yellow", as it has been assumed to be true.
1. We know that "All lemons are yellow", as it has been assumed to be true.
1. Therefore, the two-part statement "All lemons are yellow *or* unicorns exist" must also be true, since the first part of the statement ("All lemons are yellow") has already been assumed, and the use of "*or*" means that if even one part of the statement is true, the statement as a whole must be true as well.
1. However, since we also know that "Not all lemons are yellow" (as this has been assumed), the first part is false, and hence the second part must be true to ensure the two-part statement to be true, i.e., unicorns exist (this inference is known as the [disjunctive syllogism](wikipedia://en/7963)).
1. The procedure may be repeated to prove that unicorns do *not* exist (hence proving an additional contradiction where unicorns do and do not exist), as well as any other [well-formed formula](wikipedia://en/404582). Thus, there is an *explosion* of provable statements.

::: {#o9 .definition cue=dfni name="paraconsistent logics"}

In a different solution to the problems posed by the principle of explosion, some mathematicians have devised alternative theories of [logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_%28mathematics%29) called [**paraconsistent logics**](wikipedia://en/421085), which allow some contradictory statements to be proven without affecting the truth value of (all) other statements.[^o8]

:::

## Symbolic representation {#Symbolic_representation}

In [symbolic logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_logic), the principle of explosion can be expressed schematically in the following way:[^o11][^o12] $P, \lnot P \vdash Q$

For any statements *P* and *Q*, if *P* and not-*P* are both true, then it logically follows that *Q* is true.

::: {#o14 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Below is the **Lewis argument**,[^o18] a formal proof of the principle of explosion using [symbolic logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_logic).

::: {#o15 .table cue=table}

| Step | Proposition | Derivation |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $P\land\neg P$ | Premise |
| 2 | $P$ | [Conjunction elimination](wikipedia://en/788704) (1) |
| 3 | $\neg P$ | [Conjunction elimination](wikipedia://en/788704) (1) |
| 4 | $P \lor Q$ | [Disjunction introduction](wikipedia://en/8528) (2) |
| 5 | $Q$ | [Disjunctive syllogism](wikipedia://en/7963) (4,3) |

:::

This proof was published by [C. I. Lewis](wikipedia://en/433707) and is named after him, though versions of it were known to medieval logicians.[^o16][^o17][^o18]

This is just the symbolic version of the informal argument given in the introduction, with $P$ standing for "all lemons are yellow" and $Q$ standing for "Unicorns exist". We start out by assuming that (1) all lemons are yellow and that (2) not all lemons are yellow. From the proposition that all lemons are yellow, we infer that (3) either all lemons are yellow or unicorns exist. But then from this and the fact that not all lemons are yellow, we infer that (4) unicorns exist by disjunctive syllogism.

### Semantic argument {#Semantic_argument}

::: {#o20 .definition cue=dfni name="semantic consequence"}

An alternate argument for the principle stems from [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858). A [sentence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence) $P$ is a **[semantic consequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semantic_consequence)** of a set of sentences $\Gamma$ only if every model of $\Gamma$ is a model of $P$. However, there is no model of the contradictory set $(P \wedge \lnot P)$. *[A fortiori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_fortiori)*, there is no model of $(P \wedge \lnot P)$ that is not a model of $Q$. Thus, vacuously, every model of $(P \wedge \lnot P)$ is a model of $Q$. Thus $Q$ is a semantic consequence of $(P \wedge \lnot P)$.

:::

:::

## Paraconsistent logic {#Paraconsistent_logic}

[Paraconsistent logics](wikipedia://en/421085) have been developed that allow for [subcontrary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcontrary)-forming operators. [Model-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_semantics_%28logic%29) paraconsistent logicians often deny the assumption that there can be no model of $\{\phi , \lnot \phi \}$ and devise semantical systems in which there are such models. Alternatively, they reject the idea that propositions can be classified as true or false. [Proof-theoretic](wikipedia://en/910505) paraconsistent logics usually deny the validity of one of the steps necessary for deriving an explosion, typically including [disjunctive syllogism](wikipedia://en/7963), [disjunction introduction](wikipedia://en/8528), and *[reductio ad absurdum](wikipedia://en/22456146)*.

## Usage {#Usage}

The [metamathematical](wikipedia://en/183508) value of the principle of explosion is that for any logical system where this principle holds, any derived [theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_theory) which proves [⊥](wikipedia://en/229823) (or an equivalent form, $\phi \land \lnot \phi$) is worthless because all its [statements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29) would become [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977), making it impossible to distinguish [truth](wikipedia://en/29920) from falsehood. That is to say, the principle of explosion is an argument for the [law of non-contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_non-contradiction) in classical logic, because without it all truth statements become meaningless.

Reduction in proof strength of logics without the principle of explosion is discussed in [minimal logic](wikipedia://en/26267678).
