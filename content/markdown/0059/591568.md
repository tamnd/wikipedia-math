# Trigonometric polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trigonometric polynomial"}

In the [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) subfields of [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) and [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), a **trigonometric polynomial** is a finite [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) sin(*nx*) and cos(*nx*) with *n* taking on the values of one or more [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). The coefficients may be taken as real numbers, for real-valued functions. For [complex coefficients](wikipedia://en/5826), there is no difference between such a function and a finite [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038).

:::

Trigonometric polynomials are widely used, for example in [trigonometric interpolation](wikipedia://en/879681) applied to the [interpolation](wikipedia://en/14569) of [periodic functions](wikipedia://en/76848). They are used also in the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811).

The term *trigonometric polynomial* for the real-valued case can be seen as using the [analogy](wikipedia://en/103533): the functions sin(*nx*) and cos(*nx*) are similar to the [monomial basis](wikipedia://en/976666) for [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000). In the complex case the trigonometric polynomials are spanned by the positive and negative powers of $e^{ix}$, i.e., [Laurent polynomials](wikipedia://en/61351) in $z$ under the [change of variables](wikipedia://en/2146848) $x \mapsto z := e^{ix}$.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Any function *T* of the form

$$T(x) = a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^N a_n \cos nx + \sum_{n=1}^N b_n \sin nx \qquad (x \in \mathbb{R})$$

with complex-valued coefficients $a_n$ and $b_n$ and at least one of the highest-degree coefficients $a_N$ and $b_N$ non-zero, is called a *complex trigonometric polynomial* of degree *N*.[^o4] The cosine and sine [are the even and odd parts of the exponential of an imaginary variable](wikipedia://en/30367#Euler's_formula_and_the_exponential_function),

$$\cos nx = \tfrac12\bigl(e^{inx} + e^{-inx}\bigr), \quad \sin nx = -\tfrac12i\bigl(e^{inx} - e^{-inx}\bigr),$$

so the trigonometric polynomial can alternately be written as

$$T(x) = \sum_{n=-N}^N c_n e^{inx} \qquad (x \in \mathbb{R}),$$

with complex coefficients $c_0 = a_0$ and

$$\quad c_k = \tfrac12(a_k - b_ki), \quad c_{-k} = \tfrac12(a_k + b_ki),$$

for all $k$ from 1 to $N$.

If the coefficients $a_n$ and $b_n$ are real for all $n$, then $T$ is called a *real trigonometric polynomial*.[^n1] When using the exponential form, the complex coefficients satisfy $c_{-n} = \overline{c}_{n}$ for all $n\in[-N,N]$.[^n2]

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

A trigonometric polynomial can be considered a [periodic function](wikipedia://en/76848) on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), with [period](wikipedia://en/76848) some divisor of $2\pi$, or as a function on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).

Trigonometric polynomials are [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in the space of [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) on the unit circle, with the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755);[^o9] this is a special case of the [Stone–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/28858). More concretely, for every continuous function $f$ and every $\epsilon > 0$ there exists a trigonometric polynomial $T$ such that $|f(z) - T(z)| < \epsilon$ for all $z$. [Fejér's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fejér's_theorem) states that the arithmetic means of the partial sums of the [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of $f$ converge uniformly to $f$ provided $f$ is continuous on the circle; these partial sums can be used to approximate $f$.

A trigonometric polynomial of degree $N$ has a maximum of $2N$ roots in a real interval $[a, a+2\pi)$ unless it is the zero function.[^o10]

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Fejér-Riesz theorem"}

## Fejér-Riesz theorem {#Fejér-Riesz_theorem}

The Fejér-Riesz theorem states that every positive *real* trigonometric polynomial

$$t(x) = \sum_{n=-N}^{N} c_n e^{i n x},$$

satisfying $t(x)>0$ for all $x\in\mathbb{R}$, can be represented as the square of the [modulus](wikipedia://en/991#Complex_numbers) of another (usually *complex*) trigonometric polynomial $q(x)$ such that:[^n3]

$$t(x) = |q(x)|^2 = q(x)\overline{q(x)}.$$

Or, equivalently, every [Laurent polynomial](wikipedia://en/61351)

$$w(z)=\sum_{n=-N}^{N} w_{n}z^{n},$$

with $w_n \in\mathbb{C}$ that satisfies $w(\zeta)\geq 0$ for all $\zeta \in \mathbb{T}$ can be written as:

$$w(\zeta)=|p(\zeta)|^2=p(\zeta)\overline{p(\zeta)},$$

for some polynomial

$$p(z) = p_{0} + p_{1}z + \cdots + p_{N}z^{N},$$

and $p(z)$ can be chosen to have no zeroes in the [open unit disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_unit_disk) $\mathbb{D}$.[^n4][^n5] The Fejér-Riesz theorem arises naturally in [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) and the polynomial factorization $w(\zeta)= p(\zeta)\overline{p(\zeta)}$ is also called the **spectral factorization** (or **Wiener-Hopf factorization**) of $w(\zeta)$.[^n6]

:::
