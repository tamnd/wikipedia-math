# Szemerédi's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Szemerédi's theorem"}

In [arithmetic combinatorics](wikipedia://en/18243381), **Szemerédi's theorem** is a result concerning [arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/168389) in subsets of the integers. In 1936, [Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) and [Turán](wikipedia://en/614998) conjectured[^o2] that every set of integers *A* with positive [natural density](wikipedia://en/1819983) contains a *k*-term [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389) for every *k*. [Endre Szemerédi](wikipedia://en/663489) proved the conjecture in 1975.

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

A subset *A* of the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) is said to have positive upper density if

$$\limsup_{n \to \infty}\frac{|A\cap \{1, 2, 3, \dotsc, n\}|}{n} > 0.$$

Szemerédi's theorem asserts that a subset of the natural numbers with positive upper density contains an arithmetic progression of length *k* for all positive integers *k*.

An often-used equivalent finitary version of the theorem states that for every positive integer *k* and real number $\delta \in (0, 1]$, there exists a positive integer

$$N = N(k,\delta)$$

such that every subset of {1, 2, ..., *N*} of size at least $\delta N$ contains an arithmetic progression of length *k*.

Another formulation uses the function *r*<sub>*k*</sub>(*N*), the size of the largest subset of {1, 2, ..., *N*} without an arithmetic progression of length *k*. Szemerédi's theorem is equivalent to the asymptotic bound

$$r_k(N) = o(N).$$

That is, *r*<sub>*k*</sub>(*N*) grows less than linearly with *N*.

:::

## History {#History}

[Van der Waerden's theorem](wikipedia://en/157178), a precursor of Szemerédi's theorem, was proved in 1927.

The cases *k* = 1 and *k* = 2 of Szemerédi's theorem are trivial. The case *k* = 3, known as [Roth's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roth's_Theorem_on_Arithmetic_Progressions), was established in 1953 by [Klaus Roth](wikipedia://en/787827)[^o6] via an adaptation of the [Hardy–Littlewood circle method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Littlewood_circle_method). Szemerédi[^o7] next proved the case *k* = 4 through combinatorics. Using an approach similar to the one he used for the case *k* = 3, Roth[^o8] gave a second proof for *k* = 4 in 1972.

The general case was settled in 1975, also by Szemerédi,[^o9] who developed an ingenious and complicated extension of his previous combinatorial argument for *k* = 4 (called "a masterpiece of combinatorial reasoning" by [Erdős](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Erdos)[^o10]). Several other proofs are now known, the most important being those by [Hillel Furstenberg](wikipedia://en/6450152)[^o11][^o12] in 1977, using [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986), and by [Timothy Gowers](wikipedia://en/365715)[^o13] in 2001, using both [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659) and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) while also introducing what is now called the [Gowers norm](wikipedia://en/38041703). [Terence Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498) has called the various proofs of Szemerédi's theorem a "[Rosetta stone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rosetta_Stone)" for connecting disparate fields of mathematics.[^o14]

## Quantitative bounds {#Quantitative_bounds}

It is an open problem to determine the exact growth rate of *r*<sub>*k*</sub>(*N*). The best known general bounds are

$$CN\exp\left(-n2^{(n - 1)/2}\sqrt[n]{\log N} + \frac{1}{2n}\log \log N\right) \leq r_k(N) \leq \frac{N}{(\log \log N)^{2^{-2^{k + 9}}}},$$

where $n = \lceil \log k\rceil$. The lower bound is due to O'Bryant[^o16] building on the work of [Behrend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Behrend),[^o17] [Rankin](wikipedia://en/5884314),[^o18] and Elkin.[^o19][^o20] The upper bound is due to Gowers.[^o13]

For small *k*, there are tighter bounds than the general case. When *k* = 3, [Bourgain](wikipedia://en/1024131),[^o21][^o22] Heath-Brown,[^o23] Szemerédi,[^o24] [Sanders](wikipedia://en/36766438),[^o25] and [Bloom](wikipedia://en/71404791)[^o26] established progressively smaller upper bounds, and Bloom and Sisask then proved the first bound that broke the so-called "logarithmic barrier".[^o27] The current best bounds are

$N 2^{-\sqrt{8\log N}} \leq r_3(N) \leq N e^{-c(\log N)^{1/9}}$, for some constant $c>0$,

respectively due to O'Bryant,[^o16] and Bloom and Sisask[^o28] (the latter built upon the breakthrough result of Kelley and Meka,[^o29] who obtained the same upper bound, with "1/9" replaced by "1/12").

For *k* = 4, Green and Tao[^o30][^o31] proved that

$$r_4(N) \leq C\frac{N}{(\log N)^c}$$

For k=5 in 2023 and k≥5 in 2024 Leng, Sah and Sawhney proved in preprints [^o32][^o33][^o34] that:

$$r_k(N) \leq CN\exp(-(\log\log N)^c)$$

## Extensions and generalizations {#Extensions_and_generalizations}

A multidimensional generalization of Szemerédi's theorem was first proven by [Hillel Furstenberg](wikipedia://en/6450152) and [Yitzhak Katznelson](wikipedia://en/5643045) using ergodic theory.[^o36] [Timothy Gowers](wikipedia://en/365715),[^o37] [Vojtěch Rödl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vojtěch_Rödl) and Jozef Skokan[^o38][^o39] with Brendan Nagle, Rödl, and [Mathias Schacht](wikipedia://en/47982893),[^o40] and [Terence Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498)[^o41] provided combinatorial proofs.

Alexander Leibman and [Vitaly Bergelson](wikipedia://en/24810745)[^o42] generalized Szemerédi's to polynomial progressions: If $A \subset \mathbb{N}$ is a set with positive upper density and $p_1(n),p_2(n),\dotsc,p_k(n)$ are [integer-valued polynomials](wikipedia://en/585797) such that $p_i(0) = 0$, then there are infinitely many $u, n \in \mathbb{Z}$ such that $u + p_i(n) \in A$ for all $1 \leq i \leq k$. Leibman and Bergelson's result also holds in a multidimensional setting.

The finitary version of Szemerédi's theorem can be generalized to finite [additive groups](wikipedia://en/332229) including vector spaces over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615).[^o43] The finite field analog can be used as a model for understanding the theorem in the natural numbers.[^o44] The problem of obtaining bounds in the k=3 case of Szemerédi's theorem in the vector space $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ is known as the [cap set](wikipedia://en/51223601) problem.

The [Green–Tao theorem](wikipedia://en/7006166) asserts the prime numbers contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. It is not implied by Szemerédi's theorem because the primes have density 0 in the natural numbers. As part of their proof, [Ben Green](wikipedia://en/1927952) and Tao introduced a "relative" Szemerédi theorem which applies to subsets of the integers (even those with 0 density) satisfying certain [pseudorandomness](wikipedia://en/23210) conditions. A more general relative Szemerédi theorem has since been given by [David Conlon](wikipedia://en/36837348), [Jacob Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Fox), and [Yufei Zhao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yufei_Zhao).[^o45][^o46]

The [Erdős conjecture on arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/4312009) would imply both Szemerédi's theorem and the Green–Tao theorem.
