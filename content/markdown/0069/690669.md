# List coloring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List coloring"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **list coloring** is a type of [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) where each [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) can be restricted to a list of allowed colors. It was first studied in the 1970s in independent papers by [Vizing](wikipedia://en/21749361) and by [Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284), [Rubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arthur_Rubin), and Taylor.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given a graph $G$ and given a set $L(v)$ of colors for each vertex $v$ (called a **list**), a **list coloring** is a *choice function* that maps every vertex $v$ to a color in the list $L(v)$. As with graph coloring, a list coloring is generally assumed to be **proper**, meaning no two [adjacent vertices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacent_vertex) receive the same color. A graph is **$k$-choosable** (or **$k$-list-colorable**) if it has a proper list coloring no matter how one assigns a list of $k$ colors to each vertex. The **choosability** (or **list colorability** or **list chromatic number**) $\operatorname{ch}(G)$ of a graph $G$ is the least number $k$ such that $G$ is $k$-choosable.

More generally, for a function $f$ assigning a positive integer $f(v)$ to each vertex $v$, a graph $G$ is **$f$-choosable** (or **$f$-list-colorable**) if it has a list coloring no matter how one assigns a list of $f(v)$ colors to each vertex $v$. In particular, if $f(v) = k$ for all vertices $v$, $f$-choosability corresponds to $k$-choosability.

:::

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Consider the complete [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431) $G = K_{2,4}$, having six vertices $A$, $B$, $W$, $X$, $Y$, $Z$ such that $A$ and $B$ are each connected to all of $W$, $X$, $Y$, and $Z$, and no other vertices are connected. As a bipartite graph, $G$ has usual chromatic number 2: one may color $A$ and $B$ in one color and $W$, $X$, $Y$, $Z$ in another and no two adjacent vertices will have the same color. On the other hand, $G$ has list-chromatic number larger than 2, as the following construction shows: assign to $A$ and $B$ the lists {red, blue} and {green, black}. Assign to the other four vertices the lists {red, green}, {red, black}, {blue, green}, and {blue, black}. No matter which choice one makes of a color from the list of $A$ and a color from the list of $B$, there will be some other vertex such that both of its choices are already used to color its neighbors. Thus, $G$ is not 2-choosable.

On the other hand, it is easy to see that $G$ is 3-choosable: picking arbitrary colors for the vertices $A$ and $B$ leaves at least one available color for each of the remaining vertices, and these colors may be chosen arbitrarily.

*Image: List-coloring-K-3-27.svg.* A list coloring instance on the [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) $K_{3,27}$ with three colors per vertex. No matter which colors are chosen for the three central vertices, one of the outer 27 vertices will be uncolorable, showing that the list chromatic number of $K_{3,27}$ is at least four.

More generally, let $q$ be a positive integer, and let $G$ be the [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) $K_{q,q^{q}}$. Let the available colors be represented by the $q^{2}$ different two-digit numbers in [radix](wikipedia://en/701207) $q$. On one side of the bipartition, let the $q$ vertices be given sets of colors $\{i0, i1, i2, ...$} in which the first digits are equal to each other, for each of the $q$ possible choices of the first digit $i$. On the other side of the bipartition, let the $q^{q}$ vertices be given sets of colors $\{0a, 1b, 2c, ...$} in which the first digits are all distinct, for each of the $q^{q}$ possible choices of the $q$-tuple $(a, b, c, ...).$ The illustration shows a larger example of the same construction, with $q = 3$.

Then, $G$ does not have a list coloring for $L$: no matter what set of colors is chosen for the vertices on the small side of the bipartition, this choice will conflict with all of the colors for one of the vertices on the other side of the bipartition. For instance if the vertex with color set {00,01} is colored 01, and the vertex with color set {10,11} is colored 10, then the vertex with color set {01,10} cannot be colored. Therefore, the list chromatic number of $G$ is at least $q + 1$.[^o7]

Similarly, if $n=\tbinom{2k-1}{k},$ then the complete bipartite graph $K_{n,n}$ is not $k$-choosable. For, suppose that $2k - 1$ colors are available in total, and that, on a single side of the bipartition, each vertex has available to it a different $k$-tuple of these colors than each other vertex. Then, each side of the bipartition must use at least $k$ colors, because every set of $k - 1$ colors will be disjoint from the list of one vertex. Since at least $k$ colors are used on one side and at least $k$ are used on the other, there must be one color which is used on both sides, but this implies that two adjacent vertices have the same color. In particular, the [utility graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utility_graph) $K_{3,3}$ has list-chromatic number at least three, and the graph $K_{10,10}$ has list-chromatic number at least four.[^o8]

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

For a graph $G$, let $\chi(G)$ denote the [chromatic number](wikipedia://en/426743) and $\Delta(G)$ the [maximum degree](wikipedia://en/325802) of $G$. The list coloring number $\operatorname{ch}(G)$ satisfies the following properties.

1. $\operatorname{ch}(G) \ge \chi(G)$. A $k$-list-colorable graph must in particular have a list coloring when every vertex is assigned the same list of $k$ colors, which corresponds to a usual $k$-coloring.
1. $\operatorname{ch}(G)$ cannot be bounded in terms of chromatic number in general, that is, there is no function $f$ such that $\operatorname{ch}(G) \le f(\chi(G))$ holds for every graph $G$. In particular, as the complete bipartite graph examples show, there exist graphs with $\chi(G) = 2$ but with $\operatorname{ch}(G)$ arbitrarily large.[^o7]
1. $\operatorname{ch}(G) \le \chi(G) \ln(n)$ where $n$ is the number of vertices of $G$.[^o11][^o12]
1. $\operatorname{ch}(G) \le \Delta(G) + 1$.[^o8][^o13]
1. $\operatorname{ch}(G) \le 5$ if $G$ is a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314).[^o14]
1. $\operatorname{ch}(G) \le 3$ if $G$ is a [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431) planar graph.[^o15]

:::

## Computing choosability and (*a*, *b*)-choosability {#Computing_choosability_and_(a,_b)-choosability}

Two algorithmic problems have been considered in the literature:

1. $k$-*choosability*: decide whether a given graph is $k$-choosable for a given $k$, and
1. $(a, b)$-*choosability*: decide whether a given graph is $f$-choosable for a given function $f : V \to \{a,\dots,b\}$.

It is known that $k$-choosability in bipartite graphs is $\Pi^p_2$-complete for any $k \ge 3$, and the same applies for 4-choosability in planar graphs, 3-choosability in planar [triangle-free graphs](wikipedia://en/7667996), and (2, 3)-choosability in [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431) planar graphs.[^o17][^o18] For $P_{5}$-free graphs, that is, graphs [excluding](wikipedia://en/13547826) a 5-vertex [path graph](wikipedia://en/4587190), $k$-choosability is [fixed-parameter tractable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed-parameter_tractable). [^o19]

It is possible to test whether a graph is 2-choosable in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) by repeatedly deleting vertices of degree zero or one until reaching the [2-core](wikipedia://en/27545816) of the graph, after which no more such deletions are possible. The initial graph is 2-choosable [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its 2-core is either an even cycle or a [theta graph](wikipedia://en/2472919) formed by three paths with shared endpoints, with two paths of length two and the third path having any even length.[^o8]
