# Bound state

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bound state"}

A **bound state** is a composite of two or more fundamental building blocks, such as particles, atoms, or bodies, that behaves as a single object and in which energy is required to split them.[^o2]

:::

In [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics), a bound state is a [quantum state](wikipedia://en/30876419) of a [particle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particle) subject to a [potential](wikipedia://en/23703) such that the particle has a tendency to remain localized in one or more regions of space.[^o3] The potential may be external or it may be the result of the presence of another particle; in the latter case, one can equivalently define a bound state as a state representing two or more particles whose [interaction energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interaction_energy) exceeds the total energy of each separate particle. One consequence is that, given a potential [vanishing at infinity](wikipedia://en/461986), negative-energy states must be bound. The [energy spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_spectrum) of the set of bound states are most commonly discrete, unlike [scattering states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_state) of [free particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_particle), which have a continuous spectrum.

Although not bound states in the strict sense, [metastable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metastable) states with a net positive interaction energy, but long decay time, are often considered unstable bound states as well and are called "quasi-bound states".[^o4] Examples include [radionuclides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radionuclides) and [Rydberg atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rydberg_atom).[^o5]

In [relativistic](wikipedia://en/26962) [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267), a stable bound state of $n$ particles with masses $\{m_k\}_{k=1}^n$ corresponds to a [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) in the [S-matrix](wikipedia://en/622053) with a [center-of-mass energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_mass_frame) less than $\textstyle\sum_k m_k$. An [unstable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unstable) bound state shows up as a pole with a [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) center-of-mass energy.

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Particle overview.svg.* An overview of the various families of elementary and composite particles, and the theories describing their interactions

- A [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) and an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) can move separately; when they do, the total center-of-mass energy is positive, and such a pair of particles can be described as an ionized atom. Once the electron starts to "orbit" the proton, the energy becomes negative, and a bound state – namely the [hydrogen atom](wikipedia://en/14225) – is formed. Only the lowest-energy bound state, the [ground state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_state), is stable. Other [excited states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excited_state) are unstable and will decay into stable (but not other unstable) bound states with less energy by emitting a [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon).
- A [positronium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positronium) "atom" is an [unstable bound state](wikipedia://en/41660) of an [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) and a [positron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron). It decays into [photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon).
- Any state in the [quantum harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719) is bound, but has positive energy. Note that $\lim_{x\to\pm\infty}{V_{\text{QHO}}(x)} = \infty$ , so the below does not apply.
- A [nucleus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_nucleus) is a bound state of [protons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) and [neutrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron) ([nucleons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nucleon)).
- The [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton) itself is a bound state of three [quarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quark) (two [up](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Up_quark) and one [down](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Down_quark); one [red](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_charge), one [green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_charge) and one [blue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_charge)). However, unlike the case of the hydrogen atom, the individual quarks can never be isolated. See [confinement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_confinement).
- The [Hubbard](wikipedia://en/1702398) and [Jaynes–Cummings–Hubbard (JCH)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaynes-Cummings-Hubbard_model) models support similar bound states. In the Hubbard model, two repulsive [bosonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bosonic) [atoms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atoms) can form a bound pair in an [optical lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_lattice).[^o8][^o9][^o10] The JCH Hamiltonian also supports two-[polariton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polariton) bound states when the photon-atom interaction is sufficiently strong.[^o11]

## Definition {#Definition}

Let [$\sigma$-finite measure space](wikipedia://en/45270#Important_classes_of_measure_spaces) $(X, \mathcal A, \mu)$ be a [probability space](wikipedia://en/45270#Important_classes_of_measure_spaces) associated with [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $H$. Define a [one-parameter group of unitary operators](wikipedia://en/893698) $(U_t)_{t\in \mathbb{R}}$, a [density operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_operator) $\rho = \rho(t_0)$ and an [observable](wikipedia://en/294248) $T$ on $H$. Let $\mu(T,\rho)$ be the induced probability distribution of $T$ with respect to $\rho$. Then the evolution

$$\rho(t_0)\mapsto [U_t(\rho)](t_0) = \rho(t_0 +t)$$

is **bound** with respect to $T$ if

$$\lim_{R \rightarrow \infty}{\sup_{t \geq t_0}{\mu(T,\rho(t))(\mathbb{R}_{> R})}} = 0,$$

where $\mathbb{R}_{>R} = \lbrace x \in \mathbb{R} \mid x > R \rbrace$.[^o14]

A quantum particle is in a **bound state** if at no point in time it is found "too far away" from any finite region $R\subset X$. Using a [wave function](wikipedia://en/145343) representation, for example, this means[^o15]

$$\begin{aligned} 0 &= \lim_{R\to\infty}{\mathbb{P}(\text{particle measured inside }X\setminus R)} \\ &= \lim_{R\to\infty}{\int_{X\setminus R}|\psi(x)|^2\,d\mu(x)}, \end{aligned}$$

such that

$$\int_X{|\psi(x)|^{2}\,d\mu(x)} < \infty.$$

In general, a quantum state is a bound state *if and only if* it is finitely [normalizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_amplitude#Normalization) for all times $t\in\mathbb{R}$ and remains spatially localized.[^o16] Furthermore, a bound state lies within the [pure point part](wikipedia://en/206137#Classification_of_points_in_the_spectrum) of the spectrum of $T$ *if and only if* it is an [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of $T$.[^o17]

More informally, "boundedness" results foremost from the choice of [domain of definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domain_of_definition) and characteristics of the state rather than the observable.[^o18] For a concrete example: let $H := L^2(\mathbb{R})$ and let $T$ be the [position operator](wikipedia://en/4838571). Given compactly supported $\rho = \rho(0) \in H$ and $[-1,1] \subseteq \mathrm{Supp}(\rho)$.

- If the state evolution of $\rho$ "moves this wave package to the right", e.g., if $[t-1,t+1] \in \mathrm{Supp}(\rho(t))$ for all $t \geq 0$, then $\rho$ is not bound state with respect to position.
- If $\rho$ does not change in time, i.e., $\rho(t) = \rho$ for all $t \geq 0$, then $\rho$ is bound with respect to position.
- More generally: If the state evolution of $\rho$ "just moves $\rho$ inside a bounded domain", then $\rho$ is bound with respect to position.

## Properties {#Properties}

As finitely normalizable states must lie within the [pure point part](wikipedia://en/206137#Classification_of_points_in_the_spectrum) of the spectrum, bound states must lie within the pure point part. However, as [Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) and [Wigner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wigner) pointed out, it is possible for the energy of a bound state to be located in the continuous part of the spectrum. This phenomenon is referred to as [bound state in the continuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bound_state_in_the_continuum).[^o21][^o22]

### Position-bound states {#Position-bound_states}

Consider the one-particle Schrödinger equation. If a state has energy $E < \max{\left(\lim_{x\to\infty}{V(x)}, \lim_{x\to-\infty}{V(x)}\right)}$, then the wavefunction $\psi$ satisfies, for some $X > 0$

$$\frac{\psi^{\prime\prime}}{\psi}=\frac{2m}{\hbar^2}(V(x)-E) > 0\text{ for }x > X$$

so that $\psi$ is exponentially suppressed at large $x$. This behaviour is well-studied for smoothly varying potentials in the [WKB approximation](wikipedia://en/700154) for wavefunction, where an oscillatory behaviour is observed if the right hand side of the equation is negative and growing/decaying behaviour if it is positive.[^o24] Hence, negative energy-states are bound if $V(x)$ vanishes at infinity.

### Non-degeneracy in one-dimensional bound states {#Non-degeneracy_in_one-dimensional_bound_states}

One-dimensional bound states can be shown to be non-degenerate in energy for well-behaved wavefunctions that decay to zero at infinities. This need not hold true for wavefunctions in higher dimensions. Due to the property of non-degenerate states, one-dimensional bound states can always be expressed as real wavefunctions.

::: {#o27 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Node theorem"}

### Node theorem {#Node_theorem}

Node theorem states that $n\text{th}$ bound wavefunction ordered according to increasing energy has exactly $n-1$ nodes, i.e., points $x=a$ where $\psi(a)=0 \neq \psi'(a)$. Due to the form of Schrödinger's time independent equations, it is not possible for a physical wavefunction to have $\psi(a) = 0 = \psi'(a)$ since it corresponds to $\psi(x)=0$ solution.[^o28]

:::

## Requirements {#Requirements}

A [boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) with mass $m_{\chi}$ [mediating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_boson) a [weakly coupled interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coupling_%28physics%29#Particle_physics_and_quantum_field_theory) produces an [Yukawa-like](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yukawa_potential) interaction potential,

$$V(r) = \pm\frac{\alpha_\chi}{r} e^{- \frac{r}{\lambda\!\!\!\frac{}{\ }_\chi}},$$

where $\alpha_\chi=g^2/4\pi$, $g$ is the gauge coupling constant, and $\text{ƛ}_{i} = \tfrac{\hbar}{m_{i}c}$ is the [reduced Compton wavelength](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_Compton_wavelength). A [scalar boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalar_boson) produces a universally attractive potential, whereas a vector attracts particles to antiparticles but repels like pairs. For two particles of mass $m_{1}$ and $m_{2}$, the [Bohr radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_radius) of the system becomes

$$a_0=\frac{{\lambda\!\!\!^{{}^\underline{\ \ }}}_1 + {\lambda\!\!\!^{{}^\underline{\ \ }}}_2}{\alpha_\chi}$$

and yields the dimensionless number

$$D=\frac{{\lambda\!\!\!^{{}^\underline{\ \ }}}_\chi}{a_0} = \alpha_\chi\frac{{\lambda\!\!\!^{{}^\underline{\ \ }}}_\chi}{{\lambda\!\!\!^{{}^\underline{\ \ }}}_1 + {\lambda\!\!\!^{{}^\underline{\ \ }}}_2} = \alpha_\chi\frac{m_1+m_2}{m_\chi}.$$

In order for the first bound state to exist at all, $D\gtrsim 0.8$. Because the [photon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon) is massless, $D$ is infinite for [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism). For the [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction), the [Z boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_boson)'s mass is \91.1876 ± \0.0021 GeV/c2, which prevents the formation of bound states between most particles, as it is \97.2 times the [proton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proton)'s mass and 178,000 times the [electron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron)'s mass.

Note, however, that, if the [Higgs interaction](wikipedia://en/644550) did not break electroweak symmetry at the [electroweak scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electroweak_scale), then the SU(2) [weak interaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weak_interaction) would become [confining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confinement_%28physics%29).[^o30]
