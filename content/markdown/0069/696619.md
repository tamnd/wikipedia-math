# Binomial series

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binomial series"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **binomial series** is a generalization of the [binomial formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_formula) to cases where the [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent) is not a positive integer:

:::

::: {#o2 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\begin{aligned} (1+x)^\alpha &= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \!\binom\alpha k x^k \\ &= 1 +\alpha x +\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)}{2!} x^2 +\frac{\alpha(\alpha-1)(\alpha-2)}{3!} x^3 +\cdots \end{aligned} \tag{1}$$

:::

where $\alpha$ is any [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826), and the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) on the right-hand side is expressed in terms of the [(generalized) binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668#Generalization_and_connection_to_the_binomial_series)

$$\binom{\alpha}{k} = \frac{\alpha (\alpha-1) (\alpha-2) \cdots (\alpha-k+1)}{k!}.$$

The binomial series is the [MacLaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacLaurin_series) for the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f(x)=(1+x)^\alpha$. It converges when $|x| < 1$.

If $\alpha$ is a nonnegative [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n$ then the $x^{n + 1}$ term and all later terms in the series are $0$, since each contains a factor of $(n - n)$. In this case, the series is a finite polynomial, equivalent to the binomial formula.

## Convergence {#Convergence}

### Conditions for convergence {#Conditions_for_convergence}

Whether ((1)) [converges](wikipedia://en/1743431) depends on the values of the complex numbers $\alpha$ and $x$. More precisely:

1. If $|x| < 1$, the series converges [absolutely](wikipedia://en/61532) for any complex number $\alpha$.
1. If $|x| = 1$, the series converges absolutely [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) either $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha) > 0$ or $\alpha = 0$, where $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)$ denotes the [real part](wikipedia://en/5826) of $\alpha$.
1. If $|x| = 1$ and $x \ne -1$, the series converges if and only if $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha) > -1$.
1. If $x = -1$, the series converges if and only if either $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha) > 0$ or $\alpha = 0$.
1. If $|x| > 1$, the series [diverges](wikipedia://en/876428) except when $\alpha$ is a non-negative integer, in which case the series is a finite sum.

In particular, if $\alpha$ is not a non-negative integer, the situation at the boundary of the [disk of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476), $\lvert x\rvert = 1$, is summarized as follows:

- If $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha) > 0$, the series converges absolutely.
- If $-1 < \operatorname{Re}(\alpha) \le 0$, the series converges [conditionally](wikipedia://en/1198956) if $x \ne -1$ and diverges if $x = -1$.
- If $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha) \le -1$, the series diverges.

### Identities to be used in the proof {#Identities_to_be_used_in_the_proof}

The following hold for any complex number $\alpha$:

$${\alpha \choose 0} \!= 1,$$

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$${\alpha \choose k+1} \!= \!{\alpha\choose k} \frac{\alpha-k}{k+1}, \tag{2}$$

:::

::: {#o7 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$${\alpha \choose k-1} \!+ \!{\alpha\choose k} \!= \!{\alpha+1 \choose k}. \tag{3}$$

:::

Unless $\alpha$ is a nonnegative integer (in which case the binomial coefficients vanish as $k$ is larger than $\alpha$), a useful [asymptotic](wikipedia://en/641995) relationship for the binomial coefficients is, in [Landau notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau_notation):

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$${\alpha \choose k} \!= \frac{(-1)^k} {\Gamma(-\alpha)k^ {1+\alpha} } \,(1+o(1)), \quad\text{as }k\to\infty. \tag{4}$$

:::

This is essentially equivalent to Euler's definition of the [Gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316):

$$\Gamma(z) = \lim_{k \to \infty} \frac{k! \,k^z}{z(z+1)\cdots(z+k)},$$

and implies immediately the coarser bounds

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\frac {m} {k^{1+\operatorname{Re}\,\alpha}}\le \left|{\alpha \choose k}\right| \le \frac {M} {k^{1+\operatorname{Re}\alpha}}, \tag{5}$$

:::

for some positive constants $m$ and $M$ .

Formula ((2)) for the generalized binomial coefficient can be rewritten as

::: {#o10 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$${\alpha \choose k} \!= \prod_{j=1}^k \!\left(\frac{\alpha + 1}j - 1 \right). \tag{6}$$

:::

::: {#o12 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

To prove (i) and (v), apply the [ratio test](wikipedia://en/421341) and use formula ((2)) above to show that whenever $\alpha$ is not a nonnegative integer, the [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476) is exactly 1. Part (ii) follows from formula ((5)), by comparison with the [$p$-series](wikipedia://en/4576951#p-series_test)

$$\sum_{k=1}^\infty \frac1{k^p},$$

with $p=1+\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)$. To prove (iii), first use formula ((3)) to obtain

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$(1 + x) \sum_{k=0}^n \!{\alpha \choose k} x^k =\sum_{k=0}^n \!{\alpha+1\choose k} x^k + {\alpha \choose n} x^{n+1}, \tag{7}$$

:::

and then use (ii) and formula ((5)) again to prove convergence of the right-hand side when $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)> - 1$ is assumed. On the other hand, the series does not converge if $|x|=1$ and $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha) \le - 1$, again by formula ((5)). Alternatively, we may observe that for all $j$, $\left| \frac{\alpha + 1}j - 1 \right| \ge 1 - \frac{\operatorname{Re} (\alpha) + 1}j \ge 1$. Thus, by formula ((6)), for all $k, \left|{\alpha \choose k} \right| \ge 1$. This completes the proof of (iii). Turning to (iv), we use identity ((7)) above with $x=-1$ and $\alpha-1$ in place of $\alpha$, along with formula ((4)), to obtain

$$\sum_{k=0}^n \!{\alpha\choose k} (-1)^k = \!{\alpha-1 \choose n} (-1)^n= \frac1{\Gamma(-\alpha+1)n^\alpha} (1+o(1))$$

as $n\to\infty$. Assertion (iv) now follows from the asymptotic behavior of the sequence $n^{-\alpha} = e^{-\alpha \log(n)}$. (Precisely, $\left|e^{-\alpha\log n}\right| = e^{-\operatorname{Re}(\alpha) \log n}$ certainly converges to $0$ if $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)>0$ and diverges to $+\infty$ if $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)<0$. If $\operatorname{Re}(\alpha)=0$, then $n^{-\alpha} = e^{-i \operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\log n}$ converges if and only if the sequence $\operatorname{Im}(\alpha)\log n$ converges $\bmod{2\pi}$, which is certainly true if $\alpha=0$ but false if $\operatorname{Im}(\alpha) \ne 0$: in the latter case the sequence is dense $\!\bmod{2\pi}$, due to the fact that $\log n$ diverges and $\log (n+1)-\log n$ converges to zero).

:::

## Summation of the binomial series {#Summation_of_the_binomial_series}

The usual argument to compute the sum of the binomial series goes as follows. [Differentiating](wikipedia://en/7921) term-wise the binomial series within the disk of convergence $\lvert x\rvert < 1$ and using formula ((1)), one has that the sum of the series is an [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478) solving the [ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/32742753) $(1 + x)u'(x) - \alpha u(x) = 0$ with [initial condition](wikipedia://en/1186804) $u(0) = 1$.

The unique solution of this problem is the function $u(x) = (1 + x)^{\alpha}$. Indeed, multiplying by the [integrating factor](wikipedia://en/1912874) $(1 + x)^{-\alpha-1}$ gives

$$0=(1+x)^{-\alpha}u'(x) - \alpha (1+x)^{-\alpha-1} u(x)= \big[(1+x)^{-\alpha}u(x)\big]'\,,$$

so the function $(1 + x)^{-\alpha}u(x)$ is a constant, which the initial condition tells us is $1$. That is, $u(x) = (1 + x)^{\alpha}$ is the sum of the binomial series for $\lvert x\rvert < 1$.

The equality extends to $\lvert x\rvert = 1$ whenever the series converges, as a consequence of [Abel's theorem](wikipedia://en/237305) and by [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) of $(1 + x)^{\alpha}$.

## Negative binomial series {#Negative_binomial_series}

Closely related is the *negative binomial series* defined by the [MacLaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacLaurin_series) for the function $g(x)=(1-x)^{-\alpha}$, where $\alpha \in \mathbb{C}$ and $|x| < 1$. Explicitly,

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{(1 - x)^\alpha} &= \sum_{k=0}^{\infty} \; \frac{g^{(k)}(0)}{k!} \; x^k \\ &= 1 + \alpha x + \frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)}{2!} x^2 + \frac{\alpha(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)}{3!} x^3 + \cdots, \end{aligned}$$

which is written in terms of the [multiset coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiset_coefficient)

$$\left(\!\!{\alpha\choose k}\!\!\right) = {\alpha+k-1 \choose k} = \frac{\alpha (\alpha+1) (\alpha+2) \cdots (\alpha+k-1)}{k!}\,.$$

When $\alpha$ is a positive integer, several common sequences are apparent. The case $\alpha = 1$ gives the series $1 + x + x^{2} + x^{3} + ...$, where the coefficient of each term of the series is simply $1$. The case $\alpha = 2$ gives the series $1 + 2x + 3x^{2} + 4x^{3} + ...$, which has the counting numbers as coefficients. The case $\alpha = 3$ gives the series $1 + 3x + 6x^{2} + 10x^{3} + ...$, which has the [triangle numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangle_numbers) as coefficients. The case $\alpha = 4$ gives the series $1 + 4x + 10x^{2} + 20x^{3} + ...$, which has the [tetrahedral numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_numbers) as coefficients, and similarly for higher integer values of $\alpha$.

The negative binomial series includes the case of the [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630), the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958)[^o16]

$$\frac{1}{1-x} = \sum_{n=0}^\infty x^n$$

(which is the negative binomial series when $\alpha=1$, convergent in the disc $|x|<1$) and, more generally, series obtained by differentiation of the geometric power series:

$$\frac{1}{(1-x)^n} = \frac{1}{(n-1)!}\frac{d^{n-1}}{dx^{n-1}}\frac{1}{1-x}$$

with $\alpha=n$, a positive integer.[^o17]

## History {#History}

The first results concerning binomial series for other than positive-integer exponents were given by Sir [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) in the study of [areas](wikipedia://en/1209) enclosed under certain curves. [John Wallis](wikipedia://en/239290) built upon this work by considering expressions of the form $y = (1 - x^{2})^{m}$ where $m$ is a fraction. He found that (written in modern terms) the successive coefficients $c_{k}$ of $(-x^{2})^{k}$ are to be found by multiplying the preceding coefficient by $\tfrac{m - (k - 1)}{k}$ (as in the case of integer exponents), thereby implicitly giving a formula for these coefficients. He explicitly writes the following instances

$$(1-x^2)^{1/2}=1-\frac{x^2}2-\frac{x^4}8-\frac{x^6}{16}\cdots$$

$$(1-x^2)^{3/2}=1-\frac{3x^2}2+\frac{3x^4}8+\frac{x^6}{16}\cdots$$

$$(1-x^2)^{1/3}=1-\frac{x^2}3-\frac{x^4}9-\frac{5x^6}{81}\cdots$$

The binomial series is therefore sometimes referred to as [Newton's binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677#Newton's_generalized_binomial_theorem). Newton gives no proof and is not explicit about the nature of the series. Later, on 1826 [Niels Henrik Abel](wikipedia://en/21573) discussed the subject in a paper published on *[Crelle's Journal](wikipedia://en/973547)*, treating notably questions of convergence.[^n1]
