# Invariant subspace problem

*Image: Eigenvalue equation.svg.* The vector $x$ is an [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $A$. Every operator on a non-trivial complex [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) has an eigenvector, solving the invariant subspace problem for these spaces.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Invariant subspace problem"}

In the field of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) known as [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **invariant subspace problem** is a partially unresolved problem asking whether every [bounded operator](wikipedia://en/455961) on a [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) sends some non-trivial [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) to itself. Many variants of the problem have been solved, by restricting the class of bounded operators considered or by specifying a particular class of Banach spaces. The problem is still [open](wikipedia://en/1333335) for [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) (in other words, each example, found so far, of an operator with no non-trivial invariant subspaces is an operator that acts on a Banach space that is not [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to a separable Hilbert space).

:::

## History {#History}

The problem seems to have been stated in the mid-20th century after work by [Beurling](wikipedia://en/286530) and [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942),[^o3] who found (but never published) a positive solution for the case of [compact operators](wikipedia://en/577441). It was then posed by [Paul Halmos](wikipedia://en/318598) for the case of operators $T$ such that $T^2$ is compact. This was resolved affirmatively, for the more general class of polynomially compact operators (operators $T$ such that $p(T)$ is a compact operator for a suitably chosen nonzero [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $p$), by [Allen R. Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allen_R._Bernstein) and [Abraham Robinson](wikipedia://en/46024) in 1966 (see Non-standard analysis for a summary of the proof).

For [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989), the first example of an operator without an invariant subspace was constructed by [Per Enflo](wikipedia://en/7180897). He proposed a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) to the invariant subspace problem in 1975, publishing an outline in 1976. Enflo submitted the full article in 1981 and the article's complexity and length delayed its publication to 1987.[^o4] Enflo's long "manuscript had a world-wide circulation among mathematicians"[^o3] and some of its ideas were described in publications besides Enflo (1976).[^o5] Enflo's works inspired a similar construction of an operator without an invariant subspace for example by [Bernard Beauzamy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Beauzamy), who acknowledged Enflo's ideas.[^o4]

In the 1990s, Enflo developed a "constructive" approach to the invariant subspace problem on Hilbert spaces.[^o6]

In May 2023, a preprint of Enflo appeared on arXiv,[^o7] which, if correct, solves the problem for Hilbert spaces and completes the picture.

In July 2023, a second and independent preprint of Neville appeared on arXiv,[^o8] claiming the solution of the problem for separable Hilbert spaces.

::: {#o10 .statement cue=heading name="Precise statement"}

## Precise statement {#Precise_statement}

Formally, the **invariant subspace problem** for a complex [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $H$ of [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) > 1 is the question whether every [bounded linear operator](wikipedia://en/455961) $T: H \to H$ has a non-trivial [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [$T$-invariant subspace](wikipedia://en/585826): a closed [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) $W$ of $H$, which is different from $\{0\}$ and from $H$, such that $T(W)\subset W$.

A negative answer to the problem is closely related to properties of the [orbits](wikipedia://en/252250) $T$. If $x$ is an element of the Banach space $H$, the orbit of $x$ under the action of $T$, denoted by $[x]$, is the subspace generated by the sequence $\{ T^{n}(x)\,:\, n \ge 0\}$. This is also called the [$T$-cyclic subspace](wikipedia://en/41392084) generated by $x$. From the definition it follows that $[x]$ is a $T$-invariant subspace. Moreover, it is the *minimal* $T$-invariant subspace containing $x$: if $W$ is another invariant subspace containing $x$, then necessarily $T^n(x) \in W$ for all $n \ge 0$ (since $W$ is $T$-invariant), and so $[x]\subset W$. If $x$ is non-zero, then $[x]$ is not equal to $\{0\}$, so its closure is either the whole space $H$ (in which case $x$ is said to be a [cyclic vector](wikipedia://en/58713633) for $T$) or it is a non-trivial $T$-invariant subspace. Therefore, a counterexample to the invariant subspace problem would be a Banach space $H$ and a bounded operator $T: H \to H$ for which every non-zero vector $x\in H$ is a [cyclic vector](wikipedia://en/58713633) for $T$. (Where a "cyclic vector" $x$ for an operator $T$ on a Banach space $H$ means one for which the orbit $[x]$ of $x$ is dense in $H$.)

### Known special cases {#Known_special_cases}

While the case of the invariant subspace problem for separable Hilbert spaces is still open, several other cases have been settled for [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) (over the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of complex numbers):

- For finite-dimensional complex vector spaces, every operator admits an eigenvector, so it has a 1-dimensional invariant subspace.
- The conjecture is true if the Hilbert space $H$ is not [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) (i.e. if it has an [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063)). In fact, if $x$ is a non-zero vector in $H$, the norm closure of the linear orbit $[x]$ is separable (by construction) and hence a proper subspace and also invariant.
- von Neumann showed[^o12] that any compact operator on a Hilbert space of dimension at least 2 has a non-trivial invariant subspace.
- The [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) shows that all [normal operators](wikipedia://en/190167) admit invariant subspaces.
- (Aronszajn Smith 1954) proved that every [compact operator](wikipedia://en/577441) on any Banach space of dimension at least 2 has an invariant subspace.
- (Bernstein Robinson 1966) proved using [non-standard analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_analysis) that if the operator $T$ on a Hilbert space is polynomially compact (in other words $p(T)$ is compact for some nonzero polynomial $p$) then $T$ has an invariant subspace. Their proof uses the original idea of embedding the infinite-dimensional Hilbert space in a [hyperfinite](wikipedia://en/25317960)-dimensional Hilbert space (see [Non-standard analysis#Invariant subspace problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_analysis#Invariant_subspace_problem)).
- (Halmos 1966), after having seen Robinson's preprint, eliminated the non-standard analysis from it and provided a shorter proof in the same issue of the same journal.
- (Lomonosov 1973) gave a very short proof using the [Schauder fixed point theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schauder_fixed_point_theorem) that if the operator $T$ on a Banach space commutes with a non-zero compact operator then $T$ has a non-trivial invariant subspace. This includes the case of polynomially compact operators because an operator commutes with any polynomial in itself. More generally, he showed that if $S$ commutes with a non-scalar operator $T$ that commutes with a non-zero compact operator, then $S$ has an invariant subspace.[^o17]
- The first example of an operator on a Banach space with no non-trivial invariant subspaces was found by (txt), and his example was simplified by (Beauzamy 1985).
- The first counterexample on a "classical" Banach space was found by (txt), who described an operator on the classical Banach space $l_1$ with no invariant subspaces.
- Later (txt) constructed an operator on $l_1$ without even a non-trivial closed invariant *subset*, that is that for every vector $x$ the *set* $\{ T^{n}(x)\,:\, n \ge 0\}$ is dense, in which case the vector is called [hypercyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypercyclic_vector) (the difference with the case of cyclic vectors is that we are not taking the subspace generated by the points $\{ T^{n}(x)\,:\, n \ge 0\}$ in this case).
- (Atzmon 1983) gave an example of an operator without invariant subspaces on a [nuclear](wikipedia://en/2702957) [Fréchet space](wikipedia://en/233801).
- (Śliwa 2008) proved that any infinite-dimensional Banach space of countable type over a [non-Archimedean field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-Archimedean_field) admits a bounded linear operator without a non-trivial closed invariant subspace. This completely solves the non-Archimedean version of this problem, posed by van Rooij and Schikhof in 1992.
- (Argyros Haydon 2011) gave the construction of an infinite-dimensional Banach space such that every continuous operator is the sum of a compact operator and a scalar operator, so in particular every operator has an invariant subspace.
