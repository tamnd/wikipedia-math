# Polyform

*Image: All 18 Pentominoes.svg.* The 18 one-sided [pentominoes](wikipedia://en/23712): polyforms consisting of five squares.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polyform"}

In [recreational mathematics](wikipedia://en/26446), a **polyform** is a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) figure or solid compound constructed by joining together identical basic [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621). The basic polygon is often (but not necessarily) a [convex](wikipedia://en/660019) plane-filling polygon, such as a [square](wikipedia://en/659939) or a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). More specific names have been given to polyforms resulting from specific basic polygons, as detailed in the table below. For example, a square basic polygon results in the well-known [polyominoes](wikipedia://en/72634).

:::

## Construction rules {#Construction_rules}

The rules for joining the polygons together may vary, and must therefore be stated for each distinct type of polyform. Generally, however, the following rules apply:

1. Two basic polygons may be joined only along a common edge, and must share the entirety of that edge.
1. No two basic polygons may overlap.
1. A polyform must be connected (that is, all one piece; see [connected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph), [connected space](wikipedia://en/6233)). Configurations of disconnected basic polygons do not qualify as polyforms.
1. The mirror image of an asymmetric polyform is not considered a distinct polyform (polyforms are "double sided").

These construction rules are not meant to be set in stone, but rather serve as general guidelines as to how polyforms may be constructed. Modifications of the first construction rule, for example, lead to different polyforms. Joining at a common vertex may lead to [polykings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyking), and being joined not by edge, but by the chess movement of the knight may lead to [polyknights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyknight).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Polyforms can also be considered in higher dimensions. In 3-dimensional space, basic [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra) can be joined along congruent faces. Joining [cubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube_%28geometry%29) in this way produces the [polycubes](wikipedia://en/710426), and joining [tetrahedrons](wikipedia://en/30606) in this way produces the polytetrahedrons. 2-dimensional polyforms can also be folded out of the plane along their edges, in similar fashion to a [net](wikipedia://en/1148129); in the case of polyominoes, this results in [polyominoids](wikipedia://en/22339952).

One can allow more than one basic polygon. The possibilities are so numerous that the exercise seems pointless, unless extra requirements are brought in. For example, the [Penrose tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrose_tile) define extra rules for joining edges, resulting in interesting polyforms with a kind of pentagonal symmetry.

When the base form is a polygon that tiles the plane, rule 1 may be broken. For instance, squares may be joined orthogonally at vertices, as well as at edges, to form hinged/[pseudo-polyominoes](wikipedia://en/32965449), also known as polyplets or polykings.

:::

## Types and applications {#Types_and_applications}

Polyforms are a rich source of problems, [puzzles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Puzzle) and [games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game). The basic [combinatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial) problem is counting the number of different polyforms, given the basic polygon and the construction rules, as a function of *n*, the number of basic polygons in the polyform.
