# List of countries by GDP (PPP) per capita

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of countries by GDP (PPP) per capita"}

*Image: GDP PPP per cápita by countries 2026.png.* }

A country's [gross domestic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) (GDP) at [purchasing power parity](wikipedia://en/64489) (PPP) per capita is the PPP value of all final [goods and services](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Goods_and_services) produced within an [economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economy) in a given year, divided by the average (or mid-year) population for the same year. This is similar to nominal GDP per capita but adjusted for the [cost of living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_living) in each country.

:::

In 2026, the estimated average GDP per capita (PPP) of all of the countries was \$25,591. For rankings regarding wealth, see [list of countries by wealth per adult](wikipedia://en/40103183).

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Method"}

## Method {#Method}

The [gross domestic product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product) (GDP) [per capita](wikipedia://en/357788) figures on this page are derived from PPP calculations. Such calculations are prepared by various organizations, including the [IMF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IMF) and the [World Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Bank). As estimates and assumptions have to be made, the results produced by different organizations for the same country are not hard facts and tend to differ, sometimes substantially, so they should be used with caution.

Comparisons of national wealth are frequently made based on *nominal* GDP and [savings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savings) (not just income), which do not reflect differences in the [cost of living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost_of_living) in different countries (*see [List of countries by GDP (nominal) per capita](wikipedia://en/1050736)*); hence, using a PPP basis is arguably more useful when comparing generalized differences in [living standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Living_standards) between economies because PPP takes into account the relative cost of living and the [inflation rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inflation_rates) of the countries, rather than using only [exchange rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exchange_rates), which may distort the real differences in income.

This is why GDP (PPP) per capita is considered one of the indicators of a country's standard of living,[^o4][^o5] The relation between [GDP per capita and standard of living](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gross_domestic_product#Standard_of_living) has been criticized. Alternative measures of standard of living include [list of countries by average wage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_average_wage), [median income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_income) and [disposable household and per capita income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disposable_household_and_per_capita_income). GDP (PPP) and GDP (PPP) per capita are usually measured by [international dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_dollar), which is a hypothetical [currency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Currency) that has the same purchasing power in every economy as the [U.S. dollar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U.S._dollar) in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States). The share of the shadow economy is significant in many European countries, ranging from less than 10 to over 40% of GDP.[^o6] Since 2014, EU member states have been encouraged by Eurostat, the official statistics body, to include some illegal activities.[^o7][^o8][^o9]

:::

## Distorted GDP-per-capita for tax havens {#Distorted_GDP-per-capita_for_tax_havens}

There are many natural economic reasons for GDP-per-capita to vary between jurisdictions (e.g. places rich in oil and gas tend to have high GDP-per-capita figures). However, it is increasingly being recognized that [tax havens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_havens), or [corporate tax havens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_tax_haven), have distorted economic data which produces artificially high, or inflated, GDP-per-capita figures.[^o37] It is estimated that over 15% of global jurisdictions are tax havens (see [tax haven lists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tax_haven#Tax_haven_lists)).[^o38] An IMF investigation estimates that circa 40% of global foreign direct investment flows, which heavily influence the GDP of various jurisdictions, are described as "phantom" transactions.[^o39]

A stunning \$12 trillion—almost 40 per cent of all foreign direct investment positions globally—is completely artificial: it consists of financial investment passing through empty corporate shells with no real activity. These investments in empty corporate shells almost always pass through well-known tax havens. The eight major pass-through economies—the Netherlands, Luxembourg, Hong Kong SAR, the British Virgin Islands, Bermuda, the Cayman Islands, Ireland, and Singapore—host more than 85 per cent of the world's investment in special purpose entities, which are often set up for tax reasons.

In 2017, Ireland's economic data became so distorted by U.S. multinational tax avoidance strategies (see [leprechaun economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leprechaun_economics)), also known as [BEPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BEPS) actions, that Ireland effectively abandoned GDP (and GNP) statistics as credible measures of its economy, and created a replacement statistic called [modified gross national income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_gross_national_income) (or GNI\*). Ireland is one of the world's largest [corporate tax havens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corporate_tax_haven).

Ireland has, more or less, stopped using GDP to measure its economy. And on current trends \[because Irish GDP is distorting EU-28 aggregate data\], the eurozone taken as a whole may need to consider something similar.

The statistical distortions created by the impact on the Irish National Accounts of the global assets and activities of a handful of large multinational corporations have now become so large as to make a mockery of conventional uses of Irish GDP.
