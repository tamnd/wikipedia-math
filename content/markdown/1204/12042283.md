# I know that I know nothing

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="I know that I know nothing"}

"**I know that I know nothing**" is a saying derived from [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s account of the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greece) [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates): *"For I was conscious that I knew practically nothing..."* ([Plato](wikipedia://en/22954), Apology 22d, translated by Harold North Fowler, 1966).[^o2] It is also sometimes called the **Socratic paradox**, although this name is often instead used to refer to other seemingly paradoxical claims made by [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) in [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s dialogues (most notably, [Socratic intellectualism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_intellectualism) and the Socratic fallacy).[^o3]

:::

This saying is also connected or conflated with the [answer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_oracular_statements_from_Delphi#440_BC) to a question [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) (according to [Xenophon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenophon)) or [Chaerephon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chaerephon) (according to [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)) is said to have posed to the [Pythia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythia), the [Oracle of Delphi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oracle_of_Delphi), in which the oracle stated something to the effect of "Socrates is the wisest person in [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens)."[^o4] [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates), believing the oracle but also completely convinced that he knew nothing, was said to have concluded that *nobody* knew anything, and that he was only wiser than others because he was the only person who recognized his own ignorance.

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The phrase, originally from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) ("*ipse se nihil scire id unum sciat*"),[^o6] is a possible paraphrase from a [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) text (see below). It is also quoted as "*scio me nihil scire*" or "*scio me nescire*".[^o7] It was later [back-translated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Back-translation) to [Katharevousa Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katharevousa_Greek) as "\[ἓν οἶδα ὅτι\] οὐδὲν οἶδα" (\[*hèn oîda hóti*\] *oudèn oîda*).[^o8]

## In Plato {#In_Plato}

This is technically a shorter paraphrasing of [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates)' statement, "I neither know nor think I know" (in [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954), *Apology* 21d). The paraphrased saying, though widely attributed to [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s Socrates in both ancient and modern times, actually occurs nowhere in [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s works in precisely the form "I know I know nothing."[^o10] As of 2008, two prominent [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) scholars had argued that the claim should not be attributed to [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s Socrates.[^o11]

Evidence that [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) does not actually claim to know nothing can be found at *Apology* 29b-c, where he claims twice to know something. See also *Apology* 29d, where [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) indicates that he is so confident in his claim to knowledge at 29b-c that he is willing to die for it.

That said, in the *[Apology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apology_%28Plato%29)*, [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) relates that [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) accounts for his seeming wiser than any other person because he does not imagine that he knows what he does not know.[^o12]

> ... ἔοικα γοῦν τούτου γε σμικρῷ τινι αὐτῷ τούτῳ σοφώτερος εἶναι, ὅτι ἃ μὴ οἶδα οὐδὲ οἴομαι εἰδέναι. ... I seem, then, in just this little thing to be wiser than this man at any rate, that what I do not know I do not think I know either. \[Henry Cary literal translation of 1897\]

A more commonly used translation puts it,

> Although I do not suppose that either of us knows anything really beautiful and good, I am better off than he is – for he knows nothing, and thinks he knows. I neither know nor think I know. \[[Benjamin Jowett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Jowett) translation\].

Regardless, the context in which this passage occurs is the same, independently of any specific translation. That is, [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) having gone to a "wise" man, and having discussed with him, withdraws and thinks the above to himself. [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates), since he denied any kind of knowledge, then tried to find someone wiser than himself among politicians, poets, and craftsmen. It appeared that politicians claimed wisdom without knowledge; poets could touch people with their words, but did not know their meaning; and craftsmen could claim knowledge only in specific and narrow fields. The interpretation of the Oracle's answer might be [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates)' awareness of his own ignorance.[^o13]

[Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) also deals with this phrase in [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s dialogue *[Meno](wikipedia://en/594486)* when he says:[^o14]

> καὶ νῦν περὶ ἀρετῆς ὃ ἔστιν ἐγὼ μὲν οὐκ οἶδα, σὺ μέντοι ἴσως πρότερον μὲν ᾔδησθα πρὶν ἐμοῦ ἅψασθαι, νῦν μέντοι ὅμοιος εἶ οὐκ εἰδότι.
>
> So now I do not know what virtue is; perhaps you knew before you contacted me, but now you are certainly like one who does not know. ([G. M. A. Grube](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Grube) translation)

Here, [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) aims at the change of Meno's opinion, who was a firm believer in his own opinion and whose claim to knowledge [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) had disproved.

It is essentially the question that begins "[post-Socratic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Socratic)" Western philosophy. Socrates begins all wisdom with wondering, thus one must begin with admitting one's ignorance. After all, Socrates' dialectic method of teaching was based on that he as a teacher knew nothing, so he would derive knowledge from his students by dialogue.

There is also a passage by [Diogenes Laërtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes_Laërtius) in his work *[Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lives_and_Opinions_of_Eminent_Philosophers)* written hundreds of years after [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954), where he lists, among the things that Socrates used to say:[^o15] "εἰδέναι μὲν μηδὲν πλὴν αὐτὸ τοῦτο εἰδέναι", or "that he knew nothing except that he knew that very fact (i.e. that he knew nothing)".

Again, closer to the quote, there is a passage in [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954)'s *Apology*, where Socrates says that after discussing with someone he started thinking that:[^o12]

> τούτου μὲν τοῦ ἀνθρώπου ἐγὼ σοφώτερός εἰμι· κινδυνεύει μὲν γὰρ ἡμῶν οὐδέτερος οὐδὲν καλὸν κἀγαθὸν εἰδέναι, ἀλλ᾽ οὗτος μὲν οἴεταί τι εἰδέναι οὐκ εἰδώς, ἐγὼ δέ, ὥσπερ οὖν οὐκ οἶδα, οὐδὲ οἴομαι· ἔοικα γοῦν τούτου γε σμικρῷ τινι αὐτῷ τούτῳ σοφώτερος εἶναι, ὅτι ἃ μὴ οἶδα οὐδὲ οἴομαι εἰδέναι.
>
> I am wiser than this man, for neither of us appears to know anything great and good; but he fancies he knows something, although he knows nothing; whereas I, as I do not know anything, do not fancy I do. In this trifling particular, then, I appear to be wiser than he, because I do not fancy I know what I do not know.

It is also a curiosity that there is more than one passage in the narratives in which Socrates claims to have knowledge on some topic, for instance on love:[^o16]

> How could I vote 'No,' when the only thing I know is the art of love? (τὰ ἐρωτικά)[^o17]
>
> I know virtually nothing, except a certain small subject – love (τῶν ἐρωτικῶν), although on this subject, I'm thought to be amazing (δεινός), better than anyone else, past or present.[^o18]

## Alternative usage {#Alternative_usage}

"[Socratic paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socratic_paradox_%28disambiguation%29)" may also refer to statements of Socrates that seem contrary to common sense, such as that "no one desires evil".[^o20]

The words of the [apostle St. Paul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apostle_St._Paul) are also found paralleling this saying in 1Cor, "*Anyone who claims to know something does not yet have the necessary knowledge.*"[^o21]
