# Pfaffian

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pfaffian"}

*Image: Johann\_Friedrich\_Pfaff.jpg.* Johann Friedrich Pfaff

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of an *m*-by-*m* [skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055) can always be written as the square of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) in the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) entries, a polynomial with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) coefficients that only depends on *m*. When *m* is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135), the polynomial is zero, and when *m* is [even](wikipedia://en/143135), it is a nonzero polynomial of [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) *m*/2, and is unique up to multiplication by ±1. The convention on skew-symmetric [tridiagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tridiagonal) matrices, given below in the examples, then determines one specific polynomial, called the **Pfaffian** polynomial. The value of this polynomial, when applied to the entries of a skew-symmetric matrix, is called the **Pfaffian** of that matrix. The term Pfaffian was introduced by (txt), who indirectly named them after [Johann Friedrich Pfaff](wikipedia://en/1669324).

:::

Explicitly, for a skew-symmetric matrix $A$,

$$\operatorname{pf}(A)^2=\det(A),$$

which was first [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by (txt), who cites [Jacobi](wikipedia://en/210340) for introducing these polynomials in work on [Pfaffian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pfaffian_system) [systems of differential equations](wikipedia://en/63871781). Cayley obtains this relation by specialising a more general result on matrices that deviate from skew symmetry only in the first row and the first column. The determinant of such a matrix is the product of the Pfaffians of the two matrices obtained by first setting in the original matrix the upper left entry to zero and then copying, respectively, the negative [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of the first row to the first column and the negative transpose of the first column to the first row. This is proved by [induction](wikipedia://en/18881) by expanding the determinant on [minors](wikipedia://en/262107) and employing the recursion formula below.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

$$A = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a \\ -a & 0 \end{bmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{pf}(A) = a.$$

$$B = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a & b \\ -a & 0 & c \\ -b & -c & 0 \end{bmatrix},\qquad\operatorname{pf}(B) = 0.$$

(3 is odd, so the Pfaffian of B is 0)

$$\operatorname{pf}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & a & b & c \\ -a & 0 & d & e \\ -b & -d & 0& f \\-c & -e & -f & 0 \end{bmatrix}=af-be+dc.$$

The Pfaffian of a 2*n* × 2*n* skew-symmetric [tridiagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/519218) is equal to

$$\operatorname{pf}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & a_1 & 0 & 0\\ -a_1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 &0 & a_2 \\ 0 & 0 & -a_2 &0&\ddots\\ &&&\ddots&\ddots& \\ &&&& &0&a_n\\ &&&&&-a_n&0 \end{bmatrix} = a_1 a_2\cdots a_n.$$

(Note that any skew-symmetric matrix can be reduced to this form by conjugation with a unitary matrix; see [Spectral theory of a skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055#Spectral_theory).)

:::

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let *A* = (*a*<sub>*ij*</sub>) be a 2*n* × 2*n* skew-symmetric matrix. The Pfaffian of *A* is explicitly defined by the formula

$$\operatorname{pf}(A) = \frac{1}{2^n n!}\sum_{\sigma\in S_{2n}}\operatorname{sgn}(\sigma)\prod_{i=1}^{n}a_{\sigma(2i-1),\sigma(2i)} \,,$$

where *S*<sub>2*n*</sub> is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) of degree 2*n* and sgn(σ) is the [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28permutation%29) of σ.

One can make use of the skew-symmetry of *A* to avoid summing over all possible [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027). Let Π be the set of all [partitions](wikipedia://en/340240) of $\{1, 2, ..., 2n\}$ into pairs without regard to order. There are (2*n*)!/(2<sup>*n*</sup>*n*!) = (2*n* − 1)[!!](wikipedia://en/507208) such partitions. A permutation σ specifies a partition

$$\{\{\sigma(1), \sigma(2)\},\{\sigma(3), \sigma(4)\}, \cdots, \{\sigma(2n-1), \sigma(2n)\}\}.$$

Because of the skew-symmetry of *A*, all of the 2<sup>*n*</sup>*n*! permutations specifying a given partition contribute the same amount to the sum. Thus the number of terms in the sum can be reduced by picking a single permutation for each possible partition. An element α ∈ Π can be written as

$$\alpha = \{(i_1,j_1),(i_2,j_2),\cdots,(i_n,j_n)\}$$

with *i*<sub>*k*</sub> \< *j*<sub>*k*</sub> and $i_1 < i_2 < \cdots < i_n$. Let

$$\pi_\alpha = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 & 4 & \cdots & 2n -1 & 2n \\ i_1 & j_1 & i_2 & j_2 & \cdots & i_n & j_{n} \end{bmatrix}$$

be the corresponding permutation. Given a partition α as above, define

$$A_\alpha = \operatorname{sgn}(\pi_\alpha)a_{i_1,j_1}a_{i_2,j_2}\cdots a_{i_n,j_n}.$$

The Pfaffian of *A* is then given by

$$\operatorname{pf}(A) = \sum_{\alpha\in\Pi} A_\alpha.$$

The Pfaffian of a *n* × *n* skew-symmetric matrix for *n* odd is defined to be zero, as the determinant of an odd skew-symmetric matrix is zero, since for a skew-symmetric matrix,

$$\det A = \det A^\text{T} = \det(-A) = (-1)^n \det A,$$

and for *n* odd, this implies $\det A = 0$.

### Recursive definition {#Recursive_definition}

By convention, the Pfaffian of the 0 × 0 matrix is equal to one. The Pfaffian of a skew-symmetric 2*n* × 2*n* matrix *A* with *n* > 0 can be computed recursively as

$$\operatorname{pf}(A) = \sum_{{j=1}\atop{j\neq i}}^{2n}(-1)^{i+j+1+\theta(i-j)}a_{ij}\operatorname{pf}(A_{\hat{\imath}\hat{\jmath}}),$$

where the index *i* can be selected arbitrarily, $\theta(i-j)$ is the [Heaviside step function](wikipedia://en/87299), and $A_{\hat{\imath}\hat{\jmath}}$ denotes the matrix *A* with both the *i*-th and *j*-th rows and columns removed.[^o9] Note how for the special choice $i=1$ this reduces to the simpler expression:

$$\operatorname{pf}(A) = \sum_{j=2}^{2n}(-1)^{j}a_{1j}\operatorname{pf}(A_{\hat{1}\hat{\jmath}}).$$

::: {#o11 .definition cue=heading name="Alternative definitions"}

### Alternative definitions {#Alternative_definitions}

One can associate to any skew-symmetric 2*n* × 2*n* matrix *A* = (*a*<sub>*ij*</sub>) a [bivector](wikipedia://en/221537)

$$\omega = \sum_{i<j} a_{ij}\;e_i \wedge e_j ,$$

where $\{e_{1}, e_{2}, ..., e_{2n}\}$ is the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) of **R**<sup>2*n*</sup>. The Pfaffian is then defined by the equation

$$\frac{1}{n!}\omega^n = \operatorname{pf}(A)\;e_1\wedge e_2\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{2n},$$

where *ω*<sup>*n*</sup> denotes the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product) of *n* copies of *ω*.

Equivalently, we can consider the bivector (which is more convenient when we do not want to impose the summation constraint $i < j$):

$$\omega'=2 \omega = \sum_{i, j} a_{ij}\;e_i\wedge e_j,$$

which gives $\omega'^n = 2^n n! \operatorname{pf}(A)\;e_1\wedge e_2\wedge\cdots\wedge e_{2n}.$

:::

### Odd generalization {#Odd_generalization}

A non-zero generalisation of the Pfaffian to odd-dimensional matrices is given in the work of de Bruijn on multiple integrals involving determinants.[^o13] In particular for any *m* × *m* matrix *A*, we use the formal definition above but set $n = \lfloor m/2\rfloor$. For *m* odd, one can then show that this is equal to the usual Pfaffian of an (*m*+1) × (*m*+1)-dimensional skew symmetric matrix where we have added an (*m*+1)th column consisting of *m* elements 1, an (*m*+1)th row consisting of *m* elements −1, and the corner element is zero. The usual properties of Pfaffians, for example the relation to the determinant, then apply to this extended matrix.

:::

## Properties and identities {#Properties_and_identities}

Pfaffians have the following properties, which are similar to those of determinants.

- Multiplication of a row and a column by a constant is equivalent to multiplication of the Pfaffian by the same constant.
- Simultaneous interchange of two different rows and corresponding columns changes the sign of the Pfaffian.
- A multiple of a row and corresponding column added to another row and corresponding column does not change the value of the Pfaffian.

Using these properties, Pfaffians can be computed quickly, akin to the computation of determinants.

### Miscellaneous {#Miscellaneous}

For a 2*n* × 2*n* skew-symmetric matrix *A*

$$\operatorname{pf}(A^\text{T}) = (-1)^n\operatorname{pf}(A).$$

$$\operatorname{pf}(\lambda A) = \lambda^n \operatorname{pf}(A).$$

$$\operatorname{pf}(A)^2 = \det(A).$$

For an arbitrary 2*n* × 2*n* matrix *B*,

$$\operatorname{pf}(BAB^\text{T})= \det(B)\operatorname{pf}(A).$$

Substituting in this equation *B = A<sup>m</sup>*, one gets for all integer *m*

$$\operatorname{pf}(A^{2m+1})= (-1)^{nm}\operatorname{pf}(A)^{2m+1}.$$

::: {#o16 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof of \\operatorname{pf}(BAB^\\text{T})= \\det(B)\\operatorname{pf}(A):"}

**Proof of $\operatorname{pf}(BAB^\text{T})= \det(B)\operatorname{pf}(A)$:.**

As previously said, $A \rightarrow \sum_{ij} A_{ij} e_i \wedge e_j \xrightarrow[]{\wedge n} {2^n n!}Pf(A) e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2n}.$ The same with $BAB^\mathrm{T}$:

$$\begin{aligned}&BAB^\mathrm{T} \rightarrow \sum_{ijkl}B_{ik}B_{jl}A_{kl} e_i \wedge e_j = \sum_{kl} A_{kl} f_k \wedge f_l\\& \xrightarrow{\wedge n} {2^n n!}Pf(A) f_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge f_{2n} = {2^n n!}Pf(BAB^\mathrm{T}) e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2n},\end{aligned}$$

where we defined $f_k = \sum_i B_{ik}e_i$.

Since $f_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge f_{2n} = \det(B) e_1 \wedge \cdots \wedge e_{2n},$ the proof is finished.

:::

::: {#o17 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof of \\operatorname{pf}(A)^2 = \\det(A):"}

**Proof of $\operatorname{pf}(A)^2 = \det(A)$:.**

Since $\operatorname{pf}(A)^2 = \det(A)$ is an equation of polynomials, it suffices to prove it for real matrices, and it would automatically apply for [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) matrices as well.

By the [spectral theory of skew-symmetric real matrices](wikipedia://en/174055#Spectral_theory), $A = Q\Sigma Q^\mathrm{T}$, where $Q$ is [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/105620) and

$$\Sigma = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & a_1 & 0 & 0\\ -a_1 & 0 & 0 & 0\\ 0 & 0 &0 & a_2 \\ 0 & 0 & -a_2 &0&\ddots\\ &&&\ddots&\ddots& \\ &&&& &0&a_n\\ &&&&&-a_n&0 \end{bmatrix}$$

for real numbers $a_k$. Now apply the previous theorem, we have $pf(A)^2 = pf(\Sigma)^2 \det(Q)^2 = pf(\Sigma)^2 = \left(\prod a_i\right)^2 = \det(A)$.

:::

### Derivative identities {#Derivative_identities}

If *A* depends on some variable *x*<sub>*i*</sub>, then the gradient of a Pfaffian is given by

$$\frac{1}{\operatorname{pf}(A)}\frac{\partial\operatorname{pf}(A)}{\partial x_i}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(A^{-1}\frac{\partial A}{\partial x_i}\right),$$

and the [Hessian](wikipedia://en/412108) of a Pfaffian is given by

$$\frac{1}{\operatorname{pf}(A)}\frac{\partial^2\operatorname{pf}(A)}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}=\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(A^{-1}\frac{\partial^2 A}{\partial x_i\partial x_j}\right)-\frac{1}{2}\operatorname{tr}\left(A^{-1}\frac{\partial A}{\partial x_i}A^{-1}\frac{\partial A}{\partial x_j}\right)+\frac{1}{4}\operatorname{tr}\left(A^{-1}\frac{\partial A}{\partial x_i}\right)\operatorname{tr}\left(A^{-1}\frac{\partial A}{\partial x_j}\right).$$

The following formula applies even if the matrix $A$ is singular for some values of the variable $x$:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial x_k}{\operatorname{pf}(A)}=\sum_{1\leq i<j\leq 2n}(-1)^{i+j+1}\frac{\partial A_{ij}}{\partial x_k} {\operatorname{pf}(A_{\hat{i}\hat{j}})},$$

where $A_{\hat{i}\hat{j}}$ is the $(2n-2)\times (2n-2)$ skew-symmetric matrix obtained from $A$ by deleting rows and columns $(i,j)$.

### Trace identities {#Trace_identities}

The product of the Pfaffians of skew-symmetric matrices *A* and *B* can be represented in the form of an exponential

$$\textrm{pf}(A)\,\textrm{pf}(B) = \exp(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\log(A^\text{T}B)).$$

Suppose *A* and *B* are 2*n* × 2*n* skew-symmetric matrices, then

$$\mathrm{pf}(A)\,\mathrm{pf}(B) = \tfrac{1}{n!} B_n(s_1, s_2, \ldots, s_n), \qquad \mathrm{where} \qquad s_l = - \tfrac{1}{2}(l - 1)!\,\mathrm{tr}((AB)^l)$$

and *B*<sub>*n*</sub>(*s*<sub>1</sub>,*s*<sub>2</sub>,...,*s*<sub>n</sub>) are [Bell polynomials](wikipedia://en/572498).

### Block matrices {#Block_matrices}

For a block-diagonal matrix

$$A_1\oplus A_2=\begin{bmatrix} A_1 & 0 \\ 0 & A_2 \end{bmatrix},$$

$$\operatorname{pf}(A_1\oplus A_2) =\operatorname{pf}(A_1)\operatorname{pf}(A_2).$$

For an arbitrary *n* × *n* matrix *M*:

$$\operatorname{pf}\begin{bmatrix} 0 & M \\ -M^\text{T} & 0 \end{bmatrix} = (-1)^{n(n-1)/2}\det M.$$

It is often required to compute the Pfaffian of a skew-symmetric matrix $S$ with the block structure

$$S = \begin{pmatrix} M & Q \\ -Q^\mathrm{T} & N \end{pmatrix}\,$$

where $M$ and $N$ are skew-symmetric matrices and $Q$ is a general rectangular matrix.

When $M$ is [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122), one has

$$\operatorname{pf}(S) = \operatorname{pf}(M)\operatorname{pf}(N+ Q^\mathrm{T} M^{-1} Q).$$

This can be seen from Aitken block-diagonalization formula,[^o21][^o22][^o23]

$$\begin{pmatrix}M& 0\\ 0 & N+Q^\mathrm{T} M^{-1} Q\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}I& 0\\ Q^\mathrm{T} M^{-1} & I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} M & Q\\ -Q^\mathrm{T} & N\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}I& -M^{-1} Q\\ 0 & I \end{pmatrix}.$$

This decomposition involves a [congruence transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_transformations) that allow to use the Pfaffian property $\operatorname{pf}(BAB^\mathrm{T}) = \operatorname{det}(B)\operatorname{pf}(A)$.

Similarly, when $N$ is invertible, one has

$$\operatorname{pf}(S) = \operatorname{pf}(N)\operatorname{pf}(M+ Q N^{-1} Q^\mathrm{T}),$$

as can be seen by employing the decomposition

$$\begin{pmatrix}M+Q N^{-1} Q^\mathrm{T}& 0\\ 0 & N\end{pmatrix} = \begin{pmatrix}I& -Q N^{-1}\\ 0 & I\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix} M& Q\\ -Q^\mathrm{T} & N\end{pmatrix} \begin{pmatrix}I& 0\\ N^{-1} Q^\mathrm{T}& I \end{pmatrix}.$$

## Calculating the Pfaffian numerically {#Calculating_the_Pfaffian_numerically}

Suppose *A* is a *2n × 2n* skew-symmetric matrices, then

$$\textrm{pf}(A) = i^{(n^2)} \exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\log((\sigma_y\otimes I_n)^\mathrm{T}\cdot A)\right),$$

where $\sigma_y$ is the second [Pauli matrix](wikipedia://en/24868), $I_n$ is an [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) of dimension *n* and we took the trace over a [matrix logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_logarithm).

This equality is based on the [trace identity](wikipedia://en/530060#Trace_identities)

$$\textrm{pf}(A)\,\textrm{pf}(B) = \exp\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\mathrm{tr}\log(A^\text{T}B)\right)$$

and on the observation that $\textrm{pf}(\sigma_y\otimes I_n)=(-i)^{n^2}$.

Since calculating the [logarithm of a matrix](wikipedia://en/2931687) is a computationally demanding task, one can instead compute all [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of $((\sigma_y\otimes I_n)^\mathrm{T}\cdot A)$, take the log of all of these and sum them up. This procedure merely exploits the [property](wikipedia://en/2931687#Properties) $\operatorname{tr}{\log{(AB)}} = \operatorname{tr}{\log{(A)}} + \operatorname{tr}{\log{(B)}}$. This can be implemented in [Mathematica](wikipedia://en/49024) with a single statement:

`Pf[x_] := Module[{n = Dimensions[x]{{brackets|1}} / 2}, I^(n^2) Exp[ 1/2 Total[ Log[Eigenvalues[ Dot[Transpose[KroneckerProduct[PauliMatrix[2], IdentityMatrix[n]]], x] ]]]]]`

However, this algorithm is unstable when the Pfaffian is large. The eigenvalues of $(\sigma_y\otimes I_n)^\mathrm{T}\cdot A$ will generally be complex, and the logarithm of these complex eigenvalues are generally taken to be in $[-\pi, \pi]$. Under the summation, for a real valued Pfaffian, the argument of the exponential will be given in the form $x + k\pi/2$ for some integer $k$. When $x$ is very large, rounding errors in computing the resulting sign from the complex phase can lead to a non-zero imaginary component.

For other (more) efficient algorithms see (Wimmer 2012).

## Applications {#Applications}

- The Pfaffian is an [invariant polynomial](wikipedia://en/1798843) of a skew-symmetric matrix under a proper [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/173954) [change of basis](wikipedia://en/1179451). As such, it is important in the theory of [characteristic classes](wikipedia://en/363530). In particular, it can be used to define the [Euler class](wikipedia://en/2932393) of a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) that is used in the [generalized Gauss–Bonnet theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Gauss–Bonnet_theorem).
- There exist programs for the numerical computation of the Pfaffian on various platforms (Python, [Matlab](wikipedia://en/20412), Mathematica) (Wimmer 2012).
- The number of [perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/44031) in a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is given by a Pfaffian, hence is [polynomial time](wikipedia://en/405944) computable via the [FKT algorithm](wikipedia://en/30159370). This is surprising given that for general graphs, the problem is very difficult (so called [\#P-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharp-P-complete)). This result is used to calculate the number of [domino tilings](wikipedia://en/10565476) of a rectangle, the [partition function](wikipedia://en/312252) of [Ising models](wikipedia://en/292744) in physics, or of [Markov random fields](wikipedia://en/1323985) in [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) ((Globerson Jaakkola 2007); (Schraudolph Kamenetsky 2009)), where the underlying graph is planar. It is also used to derive efficient algorithms for some otherwise seemingly intractable problems, including the efficient simulation of certain types of [restricted quantum computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Restricted_quantum_computation). See [Holographic algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holographic_algorithm) for more information.
