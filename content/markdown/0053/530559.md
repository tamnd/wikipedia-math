# Ideal (order theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ideal"}

In [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924), an **ideal** is a special subset of a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572) (poset). Although this term historically was derived from the notion of a [ring ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_ideal) of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), it has subsequently been generalized to a different notion. Ideals are of great importance for many constructions in order and [lattice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_theory).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A subset $I$ of a partially ordered set $(P, \leq)$ is an **ideal**, if the following conditions hold:[^o4][^o5]

1. $I$ is [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty),
1. for every *x* in $I$ and *y* in *P*, $y \le x$ implies that *y* is in $I$ ($I$ is a [lower set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_set)),
1. for every *x*, *y* in $I$, there is some element *z* in $I$, such that $x \le z$ and $y \le z$ ($I$ is a [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165)).

While this is the most general way to define an ideal for arbitrary posets, it was originally defined for [lattices](wikipedia://en/469304) only. In this case, the following equivalent definition can be given: a subset $I$ of a lattice $(P, \leq)$ is an ideal [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is a lower set that is closed under finite [joins](wikipedia://en/3956618) ([suprema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suprema)); that is, it is nonempty and for all *x*, *y* in $I$, the element $x \vee y$ of *P* is also in $I$.[^n1]

A weaker notion of **order ideal** is defined to be a subset of a poset $P$ that satisfies the above conditions 1 and 2. In other words, an order ideal is simply a [lower set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_set). Similarly, an ideal can also be defined as a "directed lower set".

The [dual](wikipedia://en/600618) notion of an ideal, i.e., the concept obtained by reversing all ≤ and exchanging $\vee$ with $\wedge,$ is a [filter](wikipedia://en/19719).

[Frink ideals](wikipedia://en/21687353), [pseudoideals](wikipedia://en/21687566) and [Doyle pseudoideals](wikipedia://en/21687566) are different generalizations of the notion of a lattice ideal.

An ideal or filter is said to be **proper** if it is not equal to the whole set *P*.[^n1]

The smallest ideal that contains a given element *p* is a principal ideal and *p* is said to be a principal element of the ideal in this situation. The principal ideal $\downarrow p$ for a principal *p* is thus given by $\downarrow p = \{x \in P\mid x \le p\}$.

### Terminology and history {#Terminology_and_history}

The above definitions of "ideal" and "order ideal" are the standard ones,[^n1][^n2][^n3] but there is some confusion in terminology. Sometimes the words and definitions such as "ideal", "order ideal", "[Frink ideal](wikipedia://en/21687353)", or "partial order ideal" mean one another.[^o12][^o13]

Ideals were introduced by [Marshall H. Stone](wikipedia://en/758331) first for [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959),[^o14] where the name was derived from the ring ideals of abstract algebra. He adopted this terminology because, using the [isomorphism of the categories](wikipedia://en/489935) of [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959) and of [Boolean rings](wikipedia://en/54356), the two notions do indeed coincide.

Generalization to any posets was done by [Frink](wikipedia://en/38498178).[^o15]

:::

## Prime ideals {#Prime_ideals}

An important special case of an ideal is constituted by those ideals whose set-theoretic complements are filters, i.e. ideals in the inverse order. Such ideals are called **prime ideals**. Also note that, since we require ideals and filters to be non-empty, every prime ideal is necessarily proper. For lattices, prime ideals can be characterized as follows:

A subset $I$ of a lattice $(P, \leq)$ is a prime ideal, if and only if

1. $I$ is a proper ideal of *P*, and
1. for all elements *x* and *y* of *P*, $x \wedge y$ in $I$ implies that $x \in I$ or $y \in I$.

It is easily checked that this is indeed equivalent to stating that $P \setminus I$ is a filter (which is then also prime, in the dual sense).

For a [complete lattice](wikipedia://en/44777) the further notion of a **completely prime ideal** is meaningful. It is defined to be a proper ideal $I$ with the additional property that, whenever the meet ([infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum)) of some arbitrary set $A$ is in $I$, some element of *A* is also in $I$. So this is just a specific prime ideal that extends the above conditions to infinite meets.

The existence of prime ideals is in general not obvious, and often a satisfactory amount of prime ideals cannot be derived within ZF ([Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) without the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840)). This issue is discussed in various [prime ideal theorems](wikipedia://en/314919), which are necessary for many applications that require prime ideals.

:::

## Maximal ideals {#Maximal_ideals}

An ideal $I$ is a maximal ideal if it is proper and there is no *proper* ideal *J* that is a strict superset of $I$. Likewise, a filter *F* is maximal if it is proper and there is no proper filter that is a strict superset.

When a poset is a [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883), maximal ideals and filters are necessarily prime, while the converse of this statement is false in general.

Maximal filters are sometimes called [ultrafilters](wikipedia://en/31911), but this terminology is often reserved for Boolean algebras, where a maximal filter (ideal) is a filter (ideal) that contains exactly one of the elements {*a*, ¬*a*}, for each element *a* of the Boolean algebra. In Boolean algebras, the terms *prime ideal* and *maximal ideal* coincide, as do the terms *prime filter* and *maximal filter*.

There is another interesting notion of maximality of ideals: Consider an ideal $I$ and a filter *F* such that $I$ is [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) from *F*. We are interested in an ideal *M* that is maximal among all ideals that contain $I$ and are disjoint from *F*. In the case of distributive lattices such an *M* is always a prime ideal. A proof of this statement follows.

::: {#o19 .proof cue=math-proof}

Assume the ideal *M* is maximal with respect to disjointness from the filter *F*. Suppose for a contradiction that *M* is not prime, i.e. there exists a pair of elements *a* and *b* such that $a \land b$ in *M* but neither *a* nor *b* are in *M*. Consider the case that for all *m* in *M*, $m \lor a$ is not in *F*. One can construct an ideal *N* by taking the downward closure of the set of all binary joins of this form, i.e. $N = \{x\mid x \le m \lor a\text{ for }\text{ some } m \in M\}$. It is readily checked that *N* is indeed an ideal disjoint from *F* which is strictly greater than *M*. But this contradicts the maximality of *M* and thus the assumption that *M* is not prime.

For the other case, assume that there is some *m* in *M* with $m \lor a$ in *F*. Now if any element *n* in *M* is such that $n \lor b$ is in *F*, one finds that $(m \lor n) \lor b$ and $(m \lor n) \lor a$ are both in *F*. But then their meet is in *F* and, by distributivity, $(m \lor n) \lor (a \land b)$ is in *F* too. On the other hand, this finite join of elements of *M* is clearly in *M*, such that the assumed existence of *n* contradicts the disjointness of the two sets. Hence all elements *n* of *M* have a join with *b* that is not in *F*. Consequently one can apply the above construction with *b* in place of *a* to obtain an ideal that is strictly greater than *M* while being disjoint from *F*. This finishes the proof.

:::

However, in general it is not clear whether there exists any ideal *M* that is maximal in this sense. Yet, if we assume the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) in our set theory, then the existence of *M* for every disjoint filter–ideal-pair can be shown. In the special case that the considered order is a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959), this theorem is called the [Boolean prime ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/314919). It is strictly weaker than the axiom of choice and it turns out that nothing more is needed for many order-theoretic applications of ideals.

::: {#o21 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The construction of ideals and filters is an important tool in many applications of order theory.

- In [Stone's representation theorem for Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/604111), the maximal ideals (or, equivalently via the negation map, ultrafilters) are used to obtain the set of points of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), whose [clopen sets](wikipedia://en/233398) are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the original Boolean algebra.
- Order theory knows many [completion procedures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completion_%28order_theory%29) to turn posets into posets with additional [completeness](wikipedia://en/549445) properties. For example, the [ideal completion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_completion) of a given partial order *P* is the set of all ideals of *P* ordered by subset inclusion. This construction yields the [free](wikipedia://en/333835) [dcpo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_complete_partial_order) generated by *P*. An ideal is principal if and only if it is [compact](wikipedia://en/680261) in the ideal completion, so the original poset can be recovered as the sub-poset consisting of compact elements. Furthermore, every [algebraic dcpo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_poset) can be reconstructed as the ideal completion of its set of compact elements.

:::

## Prime and maximal spectra {#Prime_and_maximal_spectra}

Let $\operatorname{Spec}(L), \operatorname{Max}(L)$ be the set of all prime ideals and the set of all maximal ideals in a distributive lattice $L$. As in algebraic geometry, they can be given the Zariski topologies. Then

- $\operatorname{Spec}(L)$ is a [spectral space](wikipedia://en/544273): a compact sober space satisfying some conditions. In particular, it is $T_0$. It is Hausdorff if and only if $L$ is a Boolean algebra.[^o23]
- $\operatorname{Max}(L)$ is a compact $T_1$-space.[^o24] Conversely every compact $T_1$-space is of this form: $L$ can be taken to be the topology of the space.
- $\operatorname{Spec}(L) = \operatorname{Max}(L)$ if and only if $L$ is a Boolean algebra.[^o25]
- Given a topological space $X$, a [base](wikipedia://en/59220) $B$ for the topology is called a [Wallman base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallman_base) if it satisfies some conditions (which exists if $X$ is $T_1$). When $X$ is $T_0$ and such $B$ exists, the natural map

$$X \to \operatorname{Max}(B), \, x \mapsto \{ U \in B \mid x \not\in B \}$$

is a (well-defined) embedding with dense image.[^o26] If $X$ is completely regular, then $B$ can be chosen so that this gives the [Stone-Čech compactification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stone-Čech_compactification) after modifying map to have the universal property.[^o27]

- A topological space $X$ is [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) if and only if the natural inclusion $\operatorname{Max}(\tau_X) \hookrightarrow \operatorname{Spec}(\tau_X)$ splits, where $\tau_X$ is the topology of $X$.[^o28]

Here is a relation to ring-theoretic spectra. Given a commutative ring $A$, one can associate to it the distributive lattice $L(A)$ so that $L(A)$ and $A$ have the same prime and maximal spectra.[^o29] [Hochster's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hochster's_theorem) says that, conversely, given a distributive lattice $L$, there is a commutative ring $A$ such that $L \simeq L(A)$, in a non-functorial way.[^o30] Thus, the above results can be (and often are) stated in ring-theoretic terms.
