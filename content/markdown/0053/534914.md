# Spectral graph theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spectral graph theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **spectral [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401)** is the study of the properties of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) in relationship to the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268), [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue), and [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of matrices associated with the graph, such as its [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) or [Laplacian matrix](wikipedia://en/1448472).

:::

The adjacency matrix of a simple undirected graph is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) and is therefore [orthogonally diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/25150322); its eigenvalues are real [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981).

While the adjacency matrix depends on the vertex labeling, its [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206136) is a [graph invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_invariant), although not a [complete](wikipedia://en/9903220) one.

Spectral graph theory is also concerned with graph parameters that are defined via multiplicities of eigenvalues of matrices associated to the graph, such as the [Colin de Verdière number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_de_Verdière_graph_invariant).

## Cospectral graphs {#Cospectral_graphs}

Two graphs are called **cospectral** or **isospectral** if the adjacency matrices of the graphs are [isospectral](wikipedia://en/386031), that is, if the adjacency matrices have the same eigenvalues with multiplicity.

*Image: Isospectral enneahedra.svg.* Two cospectral [enneahedra](wikipedia://en/33329655), the smallest possible cospectral [polyhedral graphs](wikipedia://en/24656270)

Cospectral graphs need not be [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577), but isomorphic graphs are always cospectral.

### Graphs determined by their spectrum {#Graphs_determined_by_their_spectrum}

A graph $G$ is said to be determined by its spectrum if any other graph with the same spectrum as $G$ is isomorphic to $G$.

Some first examples of families of graphs that are determined by their spectrum include:

- The [complete graphs](wikipedia://en/85816).
- The finite [starlike trees](wikipedia://en/53828785).

### Cospectral mates {#Cospectral_mates}

Two graphs are said to be cospectral mates if they are cospectral but are non-isomorphic.

The smallest pair of cospectral mates is {*K*<sub>1,4</sub>, *C*<sub>4</sub> ∪ *K*<sub>1</sub>}, comprising the 5-vertex [star](wikipedia://en/19310129) and the [graph union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_union) of the 4-vertex [cycle](wikipedia://en/168609) and the single-vertex graph. The first example of cospectral graphs was reported by Collatz and Sinogowitz[^o6] in 1957.

The smallest pair of [polyhedral](wikipedia://en/24656270) cospectral mates are [enneahedra](wikipedia://en/33329655) with eight vertices each.[^o7]

### Finding cospectral graphs {#Finding_cospectral_graphs}

[Almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) [trees](wikipedia://en/48560) are cospectral, i.e., as the number of vertices grows, the fraction of trees for which there exists a cospectral tree goes to 1.[^n1]

A pair of [regular graphs](wikipedia://en/85821) are cospectral if and only if their complements are cospectral.[^o10]

A pair of [distance-regular graphs](wikipedia://en/7768943) are cospectral if and only if they have the same intersection array.

Cospectral graphs can also be constructed by means of the [Sunada method](wikipedia://en/386031).[^o11]

Another important source of cospectral graphs are the point-collinearity graphs and the line-intersection graphs of [point-line geometries](wikipedia://en/1576323). These graphs are always cospectral but are often non-isomorphic.[^o12]

## Cheeger inequality {#Cheeger_inequality}

The famous [Cheeger's inequality](wikipedia://en/16531739#Cheeger.27s_inequality) from [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243) has a discrete analogue involving the Laplacian matrix; this is perhaps the most important theorem in spectral graph theory and one of the most useful facts in algorithmic applications. It approximates the sparsest cut of a graph through the second eigenvalue of its Laplacian.

### Cheeger constant {#Cheeger_constant}

The **Cheeger constant** (also **Cheeger number** or **isoperimetric number**) of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is a numerical measure of whether or not a graph has a "bottleneck". The Cheeger constant as a measure of "bottleneckedness" is of great interest in many areas: for example, constructing well-connected [networks of computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_networking), [card shuffling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shuffling), and [low-dimensional topology](wikipedia://en/621732) (in particular, the study of [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/241291) 3-[manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470)).

More formally, the Cheeger constant *h*(*G*) of a graph *G* on *n* vertices is defined as

$$h(G) = \min_{0 < |S| \le \frac{n}{2} } \frac{|\partial(S)|}{|S|},$$

where the minimum is over all nonempty sets *S* of at most *n*/2 vertices and ∂(*S*) is the *edge boundary* of *S*, i.e., the set of edges with exactly one endpoint in *S*.[^o16]

::: {#o18 .statement cue=named-heading name="Cheeger inequality"}

### Cheeger inequality {#Cheeger_inequality}

When the graph *G* is *d*-regular, there is a relationship between *h*(*G*) and the spectral gap *d* − λ<sub>2</sub> of *G*. An inequality due to Dodziuk[^o19] and independently [Alon](wikipedia://en/4939651) and [Milman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitali_Milman)[^n2] states that[^o21]

$$\frac{1}{2}(d - \lambda_2) \le h(G) \le \sqrt{2d(d - \lambda_2)}.$$

This inequality is closely related to the [Cheeger bound](wikipedia://en/4009827) for [Markov chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_chains) and can be seen as a discrete version of [Cheeger's inequality](wikipedia://en/16531739#Cheeger.27s_inequality) in [Riemannian geometry](wikipedia://en/195243).

For general connected graphs that are not necessarily regular, an alternative inequality is given by Chung[^o22]

$$\frac{1}{2} {\lambda} \le {\mathbf h}(G) \le \sqrt{2 \lambda},$$

where $\lambda$ is the least nontrivial eigenvalue of the normalized Laplacian, and ${\mathbf h}(G)$ is the (normalized) Cheeger constant

$${\mathbf h}(G) = \min_{\emptyset \not =S\subset V(G)}\frac{|\partial(S)|}{\min({\mathrm{vol}}(S), {\mathrm{vol}}(\bar{S}))}$$

where ${\mathrm{vol}}(Y)$ is the sum of degrees of vertices in $Y$.

:::

## Hoffman–Delsarte inequality {#Hoffman–Delsarte_inequality}

There is an eigenvalue bound for [independent sets](wikipedia://en/524501) in [regular graphs](wikipedia://en/85821), originally due to [Alan J. Hoffman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_J._Hoffman) and Philippe Delsarte.[^o25]

Suppose that $G$ is a $k$-regular graph on $n$ vertices with least eigenvalue $\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}$. Then:

$$\alpha(G) \leq \frac{n}{1 - \frac{k}{\lambda_{\mathrm{min}}}}$$

where $\alpha(G)$ denotes its [independence number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_number).

This bound has been applied to establish e.g. algebraic proofs of the [Erdős–Ko–Rado theorem](wikipedia://en/296188) and its analogue for intersecting families of subspaces over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615).[^o26]

For general graphs which are not necessarily regular, a similar upper bound for the independence number can be derived by using the maximum eigenvalue $\lambda'_{max}$ of the normalized Laplacian[^o22] of $G$:

$$\alpha(G) \leq n (1-\frac {1}{\lambda'_{\mathrm{max}}}) \frac {\mathrm{max deg}}{\mathrm{min deg}}$$

where ${\mathrm{max deg}}$ and ${\mathrm{min deg}}$ denote the maximum and minimum degree in $G$, respectively. This a consequence of a more general inequality (pp. 109 in [^o22]):

$${\mathrm{vol}}(X) \leq (1-\frac {1}{\lambda'_{\mathrm{max}}}) {\mathrm{vol}}(V(G))$$

where $X$ is an independent set of vertices and ${\mathrm{vol}}(Y)$ denotes the sum of degrees of vertices in $Y$ .

:::

## Historical outline {#Historical_outline}

Spectral graph theory emerged in the 1950s and 1960s. Besides [graph theoretic](wikipedia://en/12401) research on the relationship between structural and spectral properties of graphs, another major source was research in [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry), but the connections between these two lines of work were not discovered until much later.[^o28] The 1980 monograph *Spectra of Graphs*[^o29] by Cvetković, Doob, and Sachs summarised nearly all research to date in the area. In 1988 it was updated by the survey *Recent Results in the Theory of Graph Spectra*.[^o30] The 3rd edition of *Spectra of Graphs* (1995) contains a summary of the further recent contributions to the subject.[^o28]

The field of discrete geometric analysis, created and developed by [Toshikazu Sunada](wikipedia://en/22019166) in the 2000s, deals with spectral graph theory in terms of discrete Laplacians associated with weighted graphs.[^o31] It finds application in various other fields, including [shape analysis](wikipedia://en/34878672).

A more recent development in spectral graph theory is vertex-frequency analysis, a set of techniques for solving problems in many real-life applications such as [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324).[^o32][^o33][^o34][^o35]
