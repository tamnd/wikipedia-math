# Category of metric spaces

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Category of metric spaces"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), **Met** is a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) that has [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) as its [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) and [metric maps](wikipedia://en/469760) ([continuous](wikipedia://en/6122#Continuous_functions_between_metric_spaces) [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) between metric spaces that do not increase any pairwise distance) as its [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891). This is a category because the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of two metric maps is again a metric map. It was first considered by (Isbell 1964).

:::

## Arrows {#Arrows}

The [monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59538) in **Met** are the [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) metric maps. The [epimorphisms](wikipedia://en/59539) are the metric maps for which the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of the map has a [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) [image](wikipedia://en/579311) in the [range](wikipedia://en/275991). The [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) are the [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777), i.e. metric maps which are injective, [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), and distance-preserving.

As an example, the inclusion of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) into the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) is a monomorphism and an epimorphism, but it is clearly not an isomorphism; this example shows that **Met** is not a [balanced category](wikipedia://en/77326358).

## Objects {#Objects}

The [empty](wikipedia://en/9566) metric space is the [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object) of **Met**; any [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) metric space is a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object). Because the initial object and the terminal objects differ, there are no [zero objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object) in **Met**.

The [injective objects](wikipedia://en/473779) in **Met** are called [injective metric spaces](wikipedia://en/7840768). Injective metric spaces were introduced and studied first by (Aronszajn Panitchpakdi 1956), prior to the study of **Met** as a category; they may also be defined intrinsically in terms of a [Helly property](wikipedia://en/670453) of their metric balls, and because of this alternative definition Aronszajn and Panitchpakdi named these spaces *hyperconvex spaces*. Any metric space has a smallest injective metric space into which it can be isometrically [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119), called its metric envelope or [tight span](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tight_span).

## Products and functors {#Products_and_functors}

The [product](wikipedia://en/237868) of a finite [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of metric spaces in **Met** is a metric space that has the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of the spaces as its points; the distance in the product space is given by the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of the distances in the base spaces. That is, it is the [product metric](wikipedia://en/6812823) with the [sup norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sup_norm). However, the product of an infinite set of metric spaces may not exist, because the distances in the base spaces may not have a supremum. That is, **Met** is not a [complete category](wikipedia://en/62781), but it is finitely complete. There is no [coproduct](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coproduct_%28category_theory%29) in **Met**.

The [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783) **Met** → **[Set](wikipedia://en/26829)** assigns to each metric space the underlying [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of its points, and assigns to each metric map the underlying set-theoretic function. This functor is [faithful](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faithful_functor), and therefore **Met** is a [concrete category](wikipedia://en/167001).

## Related categories {#Related_categories}

**Met** is not the only category whose objects are metric spaces; others include the category of [uniformly continuous functions](wikipedia://en/32337), the category of [Lipschitz functions](wikipedia://en/51731) and the category of [quasi-Lipschitz mappings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-Lipschitz_mapping). The metric maps are both uniformly continuous and Lipschitz, with Lipschitz constant at most one.
