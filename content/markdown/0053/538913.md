# Indefinite and fictitious numbers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Indefinite and fictitious numbers"}

**Indefinite and fictitious numbers** are words, phrases and quantities used to describe an indefinite size, used for comic effect, for exaggeration, as [placeholder names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placeholder_name), or when precision is unnecessary or undesirable. Other descriptions of this concept include: "non-numerical vague quantifier"[^o2] and "indefinite hyperbolic numerals".[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Umpteen"}

## Umpteen {#Umpteen}

**Umpteen**, **umteen** or **umpty**[^o12] is an unspecified but large [number](wikipedia://en/21690), used in a humorous fashion or to imply that it is not worth the effort to pin down the actual figure. Despite the *\-teen* ending, which would seem to indicate that it lies between 12 and 20, umpteen can be much larger.

The oldest reference to "umpty" — in a June 17, 1848 issue of the *Louisville [Morning Courier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morning_Courier)* — indicates that at that time it was slang for empty.[^o6] This is corroborated by a humorous short story in the North Carolina *[Hillsborough Recorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hillsborough_Recorder)* of June 30, 1852.[^o7]

By 1905, "umpty", in the expression "umpty-seven", had come to imply a multiple of ten.[^o8][^o9] *Umpty* came from a verbalization of a dash in [Morse code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_code).[^o8]

"Umpteen", adding the ending *\-teen*, as in "thirteen", is first attested in 1884,[^o10][^o11][^o12][^o13] and has become by far the most common form.[^o14]

In Norwegian, *ørten* is used in a similar way, playing on the numbers from *tretten* (13) to *nitten* (19), but often signifying a much larger number.[^o15]

Similarly, though with a larger base, Portuguese has *milhentos*, which is derived from the words *mil(har)* (1000) and the suffix *\-entos*, present in words like *trezentos* (300) or *quinhentos* (500), roughly meaning "hundred".[^o16]

Spanish uses tropecientos/tropecientas[^o17] in Spain and chorrocientos/chorrocientas[^o18] in El Salvador and Mexico referring to a high number in a colloquial way. Cientos means "hundreds".

:::

## Zillion {#Zillion}

Words with the [suffix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suffix) *\-illion* (e.g., **zillion**,[^o20] **gazillion**,[^o21] **bazillion**,[^o22] **jillion**,[^o23] **bajillion**,[^o24] **squillion**,[^o25] and others) are often used as informal names for unspecified large numbers by analogy to [names of large numbers](wikipedia://en/682403) such as *[million](wikipedia://en/1045999)* (10<sup>6</sup>), *[billion](wikipedia://en/1136363)* (10<sup>9</sup>) and *[trillion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trillion)* (10<sup>12</sup>). In [Estonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Estonian_language), the compound word *mustmiljon* ("black million") is used to mean an unfathomably large number. In Hungarian, *csilliárd* is used in the same "indefinitely large number" sense as "zillion" in English, and is thought to be a humorous portmanteau of the words *csillag* ("star", referring to the vast number of stars) and *milliárd* ("billion", cf. [long scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_and_short_scales#Long_scale)).

These words are intended to denote a number that is large enough to be unfathomable and are typically used as [hyperbole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbole) or for comic effect. They have no precise value or order. They form [ordinals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_numeral) and [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) with the usual suffix *\-th*, e.g. "I asked her for the jillionth time", or are used with the suffix "-aire" to describe a wealthy person.

::: {#o27 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Sagan (number)"}

## Sagan {#Sagan}

A sagan or sagan unit is a facetious name for a very large number inspired by [Carl Sagan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sagan)'s association with the phrase "[billions and billions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Sagan#"Billions_and_billions")".[^o28]

:::

## Specific values used as indefinite {#Specific_values_used_as_indefinite}

In context, a specific numeric value may be used to mean an unspecific quantity. Following are examples.

Some words that have a precise numerical definition can be used indefinitely. These include *couple* (2),[^o30] *[dozen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dozen)* (12), *[score](wikipedia://en/208259)* (20), and *[myriad](wikipedia://en/20088)* (10,000).

When a quantity word is prefixed with an [indefinite article](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_article) then it is sometimes intended or interpreted to be indefinite. For example, "one million" is clearly definite, but "a million" could be used to mean either a definite (she has a million followers now) or an indefinite value (she signed what felt like a million papers).

The title *[The Book of One Thousand and One Nights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_Thousand_and_One_Nights)* implies a large number of nights.[^o35] Many book titles use this convention as well, such as *1,001 Uses for ...*.

In Basque, bost, "5", also means "a lot".[^o31] Similarly, hamaika, "11", also means "a lot".[^o32]

In [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_language), , 108,000 [li](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Li_%28unit%29), means a great distance.

In [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language), *hundrede og sytten* ("[a hundred and seventeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/117_%28number%29)") can mean any arbitrary number.[^o33]

In [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), 36 and 36,000 are occasionally used as a synonym for "very many".

In Hebrew and other Middle Eastern traditions, the number [40](wikipedia://en/341671#In_religion) is used to express a large but unspecific number,[^o34][^o35] as in the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible)'s "forty days and forty nights", *[Ali Baba and the Forty Thieves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ali_Baba_and_the_Forty_Thieves)*, and the [Forty Martyrs of Sebaste](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forty_Martyrs_of_Sebaste).[^o36][^o37] This usage is sometimes found in English as well (for example, "forty winks").[^o38][^o39]

In [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_language) there are several expressions meaning "very many". A traditional expression is *mint égen a csillag* ("as many as the stars" in the sky). Sometimes specific numbers (e.g., 36,000 or 60,000) are used like in Danish or in French. *Kismillió* ("little million") is somewhat old, but a few decades ago it was still in use. From the end of the 20th century *csillió* began to spread. *Csillió* is a new word: it may be the result of combining the words *csillag* (star),[^o40] and *millió* (million).[^o41] Its enhanced version is *csilliárd* combining *csillag* and *milliárd* (billion).[^o42]

In [Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_language), 100,000 (*céad míle*) is used, as in the phrase *céad míle fáilte*, "a hundred thousand welcomes" or [Gabriel Rosenstock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gabriel_Rosenstock)'s poetic phrase mo chéad míle grá ("my hundred thousand loves").[^o43]

In [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_language), 八千, [8000](wikipedia://en/565218), is used: 八千草 (*lit.* 8,000 herbs) means a variety of herbs and 八千代 (*lit.* 8,000 generations) means eternity.

In [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_language), sescenti ([600](wikipedia://en/465007)) was used to mean a very large number, perhaps from the size of a Roman [cohort](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohort_%28military_unit%29).[^o44] The modern word *million* derives from an Italian [augmentative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augmentative) of the Latin word for thousand, mille.[^o45]

In [Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language), *tysiąc pięćset sto dziewięćset* ("one thousand five hundred one hundred nine hundred") is used, to refer to an indefinitely large number.[^o46]

In [Scottish Gaelic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_Gaelic), 100,000 (*ceud mìle*) is used to mean a great number, as in the phrase *ceud mìle fàilte*, "a hundred thousand welcomes."[^o47]

In [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language), femtioelva or sjuttioelva is used ( "fifty-eleven" and "seventy-eleven", although never actually intended to refer to the numbers 61 and 81).

In [Thai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thai_language), ร้อยแปด (*roi paed)* means both 108 and miscellaneous, various, plentiful.[^o48]

In [Welsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Welsh_language), *cant a mil*, literally "a hundred and thousand", is used to mean a large number in a similar way to English "a hundred and one".[^o49] It is used in phrases such as *cant a mil o bethau i'w wneud* "a hundred and one things to do" i.e. "many, many things to do".

The number [10,000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/10,000_%28number%29) is used to express an even larger approximate number, as in Hebrew רבבה *r<sup>e</sup>vâvâh*,[^o50] rendered into Greek as μυριάδες, and to English *[myriad](wikipedia://en/20088)*.[^o51] Similar usage is found in the [East Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/East_Asian_cultural_sphere) 萬 or 万 (*lit.* 10,000; ), and the [South Asian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Asian_languages) [lakh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakh) (*lit.* 100,000).[^o52]
