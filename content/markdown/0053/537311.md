# Degree of truth

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Degree of truth"}

In classical [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), propositions are typically unambiguously considered as being true or false. For instance, the proposition *one is both equal and not equal to itself* is regarded as simply false, being contrary to the [Law of Noncontradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Noncontradiction); while the proposition *one is equal to one* is regarded as simply true, by the [Law of Identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_Identity). However, some mathematicians, computer scientists, and philosophers have been attracted to the idea that a proposition might be *more or less* true, rather than wholly true or wholly false. Consider *this pizza is hot*.

:::

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), this idea can be developed in terms of [fuzzy logic](wikipedia://en/49180). In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), it has found application in [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence). In [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy), the idea has proved particularly appealing in the case of [vagueness](wikipedia://en/32369). Degrees of truth is an important concept in law.

The term is an older concept than [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134). Instead of determining the objective probability, only a subjective assessment is defined.[^o2]

In [adjudicative processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjudication), 'substantive truth' is distinct from 'formal legal truth' which comes in four degrees: [hearsay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearsay), [balance of probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burden_of_proof_%28law%29#Preponderance_of_the_evidence), proven beyond [reasonable doubt](wikipedia://en/1548556) and [absolute truth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universality_%28philosophy%29) ([knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge) reserved unto [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God)).[^o3]
