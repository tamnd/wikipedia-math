# Hinge loss

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hinge loss"}

*Image: Hinge loss vs zero one loss.svg.* The vertical axis represents the value of the hinge loss (in blue) and zero-one loss (in green) for fixed $t = 1$, while the horizontal axis represents the value of the prediction $y$. The plot shows that the hinge loss penalizes predictions $y < 1$, corresponding to the notion of a margin in a support vector machine.

In [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), **hinge loss** is a [loss function](wikipedia://en/442137) used for training [classifiers](wikipedia://en/1579244). Hinge loss is used for "maximum-margin" classification, most notably for [support vector machines](wikipedia://en/65309) (SVMs).[^o2]

:::

For an intended output $t = \pm1$ and a classifier score $y$, the hinge loss of the prediction $y$ is defined as:

$$\ell(y) = \max(0, 1-t \cdot y)$$

Note that $y$ should be the "raw" output of the classifier's decision function, not the predicted class label. For instance, in linear SVMs, $y = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x} + b$, where $(\mathbf{w},b)$ are the parameters of the [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) and $\mathbf{x}$ is the input variable(s).

When $t$ and $y$ have the same sign (meaning $y$ predicts the correct class) and $|y| \ge 1$, the hinge loss $\ell(y) = 0$. When they have opposite signs, $\ell(y)$ increases linearly with $y$, and similarly if $|y| < 1$, even if it has the same sign (correct prediction, but not by enough margin).

Hinge loss is not a [proper scoring rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_scoring_rule).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Extensions"}

## Extensions {#Extensions}

While binary SVMs are commonly extended to [multiclass classification](wikipedia://en/26338110) in a one-vs-all or one-vs-one fashion,[^o5] it is also possible to extend hinge loss itself for such an end. Several different variations of multiclass hinge loss have been proposed.[^o6] For example, Crammer and Singer[^o7] defined it for a linear classifier as[^o8]

$$\ell(y) = \max(0, 1 + \max_{y \ne t} \mathbf{w}_y \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{w}_t \mathbf{x}),$$

where $t$ is the target label, $\mathbf{w}_t$ and $\mathbf{w}_y$ are the model parameters.

Weston and Watkins provided a similar definition, but with a sum rather than a max:[^o9][^o6]

$$\ell(y) = \sum_{y \ne t} \max(0, 1 + \mathbf{w}_y \mathbf{x} - \mathbf{w}_t \mathbf{x}).$$

In [structured prediction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structured_prediction), hinge loss can be further extended to structured output spaces. [Structured SVMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structured_support_vector_machine) with margin rescaling use the following variant, where $\mathbf{w}$ denotes the SVM's parameters, $\mathbf{y}$ the SVM's predictions, $\varphi$ the joint feature function, and $\Delta$ the [Hamming loss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamming_loss):

$$\begin{aligned} \ell(\mathbf{y}) & = \max(0, \Delta(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{t}) + \langle \mathbf{w}, \phi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \rangle - \langle \mathbf{w}, \phi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t}) \rangle) \\ & = \max(0, \max_{y \in \mathcal{Y}} \left( \Delta(\mathbf{y}, \mathbf{t}) + \langle \mathbf{w}, \phi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}) \rangle \right) - \langle \mathbf{w}, \phi(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{t}) \rangle) \end{aligned}.$$

:::

## Optimization {#Optimization}

Hinge loss is a [convex function](wikipedia://en/245568), so many of the usual convex optimizers used in machine learning can work with it. It is not [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206), but has a [subgradient](wikipedia://en/3462862#The_subgradient) with respect to model parameters $\mathbf{w}$ of a linear SVM with score function $y = \mathbf{w} \cdot \mathbf{x}$ that is given by

$$\frac{\partial\ell}{\partial w_i} = \begin{cases} -t \cdot x_i & \text{if } t \cdot y < 1, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$

*Image: Hinge loss variants.svg.* Plot of three variants of hinge loss as a function of $z = ty$: the "ordinary" variant (blue), its square (green), and the piece-wise smooth version by Rennie and Srebro (red). The y-axis is the $l(y)$ hinge loss, and the x-axis is the parameter $t$

However, since the derivative of the hinge loss at $ty = 1$ is undefined, [smoothed](wikipedia://en/1408000) versions may be preferred for optimization, such as Rennie and Srebro's[^o11]

$$\ell(y) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} - ty & \text{if} ~~ ty \le 0, \\ \frac{1}{2} (1 - ty)^2 & \text{if} ~~ 0 < ty < 1, \\ 0 & \text{if} ~~ 1 \le ty \end{cases}$$

or the quadratically smoothed

$$\ell_\gamma(y) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2\gamma} \max(0, 1 - ty)^2 & \text{if} ~~ ty \ge 1 - \gamma, \\ 1 - \frac{\gamma}{2} - ty & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

suggested by Zhang.[^o12] The [modified Huber loss](wikipedia://en/26357083#Variant_for_classification) $L$ is a special case of this loss function with $\gamma = 2$, specifically $L(t,y) = 4 \ell_2(y)$.
