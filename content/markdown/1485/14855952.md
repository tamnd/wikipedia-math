# Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Johnson–Lindenstrauss lemma** is a result named after [William B. Johnson](wikipedia://en/38341443) and [Joram Lindenstrauss](wikipedia://en/20653945) concerning low-distortion [embeddings](wikipedia://en/52119) of points from high-dimensional into low-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). The [lemma](wikipedia://en/18634) states that a set of points in a high-dimensional space can be embedded into a space of much lower dimension in such a way that distances between the points are [nearly preserved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stretch_factor). In the classical proof of the lemma, the embedding is a [random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) [orthogonal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection).

:::

The lemma has applications in [compressed sensing](wikipedia://en/11403316), [manifold learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_learning), [dimensionality reduction](wikipedia://en/579867), [graph embedding](wikipedia://en/8149170), and [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing). Much of the data stored and manipulated on computers, including text and images, can be represented as points in a high-dimensional space (see [vector space model](wikipedia://en/20948989) for the case of text). However, the essential algorithms for working with such data tend to become bogged down very quickly as dimension increases. It is therefore desirable to reduce the dimensionality of the data in a way that preserves its relevant structure.

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Given $0 < \varepsilon < 1$, a set $X$ of $N$ points in $\mathbb{R}^n$ , and an integer $k > 8(\ln N)/\varepsilon^2$,[^o5] there is a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $f: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^k$ such that

$$(1-\varepsilon)\|u-v\|^2 \leq \|f(u) - f(v)\|^2 \leq (1+\varepsilon)\|u-v\|^2$$

for all $u,v \in X$.

The formula can be rearranged:

$$(1+\varepsilon)^{-1}\|f(u)-f(v)\|^2 \leq \|u-v\|^2 \leq (1-\varepsilon)^{-1}\|f(u)-f(v)\|^2$$

Alternatively, for any $\varepsilon\in(0,1)$ and any integer $k\ge15(\ln N)/\varepsilon^2$[^o6] there exists a linear function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \rightarrow \mathbb{R}^k$ such that the restriction $f|_X$ is $(1+\varepsilon)$-[bi-Lipschitz](wikipedia://en/51731#Definition).[^o7]

Also, the lemma is tight up to a constant factor, i.e. there exists a set of points of size *N* that needs dimension

$$\Omega \left(\frac{\log(N)}{\varepsilon^2}\right)$$

in order to preserve the distances between all pairs of points within a factor of $(1 \pm \varepsilon)$.[^o8]

The classical proof of the lemma takes $f$ to be a scalar multiple of an orthogonal projection $P$ onto a random subspace of dimension $k$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$. An orthogonal projection collapses some dimensions of the space it is applied to, which reduces the length of all vectors, as well as distance between vectors in the space. Under the conditions of the lemma, [concentration of measure](wikipedia://en/788497) ensures there is a nonzero chance that a random orthogonal projection reduces pairwise distances between all points in $X$ by roughly a constant factor $c$. Since the chance is nonzero, such projections must exist, so we can choose one $P$ and set $f(v) = Pv/c$.

To obtain the projection algorithmically, it suffices [with high probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/With_high_probability) to repeatedly sample orthogonal projection matrices at random. If you keep rolling the dice, you will eventually obtain one in polynomial random time.

:::

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The proof below is based on the course notes of Afonso Bandeira that can be found at the following link.[^o11]

Construct a [random matrix](wikipedia://en/1648765) $A \sim \mathcal N(0,1)^{k \times n}$, obtained by sampling each entry from the standard [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). Define $P := \frac{A}{\sqrt k}$, then for any nonzero vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^n$, let the projected vector be $\hat x := A x$. Standard geometric arguments show that $r := \frac{\|\hat x\|^2}{\| x\|^2}$ is [chi-square distributed](wikipedia://en/113424), that is, $r \sim \chi^2(k)$. Thus, it satisfies a [concentration inequality for the chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424#Concentration):

$$\Pr(r \in (1\pm \epsilon ) k ) \geq 1 - 2e^{-\frac k2 \left(\frac 12 \epsilon^2 - \frac 13 \epsilon^3\right)}$$

By the union bound, the probability that this relation is true for all of $x_1, \dots, x_N$ is greater than $1 - 2N e^{-\frac k2 (\frac 12 \epsilon^2 - \frac 13 \epsilon^3)}$.

When $k \geq \frac{4\ln 2N}{\epsilon^2\left(1 - 2\epsilon / 3\right)}$, the probability is nonzero.

More generally, when $k \geq \frac{4(d+1)\ln 2N}{\epsilon^2\left(1 - \frac{2\epsilon}{3}\right)}$, the probability is greater than, or equal to $1 - \frac{1}{(2N)^d}$, allowing arbitrarily high probability of success per sample, and *a fortiori* polynomial random time.

:::

## Alternate statement {#Alternate_statement}

A related lemma is the distributional JL lemma. This lemma states that for any $0 < \varepsilon, \delta < 1/2$ and positive integer $d$, there exists a distribution over $\mathbb{R}^{k \times d}$ from which the matrix $A$ is drawn such that for $k = O(\varepsilon^{-2} \log(1/\delta))$ and for any unit-length vector $x \in \mathbb{R}^{d}$, the claim below holds.[^o13]

$$P(|\Vert Ax\Vert_2^2-1|>\varepsilon)<\delta$$

One can obtain the JL lemma from the distributional version by setting $x = (u-v)/\|u-v\|_2$ and $\delta < 1/n^2$ for some pair *u*,*v* both in *X*. Then the JL lemma follows by a union bound over all such pairs.

## Sparse JL transform {#Sparse_JL_transform}

### Database-friendly JL transform {#Database-friendly_JL_transform}

(Achlioptas, 2003)[^o16] proposed "database-friendly" JL transform, using matrices with only entries from (-1, 0, +1).

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Achlioptas, 2003, Theorem 1.1)"}

**Theorem (Achlioptas, 2003, Theorem 1.1).**

Let the random $k \times n$ projection matrix $R$ have entries drawn i.i.d., either from

$$R_{i j}= \begin{cases}+1 & \text { with probability } 1 / 2 \\ -1 & \text { with probability } 1 / 2\end{cases}$$

or from

$$R_{i j}= \begin{cases}+\sqrt{3} & \text { with probability } 1 / 6 \\ 0 & \text { with probability } 2/3 \\ -\sqrt{3} & \text { with probability } 1 / 6 \end{cases}$$

Given a vector $v$, we define the random projection $f(v)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{k}} R v$. Then for any vector $v\in \mathbb{R}^n$, we have

$$\begin{aligned} &-\ln Pr(\|f(v)\|_2^2 \geq (1+\epsilon) \|v\|_2^2) \geq \frac k2 \left(\frac{\epsilon^2}{2} -\frac{\epsilon^3}{3} \right) \quad &\forall \epsilon > 0 \\ &-\ln Pr(\|f(v)\|_2^2 \leq (1-\epsilon) \|v\|_2^2) \geq \frac k2 \left(\frac{\epsilon^2}{2} -\frac{\epsilon^3}{3} \right) \quad &\forall \epsilon \in (0, 1) \end{aligned}$$

:::

Fix some [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$. Define $Q_i := \sum_j R_{ij} v_j$. We have $\|f(v)\|_2^2 = \frac{1}{k} \sum_i Q_i^2$.

Now, since the $Q_1, \dots, Q_k$ are IID, we want to apply a Chernoff concentration bound for $\frac 1k \sum_i Q_i^2$ around 1. This requires upper-bounding the [cumulant generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulant_generating_function) (CGF).

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Moment bounds (Achlioptas, 2003, Section 6)"}

**Moment bounds (Achlioptas, 2003, Section 6).**

For any $k \in 1, 2, \dots$, the moment of $Q_i$ is upper-bound by the standard gaussian $Z \sim N(0, 1)$:

$$E[Q_i^{2k-1}] = 0 = E[Z^{2k-1}], \quad E[Q_i^{2k}] \leq E[Z^{2k}]$$

:::

::: {#o19 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof"}

::: {#o20 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof.**

$E[Q_i^{2k-1}] = 0$ is easy: just apply the fact that $E[R_{ij_1}\dots R_{ij_l}] = 0$ when $l$ is odd, since we can decompose it into a product of expectations, and one of those is the expectation of an odd power of Radamacher, which is zero.

Now, the trick is that we can rewrite $Z$ as $Z = \sum_i Z_iv_i$, where each $Z_1, \dots, Z_d$ is a standard gaussian. Then we need to compare:

$$E[Q_i^{2k}] = \sum_{j_1, j_2, \dots, j_{2k-1}, j_{2k}}E[R_{ij_1}R_{ij_2}\dots R_{ij_{2k-1}}R_{ij_{2k}}] v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}}$$

and

$$E[Z^{2k}] = \sum_{j_1, j_2, \dots, j_{2k-1}, j_{2k}}E[Z_{j_1}Z_{j_2}\dots Z_{j_{2k-1}}Z_{j_{2k}}] v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}}$$

In the top sum, a term

$$E[R_{ij_1}R_{ij_2}\dots R_{ij_{2k-1}}R_{ij_{2k}}] v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}}$$

decomposes into a product of expectations, times $v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}}$. The product of expectations is zero, unless the indices $j_1, j_2, \dots, j_{2k}$ are paired off. In that case, the term $v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}}$ is the square of something, and so

$$v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}} \geq 0$$

while $R_{ij_1}R_{ij_2}\dots R_{ij_{2k-1}}R_{ij_{2k}}$ is also the square of $\pm 1$, and so

$$E[R_{ij_1}R_{ij_2}\dots R_{ij_{2k-1}}R_{ij_{2k}}] = 1$$

In the bottom sum, we run a similar argument with each such term

$$E[Z_{j_1}Z_{j_2}\dots Z_{j_{2k-1}}Z_{j_{2k}}] v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}}$$

but in this case, since we have $E[Z^{2k}] = (2k-1)!! \geq 1$, we find that in each case,

$$E[R_{ij_1}R_{ij_2}\dots R_{ij_{2k-1}}R_{ij_{2k}}] v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}} \leq E[Z_{j_1}Z_{j_2}\dots Z_{j_{2k-1}}Z_{j_{2k}}] v_{j_1}v_{j_2}\dots v_{j_{2k-1}}v_{j_{2k}}$$

And so, summing all of them up, $E[Q_i^{2k}] \leq E[Z^{2k}]$.

The same argument works for the other case. Specifically, if $R_{ij}$ is distributed like that, then $E[R_{ij}^{2k}] =3^{k-1}\leq (2k-1)!!$, and the proof goes through exactly the same way.

:::

:::

Now that $Q_i$ is stochastically dominated by the standard gaussian, and $E[Q_i^2] = 1$, it remains to perform a [Chernoff bound](wikipedia://en/741759) for $Q_i^2$, which requires bounding the cumulant generating function on both ends.

::: {#o21 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof.**

For any $t \in (0, 1/2)$, we can compute the cumulant generating function

$$\begin{aligned} K_{Q_i^2}(t) &= \ln E[e^{Q_i^2t}] \\ &= \ln\sum_k \frac{t^k}{k!}E[Q_i^{2k}] \\ &\leq \ln\left( 1 + \sum_k \frac{t^k}{k!}(2k-1)!!\right) \\ &= -\frac 12 \ln(1-2t) \end{aligned}$$

Similarly, for any $t\in (0, k/2)$,

$$K_{\frac 1k \sum_i Q_i^2}(t) = \sum_i K_{ Q_i^2}(t/k) \leq -\frac k2 \ln(1-2t/k)$$

So by the standard Chernoff bound method, for any $t \in (0, k/2)$ and any $\epsilon > 0$,

$$-\ln Pr\left(\frac 1k \sum_i Q_i^2 \geq 1+\epsilon\right) \geq (1+\epsilon)t + \frac k2 \ln(1-2t/k)$$

The right side is maximized at $t = \frac{k\epsilon}{2(1+\epsilon)}$, at which point we have

$$-\ln Pr\left(\frac 1k \sum_i Q_i^2 \geq 1 + \epsilon \right) \geq \frac k2 (\epsilon - \ln(1+\epsilon)) \geq \frac k2 (\epsilon^2/2 - \epsilon^3/3)$$

That’s one half of the bound done. For the other half, begin with some $t > 0$, and expand the exponential to the second order:

$$\begin{aligned} K_{Q_i^2}(-t) &= \ln E[e^{-Q_i^2t}] \\ &\leq \ln E[1-Q_i^2t + Q_i^4t^2/2] \\ &\leq \ln (1 - t + 3t^2/2)\\ \end{aligned}$$

$$K_{\frac 1k \sum_i Q_i^2}(-t) \leq k\ln (1 - t/k + 3t^2/(2k^2))$$

So by the standard Chernoff bound method, for any $t > 0$ and any $\epsilon \in (0, 1)$,

$$-\ln Pr\left(\frac 1k \sum_i Q_i^2 \leq 1 - \epsilon \right) \geq -k[(1-\epsilon)(t/k) + \ln (1 - t/k + 3t^2/(2k^2))]$$

Plug in $t = \frac{k\epsilon}{2(1+\epsilon)}$, and simplify, we find the right side is

$$\geq k \left(\frac{(\epsilon-1) \epsilon}{2(\epsilon+1)}-\ln\left(\frac{7 \epsilon^2+12 \epsilon+8}{8(\epsilon+1)^2}\right) \right)$$

and expand to third Taylor power,

$$\geq k(\epsilon^2/4 - 7\epsilon^3/48) > \frac k2 (\epsilon^2/2 - \epsilon^3/3)$$

:::

### Sparser JL transform on well-spread vectors {#Sparser_JL_transform_on_well-spread_vectors}

(Matoušek, 2008)[^o23] proposed a variant of the above JL transform that is even more sparsified, though it only works on "well-spread" vectors.

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Matoušek 2008, Theorem 4.1)"}

**Theorem (Matoušek 2008, Theorem 4.1).**

Assume that $n \in \mathbb{N}, \epsilon \in (0, 1/2), \delta \in (0, 1), \alpha \in [n^{-1/2}, 1], q \in [C_0 \alpha^2 \ln(n/\epsilon \delta), 1], k \in [C_1 \epsilon^{-2}\ln\frac 4\delta, n]$, where $C_0, C_1$ are absolute constants, and *k* is an integer.

Let $R$ be a $k \times n$ matrix sampled IID with

$$R_{ij}= \begin{cases}+q^{-1 / 2} & \text { with probability } \frac{1}{2} q \\ -q^{-1 / 2} & \text { with probability } \frac{1}{2} q \\ 0 & \text { with probability } 1-q\end{cases}$$

Then, for any unit vector $v \in \mathbb{R}^n$ such that $\|v\|_\infty \leq \alpha$, we have

$$Pr(\|f(v)\|_2^2 \in [1\pm \epsilon] ) \geq 1-\delta$$

where $f(v) = \frac{1}{\sqrt k}Rv$.

:::

The above cases are generalized to the case for matrices with independent, mean-zero, unit variance, [subgaussian](wikipedia://en/47243817) entries in (Dirksen, 2016).[^o25]

## Speeding up the JL transform {#Speeding_up_the_JL_transform}

Given *A*, computing the matrix vector product takes $O(kd)$ time. There has been some work in deriving distributions for which the matrix vector product can be computed in less than $O(kd)$ time.

There are two major lines of work. The first, *Fast Johnson Lindenstrauss Transform* (FJLT),[^o27] was introduced by Ailon and [Chazelle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Chazelle) in 2006. This method allows the computation of the matrix vector product in just $d\log d + k^{2+\gamma}$ for any constant $\gamma>0$.

Another approach is to build a distribution supported over matrices that are sparse.[^o28] This method allows keeping only an $\varepsilon$ fraction of the entries in the matrix, which means the computation can be done in just $kd\varepsilon$ time. Furthermore, if the vector has only $b$ non-zero entries, the Sparse JL takes time $kb\varepsilon$, which may be much less than the $d\log d$ time used by Fast JL.

## Tensorized random projections {#Tensorized_random_projections}

It is possible to combine two JL matrices by taking the so-called [face-splitting product](wikipedia://en/64530953#Face-splitting_product), which is defined as the tensor products of the rows (was proposed by [V. Slyusar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vadym_Slyusar)[^o30] in 1996[^o31][^o32][^o33][^o34][^o35] for [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar) and [digital antenna array](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_antenna_array) applications). More directly, let ${C}\in\mathbb R^{3\times 3}$ and ${D}\in\mathbb R^{3\times 3}$ be two matrices. Then the [face-splitting product](wikipedia://en/64530953#Face-splitting_product) ${C}\bullet {D}$ is[^o31][^o32][^o33][^o34][^o35]

$${C} \bullet {D} = \left[ \begin{array} { c } {C}_1 \otimes {D}_1\\\hline {C}_2 \otimes {D}_2\\\hline {C}_3 \otimes {D}_3\\ \end{array} \right].$$

This idea of tensorization was used by Kasiviswanathan et al. for [differential privacy](wikipedia://en/23473696).[^o36]

JL matrices defined like this use fewer random bits, and can be applied quickly to vectors that have tensor structure, due to the following identity:[^o33]

$$(\mathbf{C} \bullet \mathbf{D})(x\otimes y) = \mathbf{C}x \circ \mathbf{D} y = \left[ \begin{array} { c } (\mathbf{C}x)_1 (\mathbf{D} y)_1 \\ (\mathbf{C}x)_2 (\mathbf{D} y)_2 \\ \vdots \end{array}\right],$$

where $\circ$ is the element-wise ([Hadamard](wikipedia://en/34260496)) product. Such computations have been used to efficiently compute [polynomial kernels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_kernel) and many other .[^o37]

In 2020[^o38] it was shown that if the matrices $C_1, C_2, \dots, C_c$ are independent $\pm1$ or Gaussian matrices, the combined matrix $C_1 \bullet \dots \bullet C_c$ satisfies the distributional JL lemma if the number of rows is at least

$$O(\epsilon^{-2}\log1/\delta + \epsilon^{-1}(\tfrac1c\log1/\delta)^c).$$

For large $\epsilon$ this is as good as the completely random Johnson-Lindenstrauss, but a matching lower bound in the same paper shows that this exponential dependency on $(\log1/\delta)^c$ is necessary. Alternative JL constructions are suggested to circumvent this.
