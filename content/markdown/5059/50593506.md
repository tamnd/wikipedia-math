# Gregory coefficients

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gregory coefficients"}

**Gregory coefficients** $G_{n}$, also known as **reciprocal logarithmic numbers**, **Bernoulli numbers of the second kind**, or **Cauchy numbers of the first kind**,[^o2][^o3][^o21][^o4][^o5][^o23][^o6][^o7][^o8][^o26][^o27][^o9][^o10] are the rational numbers that occur in the [Maclaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_series) expansion of the reciprocal logarithm

:::

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{z}{\ln(1+z)} & = 1+\frac12 z - \frac{1}{12}z^2 + \frac{1}{24}z^3 - \frac{19}{720}z^4 + \frac{3}{160}z^5 - \frac{863}{60480}z^6 + \cdots \\ & = 1 + \sum_{n=1}^\infty G_n z^n\,,\qquad |z|<1\,. \end{aligned}$$

Gregory coefficients are alternating $G_{n} = (-1)^{n-1}|G_{n}|$ for $n > 0$ and decreasing in [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991). These numbers are named after [James Gregory](wikipedia://en/220425) who introduced them in 1670 in the [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089) context. They were subsequently rediscovered by many mathematicians and often appear in works of modern authors, who do not always recognize them.[^o2][^o5][^o15][^o11][^o39][^o19]

## Computation and representations {#Computation_and_representations}

The simplest way to compute Gregory coefficients is to use the recurrence formula

$$|G_n| = -\sum_{k=1}^{n-1} \frac{|G_k|}{n+1-k} + \frac 1 {n+1}$$

with $G_{1} = \tfrac{1}{2}$.[^o15][^o20] Gregory coefficients may be also computed explicitly via the following differential

$$n! G_n=\left[\frac{\textrm d^n}{\textrm dz^n}\frac{z}{\ln(1+z)}\right]_{z=0},$$

or the integral

$$G_n=\frac 1 {n!} \int_0^1 x(x-1)(x-2)\cdots(x-n+1)\, dx = \int_0^1 \binom x n \, dx,$$

which can be proved by integrating $(1+z)^x$ between 0 and 1 with respect to $x$, once directly and the second time using the [binomial series](wikipedia://en/696619) expansion first.

It implies the finite summation formula

$$n! G_n= \sum_{\ell=0}^n \frac{s(n,\ell)}{\ell+1} ,$$

where $s(n,\ell)$ are the signed [Stirling numbers of the first kind](wikipedia://en/2229296).

and [Schröder's](wikipedia://en/448015) integral formula[^o16][^o17]

$$G_n=(-1)^{n-1} \int_0^\infty \frac{dx}{(1+x)^n(\ln^2 x + \pi^2)},$$

## Bounds and asymptotic behavior {#Bounds_and_asymptotic_behavior}

The Gregory coefficients satisfy the bounds

$$\frac{1}{6n(n-1)}<\big|G_n\big|<\frac{1}{6n},\qquad n>2,$$

given by [Johan Steffensen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johan_Steffensen).[^o11] These bounds were later improved by various authors. The best known bounds for them were given by Blagouchine.[^o19] In particular,

$$\frac{\,1\,}{\,n\ln^2\! n\,} \,-\, \frac{\,2\,}{\,n\ln^3\! n\,} \leqslant\,\big|G_n\big|\, \leqslant\, \frac{\,1\,}{\,n\ln^2\! n\,} - \frac{\,2\gamma \, }{\,n\ln^3\! n\,} \,, \qquad\quad n\geqslant5\,.$$

Asymptotically, at large index $n$, these numbers behave as[^o3][^o19][^o16]

$$\big|G_n\big|\sim \frac{1}{n\ln^2 n}, \qquad n\to\infty.$$

More accurate description of $G_{n}$ at large $n$ may be found in works of Van Veen,[^o20] Davis,[^o21] Coffey,[^o22] Nemes[^o23] and Blagouchine.[^o19]

## Series with Gregory coefficients {#Series_with_Gregory_coefficients}

Series involving Gregory coefficients may be often calculated in a closed-form. Basic series with these numbers include

$$\begin{aligned} &\sum_{n=1}^\infty\big|G_n\big|=1 \\[2mm] &\sum_{n=1}^\infty G_n=\frac{1}{\ln2} -1 \\[2mm] &\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n}=\gamma, \end{aligned}$$

where $\gamma = 0.5772156649...$ is [Euler's constant](wikipedia://en/148594). These results are very old, and their history may be traced back to the works of [Gregorio Fontana](wikipedia://en/6942423) and [Lorenzo Mascheroni](wikipedia://en/88116).[^o19][^o25] More complicated series with the Gregory coefficients were calculated by various authors. Kowalenko,[^o7] Alabdulmohsin [^o26][^o27] and some other authors calculated

$$\begin{array}{l} \displaystyle \sum_{n=2}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n-1}= -\frac{1}{2} + \frac{\ln2\pi}{2} -\frac{\gamma}{2} \\[6mm] \displaystyle \displaystyle\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}\!\frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n+1}= 1- \ln2. \end{array}$$

Alabdulmohsin[^o26][^o27] also gives these identities with

$$\begin{aligned} & \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n (\big|G_{3n+1}\big| + \big|G_{3n+2}\big|) = \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi} \\[2mm] & \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n (\big|G_{3n+2}\big| + \big|G_{3n+3}\big|) = \frac{2\sqrt{3}}{\pi} - 1 \\[2mm] & \sum_{n=0}^\infty (-1)^n (\big|G_{3n+3}\big| + \big|G_{3n+4}\big|) = \frac{1}{2}- \frac{\sqrt{3}}{\pi}. \end{aligned}$$

Candelperger, Coppo[^o28][^o29] and Young[^o6] showed that

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|\cdot H_n}{n}=\frac{\pi^2}{6}-1,$$

where $H_{n}$ are the [harmonic numbers](wikipedia://en/214729). Blagouchine[^o19] provides the following identities

$$\begin{aligned} & \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{G_n}{n} =\operatorname{li}(2)-\gamma \\[2mm] & \sum_{n=3}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n-2} = -\frac{1}{8} + \frac{\ln2\pi}{12} - \frac{\zeta'(2)}{\,2\pi^2}\\[2mm] & \sum_{n=4}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n-3} = -\frac{1}{16} + \frac{\ln2\pi}{24} - \frac{\zeta'(2)}{4\pi^2} + \frac{\zeta(3)}{8\pi^2}\\[2mm] & \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n+2} =\frac{1}{2}-2\ln2 +\ln3 \\[2mm] & \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n+3} =\frac{1}{3}-5\ln2+3\ln3 \\[2mm] & \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n+k} =\frac{1}{k}+\sum_{m=1}^k (-1)^m \binom{k}{m}\ln(m+1) \,, \qquad k=1, 2, 3,\ldots\\[2mm] & \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\big|G_n\big|}{n^2} =\int_0^1 \frac{-\operatorname{li}(1-x)+\gamma+\ln x} x \, dx \\[2mm] & \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{G_n}{n^2} =\int_0^1\frac{\operatorname{li}(1+x)-\gamma-\ln x}{x}\, dx, \end{aligned}$$

where $\operatorname{li}(z)$ is the [integral logarithm](wikipedia://en/47137) and $\tbinom{k}{m}$ is the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668). It is also known that the [zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809), the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316), the [polygamma functions](wikipedia://en/406932), the [Stieltjes constants](wikipedia://en/1479085) and many other [special functions](wikipedia://en/23709110) and constants may be expressed in terms of [infinite series](wikipedia://en/15287) containing these numbers.[^o2][^o19][^o20][^o33][^o34]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Various generalizations are possible for the Gregory coefficients. Many of them may be obtained by modifying the parent generating equation. For example, Van Veen[^o20] considered

$$\left(\frac{\ln(1+z)}{z}\right)^s= s\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{n!}K^{(s)}_n \,,\qquad |z|<1\,,$$

and hence

$$n!G_n=-K_n^{(-1)}$$

Equivalent generalizations were later proposed by Kowalenko[^o8] and Rubinstein.[^o37] In a similar manner, Gregory coefficients are related to the generalized [Bernoulli numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_numbers)

$$\left(\frac{t}{e^t-1}\right)^s= \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{t^k}{k!} B^{(s)}_k , \qquad |t|<2\pi\,,$$

see,[^o20][^o33] so that

$$n!G_n=-\frac{B_n^{(n-1)}}{n-1}$$

Jordan[^o2][^o39][^o38] defines polynomials $\psi_{n}(s)$ such that

$$\frac{z(1+z)^s}{\ln(1+z)}= \sum_{n=0}^\infty z^n \psi_n(s) \,,\qquad |z|<1\,,$$

and call them *[Bernoulli polynomials of the second kind](wikipedia://en/58580000)*. From the above, it is clear that $G_{n} = \psi_{n}(0)$. Carlitz[^o39] generalized Jordan's polynomials $\psi_{n}(s)$ by introducing polynomials $\beta$

$$\left(\frac{z}{\ln(1+z)}\right)^s \!\!\cdot (1+z)^x= \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{z^n}{n!}\,\beta^{(s)}_n(x) \,,\qquad |z|<1\,,$$

and therefore

$$n!G_n=\beta^{(1)}_n(0)$$

Blagouchine[^o19][^o40] introduced numbers $G_{n}(k)$ such that

$$n!G_n(k)=\sum_{\ell=1}^n \frac{s(n,\ell)}{\ell+k} ,$$

obtained their [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) and studied their asymptotics at large $n$. Clearly, $G_{n} = G_{n}(1)$. These numbers are strictly alternating $G_{n}(k) = (-1)^{n-1}|G_{n}(k)|$ and involved in various expansions for the [zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeta-function), [Euler's constant](wikipedia://en/148594) and [polygamma functions](wikipedia://en/406932). A different generalization of the same kind was also proposed by Komatsu[^o38]

$$c_n^{(k)}=\sum_{\ell=0}^n \frac{s(n,\ell)}{(\ell+1)^k},$$

so that $G_{n} = c_{n}^{(1)}/n!$ Numbers $c_{n}^{(k)}$ are called by the author *poly-Cauchy numbers*.[^o38] Coffey[^o22] defines polynomials

$$P_{n+1}(y)=\frac 1 {n!} \int_0^y x(1-x)(2-x)\cdots(n-1-x)\, dx$$

and therefore $|G_{n}| = P_{n+1}(1)$.
