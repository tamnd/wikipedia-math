# Liouville's theorem (complex analysis)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Liouville's theorem"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), **Liouville's theorem** states that every [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) [entire function](wikipedia://en/10372) must be [constant](wikipedia://en/320861). That is, every [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) $f$ for which there exists a positive number $M$ such that $|f(z)| \leq M$ for all $z\in\mathbb{C}$ is constant. Equivalently, non-constant holomorphic functions on $\mathbb{C}$ have unbounded images.

:::

The theorem is named after [Joseph Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810), although the theorem was first proven by [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy) in 1844. The theorem is considerably improved by [Picard's little theorem](wikipedia://en/674050), which says that every entire function whose image omits two or more [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) must be constant.

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

> **Liouville's theorem:** Every [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) $f:\mathbb C \to \mathbb C$ for which there exists a positive number $M$ such that $|f(z)| \leq M$ for all $z\in\mathbb{C}$ is [constant](wikipedia://en/320861).

More succinctly, Liouville's theorem states that every bounded entire function must be constant.

:::

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

This important theorem has several proofs.

A standard analytical proof uses the fact that [holomorphic functions are analytic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_that_holomorphic_functions_are_analytic).

::: {#o7 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof.**

If $f$ is an entire function, it can be represented by its [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) about 0:

$$f(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k z^k$$

where (by [Cauchy's integral formula](wikipedia://en/72827))

$$a_k = \frac{f^{(k)}(0)}{k!} = {1 \over 2 \pi i} \oint_{C_r} \frac{f( \zeta )}{\zeta^{k+1}}\,d\zeta$$

and $C_r$ is the circle about 0 of radius $r > 0$. Suppose $f$ is bounded: i.e. there exists a constant $M$ such that $|f(z)|\leq M$ for all $z$. We can estimate directly

$$| a_k | \le \frac{1}{2 \pi} \oint_{C_r} \frac{ | f ( \zeta ) | }{ | \zeta |^{k+1} } \, |d\zeta| \le \frac{1}{2 \pi} \oint_{C_r} \frac{ M }{ r^{k+1} } \, |d\zeta| = \frac{M}{2 \pi r^{k+1}} \oint_{C_r} |d\zeta| = \frac{M}{2 \pi r^{k+1}} 2 \pi r = \frac{M}{r^k},$$

where in the second inequality we have used the fact that $|z|=r$ on the circle $C_r$. (This estimate is known as [Cauchy's estimate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy's_estimate).) But the choice of $r$ in the above is an arbitrary positive number. Therefore, letting $r$ tend to infinity (we let $r$ tend to infinity since $f$ is analytic on the entire plane) gives $a_k=0$ for all $k\geq 1$. Thus $f(z)=a_0$ and this proves the theorem.

:::

Another proof uses the mean value property of harmonic functions.

::: {#o9 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof[^o8].**

Given two points, choose two balls with the given points as centers and of equal radius. If the radius is large enough, the two balls will coincide except for an arbitrarily small proportion of their volume. Since $f$ is bounded, the averages of it over the two balls are arbitrarily close, and so $f$ assumes the same value at any two points.

:::

The proof can be adapted to the case where the harmonic function $f$ is merely bounded above or below. See [Harmonic function#Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/55762#Liouville's_theorem).

Another approach to prove the theorem is

::: {#o11 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof"}

**Proof[^o10].**

Suppose $|f(z)| \leq M$ for all $z$ in the complex plane, we can apply the Cauchy estimate to a disk center at any $z_0$ of any radius $\rho$ to obtain: $|f'(z_0)| \leq \frac{M}{\rho}$.

Let $\rho$ tend to $+\infty$, we obtain $f'(z_0) = 0$. Since This is true for all $z_0$, $f(z)$ is a constant.

:::

:::

## Corollaries {#Corollaries}

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Fundamental theorem of algebra"}

### Fundamental theorem of algebra {#Fundamental_theorem_of_algebra}

There is a short [proof of the fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414#Complex-analytic_proofs) using Liouville's theorem.[^o15]

::: {#o16 .proof cue=math-proof name="Proof (Fundamental theorem of algebra)"}

**Proof ([Fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414)).**

[Suppose for the sake of contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction) that there is a nonconstant polynomial $p$ with no complex root. Note that $|p(z)| \to \infty$ as $z \to \infty$. Then for sufficiently large R we have $|p(z)| > 1$ for all $z \not\in B(0,R)$.

Because $p$ has no roots, the function $q(z) = 1/p(z)$ is [entire](wikipedia://en/10372), and [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) inside $B(0, R)$, and thus it is also [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) on its [closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_ball) $\overline B(0, R)$. By the [extreme value theorem](wikipedia://en/276881), a continuous function on a closed and bounded set obtains its extreme values, implying that $1/|p(z)| \le C$ for some constant $C$ and $z \in \overline B(0, R)$.

Thus, the function $q(z)$ is bounded in $\mathbb C$, and by Liouville's theorem, is [constant](wikipedia://en/320861), which contradicts our assumption that $p$ is nonconstant.

:::

:::

### No entire function dominates another entire function {#No_entire_function_dominates_another_entire_function}

A consequence of the theorem is that "genuinely different" entire functions cannot dominate each other, i.e. if $f$ and $g$ are entire, and $|f|\leq |g|$ everywhere, then $f=\alpha g$ for some complex number $\alpha$. Consider that for $g=0$ the theorem is trivial so we assume $g\neq 0$. Consider the function $h=f/g$. It is enough to prove that $h$ can be extended to an entire function, in which case the result follows by Liouville's theorem. The holomorphy of $h$ is clear except at points in $g^{-1}(0)$. But since $h$ is bounded and all the zeroes of $g$ are isolated, any singularities must be removable. Thus $h$ can be extended to an entire bounded function which by Liouville's theorem implies it is constant.

### If *f* is less than or equal to a scalar times its input, then it is linear {#If_f_is_less_than_or_equal_to_a_scalar_times_its_input,_then_it_is_linear}

Suppose that $f$ is entire and $|f(z)|\leq M|z|$, for $M>0$. We can apply Cauchy's integral formula; we have that

$$|f'(z)|=\frac{1}{2\pi}\left|\oint_{C_r}\frac{f(\zeta)}{(\zeta-z)^2}d\zeta\right|\leq \frac{1}{2\pi} \oint_{C_r} \frac{|f(\zeta)|}{\left|(\zeta-z)^2\right|} |d \zeta|\leq \frac{1}{2\pi} \oint_{C_r} \frac{M |\zeta|}{\left| (\zeta-z)^2\right|} \left|d\zeta\right|=\frac{MI}{2\pi}$$

where $I$ is the value of the remaining integral. This shows that $f'$ is bounded and entire, so it must be constant, by Liouville's theorem. Integrating then shows that $f$ is [affine](wikipedia://en/38449) and then, by referring back to the original inequality, we have that the constant term is zero.

### Non-constant elliptic functions cannot be defined on the complex plane {#Non-constant_elliptic_functions_cannot_be_defined_on_the_complex_plane}

The theorem can also be used to deduce that the domain of a non-constant [elliptic function](wikipedia://en/69939) $f$ cannot be $\mathbb{C}$. Suppose it was. Then, if $a$ and $b$ are two periods of $f$ such that $\tfrac{a}{b}$ is not real, consider the [parallelogram](wikipedia://en/133496) $P$ whose [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) are 0, $a$, $b$, and $a+b$. Then the image of $f$ is equal to $f(P)$. Since $f$ is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_functions) and $P$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), $f(P)$ is also compact and, therefore, it is bounded. So, $f$ is constant.

The fact that the domain of a non-constant [elliptic function](wikipedia://en/69939) $f$ cannot be $\mathbb{C}$ is what Liouville actually proved, in 1847, using the theory of elliptic functions.[^o20] In fact, it was [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustin_Louis_Cauchy) who proved Liouville's theorem.[^o21][^o22]

### Entire functions have dense images {#Entire_functions_have_dense_images}

If $f$ is a non-constant entire function, then its image is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $\mathbb{C}$. This might seem to be a much stronger result than Liouville's theorem, but it is actually an easy corollary. If the image of $f$ is not dense, then there is a complex number $w$ and a real number $r > 0$ such that the open disk centered at $w$ with radius $r$ has no element of the image of $f$. Define

$$g(z) = \frac{1}{f(z) - w}.$$

Then $g$ is a bounded entire function, since for all $z$,

$$|g(z)|=\frac{1}{|f(z)-w|} < \frac{1}{r}.$$

So, $g$ is constant, and therefore $f$ is constant.

## On compact Riemann surfaces {#On_compact_Riemann_surfaces}

Any holomorphic function on a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) is necessarily constant.[^o25]

Let $f(z)$ be holomorphic on a compact Riemann surface $M$. By compactness, there is a point $p_0 \in M$ where $|f(p)|$ attains its maximum. Then we can find a chart from a neighborhood of $p_0$ to the unit disk $\mathbb{D}$ such that $f(\varphi^{-1}(z))$ is holomorphic on the unit disk and has a maximum at $\varphi(p_0) \in \mathbb{D}$, so it is constant, by the [maximum modulus principle](wikipedia://en/880710).

## Remarks {#Remarks}

Let $\mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}$ be the one-point compactification of the complex plane $\mathbb{C}$. In place of holomorphic functions defined on regions in $\mathbb{C}$, one can consider regions in $\mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}$. Viewed this way, the only possible singularity for entire functions, defined on $\mathbb{C} \subset \mathbb{C} \cup \{\infty\}$, is the point $\infty$. If an entire function $f$ is bounded in a neighborhood of $\infty$, then $\infty$ is a [removable singularity](wikipedia://en/81644) of $f$, i.e. $f$ cannot blow up or behave erratically at $\infty$. In light of the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) expansion, it is not surprising that Liouville's theorem holds.

Similarly, if an entire function has a [pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of order $n$ at $\infty$ —that is, it grows in magnitude comparably to $z^n$ in some neighborhood of $\infty$ —then $f$ is a polynomial. This extended version of Liouville's theorem can be more precisely stated: if $|f(z)|\leq M|z|^n$ for $|z|$ sufficiently large, then $f$ is a polynomial of degree at most $n$. This can be proved as follows. Again take the Taylor series representation of $f$,

$$f(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty a_k z^k.$$

The argument used during the proof using [Cauchy estimates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_estimates) shows that for all $k\geq 0$,

$$|a_k| \leq Mr^{n-k}.$$

So, if $k > n$, then

$$|a_k| \leq \lim_{r\to\infty}Mr^{n-k} = 0.$$

Therefore, $a_k = 0$.

Liouville's theorem does not extend to the generalizations of complex numbers known as [double numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) and [dual numbers](wikipedia://en/42169).[^o27]
