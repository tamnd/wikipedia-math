# Law of total variance

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Law of total variance"}

**The law of total variance** is a fundamental result in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) that expresses the variance of a random variable $Y$ in terms of its conditional variances and conditional means given another random variable $X$. Informally, it states that the overall variability of $Y$ can be split into an “unexplained” component (the average of within-group variances) and an “explained” component (the variance of group means).

:::

Formally, if $X$ and $Y$ are [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) on the same [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325), and $Y$ has finite [variance](wikipedia://en/32344), then:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)\right] + \operatorname{Var}\!\left(\operatorname{E}[Y \mid X]\right).$$

This identity is also known as the **variance decomposition formula**, the **conditional variance formula**, the **law of iterated variances**, or colloquially as **Eve’s law**,[^o2] in parallel to the “Adam’s law” naming for the [law of total expectation](wikipedia://en/312397).

In [actuarial science](wikipedia://en/189373) (particularly in [credibility theory](wikipedia://en/4152503)), the two terms $\operatorname{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$ and $\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y \mid X])$ are called the **expected value of the process variance (EVPV)** and the **variance of the hypothetical means (VHM)** respectively.[^o3]

## Explanation {#Explanation}

Let $Y$ be a random variable and $X$ another random variable on the same probability space. The law of total variance can be understood by noting:

1. $\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)$ measures how much $Y$ varies around its conditional mean $\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X].$
1. Taking the expectation of this [conditional variance](wikipedia://en/11391180) across all values of $X$ gives $\operatorname{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y \mid X)]$, often termed the “unexplained” or within-group part.
1. The variance of the conditional mean, $\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X])$, measures how much these conditional means differ (i.e. the “explained” or between-group part).

Adding these components yields the total variance $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$, mirroring how [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634) partitions variation.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Example 1 (Exam scores) {#Example_1_(Exam_scores)}

Suppose five students take an exam scored 0–100. Let $Y$ = student’s score and $X$ indicate whether the student is \*international\* or \*domestic\*:

::: {#o8 .table cue=table}

| Student | $Y$ (Score) | $X$ |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | 20 | International |
| 2 | 30 | International |
| 3 | 100 | International |
| 4 | 40 | Domestic |
| 5 | 60 | Domestic |

:::

- Mean and variance for international: $\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X=\text{Intl}] = 50,\; \operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=\text{Intl}) \approx 1266.7.$
- Mean and variance for domestic: $\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X=\text{Dom}] = 50,\; \operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=\text{Dom}) = 100.$

Both groups share the same mean (50), so the explained variance $\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X])$ is 0, and the total variance equals the average of the within-group variances (weighted by group size), i.e. 800.

### Example 2 (Mixture of two Gaussians) {#Example_2_(Mixture_of_two_Gaussians)}

Let $X$ be a coin flip taking values $\operatorname{Heads}$ with probability $h$ and $\operatorname{Tails}$ with probability $1-h$. Given Heads, $Y \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_h,\sigma_h^2)$; given Tails, $Y \sim \mathrm{Normal}(\mu_t,\sigma_t^2)$. Then $\operatorname{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)] = h\,\sigma_h^2 + (1 - h)\,\sigma_t^2,$ $\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X]) = h\,(1 - h)\,(\mu_h - \mu_t)^2,$ so $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = h\,\sigma_h^2 + (1 - h)\,\sigma_t^2 \;+\; h\,(1 - h)\,(\mu_h-\mu_t)^2.$

### Example 3 (Dice and coins) {#Example_3_(Dice_and_coins)}

Consider a two-stage experiment:

1. Roll a fair die (values 1–6) to choose one of six biased coins.
1. Flip that chosen coin; let $Y$=1 if Heads, 0 if Tails.

Then $\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X=i] = p_i, \; \operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X=i)=p_i(1-p_i).$ The overall variance of $Y$ becomes

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{E}\bigl[p_X(1 - p_X)\bigr] + \operatorname{Var}\bigl(p_X\bigr),$$

with $p_X$ uniform on $\{p_1,\dots,p_6\}.$

:::

::: {#o12 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

### Discrete/finite proof {#Discrete/finite_proof}

Let $(X_i,Y_i)$, $i=1,\ldots,n$, be observed pairs. Define $\overline{Y} = \operatorname{E}[Y].$ Then

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(Y) &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left(Y_i - \overline{Y}\right)^2 \\[1ex] &= \frac{1}{n} \sum_{i=1}^n \left[\left(Y_i - \overline{Y}_{\!\!X_i}\right) + \left(\overline{Y}_{\!\!X_i} - \overline{Y}\right)\right]^2, \end{aligned}$$

where $\overline{Y}_{X_i}=\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X=X_i].$ Expanding the square and noting the cross term cancels in summation yields: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)\right] + \operatorname{Var}\left(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X]\right).$

### General case {#General_case}

Using $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{E}[Y^2] - \operatorname{E}[Y]^2$ and the [law of total expectation](wikipedia://en/312397):

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}[Y^2] &= \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{E}(Y^2 \mid X)\right] \\ &= \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X) + (\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X])^2 \right]. \end{aligned}$$

Subtract $\operatorname{E}[Y]^2 = \left(\operatorname{E}[\operatorname{E}(Y\mid X)]\right)^2$ and regroup to arrive at $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)\right] + \operatorname{Var}\left(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X]\right).$

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

### Analysis of Variance (ANOVA) {#Analysis_of_Variance_(ANOVA)}

In a one-way [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634), the [total sum of squares](wikipedia://en/2473449) (proportional to $\operatorname{Var}(Y)$) is split into a “between-group” sum of squares ($\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X])$) plus a “within-group” sum of squares ($\operatorname{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)]$). The [F-test](wikipedia://en/318976) examines whether the explained component is sufficiently large to indicate $X$ has a significant effect on $Y$.[^o18]

### Regression and R² {#Regression_and_R²}

In [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) and related models, if $\hat{Y}=\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X],$ the fraction of variance explained is

$$\begin{aligned} R^2 = \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\hat{Y})}{\operatorname{Var}(Y)} &= \frac{\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X])}{\operatorname{Var}(Y)} \\[1ex] &= 1 - \frac{\operatorname{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)]}{\operatorname{Var}(Y)}. \end{aligned}$$

In the simple linear case (one predictor), $R^2$ also equals the square of the [Pearson correlation coefficient](wikipedia://en/221708) between $X$ and $Y$.

### Machine learning and Bayesian inference {#Machine_learning_and_Bayesian_inference}

In many [Bayesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian) and ensemble methods, one decomposes prediction uncertainty via the law of total variance. For a [Bayesian neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bayesian_neural_network) with random parameters $\theta$: $\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid \theta)\right] + \operatorname{Var}\left(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid \theta]\right),$ often referred to as “aleatoric” (within-model) vs. “epistemic” (between-model) uncertainty.[^o21]

### Actuarial science {#Actuarial_science}

[Credibility theory](wikipedia://en/4152503) uses the same partitioning: the expected value of process variance (EVPV), $\operatorname{E}[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X)],$ and the variance of hypothetical means (VHM), $\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X]).$ The ratio of explained to total variance determines how much “credibility” to give to individual risk classifications.[^o3]

### Information theory {#Information_theory}

For [jointly Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jointly_Gaussian) $(X,Y)$, the fraction $\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X])/\operatorname{Var}(Y)$ relates directly to the [mutual information](wikipedia://en/427282) $I(Y;X).$[^o24] In non-Gaussian settings, a high explained-variance ratio still indicates significant information about $Y$ contained in $X$.

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The law of total variance generalizes to multiple or nested conditionings. For example, with two conditioning variables $X_1$ and $X_2$:

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid X_1,X_2)\right] + \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X_1,X_2]\mid X_1)\right] + \operatorname{Var}(\operatorname{E}[Y\mid X_1]).$$

More generally, the [law of total cumulance](wikipedia://en/1611766) extends this approach to higher moments.
