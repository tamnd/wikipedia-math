# Hardy space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hardy space"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), the **Hardy spaces** (or **Hardy classes**) $H^p$ are [spaces](wikipedia://en/340630) of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110) on the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) or [upper half plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_half_plane). They were introduced by [Frigyes Riesz](wikipedia://en/1073126) (Riesz 1923), who named them after [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599), because of the paper (Hardy 1915). In [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478) **Hardy spaces** are spaces of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) on the real $n$-space $\mathbb{R}^n$, defined (in the sense of distributions) as boundary values of the holomorphic functions. Hardy spaces are related to the [*L<sup>p</sup>* spaces](wikipedia://en/45194).[^n1] For $1 \leq p < \infty$ these Hardy spaces are [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of [$L^p$ spaces](wikipedia://en/45194), while for $0<p<1$ the $L^p$ spaces have some undesirable properties, and the Hardy spaces are much better behaved. Hence, $H^p$ spaces can be considered extensions of $L^p$ spaces.[^n2]

:::

Hardy spaces have a number of applications, both in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) itself as well as in interdisciplinary areas such as [control theory](wikipedia://en/7039) (e.g. [$H^{\infty}$ methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H_infinity)) and [scattering theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scattering_theory).

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### On the unit disk {#On_the_unit_disk}

The Hardy space $H^p$ for $0 < p < \infty$ is the class of [holomorphic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_functions) $f$ on the open [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) $\mathbb{D} = \{ z\in \mathbb{C} : |z|<1\}$ satisfying

$$\sup_{0\,\leqslant\, r\,<\,1}\left(\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi}\left|f \left (re^{i\theta}\right )\right|^p \; \mathrm{d}\theta\right)^\frac{1}{p}<\infty.$$

If $p \geq 1$, this coincides with the definition of the Hardy space $p$-norm, denoted by $\|f\|_{H^p}.$

The space $H^\infty$ is defined as the vector space of bounded holomorphic functions on the unit disk, with norm

$${\|f\|}_{H^\infty} = \sup_{|z|< 1} \left|f(z)\right|.$$

For $0<p\leq q\leq\infty$, the class $H^q$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $H^p$, and the $H^p$-norm is increasing with $p$ (it is a consequence of [Hölder's inequality](wikipedia://en/191538) that the $L^p$-norm is increasing for [probability measures](wikipedia://en/43325), i.e. [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) with total mass 1) (Rudin 1987, Def 17.7).

$H^2$ is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), and it is unitarily equivalent to $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ via the unitary map $\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n z^n \leftrightarrow (a_n)_{n=0}^\infty$.[^o10]

### On the unit circle {#On_the_unit_circle}

The Hardy spaces can also be viewed as closed vector subspaces of the complex [*L<sup>p</sup>* spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) $\mathbb T = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1 \}$. This connection is provided by the following theorem (Katznelson 1976, Thm 3.8): Given $f \in H^{p}$ with $p\geq 1$, the radial limit

$$\tilde f\!\left(e^{i\theta}\right) = \lim_{r\to 1} \,f\!\left(re^{i\theta}\right)$$

exists for almost every $\theta$ and $\tilde f \in L^{p}(\mathbb{T})$ such that

$${\|\tilde f\|}_{L^p} = {\|f\|}_{H^p}.$$

Denote by $H^{p}(\mathbb{T})$ the vector subspace of $L^{p}(\mathbb{T})$ consisting of all limit functions $\tilde f$, when $f$ varies in $H^{p}$, one then has that for *p* ≥ 1,(Katznelson 1976)

$$g\in H^p\left(\mathbb{T}\right)\text{ if and only if } g\in L^p\left(\mathbb{T}\right)\text{ and } \hat{g}_{n} =0 \text{ for all } n < 0,$$

where the $\hat{g}_{n}$ are the [Fourier coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_coefficients) defined as

$$\hat{g}_{n} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi} g\left(e^{i\phi}\right) e^{-in\phi} \, \mathrm{d}\phi, \quad \forall n \in \mathbb{Z}.$$

The space $H^{p}(\mathbb{T})$ is a closed subspace of $L^{p}(\mathbb{T})$. Since $L^{p}(\mathbb{T})$ is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) (for $1\leq p\leq \infty$), so is $H^{p}(\mathbb{T})$.

The above can be turned around. Given a function $\tilde f \in L^p (\mathbf T)$, with *p* ≥ 1, one can regain a ([harmonic](wikipedia://en/55762)) function *f* on the unit disk by means of the [Poisson kernel](wikipedia://en/2214583) *P<sub>r</sub>*:

$$f\left(re^{i\theta}\right)=\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_0^{2\pi} P_r(\theta-\phi) \tilde f\left(e^{i\phi}\right) \,\mathrm{d}\phi, \quad r < 1,$$

and *f* belongs to *H<sup>p</sup>* exactly when $\tilde f$ is in *H<sup>p</sup>*(**T**). Supposing that $\tilde f$ is in *H<sup>p</sup>*(**T**), i.e., $\tilde f$ has Fourier coefficients (*a<sub>n</sub>*)<sub>*n*∈**Z**</sub> with *a<sub>n</sub>* = 0 for every *n* \< 0, then the associated holomorphic function *f* of *H<sup>p</sup>* is given by

$$f(z)=\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n z^n, \ \ \ |z| < 1.$$

In applications, those functions with vanishing negative Fourier coefficients are commonly interpreted as the [causal](wikipedia://en/554087) solutions. For example, the [Hardy space $H^{2}$](wikipedia://en/20041584) consists of functions whose [mean square value](wikipedia://en/66819) remains bounded as $r \to 1$ from below. Thus, the space *H*<sup>2</sup> is seen to sit naturally inside [$L^{2}$ space](wikipedia://en/28680558), and is represented by [infinite sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence) indexed by **N**; whereas *L*<sup>2</sup> consists of [bi-infinite sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-infinite_sequence) indexed by **Z**.

### On the upper half plane {#On_the_upper_half_plane}

The Hardy space on the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) $\mathbb{H} = \{x + iy \mid y > 0;\ x, y \in \mathbb{R} \}$ is defined to be the space of holomorphic functions $f$ on $\mathbb{H}$ with bounded norm, given by

$$\|f\|_{H^p} = \sup_{y>0} \left ( \int_{-\infty}^{+\infty}|f(x+ iy)|^p\, \mathrm{d}x \right)^{\frac{1}{p}}.$$

The corresponding $H^{\infty}(\mathbb{H})$ is defined as functions of bounded norm, with the norm given by

$$\|f\|_{H^\infty} = \sup_{z\in\mathbb{H}}|f(z)|.$$

The unit disk is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the upper half-plane by means of a [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493). For example, let $m: \mathbb{D}\rightarrow \mathbb{H}$ denote the Möbius transformation

$$m(z)= i \frac{1+z}{1-z}.$$

Then the linear operator $M:H^2(\mathbb{H}) \rightarrow H^2(\mathbb{D})$ defined by

$$(Mf)(z):=\frac{\sqrt{\pi}}{1-z} f(m(z)),$$

is an [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) of Hardy spaces.

A similar approach applies to, e.g., the right half-plane.

### On the real vector space {#On_the_real_vector_space}

In analysis on the real vector space $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Hardy space $H^p$ (for $0 < p \leq \infty$) consists of [tempered distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Tempered_distributions_and_Fourier_transform) $f$ such that for some [Schwartz function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_function) $\Phi$ with $\int\Phi = 1$, the [maximal function](wikipedia://en/16358287)

$$(M_\Phi f)(x)=\sup_{t>0}|(f*\Phi_t)(x)|$$

is in $L^p(\mathbb{R}^n)$, where $*$ is convolution and $\Phi_t(x) = t^{-n}\Phi(x/t)$. The $H^p$-[quasinorm](wikipedia://en/6652599) $\|f\|_{H^p}$ of a distribution $f$ of $H^p$ is defined to be the $L^p$ norm of $M_\Phi f$ (this depends on the choice of $\Phi$, but different choices of Schwartz functions $\Phi$ give equivalent norms). The $H^p$-quasinorm is a norm when $p \geq 1$, but not when $p < 1$.

If $1 < p < \infty$, the Hardy space $H^p$ is the same vector space as $L^p$, with equivalent norm. When $p=1$, the Hardy space $H^1$ is a proper subspace of $L^1$. One can find sequences in $H^1$ that are bounded in $L^1$ but unbounded in $H^1$; for example, on the line

$$f_k(x) = \mathbf{1}_{[0, 1]}(x - k) - \mathbf{1}_{[0, 1]}(x + k), \ \ \ k > 0.$$

The $L^1$ and $H^1$ norms are not equivalent on $H^1$, and $H^1$ is not closed in $L^1$. The dual of $H^1$ is the space $\operatorname{BMO}$ of functions of [bounded mean oscillation](wikipedia://en/4694026). The space $\operatorname{BMO}$ contains unbounded functions (proving again that $H^1$ is not closed in $L^1$).

If $p<1$ then the Hardy space $H^p$ has elements that are not functions, and its dual is the homogeneous Lipschitz space of order $n(1/p-1)$. When '$p < 1$, the $H^p$-quasinorm is not a norm, as it is not subadditive. The $p$-th power $\|f\|^p_{H^p}$ is subadditive for $p<1$ and so defines a metric on the Hardy space $H^p$, which defines the topology and makes $H^p$ into a complete metric space.

#### Atomic decomposition {#Atomic_decomposition}

When $0 < p \leq 1$, a bounded measurable function $f$ of compact support is in the Hardy space $H^p$ if and only if all its moments

$$\int_{\mathbf{R}^n} f(x)x_1^{i_1}\ldots x_n^{i_n}\, \mathrm{d}x,$$

whose order $i_1+\cdots+i_n$ is at most $n(1/p-1)$, vanish. For example, the integral of $f$ must vanish in order that $f \in H^p$, $0 < p \leq 1$, and as long as $p > n/(n+1)$, this is also sufficient.

If in addition $f$ has support in some ball $B$ and is bounded by $|B|^{-1/p}$, then $f$ is called an **$H^p$-atom** (here $|B|$ denotes the Euclidean volume of $B$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$). The $H^p$-quasinorm of an arbitrary $H^p$-atom is bounded by a constant depending only on $p$ and on the Schwartz function $\Phi$.

When $0 < p \leq 1$, any element $f$ of $H^p$ has an **atomic decomposition** as a convergent infinite combination of $H^p$-atoms,

$$f = \sum c_j a_j, \ \ \ \sum |c_j|^p < \infty$$

where the $a_j$ are $H^p$-atoms and the $c_j$ are scalars.

On the line, for example, the difference of Dirac distributions $f = \delta_1 - \delta_0$ can be represented as a series of [Haar functions](wikipedia://en/50905), convergent in $H^p$-quasinorm when $1/2 < p < 1$. (On the circle, the corresponding representation is valid for $0 < p < 1$, but on the line, Haar functions do not belong to $H^p$ when $p \leq 1/2$, because their maximal function is equivalent at infinity to $ax^{-2}$ for some $a \neq 0$.)

:::

## Link between real- and complex-variable Hardy spaces {#Link_between_real-_and_complex-variable_Hardy_spaces}

Real-variable techniques, mainly associated to the study of *real Hardy spaces* defined on **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, are also used in the simpler framework of the circle. It is a common practice to allow for complex functions (or distributions) in these "real" spaces. The definition that follows does not distinguish between real or complex case.

Let *P<sub>r</sub>* denote the Poisson kernel on the unit circle **T**. For a distribution *f* on the unit circle, set

$$(M f)(e^{i\theta})=\sup_{0<r<1} \left |(f * P_r) \left(e^{i\theta} \right)\right|,$$

where the *star* indicates convolution between the distribution *f* and the function e<sup>iθ</sup> → *P<sub>r</sub>*(θ) on the circle. Namely, (*f* ∗ *P<sub>r</sub>*)(e<sup>iθ</sup>) is the result of the action of *f* on the *C*<sup>∞</sup>-function defined on the unit circle by

$$e^{i\varphi} \rightarrow P_r(\theta - \varphi).$$

For 0 \< *p* \< ∞, the *real Hardy space* *H<sup>p</sup>*(**T**) consists of distributions *f* such that *M f* is in *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**).

The function *F* defined on the unit disk by *F*(*re*<sup>iθ</sup>) = (*f* ∗ *P<sub>r</sub>*)(e<sup>iθ</sup>) is harmonic, and *M f* is the *radial maximal function* of *F*. When *M f* belongs to *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**) and *p* ≥ 1, the distribution *f* "*is*" a function in *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**), namely the boundary value of *F*. For *p* ≥ 1, the *real Hardy space* *H<sup>p</sup>*(**T**) is a subset of *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**).

::: {#o19 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Hardy space conjugate function"}

### Conjugate function {#Conjugate_function}

To every real trigonometric polynomial *u* on the unit circle, one associates the real *conjugate polynomial* *v* such that *u* + i*v* extends to a holomorphic function in the unit disk,

$$u(e^{i\theta}) = \frac{a_0}{2} + \sum_{k \geqslant 1} a_k \cos(k \theta) + b_k \sin(k \theta) \longrightarrow v(e^{i\theta}) = \sum_{k \geqslant 1} a_k \sin(k \theta) - b_k \cos(k \theta).$$

This mapping *u* → *v* extends to a bounded linear operator *H* on *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**), when 1 \< *p* \< ∞ (up to a scalar multiple, it is the [Hilbert transform](wikipedia://en/574024) on the unit circle), and *H* also maps *L*<sup>1</sup>(**T**) to [weak-*L*<sup>1</sup>(**T**)](wikipedia://en/45194#Weak_Lp). When 1 ≤ *p* \< ∞, the following are equivalent for a *real valued* integrable function *f* on the unit circle:

- the function *f* is the real part of some function *g* ∈ *H<sup>p</sup>*(**T**)
- the function *f* and its conjugate *H(f)* belong to *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**)
- the radial maximal function *M f* belongs to *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**).

When 1 \< *p* \< ∞, *H(f)* belongs to *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**) when *f* ∈ *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**), hence the real Hardy space *H<sup>p</sup>*(**T**) coincides with *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**) in this case. For *p* = 1, the real Hardy space *H*<sup>1</sup>(**T**) is a proper subspace of *L*<sup>1</sup>(**T**).

The case of *p* = ∞ was excluded from the definition of real Hardy spaces, because the maximal function *M f* of an *L*<sup>∞</sup> function is always bounded, and because it is not desirable that real-*H*<sup>∞</sup> be equal to *L*<sup>∞</sup>. However, the two following properties are equivalent for a real valued function *f*

- the function *f* is the real part of some function *g* ∈ *H*<sup>∞</sup>(**T**)
- the function *f* and its conjugate *H(f)* belong to *L*<sup>∞</sup>(**T**).

:::

### For 0 \< *p* \< 1 {#For_0_<_p_<_1}

When 0 \< *p* \< 1, a function *F* in *H<sup>p</sup>* cannot be reconstructed from the real part of its boundary limit *function* on the circle, because of the lack of convexity of *L<sup>p</sup>* in this case. Convexity fails but a kind of "*complex convexity*" remains, namely the fact that *z* → |*z*|<sup>*q*</sup> is [subharmonic](wikipedia://en/3237359#Subharmonic_functions_in_the_complex_plane) for every *q* > 0. As a consequence, if

$$F(z) = \sum_{n=0}^{+\infty} c_n z^n, \quad |z| < 1$$

is in *H<sup>p</sup>*, it can be shown that *c<sub>n</sub>* = O(*n*<sup>1/*p*–1</sup>). It follows that the Fourier series

$$\sum_{n=0}^{+\infty} c_n e^{in \theta}$$

converges in the sense of distributions to a distribution *f* on the unit circle, and *F*(*re*<sup>iθ</sup>) =(*f* ∗ *P<sub>r</sub>*)(θ). The function *F* ∈ *H<sup>p</sup>* can be reconstructed from the real distribution Re(*f*) on the circle, because the Taylor coefficients *c<sub>n</sub>* of *F* can be computed from the Fourier coefficients of Re(*f*).

Distributions on the circle are general enough for handling Hardy spaces when *p* \< 1. Distributions that are not functions do occur, as is seen with functions *F*(*z*) = (1−*z*)<sup>−*N*</sup> (for |*z*| \< 1), that belong to *H<sup>p</sup>* when 0 \< *N* *p* \< 1 (and *N* an integer ≥ 1).

A real distribution on the circle belongs to real-*H<sup>p</sup>*(**T**) iff it is the boundary value of the real part of some *F* ∈ *H<sup>p</sup>*. A Dirac distribution δ<sub>*x*</sub>, at any point *x* of the unit circle, belongs to real-*H<sup>p</sup>*(**T**) for every *p* \< 1; derivatives δ′<sub>*x*</sub> belong when *p* \< 1/2, second derivatives δ′′<sub>*x*</sub> when *p* \< 1/3, and so on.

## Beurling factorization {#Beurling_factorization}

For 0 \< *p* ≤ ∞, every non-zero function *f* in *H<sup>p</sup>* can be written as the product *f* = *Gh* where *G* is an *outer function* and *h* is an *inner function*, as defined below (Rudin 1987, Thm 17.17). This "[Beurling](wikipedia://en/286530) factorization" allows the Hardy space to be completely characterized by the spaces of inner and outer functions.[^o23][^o24]

One says that *G*(*z*) is an **outer (exterior) function** if it takes the form

$$G(z) = c\, \exp\left(\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\frac{e^{i\theta}+z}{e^{i\theta}-z} \log\!\left(\varphi\!\left(e^{i\theta} \right)\right)\, \mathrm{d}\theta \right)$$

for some complex number *c* with |*c*| = 1, and some positive measurable function $\varphi$ on the unit circle such that $\log(\varphi)$ is integrable on the circle. In particular, when $\varphi$ is integrable on the circle, *G* is in *H*<sup>1</sup> because the above takes the form of the [Poisson kernel](wikipedia://en/2214583) (Rudin 1987, Thm 17.16). This implies that

$$\lim_{r\to 1^-}\left|G\left (re^{i\theta} \right)\right| = \varphi \left(e^{i\theta}\right )$$

for almost every θ.

One says that *h* is an **inner (interior) function** if and only if |*h*| ≤ 1 on the unit disk and the limit

$$\lim_{r\to 1^-} h(re^{i\theta})$$

exists for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) θ and its [modulus](wikipedia://en/991) is equal to 1 a.e. In particular, *h* is in *H*<sup>∞</sup>. The inner function can be further factored into a form involving a [Blaschke product](wikipedia://en/981855).

The function *f*, decomposed as *f* = *Gh*, is in *H<sup>p</sup>* if and only if φ belongs to *L<sup>p</sup>*(**T**), where φ is the positive function in the representation of the outer function *G*.

Let *G* be an outer function represented as above from a function φ on the circle. Replacing φ by φ<sup>α</sup>, α > 0, a family (*G*<sub>α</sub>) of outer functions is obtained, with the properties:

*G*<sub>1</sub> = *G*, *G*<sub>α+β</sub> = *G*<sub>α</sub> *G*<sub>β</sub> and |*G*<sub>α</sub>| = |*G*|<sup>α</sup> almost everywhere on the circle.

It follows that whenever 0 \< *p*, *q*, *r* \< ∞ and 1/*r* = 1/*p* + 1/*q*, every function *f* in *H<sup>r</sup>* can be expressed as the product of a function in *H<sup>p</sup>* and a function in *H<sup>q</sup>*. For example: every function in *H*<sup>1</sup> is the product of two functions in *H*<sup>2</sup>; every function in *H<sup>p</sup>*, *p* \< 1, can be expressed as product of several functions in some *H<sup>q</sup>*, *q* > 1.

## Martingale *H<sup>p</sup>* {#Martingale_Hp}

Let (*M<sub>n</sub>*)<sub>*n*≥0</sub> be a [martingale](wikipedia://en/274536) on some probability space (Ω, Σ, *P*), with respect to an increasing sequence of σ-fields (Σ<sub>*n*</sub>)<sub>*n*≥0</sub>. Assume for simplicity that Σ is equal to the σ-field generated by the sequence (Σ<sub>*n*</sub>)<sub>*n*≥0</sub>. The *maximal function* of the martingale is defined by

$$M^* = \sup_{n \ge 0} \, |M_n|.$$

Let 1 ≤ *p* \< ∞. The martingale (*M<sub>n</sub>*)<sub>*n*≥0</sub> belongs to *martingale*-*H<sup>p</sup>* when *M\** ∈ *L<sup>p</sup>*.

If *M\** ∈ *L<sup>p</sup>*, the martingale (*M<sub>n</sub>*)<sub>*n*≥0</sub> is bounded in *L<sup>p</sup>*; hence it converges almost surely to some function *f* by the [martingale convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/12261058). Moreover, *M<sub>n</sub>* converges to *f* in *L<sup>p</sup>*-norm by the [dominated convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/351853); hence *M<sub>n</sub>* can be expressed as conditional expectation of *f* on Σ<sub>*n*</sub>. It is thus possible to identify martingale-*H<sup>p</sup>* with the subspace of *L<sup>p</sup>*(Ω, Σ, *P*) consisting of those *f* such that the martingale

$$M_n = \operatorname E \bigl( f | \Sigma_n \bigr)$$

belongs to martingale-*H<sup>p</sup>*.

[Doob's maximal inequality](wikipedia://en/8522128) implies that martingale-*H<sup>p</sup>* coincides with *L<sup>p</sup>*(Ω, Σ, *P*) when 1 \< *p* \< ∞. The interesting space is martingale-*H*<sup>1</sup>, whose dual is martingale-BMO (Garsia 1973).

The Burkholder–Gundy inequalities (when *p* > 1) and the Burgess Davis inequality (when *p* = 1) relate the *L<sup>p</sup>*-norm of the maximal function to that of the *square function* of the martingale

$$S(f) = \left( |M_0|^2 + \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} |M_{n+1} - M_n|^2 \right)^{\frac{1}{2}}.$$

Martingale-*H<sup>p</sup>* can be defined by saying that *S*(*f*)∈ *L<sup>p</sup>* (Garsia 1973).

Martingales with continuous time parameter can also be considered. A direct link with the classical theory is obtained via the complex [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/149984) (*B<sub>t</sub>*) in the complex plane, starting from the point *z* = 0 at time *t* = 0. Let τ denote the hitting time of the unit circle. For every holomorphic function *F* in the unit disk,

$$M_t = F(B_{t \wedge \tau})$$

is a martingale, that belongs to martingale-*H<sup>p</sup>* iff *F* ∈ *H<sup>p</sup>* (Burkholder Gundy Silverstein 1971).

::: {#o31 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

In this example, Ω = \[0, 1\] and Σ<sub>*n*</sub> is the finite field generated by the [dyadic](wikipedia://en/30151810) partition of \[0, 1\] into 2<sup>*n*</sup> intervals of length 2<sup>−*n*</sup>, for every *n* ≥ 0. If a function *f* on \[0, 1\] is represented by its expansion on the [Haar system](wikipedia://en/50905) (*h<sub>k</sub>*)

$$f = \sum c_k h_k,$$

then the martingale-*H*<sup>1</sup> norm of *f* can be defined by the *L*<sup>1</sup> norm of the square function

$$\int_0^1 \Bigl( \sum |c_k h_k(x)|^2 \Bigr)^{\frac{1}{2}} \, \mathrm{d}x.$$

This space, sometimes denoted by *H*<sup>1</sup>(δ), is isomorphic to the classical real *H*<sup>1</sup> space on the circle (Müller 2005). The Haar system is an [unconditional basis](wikipedia://en/3460662) for *H*<sup>1</sup>(δ).

:::
