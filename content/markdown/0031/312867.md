# Local analysis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Local analysis"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **local analysis** is the practice of looking at a problem relative to each [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* first, and then later trying to integrate the information gained at each prime into a 'global' picture. These are forms of the approach.

:::

## Group theory {#Group_theory}

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), local analysis was started by the [Sylow theorems](wikipedia://en/53993), which contain significant information about the structure of a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) *G* for each prime number *p* dividing the order of *G*. This area of study was enormously developed in the quest for the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445), starting with the [Feit–Thompson theorem](wikipedia://en/1656473) that groups of odd order are [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012).[^o3]

## Number theory {#Number_theory}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) one may study a [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109), for example, modulo *p* for all primes *p*, looking for constraints on solutions.[^o5] The next step is to look modulo prime powers, and then for solutions in the [*p*-adic field](wikipedia://en/51423). This kind of local analysis provides conditions for solution that are *necessary*. In cases where local analysis (plus the condition that there are real solutions) provides also *sufficient* conditions, one says that the *[Hasse principle](wikipedia://en/489707)* holds: this is the best possible situation. It does for [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478), but certainly not in general (for example for [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225)). The point of view that one would like to understand what extra conditions are needed has been very influential, for example for [cubic forms](wikipedia://en/6680683).

Some form of local analysis underlies both the standard applications of the [Hardy–Littlewood circle method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Littlewood_circle_method) in [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513), and the use of [adele rings](wikipedia://en/247978), making this one of the unifying principles across number theory.
