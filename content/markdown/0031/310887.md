# Bialgebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bialgebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **bialgebra** over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over *K* which is both a [unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra) [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) and a [counital coassociative coalgebra](wikipedia://en/310886).[^n1] The algebraic and coalgebraic structures are made compatible with a few more axioms. Specifically, the [comultiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comultiplication) and the [counit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counit) are both unital algebra [homomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homomorphisms), or equivalently, the multiplication and the unit of the algebra both are [coalgebra morphisms](wikipedia://en/310886#Further_concepts_and_facts).[^n1] (These statements are equivalent since they are expressed by the same [commutative diagrams](wikipedia://en/138484).)[^n1]

:::

Similar bialgebras are related by bialgebra homomorphisms. A bialgebra homomorphism is a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) that is both an algebra and a coalgebra homomorphism.[^n2]

As reflected in the symmetry of the commutative diagrams, the definition of bialgebra is [self-dual](wikipedia://en/310885), so if one can define a [dual](wikipedia://en/7988) of *B* (which is always possible if *B* is finite-dimensional), then it is automatically a bialgebra.

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

**(*B*, ∇, η, Δ, ε)** is a **bialgebra** over *K* if it has the following properties:

- *B* is a vector space over *K*;
- there are *K*-[linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102) (multiplication) ∇: *B* ⊗ *B* → *B* (equivalent to *K*-[multilinear map](wikipedia://en/221525) ∇: *B* × *B* → *B*) and (unit) η: *K* → *B*, such that (*B*, ∇, η) is a unital associative [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788);
- there are *K*-linear maps (comultiplication) Δ: *B* → *B* ⊗ *B* and (counit) ε: *B* → *K*, such that (*B*, Δ, ε) is a (counital coassociative) [coalgebra](wikipedia://en/310886);
- compatibility conditions expressed by the following [commutative diagrams](wikipedia://en/138484):

## Coassociativity and counit {#Coassociativity_and_counit}

The [*K*-linear map](wikipedia://en/221525) Δ: *B* → *B* ⊗ *B* is [coassociative](wikipedia://en/310886) if $(\mathrm{id}_B \otimes \Delta) \circ \Delta = (\Delta \otimes \mathrm{id}_B) \circ \Delta$.

The *K*-linear map ε: *B* → *K* is a counit if $(\mathrm{id}_B \otimes \epsilon) \circ \Delta = \mathrm{id}_B = (\epsilon \otimes \mathrm{id}_B) \circ \Delta$.

## Compatibility conditions {#Compatibility_conditions}

The four commutative diagrams can be read either as "comultiplication and counit are [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) of algebras" or, equivalently, "multiplication and unit are [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564) of coalgebras".

These statements are meaningful once we explain the natural structures of algebra and coalgebra in all the vector spaces involved besides *B*: (*K*, ∇<sub>0</sub>, η<sub>0</sub>) is a unital associative algebra in an obvious way and (*B* ⊗ *B*, ∇<sub>2</sub>, η<sub>2</sub>) is a unital associative algebra with unit and multiplication

$$\eta_2 := (\eta \otimes \eta) : K \otimes K \equiv K \to (B \otimes B)$$

$$\nabla_2 := (\nabla \otimes \nabla) \circ (id \otimes \tau \otimes id) : (B \otimes B) \otimes (B \otimes B) \to (B \otimes B),$$

so that $\nabla_2 ( (x_1 \otimes x_2) \otimes (y_1 \otimes y_2) ) = \nabla(x_1 \otimes y_1) \otimes \nabla(x_2 \otimes y_2)$ or, omitting ∇ and writing [multiplication as juxtaposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplication_as_juxtaposition), $(x_1 \otimes x_2)(y_1 \otimes y_2) = x_1 y_1 \otimes x_2 y_2$;

similarly, (*K*, Δ<sub>0</sub>, ε<sub>0</sub>) is a coalgebra in an obvious way and *B* ⊗ *B* is a coalgebra with counit and comultiplication

$$\epsilon_2 := (\epsilon \otimes \epsilon) : (B \otimes B) \to K \otimes K \equiv K$$

$$\Delta_2 := (id \otimes \tau \otimes id) \circ (\Delta \otimes \Delta) : (B \otimes B) \to (B \otimes B) \otimes (B \otimes B).$$

Then, diagrams 1 and 3 say that Δ: *B* → *B* ⊗ *B* is a homomorphism of unital (associative) algebras (*B*, ∇, η) and (*B* ⊗ *B*, ∇<sub>2</sub>, η<sub>2</sub>)

$\Delta \circ \nabla = \nabla_2 \circ (\Delta \otimes \Delta) : (B \otimes B) \to (B \otimes B)$, or simply Δ(*xy*) = Δ(*x*) Δ(*y*),

$\Delta \circ \eta = \eta_2 : K \to (B \otimes B)$, or simply Δ(1<sub>*B*</sub>) = 1<sub>*B* ⊗ *B*</sub>;

diagrams 2 and 4 say that ε: *B* → *K* is a homomorphism of unital (associative) algebras (*B*, ∇, η) and (*K*, ∇<sub>0</sub>, η<sub>0</sub>):

$\epsilon \circ \nabla = \nabla_0 \circ (\epsilon \otimes \epsilon) : (B \otimes B) \to K$, or simply ε(*xy*) = ε(*x*) ε(*y*)

$\epsilon \circ \eta = \eta_0 : K \to K$, or simply ε(1<sub>*B*</sub>) = 1<sub>*K*</sub>.

Equivalently, diagrams 1 and 2 say that ∇: *B* ⊗ *B* → *B* is a homomorphism of (counital coassociative) coalgebras (*B* ⊗ *B*, Δ<sub>2</sub>, ε<sub>2</sub>) and (*B*, Δ, ε):

$$\nabla \otimes \nabla \circ \Delta_2 = \Delta \circ \nabla : (B \otimes B) \to (B \otimes B),$$

$$\nabla_0 \circ \epsilon_2 = \epsilon \circ \nabla : (B \otimes B) \to K;$$

diagrams 3 and 4 say that η: *K* → *B* is a homomorphism of (counital coassociative) coalgebras (*K*, Δ<sub>0</sub>, ε<sub>0</sub>) and (*B*, Δ, ε):

$$\eta_2 \circ \Delta_0 = \Delta \circ \eta : K \to (B \otimes B),$$

$$\eta_0 \circ \epsilon_0 = \epsilon \circ \eta : K \to K,$$

where

$$\epsilon_0 =id_K =\eta_0.$$

## Examples {#Examples}

### Group bialgebra {#Group_bialgebra}

An example of a bialgebra is the set of functions from a finite [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* (or more generally, any finite [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652)) to $\mathbb R$, which we may represent as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $\mathbb R^G$ consisting of linear combinations of standard [basis vectors](wikipedia://en/18420) **e**<sub>*g*</sub> for each *g* ∈ *G*, which may represent a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) over *G* in the case of vectors whose coefficients are all non-negative and sum to 1. An example of suitable comultiplication operators and counits which yield a counital coalgebra are

$$\Delta(\mathbf e_g) = \mathbf e_g \otimes \mathbf e_g \,,$$

which represents making a copy of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) (which we extend to all $\mathbb R^G$ by linearity), and

$$\varepsilon(\mathbf e_g) = 1 \,,$$

(again extended linearly to all of $\mathbb R^G$) which represents "tracing out" a random variable — *i.e.,* forgetting the value of a random variable (represented by a single tensor factor) to obtain a [marginal distribution](wikipedia://en/506077) on the remaining variables (the remaining tensor factors). Given the interpretation of (Δ,ε) in terms of probability distributions as above, the bialgebra consistency conditions amount to constraints on (∇,η) as follows:

1. η is an operator preparing a normalized probability distribution which is independent of all other random variables;
1. The product ∇ maps a probability distribution on two variables to a probability distribution on one variable;
1. Copying a random variable in the distribution given by η is equivalent to having two independent random variables in the distribution η;
1. Taking the product of two random variables, and preparing a copy of the resulting random variable, has the same distribution as preparing copies of each random variable independently of one another, and multiplying them together in pairs.

A pair (∇,η) which satisfy these constraints are the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) operator

$$\nabla\bigl(\mathbf e_g \otimes \mathbf e_h\bigr) = \mathbf e_{gh} \,,$$

again extended to all $\mathbb R^G \otimes \mathbb R^G$ by linearity; this produces a normalized probability distribution from a distribution on two random variables, and has as a unit the delta-distribution $\eta = \mathbf e_{i} \;,$ where *i* ∈ *G* denotes the identity element of the group *G*.

### Other examples {#Other_examples}

Other examples of bialgebras include the [tensor algebra](wikipedia://en/363628), which can be made into a bialgebra by adding the appropriate comultiplication and counit; these are worked out in detail in that article.

Bialgebras can often be extended to [Hopf algebras](wikipedia://en/325714), if an appropriate antipode can be found; thus, all Hopf algebras are examples of bialgebras.[^n5] Similar structures with different compatibility between the product and comultiplication, or different types of multiplication and comultiplication, include [Lie bialgebras](wikipedia://en/4290894) and [Frobenius algebras](wikipedia://en/1814652). Additional examples are given in the article on [coalgebras](wikipedia://en/310886).
