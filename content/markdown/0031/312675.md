# Jacques Hadamard

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Jacques Hadamard"}

**Jacques Salomon Hadamard** (; 8 December 1865 – 17 October 1963) was a French mathematician who made major contributions in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), and [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations).[^o6]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

The son of a teacher, Amédée Hadamard, of [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish) descent, and Claire Marie Jeanne Picard, Hadamard was born in [Versailles, France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Versailles_%28city%29) and attended the [Lycée Charlemagne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Charlemagne) and [Lycée Louis-le-Grand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycée_Louis-le-Grand), where his father taught. In 1884 Hadamard entered the [École Normale Supérieure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Normale_Supérieure), having placed first in the entrance examinations both there and at the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique). His teachers included [Tannery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Tannery), [Hermite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite), [Darboux](wikipedia://en/1142530), [Appell](wikipedia://en/3359510), [Goursat](wikipedia://en/1674255), and [Picard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Émile_Picard). He published his famous thesis in 1892[^o8] containing the [Hadamard gap theorem](wikipedia://en/13021753), the [Cauchy–Hadamard theorem](wikipedia://en/13620523), [Hadamard's theorem on the radius of meromorphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard's_radius_of_meromorphy_theorem) and the [Hadamard fractional integral](wikipedia://en/229939#Hadamard_fractional_integral). Later that year, he was awarded the Grand Prix des Sciences Mathématiques for a memoir[^o9] containing the [Hadamard factorization theorem](wikipedia://en/2369798) that applies it and the results of his thesis to the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809).[^o10]

In 1892, Hadamard married Louise-Anna Trénel, also of Jewish descent, with whom he had three sons and two daughters. The following year he took up a lectureship in the [University of Bordeaux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bordeaux), where he proved his [celebrated inequality](wikipedia://en/612000) on [determinants](wikipedia://en/8468), which led to the discovery of [Hadamard matrices](wikipedia://en/507553) when equality holds. In 1896 he made two important contributions: he proved the [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692), using [complex function theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_function_theory) (also proved independently by [Charles Jean de la Vallée-Poussin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jean_de_la_Vallée-Poussin)); and he was awarded the Bordin Prize of the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) for his work on [geodesics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesics) in the [differential geometry of surfaces](wikipedia://en/15513875) and [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems). In the same year he was appointed Professor of Astronomy and Rational Mechanics in Bordeaux. His foundational work on geometry and [symbolic dynamics](wikipedia://en/1986263) continued in 1898 with the study of geodesics on surfaces of [negative curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_curvature). For his cumulative work, he was awarded the [Prix Poncelet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prix_Poncelet) in 1898.

After the [Dreyfus affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dreyfus_affair), which involved him personally because his second cousin Lucie was the wife of Dreyfus, Hadamard became politically active and a staunch supporter of [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jew) causes[^o11] though he professed to be an atheist in his religion.[^o12][^o13]

In 1897 he moved back to Paris, holding positions in the [Sorbonne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) and the [Collège de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collège_de_France), where he was appointed Professor of Mechanics in 1909. In addition to this post, he was appointed to chairs of analysis at the [École Polytechnique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Polytechnique) in 1912 and at the [École Centrale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/École_Centrale) in 1920, succeeding [Jordan](wikipedia://en/355931) and Appell. In Paris Hadamard concentrated his interests on the problems of mathematical physics, in particular [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations), the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) and the foundations of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180). He introduced the idea of *[well-posed problem](wikipedia://en/176673)* and the *[method of descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Method_of_descent)* in the theory of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564), culminating in his seminal book on the subject, based on lectures given at [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) in 1922. Later in his life he wrote on [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [mathematical education](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_education).

Hadamard was elected to the [French Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Academy_of_Sciences) in 1916, in succession to [Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740), whose complete works he helped edit. He became foreign member of the [Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Netherlands_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1920.[^o14] He was elected a foreign member of the [Academy of Sciences of the USSR](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Sciences_of_the_USSR) in 1929. He visited the Soviet Union in 1930 and 1934 and China in 1936 at the invitation of Soviet and Chinese mathematicians.

Hadamard stayed in France at the beginning of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) and escaped to southern France in 1940. The [Vichy government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vichy_France) permitted him to leave for the United States in 1941 and he obtained a visiting position at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) in New York. He moved to London in 1944 and returned to France when the war ended in 1945.

Hadamard was awarded an honorary doctorate ([LL.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legum_Doctor)) by [Yale University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yale_University) in October 1901, during celebrations for the bicentenary of the university.[^o15] He was awarded the [CNRS Gold medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CNRS_Gold_medal) for his lifetime achievements in 1956. He died in Paris in 1963, aged ninety-seven.

Hadamard's students included [Maurice Fréchet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maurice_René_Fréchet), [Paul Lévy](wikipedia://en/556462), [Szolem Mandelbrojt](wikipedia://en/1070245), and [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019).

## On creativity {#On_creativity}

In his book *Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field*,[^o17] Hadamard uses the results of introspection to study mathematical thought processes,p. 2 and tries to report and interpret observations, personal or gathered from other scholars engaged in the work of invention.p. 133 In sharp contrast to authors who identify [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language) and [cognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognition), he describes his own mathematical thinking as largely wordless, often accompanied by [mental images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mental_images) that represent the entire solution to a problem. He surveyed 100 of the leading physicists of the day (approximately 1900), asking them how they did their work.

Hadamard described the experiences of the mathematicians/theoretical physicists [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125), [Hermann von Helmholtz](wikipedia://en/53311), [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) and others as viewing entire solutions with "sudden spontaneousness".p. 13–16

Hadamard described the process as having four steps of the five-step [Graham Wallas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graham_Wallas) [creative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creativity) process model, with the first three also having been put forth by Helmholtz:p. 56 Preparation, Incubation, Illumination, and Verification (Wallas' five stages added "Intimation" prior to Illumination, a sudden feeling of being about to find the solution to a problem).[^o18]
