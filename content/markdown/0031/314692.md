# One-form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="One-form"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a **one-form** (or **covector field**) on a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) is a [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519) of degree one. One-forms are dual to [vector fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_fields) on the same manifold, in the sense that a one-form pairs naturally with a vector field to produce a [real valued function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_valued_function), or with a [tangent vector](wikipedia://en/191999) at a point to produce a real number.[^o2]

:::

The simplest example of a one-form is the [differential of a function](wikipedia://en/23997203) $df$ for a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) $f$. The natural pairing means that if $v$ is a tangent vector at a point $p$, and $\alpha$ is a one-form, then $\alpha(v)$ is a scalar. For the differential of a function, $df(v)$ is the [directional derivative](wikipedia://en/487859), or the rate at which $f$ is changing, at $p$ and along $v$.[^o3]

A one-form defines a [covector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covector), also called a linear functional, at each point on the manifold. One-forms are generally assumed to vary smoothly from point to point on the manifold, or on a region of the manifold, meaning that they produce smooth functions when paired with smooth vector fields. One-forms generate the [exterior algebra](wikipedia://en/221537) of differential forms, and the differential of a function naturally extends to the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782): the exterior derivative of a one-form is a two-form, of a two-form is a three-form, and so on.

One-forms are widely used in differential geometry. Their duality with vector fields makes them suited to encode algebraic conditions on vector fields, and conditions such as [integrability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_integrability) of systems of vector fields are often simpler to formulate in terms of one-forms, together with the exterior derivative. One forms are also used in differential geometry to encode other tensors, such as the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795); a selected basis of one-forms is called a [coframe](wikipedia://en/3131766), and special coframes are widely used throughout [Riemannian](wikipedia://en/195243) and [pseudo-Riemannian geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Riemannian_geometry).

## Definition and local coordinate description {#Definition_and_local_coordinate_description}

Formally, a one form is a smooth [section](wikipedia://en/367621) of the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600).[^o5] Equivalently, a one-form on a manifold $M$ is a smooth mapping of the [total space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_space) of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of $M$ to $\mathbb{R}$ whose restriction to each fibre is a linear functional on the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807).[^o6] Let $U$ be an open subset of $M$ and $p \in U$. Then

$$\begin{aligned} \omega: U & \rightarrow \bigcup_{p \in U} T^*_p(M) \\ p & \mapsto \omega_p \in T_p^*(M) \end{aligned}$$

defines a one-form $\omega$. $\omega_p$ is a covector.

Often one-forms are described [locally](wikipedia://en/1462457), particularly in [local coordinates](wikipedia://en/304139). In a local coordinate system, a one-form is a linear combination of the [differentials](wikipedia://en/220782) of the coordinates:

$$\alpha_x = f_1(x) \, dx_1 + f_2(x) \, dx_2 + \cdots + f_n(x) \, dx_n ,$$

where the $f_i$ are smooth functions.[^o7] From this perspective, a one-form has a [covariant](wikipedia://en/202886) transformation law on passing from one coordinate system to another. Thus a one-form is an order 1 covariant [tensor field](wikipedia://en/256738).

## Examples {#Examples}

A simple non-trivial differential one-form is the "change in angle" form $d\theta.$ This is defined as the derivative of the angle "function" $\theta(x,y)$ (which is only defined up to an additive constant), which can be explicitly defined in terms of the [atan2](wikipedia://en/3856703) function. Taking the derivative yields the following formula for the [total derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative):

$$\begin{aligned} d\theta &= \partial_x\left(\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\right) dx + \partial_y\left(\operatorname{atan2}(y,x)\right) dy \\ &= -\frac{y}{x^2 + y^2} dx + \frac{x}{x^2 + y^2} dy \end{aligned}$$

While the angle "function" cannot be continuously defined on the whole plane – the function atan2 is discontinuous along the negative $x$-axis – the formula for the derivative smoothly defines a one-form everywhere except at the origin, reflecting the fact that infinitesimal (and indeed local) changes in angle can be defined everywhere except the origin. Integrating this derivative along a path gives the total change in angle over the path, and integrating over a closed loop gives the [winding number](wikipedia://en/86113) times $2 \pi.$

In the language of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), this derivative is a one-form on the [punctured plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punctured_plane). It is [closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_differential_form) (its [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) is zero) but not [exact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exact_differential_form), meaning that it is not the derivative of a 0-form (that is, a function): the angle $\theta$ is not a globally defined smooth function on the entire punctured plane.[^o10] In fact, this form generates the first [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530) of the punctured plane. This is the most basic example of such a form, and it is fundamental in differential geometry.

:::

## Differential of a function {#Differential_of_a_function}

Let $U \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ be [open](wikipedia://en/39358) (for example, an interval $(a, b)$), and consider a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) $f: U \to \mathbb{R},$ with [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) $f'.$ The differential $df$ assigns to each point $x_0\in U$ a linear map from the tangent space $T_{x_0}U$ to the real numbers. In this case, each tangent space is naturally identifiable with the [real number line](wikipedia://en/287188), and the linear map $\mathbb{R}\to\mathbb{R}$ in question is given by scaling by $f'(x_0).$ This is the simplest example of a differential (one-)form.

## Systems of one-forms {#Systems_of_one-forms}

A system of multiple one-forms is often used to express structural information on manifolds. Systems of one-forms are often expressed as one-forms with values in a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). When that vector space has extra structure, such as a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944), that structural information acts as a linear approximation to a corresponding curved or deformed structure on the manifold.

The prototypical example of this application is a [connection one-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connection_one-form). A connection one-form takes values in a Lie algebra corresponding to the infinitesimal, or linearized, structure on the manifold. For example, the [Levi-Civita connection](wikipedia://en/249176) on a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) is a one-form taking values in the orthogonal Lie algebra. Writing down a particular connection one-form on a manifold usually requires a choice of coframe, or gauge. This choice can be eliminated by packaging together all relevant coframes into a [principal bundle](wikipedia://en/310950), in which case the connection one-form becomes a one-form on a higher-dimensional manifold that is independent of gauge choices.
