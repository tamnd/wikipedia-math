# Ehrhart polynomial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ehrhart polynomial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an [integral polytope](wikipedia://en/25293116) has an associated **Ehrhart polynomial** that encodes the relationship between the [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) and the number of [integer points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_point) the polytope contains. The theory of Ehrhart [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) can be seen as a higher-dimensional generalization of [Pick's theorem](wikipedia://en/328252) in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410).

:::

These polynomials are named after [Eugène Ehrhart](wikipedia://en/33824722) who introduced them in the 1960s.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Informally, if $P$ is a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471), and $tP$ is the polytope formed by expanding $P$ by a factor of $t$ in each dimension, then $L(P, t)$ is the number of [integer lattice](wikipedia://en/3333402) points in $tP$.

More formally, consider a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) $\mathcal{L}$ in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ and a $d$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) polytope $P$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the property that all vertices of the polytope are points of the lattice. (A common example is $\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{Z}^n$ and a polytope for which all vertices have [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) coordinates.) For any positive integer $t$, let $tP$ be the $t$-fold dilation of $P$ (the polytope formed by multiplying each vertex coordinate, in a basis for the lattice, by a factor of $t$), and let

$$L(P,t) = \#\left(tP \cap \mathcal{L}\right)$$

be the number of lattice points contained in the polytope $tP$. Ehrhart showed in 1962 that $L$ is a rational [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) of degree $d$ in $t$, i.e. there exist [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) $L_0(P),\dots,L_d(P)$ such that:

$$L(P, t) = L_d(P) t^d + L_{d-1}(P) t^{d-1} + \cdots + L_0(P)$$

for all positive integers $t$.[^o4]

### Reciprocity property {#Reciprocity_property}

The Ehrhart polynomial of the [interior](wikipedia://en/55610) of an integral polytope $P$ can be computed as:

$$L(\operatorname{int}(P), t) = (-1)^d L(P, -t),$$

where $d$ is the dimension of $P$. This result is known as Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity.[^o6][^o7]

:::

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Second dilate of a unit square.png.* This is the second dilate, $t = 2$, of a unit square. It has nine integer points.

Let $P$ be a $d$-dimensional [unit](wikipedia://en/407763) [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) whose vertices are the integer lattice points all of whose coordinates are 0 or 1. In terms of inequalities,

$$P = \left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^d : 0 \le x_i \le 1; 1 \le i \le d\right\}.$$

Then the $t$-fold dilation of $P$ is a cube with side length $t$, containing $(t + 1)^{d}$ integer points. That is, the Ehrhart polynomial of the hypercube is $L(P,t) = (t + 1)^{d}$.[^o10][^o11] Additionally, if we evaluate $L(P, t)$ at negative integers, then

$$L(P, -t) = (-1)^d (t - 1)^d = (-1)^d L(\operatorname{int}(P), t),$$

as we would expect from Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity.

Many other [figurate numbers](wikipedia://en/325260) can be expressed as Ehrhart polynomials. For instance, the [square pyramidal numbers](wikipedia://en/542343) are given by the Ehrhart polynomials of a [square pyramid](wikipedia://en/1128455) with an integer unit square as its base and with height one; the Ehrhart polynomial in this case is $\tfrac{1}{6}(t + 1)(t + 2)(2t + 3)$.[^o12]

:::

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Ehrhart quasi-polynomial"}

## Ehrhart quasi-polynomials {#Ehrhart_quasi-polynomials}

Let $P$ be a rational polytope. In other words, suppose

$$P = \left\{ x\in\mathbb{R}^d : Ax \le b\right\}$$

is bounded, where $A \in \mathbb{Q}^{k \times d}$ and $b \in \mathbb{Q}^k.$ (Equivalently, $P$ is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of finitely many points in $\mathbb{Q}^d.$) Then define

$$L(P, t) = \#\left(\left\{x\in\mathbb{Z}^d : Ax \le tb \right\} \right).$$

In this case, $L(P, t)$ is a [quasi-polynomial](wikipedia://en/10609701) in $t$. Just as with integral polytopes, Ehrhart–Macdonald reciprocity holds, that is (assuming $P$ is $d$-dimensional),

$$L(\operatorname{int}(P), t) = (-1)^d L(P, -t).$$

### Examples of Ehrhart quasi-polynomials {#Examples_of_Ehrhart_quasi-polynomials}

Let $P$ be a polygon with vertices (0,0), (0,2), (1,1) and ($\tfrac{3}{2}$, 0). The number of integer points in $tP$ will be counted by the quasi-polynomial [^o16]

$$L(P, t) = \frac{7t^2}{4} + \frac{5t}{2} + \frac{7 + (-1)^t}{8}.$$

:::

## Interpretation of coefficients {#Interpretation_of_coefficients}

If $P$ is an integral polytope, some of the coefficients of $L(P, t)$ have an easy interpretation:

- the leading coefficient, $L_d(P)$, is equal to the $d$-dimensional [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of $P$, divided by $d(L)$ (see [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) for an explanation of the content or covolume $d(L)$ of a lattice);
- the second coefficient, $L_{d-1}(P)$, can be computed as follows: the lattice $L$ induces a lattice $L_{F}$ on any face $F$ of $P$; take the $(d - 1)$-dimensional volume of $F$, divide by $2d(L_{F})$, and add those numbers for all faces of $P$;
- the constant coefficient, $L_0(P)$, is the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420) of $P$. When $P$ is a closed [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885), $L_0(P)=1.$

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="The Betke–Kneser theorem"}

## The Betke–Kneser theorem {#The_Betke–Kneser_theorem}

Ulrich Betke and [Martin Kneser](wikipedia://en/17620650)[^o20] established the following characterization of the Ehrhart coefficients. A functional $Z$ defined on integral polytopes is an $\operatorname{SL}(n,\mathbb{Z})$ and translation invariant [valuation](wikipedia://en/17457007) if and only if there are real numbers $c_0,\ldots, c_n$ such that

$$Z= c_0 L_0+\cdots +c_n L_n.$$

:::

## Ehrhart series {#Ehrhart_series}

We can define a [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) for the Ehrhart polynomial of an integral $d$-dimensional polytope $P$ as

$$\operatorname{Ehr}_P(z) = \sum_{t\ge 0} L(P, t)z^t.$$

This series can be expressed as a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210). Specifically, Ehrhart proved (1962) that there exist complex numbers $h_j^*$, $0 \le j \le d$, such that the Ehrhart series of $P$ is[^o4]

$$\operatorname{Ehr}_P(z) = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^d h_j^\ast(P) z^j}{(1 - z)^{d + 1}}.$$

[Richard P. Stanley](wikipedia://en/1633397)'s non-negativity theorem states that under the given hypotheses, each $h_j^*$ will be a non-negative integer, for $0 \le j \le d$.

Another result by Stanley shows that if $P$ is a lattice polytope contained in $Q$, then $h_j^*(P) \le h_j^*(Q)$ for all $j$.[^o22] The $h^*$-vector is in general not unimodal, but it is whenever it is symmetric and the polytope has a regular unimodular triangulation.[^o23]

### Ehrhart series for rational polytopes {#Ehrhart_series_for_rational_polytopes}

As in the case of polytopes with integer vertices, one defines the Ehrhart series for a rational polytope. For a *d*-dimensional rational polytope $P$, where $D$ is the smallest integer such that $DP$ is an integer polytope ($D$ is called the denominator of $P$), then one has

$$\operatorname{Ehr}_P(z) = \sum_{t\ge 0} L(P, t)z^t = \frac{\sum_{j=0}^{D(d+1)-1} h_j^\ast(P) z^j}{\left(1 - z^D\right)^{d + 1}},$$

where the $h_j^*$ are still non-negative integers.[^o25][^o26]

## Non-leading coefficient bounds {#Non-leading_coefficient_bounds}

The polynomial's non-leading coefficients $c_0,\dots,c_{d-1}$ in the representation

$$L(P,t) = \sum_{r=0}^d c_r t^r$$

can be upper bounded:[^o28]

$$c_r \leq |s(d,r)| c_d + \frac{1}{(d-1)!} |s(d,r+1)|$$

where $s(n,k)$ is the [Stirling number of the first kind](wikipedia://en/2229296). Lower bounds also exist.[^o29]

## Toric varieties {#Toric_varieties}

The case $n=d=2$ and $t = 1$ of these statements yields [Pick's theorem](wikipedia://en/328252). Formulas for the other coefficients are much harder to get; [Todd classes](wikipedia://en/1568489) of [toric varieties](wikipedia://en/737087), the [Riemann–Roch theorem](wikipedia://en/247261) as well as [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659) have been used for this purpose.

If $X$ is the [toric variety](wikipedia://en/737087) corresponding to the normal fan of $P$, then $P$ defines an [ample line bundle](wikipedia://en/1186896) on $X$, and the Ehrhart polynomial of $P$ coincides with the [Hilbert polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_polynomial) of this line bundle.

Ehrhart polynomials can be studied for their own sake. For instance, one could ask questions related to the roots of an Ehrhart polynomial.[^o31] Furthermore, some authors have pursued the question of how these polynomials could be classified.[^o32]

::: {#o34 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

It is possible to study the number of integer points in a polytope $P$ if we dilate some facets of $P$ but not others. In other words, one would like to know the number of integer points in semi-dilated polytopes. It turns out that such a counting function will be what is called a multivariate quasi-polynomial. An Ehrhart-type reciprocity theorem will also hold for such a counting function.[^o35]

Counting the number of integer points in semi-dilations of polytopes has applications[^o36] in enumerating the number of different dissections of regular polygons and the number of non-isomorphic unrestricted codes, a particular kind of code in the field of [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869).

:::
