# Lower limit topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lower limit topology"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **lower limit topology** or **right half-open interval topology** is a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) defined on $\mathbb{R}$, the set of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers); it is different from the standard topology on $\mathbb{R}$ (generated by the [open intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval)) and has a number of interesting properties. It is the topology generated by the [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29) of all [half-open intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-open_interval) \[*a*,*b*), where *a* and *b* are real numbers.

:::

The resulting [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is called the **Sorgenfrey line** after [Robert Sorgenfrey](wikipedia://en/24960468) or the **arrow** and is sometimes written $\mathbb{R}_l$, $\mathbb{R}_{\text{LL}}$ or $\mathbb{R}_{\text{bad}}$.[^o2] Like the [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) and the [long line](wikipedia://en/338403), the Sorgenfrey line often serves as a useful counterexample to many otherwise plausible-sounding conjectures in [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649). The [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space) of $\mathbb{R}_l$ with itself is also a useful counterexample, known as the [Sorgenfrey plane](wikipedia://en/682853).

In complete analogy, one can also define the **upper limit topology**, or **left half-open interval topology**.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- The lower limit topology is [finer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finer_topology) (has more open sets) than the standard topology on the real numbers (which is generated by the open intervals). The reason is that every open interval can be written as a (countably infinite) union of half-open intervals.
- For any real $a$ and $b$, the interval $[a,b)$ is [clopen](wikipedia://en/233398) in $\mathbb{R}_l$ (i.e., both [open](wikipedia://en/39358) and [closed](wikipedia://en/47279)). Furthermore, for all real $a$, the sets $\{x\in\mathbb{R} : x < a\}$ and $\{x \in\mathbb{R} : x \geq a\}$ are also clopen. This shows that the Sorgenfrey line is [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected).
- Any [compact subset](wikipedia://en/6042) of $\mathbb{R}_l$ must be an at most [countable set](wikipedia://en/6026). To see this, consider a non-empty compact subset $C\subseteq\mathbb{R}_l$. Fix an $x \in C$, consider the following open cover of $C$:

$$\bigl\{ [x, +\infty) \bigr\} \cup \Bigl\{ \bigl(-\infty, x - \tfrac{1}{n} \bigr) \,\Big|\, n \in \mathbb{N} \Bigr\}.$$

Since $C$ is compact, this cover has a finite subcover, and hence there exists a real number $a(x)$ such that the interval $(a(x), x]$ contains no point of $C$ apart from $x$. This is true for all $x\in C$. Now choose a rational number $q(x) \in (a(x), x]\cap\mathbb{Q}$. Since the intervals $(a(x), x]$, parametrized by $x \in C$, are pairwise disjoint, the function $q: C \to \mathbb{Q}$ is injective, and so $C$ is at most countable.

- The name "lower limit topology" comes from the following fact: a sequence (or [net](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Net_%28topology%29)) $(x_\alpha)$ in $\mathbb{R}_l$ converges to the limit $L$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it "approaches $L$ from the right", meaning for every $\epsilon>0$ there exists an index $\alpha_0$ such that $\forall\alpha \geq \alpha_0 : L \leq x_\alpha < L+\epsilon$. The Sorgenfrey line can thus be used to study [right-sided limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-sided_limit): if $f: \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427), then the ordinary right-sided limit of $f$ at $x$ (when the codomain carries the standard topology) is the same as the usual limit of $f$ at $x$ when the domain is equipped with the lower limit topology and the codomain carries the standard topology.
- In terms of [separation axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_axioms), $\mathbb{R}_l$ is a [perfectly normal Hausdorff space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfectly_normal_Hausdorff_space).
- In terms of [countability axioms](wikipedia://en/346611), $\mathbb{R}_l$ is [first-countable](wikipedia://en/904940) and [separable](wikipedia://en/27855), but not [second-countable](wikipedia://en/901459).
- In terms of compactness properties, $\mathbb{R}_l$ is [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480) and [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact), but not [σ-compact](wikipedia://en/346681) nor [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact).
- $\mathbb{R}_l$ is not [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable), since separable metric spaces are second-countable. However, the topology of a Sorgenfrey line is generated by a [quasimetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29#Quasimetrics).
- $\mathbb{R}_l$ is a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550).[^o5]
- $\mathbb{R}_l$ does not have any connected compactifications.[^o6]
