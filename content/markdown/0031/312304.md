# Symplectomorphism

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symplectomorphism"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **symplectomorphism** or **symplectic map** is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [symplectic manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356). In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), a symplectomorphism represents a transformation of [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) that is [volume-preserving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volume-preserving) and preserves the [symplectic structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_structure) of phase space, and is called a [canonical transformation](wikipedia://en/514534).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A [diffeomorphism](wikipedia://en/8564) between two [symplectic manifolds](wikipedia://en/28356) $f: (M,\omega) \rightarrow (N,\omega')$ is called a **symplectomorphism** if

$$f^*\omega'=\omega,$$

where $f^*$ is the [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) of $f$. The symplectic diffeomorphisms from $M$ to $M$ are a (pseudo-)group, called the symplectomorphism group (see below).

The infinitesimal version of symplectomorphisms gives the symplectic vector fields. A vector field $X \in \Gamma^{\infty}(TM)$ is called **symplectic** if

$$\mathcal{L}_X\omega=0.$$

Also, $X$ is symplectic if the flow $\phi_t: M\rightarrow M$ of $X$ is a symplectomorphism for every $t$. These vector fields build a Lie subalgebra of $\Gamma^{\infty}(TM)$. Here, $\Gamma^{\infty}(TM)$ is the set of [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) on $M$, and $\mathcal{L}_X$ is the [Lie derivative](wikipedia://en/294437) along the vector field $X.$

Examples of symplectomorphisms include the [canonical transformations](wikipedia://en/514534) of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) and [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), the flow associated to any Hamiltonian function, the map on [cotangent bundles](wikipedia://en/221600) induced by any diffeomorphism of manifolds, and the coadjoint action of an element of a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) on a [coadjoint orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coadjoint_orbit).

:::

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Hamiltonian isotopy"}

## Flows {#Flows}

Any smooth function on a [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356) gives rise, by definition, to a [Hamiltonian vector field](wikipedia://en/1818270) and the set of all such vector fields form a subalgebra of the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of [symplectic vector fields](wikipedia://en/1818385). The integration of the flow of a symplectic vector field is a symplectomorphism. Since symplectomorphisms preserve the [symplectic 2-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symplectic_2-form) and hence the symplectic volume form, [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312301) in [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319) follows. Symplectomorphisms that arise from Hamiltonian vector fields are known as Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms.

Since $\{H, H\} = X_{H}(H) = 0,$ the flow of a Hamiltonian vector field also preserves $H$. In physics this is interpreted as the law of conservation of [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy).

If the first [Betti number](wikipedia://en/346262) of a connected symplectic manifold is zero, symplectic and Hamiltonian vector fields coincide, so the notions of Hamiltonian isotopy and **symplectic isotopy** of symplectomorphisms coincide.

It can be shown that the equations for a geodesic may be formulated as a Hamiltonian flow, see [Geodesics as Hamiltonian flows](wikipedia://en/6207793).

:::

## The group of (Hamiltonian) symplectomorphisms {#The_group_of_(Hamiltonian)_symplectomorphisms}

The symplectomorphisms from a manifold back onto itself form an infinite-dimensional [pseudogroup](wikipedia://en/1255458). The corresponding [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) consists of symplectic vector fields. The Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms form a subgroup, whose Lie algebra is given by the Hamiltonian vector fields. The latter is isomorphic to the Lie algebra of smooth functions on the manifold with respect to the [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340), modulo the constants.

The group of Hamiltonian symplectomorphisms of $(M,\omega)$ usually denoted as $\operatorname{Ham}(M,\omega)$.

Groups of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms are [simple](wikipedia://en/292831), by a theorem of [Banyaga](wikipedia://en/8994104).[^o8] They have natural geometry given by the [Hofer norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hofer_norm). The [homotopy type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_type) of the symplectomorphism group for certain simple symplectic [four-manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-manifold), such as the product of [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859), can be computed using [Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29)'s theory of [pseudoholomorphic curves](wikipedia://en/2294542).

## Comparison with Riemannian geometry {#Comparison_with_Riemannian_geometry}

Unlike [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652), symplectic manifolds are not very rigid: [Darboux's theorem](wikipedia://en/1543358) shows that all symplectic manifolds of the same dimension are locally isomorphic. In contrast, isometries in Riemannian geometry must preserve the [Riemann curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/130526), which is thus a local invariant of the Riemannian manifold. Moreover, every function *H* on a symplectic manifold defines a [Hamiltonian vector field](wikipedia://en/1818270) *X*<sub>*H*</sub>, which exponentiates to a [one-parameter group](wikipedia://en/754851) of Hamiltonian diffeomorphisms. It follows that the group of symplectomorphisms is always very large, and in particular, infinite-dimensional. On the other hand, the group of [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of a Riemannian manifold is always a (finite-dimensional) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945). Moreover, Riemannian manifolds with large symmetry groups are very special, and a generic Riemannian manifold has no nontrivial symmetries.

## Quantizations {#Quantizations}

Representations of finite-dimensional subgroups of the group of symplectomorphisms (after ħ-deformations, in general) on [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) are called *quantizations*. When the Lie group is the one defined by a Hamiltonian, it is called a "quantization by energy". The corresponding operator from the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) to the Lie algebra of continuous linear operators is also sometimes called the *quantization*; this is a more common way of looking at it in physics.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Arnold conjecture"}

## Arnold conjecture {#Arnold_conjecture}

A celebrated conjecture of [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490) relates the *minimum* number of [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) for a Hamiltonian symplectomorphism $\varphi: M \to M$, in case $M$ is a compact [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356), to [Morse theory](wikipedia://en/348560) (see [^o13]). More precisely, the conjecture states that $\varphi$ has at least as many fixed points as the number of [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) that a smooth function on $M$ must have. A certain weaker version of this conjecture has been proved: when $\varphi$ is "nondegenerate", the number of fixed points is bounded from below by the sum of [Betti numbers](wikipedia://en/346262) of $M$ (see,[^o14][^o15]). The most important development in symplectic geometry triggered by this famous conjecture is the birth of [Floer homology](wikipedia://en/2672856) (see [^o16]), named after [Andreas Floer](wikipedia://en/2280157).

:::
