# Law of total probability

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Law of total probability"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the **law** (or **formula**) **of total probability** is a fundamental rule relating [marginal probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_probability) to [conditional probabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_probabilities). It expresses the total probability of an outcome which can be realized via several distinct [events](wikipedia://en/22961), hence the name.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The law of total probability is[^o4] a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) that states, in its discrete case, if $\left\{{B_n : n = 1, 2, 3, \ldots}\right\}$ is a finite or [countably infinite](wikipedia://en/6026) set of [mutually exclusive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutually_exclusive) and [collectively exhaustive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectively_exhaustive) events, then for any event $A$

$$P(A)=\sum_n P(A\cap B_n)$$

or, alternatively,[^o4]

$$P(A)=\sum_n P(A\mid B_n)P(B_n),$$

where, for any $n$, if $P(B_n) = 0$, then these terms are simply omitted from the summation since $P(A\mid B_n)$ is finite.

The summation can be interpreted as a [weighted average](wikipedia://en/189822), and consequently the marginal probability, $P(A)$, is sometimes called "average probability";[^o5] "overall probability" is sometimes used in less formal writings.[^o6]

The law of total probability can also be stated for conditional probabilities:

$$\begin{aligned} P( {A \mid C} ) &= \frac{{P( {A,C} )}}{{P( C )}} = \frac{{\sum\limits_n {P( {A,{B_n},C} )} }}{{P( C )}} \\[2ex] &= \frac{{\sum\limits_n P ( {A\mid {B_n}, C} )P( {{B_n}\mid C} )P( C )}}{{P( C )}} \\[2ex] &= \sum\limits_n P ( {A\mid {B_n},C} )P( {{B_n} \mid C} ) \end{aligned}$$

Taking the $B_n$ as above, and assuming $C$ is an event [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) of any of the $B_n$:

$$P(A \mid C) = \sum_n P(A \mid C,B_n) P(B_n)$$

:::

## Continuous case {#Continuous_case}

The law of total probability extends to the case of conditioning on events generated by continuous random variables. Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325). Suppose $X$ is a random variable with distribution function $F_X$, and $A$ an event on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$. Then the law of total probability states

$$P(A) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty P(A |X = x) d F_X(x).$$

If $X$ admits a density function $f_X$, then the result is

$$P(A) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty P(A |X = x) f_X(x) dx.$$

Moreover, for the specific case where $A = \{Y \in B \}$, where $B$ is a [Borel set](wikipedia://en/43327), then this yields

$$P(Y \in B) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty P(Y \in B |X = x) f_X(x) dx.$$

::: {#o9 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Suppose that two factories supply [light bulbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_bulb) to the market. Factory *X*'s bulbs work for over 5000 hours in 99% of cases, whereas factory *Y*'s bulbs work for over 5000 hours in 95% of cases. It is known that factory *X* supplies 60% of the total bulbs available and Y supplies 40% of the total bulbs available. What is the chance that a purchased bulb will work for longer than 5000 hours?

Applying the law of total probability, we have:

$$\begin{aligned} P(A) & = P(A\mid B_X) \cdot P(B_X) + P(A\mid B_Y) \cdot P(B_Y) \\[4pt] & = {99 \over 100} \cdot {6 \over 10} + {95 \over 100} \cdot {4 \over 10} = {{594 + 380} \over 1000} = {974 \over 1000} \end{aligned}$$

where

- $P(B_X)={6 \over 10}$ is the probability that the purchased bulb was manufactured by factory *X*;
- $P(B_Y)={4 \over 10}$ is the probability that the purchased bulb was manufactured by factory *Y*;
- $P(A\mid B_X)={99 \over 100}$ is the probability that a bulb manufactured by *X* will work for over 5000 hours;
- $P(A\mid B_Y)={95 \over 100}$ is the probability that a bulb manufactured by *Y* will work for over 5000 hours.

Thus each purchased light bulb has a 97.4% chance to work for more than 5000 hours.

:::

## Other names {#Other_names}

The term ***law of total probability*** is sometimes taken to mean the **law of alternatives**, which is a special case of the law of total probability applying to [discrete random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_random_variable). One author uses the terminology of the "Rule of Average Conditional Probabilities",[^o11] while another refers to it as the "continuous law of alternatives" in the continuous case.[^o12] This result is given by Grimmett and Welsh[^o13] as the **partition theorem**, a name that they also give to the related [law of total expectation](wikipedia://en/312397).
