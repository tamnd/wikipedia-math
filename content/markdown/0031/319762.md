# Logos

*Image: Logos.svg.* [Greek spelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) of *logos*

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Logos"}

***Logos*** (, ; λόγος) is a term used in [Western philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy), [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) and [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447), as well as [religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religion) (notably [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_%28Christianity%29)), that most broadly means reason, logic, order, or understanding. Among its connotations is that of a [rational](wikipedia://en/61032) form of discourse that relies on [inductive](wikipedia://en/393736) and [deductive](wikipedia://en/61093) reasoning.

:::

[Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) first systematized the usage of the word, making it one of the [three principles of rhetoric](wikipedia://en/3085836) alongside [ethos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethos) and [pathos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathos). This original use identifies the word closely to the structure and content of [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language) or [text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_%28literary_theory%29). Both [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) and [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) used the term *logos* (along with *[rhema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhema)*) to refer to [sentences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sentence_%28language%29) and [propositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proposition_%28philosophy%29).

## Background {#Background}

λόγος is related to λέγω which is cognate with lex. The word derives from a Proto-Indo-European root, \*leǵ-, which can have the meanings "I put in order, arrange, gather, choose, count, reckon, discern, say, speak". In modern usage, it typically connotes the verbs "account", "measure", "reason" or "discourse".[^o3][^o4] It is occasionally used in other contexts, such as for "ratio" in mathematics.[^o5]

## Origins of the term {#Origins_of_the_term}

*Logos* became a technical term in [Western philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_philosophy) beginning with [Heraclitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus) (c. 535), who used the term for a principle of order and knowledge.[^o7] [Ancient Greek philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy) used the term in different ways. The [sophists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophist) used the term to mean "[discourse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse)". [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) applied the term to refer to "reasoned discourse"[^o26] or "the argument" in the field of rhetoric, and considered it one of the three [modes of persuasion](wikipedia://en/3085836) alongside *[ethos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethos)* and *[pathos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathos)*.[^o8] [Pyrrhonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyrrhonism) philosophers used the term to refer to [dogmatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogma) accounts of non-evident matters. The [Stoics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoics) spoke of the *[logos spermatikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationes_seminales)* (the generative principle of the Universe) which foreshadows related concepts in [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism).[^o9]

Within [Hellenistic Judaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenistic_Judaism), [Philo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo) (c. 20 BC) integrated the term into [Jewish philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_philosophy).[^o10] Philo distinguished between *logos prophorikos* ("the utterer word or speaker"), logos spermatikos ("the speech") and the *logos endiathetos* ("the word remaining within").[^o11]

The [Gospel of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_John) identifies the [Christian Logos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_%28Christianity%29), through which all things are made, as divine ([*theos*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God_in_Christianity)),[^o12] and further identifies [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_Christ) as the *[incarnate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incarnation_%28Christianity%29) Logos*. Early translators of the Greek [New Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Testament), such as [Jerome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jerome) (in the 4th century AD), experienced frustration with the inadequacy of any single Latin word to convey the meaning of the word *logos* as used to describe Jesus Christ in the [Gospel of John](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gospel_of_John). The [Vulgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vulgate) Bible usage of in principio erat verbum was thus constrained to use the (perhaps inadequate) noun verbum for "word"; later [Romance language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romance_language) translations had the advantage of nouns such as le Verbe in French. Reformation translators took another approach. [Martin Luther](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Luther) rejected Zeitwort (verb) in favor of Wort (word), for instance, although later commentators repeatedly turned to a more dynamic use involving *the living word* as used by Jerome and [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo).[^o13] The term is also used in [Sufism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism), and the analytical psychology of [Carl Jung](wikipedia://en/42031).

Despite the conventional translation as "word", *logos* is not used for a [word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word) in the grammatical sense—for that, the term *lexis* (λέξις, grc) was used.[^o14] However, both *logos* and *lexis* derive from the same verb grc (λέγω), meaning "(I) count, tell, say, speak".[^o3][^o14][^o15]

In the ancient Greek context, the term *logos* in the sense of "word" or "discourse" also contrasted with *mythos* (μῦθος). Classical Greek usage sees reasoned argument (*logos*) as distinct from imaginative tales (*mythos*).[^o16]

## Ancient Greek philosophy {#Ancient_Greek_philosophy}

The writing of [Heraclitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus) (c. 535) was the first place where the word *logos* was given special attention in [ancient Greek philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_philosophy),[^o18] although Heraclitus seems to use the word with a meaning not significantly different from the way in which it was used in ordinary Greek of his time.[^o19] For Heraclitus, *logos* provided the link between rational discourse and the world's rational structure.[^o20]

What *logos* means here is not certain; it may mean "reason" or "explanation" in the sense of an objective cosmic law, or it may signify nothing more than "saying" or "wisdom".[^o22] Yet, an independent existence of a universal *logos* was clearly suggested by Heraclitus.[^o23]

*Image: Aristotle Altemps Inv8575.jpg.* Aristotle, 384–322 BC

Following one of the other meanings of the word, [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) gave *logos* a different technical definition in the *[Rhetoric](wikipedia://en/1692816)*, using it as meaning argument from reason, one of the three [modes of persuasion](wikipedia://en/3085836). The other two modes are *[pathos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pathos)* (πᾰ́θος, grc), which refers to persuasion by means of emotional appeal, "putting the hearer into a certain frame of mind";[^o24] and *[ethos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethos)* (ἦθος, grc), persuasion through convincing listeners of one's "moral character".[^o24] According to Aristotle, *logos* relates to "the speech itself, in so far as it proves or seems to prove".[^o24][^o25] In the words of Paul Rahe:

*Logos*, *pathos*, and *ethos* can all be appropriate at different times.[^o27] Arguments from reason (logical arguments) have some advantages, namely that [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data) are (ostensibly) difficult to manipulate, so it is harder to argue against such an argument. On the other hand, trust in the speaker—built through *ethos*—enhances the appeal of arguments from reason.

[Robert Wardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Wardy) suggests that what Aristotle rejects in supporting the use of *logos* "is not emotional appeal [per se](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_se_%28Latin%29), but rather emotional appeals that have no 'bearing on the issue', in that the *pathē* \[πᾰ́θη, grc\] they stimulate lack, or at any rate are not shown to possess, any intrinsic connection with the point at issue—as if an advocate were to try to whip an [antisemitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisemitic) audience into a fury because the accused is Jewish; or as if another in drumming up support for a politician were to exploit his listeners's reverential feelings for the politician's ancestors".[^o28]

[Stoic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoic_philosophy) began with [Zeno of Citium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zeno_of_Citium) c. 300 BC, in which the *logos* was the active [reason](wikipedia://en/42446) pervading and animating the [Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe). It was conceived as material and is usually identified with [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God) or [Nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature). The Stoics also referred to the *seminal logos* ("*[logos spermatikos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logos_spermatikos)*"), or the law of generation in the Universe, which was the principle of the active reason working in inanimate [matter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matter). Humans, too, each possess a portion of the divine *logos*.[^o30]

The Stoics took all activity to imply a *logos* or spiritual principle. As the operative principle of the world, the *logos* was *[anima mundi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anima_mundi)* to them, a concept which later influenced [Philo of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo_of_Alexandria), although he derived the contents of the term from Plato.[^o31] In his Introduction to the 1964 edition of [Marcus Aurelius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Aurelius)' *[Meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditations)*, the Anglican priest Maxwell Staniforth wrote that "*Logos* ... had long been one of the leading terms of [Stoicism](wikipedia://en/19224834), chosen originally for the purpose of explaining how deity came into relation with the universe".[^o32]

Public discourse on ancient Greek rhetoric has historically emphasized Aristotle's appeals to *logos*, *pathos*, and *ethos*, while less attention has been directed to [Isocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isocrates)' teachings about philosophy and *logos*,[^o33] and their partnership in generating an ethical, mindful *[polis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polis)*. Isocrates does not provide a single definition of *logos* in his work, but Isocratean *logos* characteristically focuses on speech, reason, and civic discourse.[^o33] He was concerned with establishing the "common good" of [Athenian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Athens) citizens, which he believed could be achieved through the pursuit of philosophy and the application of *logos*.[^o33]

## Neoplatonism {#Neoplatonism}

*Image: Roman sarcophagus of a reader identified to Plotinus and disciples.jpg.* [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) with his disciples

[Neoplatonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism) philosophers such as [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) (c. 204/5 – 270 AD) used *logos* in ways that drew on Plato and the [Stoics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoics),[^o35] but the term *logos* was interpreted in different ways throughout Neoplatonism, and similarities to Philo's concept of *logos* appear to be accidental.[^o36] The *logos* was a key element in the [meditations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meditation) of Plotinus[^o37] regarded as the first neoplatonist. Plotinus referred back to [Heraclitus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraclitus) and as far back as [Thales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thales)[^o38] in interpreting *logos* as the principle of meditation, existing as the interrelationship between the [hypostases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypostasis_%28philosophy_and_religion%29)—the [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul), the intellect (*[nous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous)*), and the [One](wikipedia://en/9273782).[^o39]

Plotinus used a trinity concept that consisted of "The One", the "Spirit", and "Soul". The comparison with the Christian [Trinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity) is inescapable, but for Plotinus these were not equal and "The One" was at the highest level, with the "Soul" at the lowest.[^o40] For Plotinus, the relationship between the three elements of his trinity is conducted by the outpouring of *logos* from the higher principle, and *[eros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eros_%28concept%29)* (loving) upward from the lower principle.[^o41] Plotinus relied heavily on the concept of *logos*, but no explicit references to Christian thought can be found in his works, although there are significant traces of them in his doctrine. Plotinus specifically avoided using the term *logos* to refer to the second person of his trinity.[^o42] However, Plotinus influenced [Gaius Marius Victorinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaius_Marius_Victorinus), who then influenced [Augustine of Hippo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo).[^o43] Centuries later, [Carl Jung](wikipedia://en/42031) acknowledged the influence of Plotinus in his use of the term.[^o44]

Victorinus differentiated between the *logos* interior to God and the *logos* related to the world by creation and [salvation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvation).[^o45]

Augustine of Hippo, often seen as the father of [medieval philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_philosophy), was also greatly influenced by Plato and is famous for his re-interpretation of Aristotle and Plato in the light of [early Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Christian) thought.[^o46] A young [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine) experimented with, but failed to achieve [ecstasy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecstasy_%28philosophy%29) using the meditations of Plotinus.[^o47] In his *[Confessions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confessions_%28St._Augustine%29)*, Augustine described *logos* as the *Divine Eternal Word*,[^o48] by which he, in part, was able to motivate the early Christian thought throughout the [Hellenized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenized) world (of which the Latin speaking West was a part)[^o49] Augustine's *logos* *had taken body* in Christ, the man in whom the *logos* (i.e. veritas or sapientia) was present as in no other man.[^o50]

## Influence in religion {#Influence_in_religion}

### Hellenistic Judaism {#Hellenistic_Judaism}

#### Philo of Alexandria {#Philo_of_Alexandria}

[Philo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo) (c. 20 BC), a [Hellenized Jew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hellenized_Jew), used the term *logos* to mean an intermediary divine being or [demiurge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demiurge).[^o10] Philo followed the Platonic distinction between imperfect matter and perfect Form, and therefore intermediary beings were necessary to bridge the enormous gap between God and the material world.[^o54] The *logos* was the highest of these intermediary beings, and was called by Philo "the first-born of God".[^o54] Philo also wrote that "the Logos of the living God is the bond of everything, holding all things together and binding all the parts, and prevents them from being dissolved and separated".[^o55]

[Plato's Theory of Forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plato's_Theory_of_Forms) was located within the *logos*, but the *logos* also acted on behalf of God in the physical world.[^o54] In particular, the [Angel of the Lord](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_of_the_Lord) in the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible) ([Old Testament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_Testament)) was identified with the *logos* by Philo, who also said that the *logos* was God's instrument in the creation of the Universe.[^o54]

#### Targums {#Targums}

The concept of *logos* also appears in the [Targums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Targum) (Aramaic translations of the Hebrew Bible dating to the first centuries AD), where the term *memra* ([Aramaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aramaic) for "word") is often used instead of 'the Lord', especially when referring to a manifestation of God that could be construed as [anthropomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropomorphic).[^o57]

### Christianity {#Christianity}

In [Christology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christology), the Logos (Λόγος)[^o4] is a name or title of [Jesus Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus_in_Christianity), seen as the preeminent expression in fulness of all the attributes, the complete thought, and the entire "knowable" reality of the infinite and spiritually transcendent Godhead. This concept is applied to [John 1:1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_1:1) in the [Douay–Rheims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Douay–Rheims) (1582), [King James](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_James_Version) (1604), as well as the [New International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_International_Version) and other versions of the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible), where "logos" is capitalized in translation as "Word"; thereby rendering the verse as:

### Gnosticism {#Gnosticism}

According to the [Gnostic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnosticism) scriptures recorded in the [Holy Book of the Great Invisible Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Book_of_the_Great_Invisible_Spirit), the Logos is an emanation of the [great spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monad_%28Gnosticism%29) that is merged with the [spiritual Adam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Kadmon#Gnosticism) called Adamas.[^o63]

### Islam {#Islam}

The concept of the *logos* also exists in [Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islam), where it was definitively articulated primarily in the writings of the classical [Sunni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni) [mystics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufism) and [Islamic philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy), as well as by certain [Shi'a](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shi'a) thinkers, during the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age).[^o65][^o66] In [Sunni Islam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunni_Islam), the concept of the *logos* has been given many different names by the denomination's [metaphysicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysician), mystics, and philosophers, including *ʿaql* ("Intellect"), *al-insān al-kāmil* ("Universal Man"), *kalimat Allāh* ("Word of God"), *haqīqa muḥammadiyya* ("The Muhammadan Reality"), and *nūr muḥammadī* ("The Muhammadan Light").

#### *ʿAql* {#ʿAql}

One of the names given to a concept very much like the Christian Logos by the classical Muslim metaphysicians is *ʿaql*, which is the "Arabic equivalent to the Greek [νοῦς](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nous) (intellect)."[^o66] In the writings of the Islamic [neoplatonist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonist) philosophers, such as [al-Farabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Farabi) (c. 872) and [Avicenna](wikipedia://en/1130) (d. 1037),[^o66] the idea of the *ʿaql* was presented in a manner that both resembled "the late Greek doctrine" and, likewise, "corresponded in many respects to the Logos Christology."[^o66]

The concept of *logos* in Sufism is used to relate the "Uncreated" (God) to the "Created" (humanity). In Sufism, for the Deist, no contact between man and God can be possible without the *logos*. The *logos* is everywhere and always the same, but its personification is "unique" within each epoch. [Jesus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesus) and [Muhammad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad) are seen as the personifications of the *logos*, and this is what enables them to speak in such absolute terms.[^o68][^o69]

One of the boldest and most radical attempts to reformulate the neoplatonic concepts into Sufism arose with the philosopher [Ibn Arabi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Arabi), who traveled widely in Spain and North Africa. His concepts were expressed in two major works *The Ringstones of Wisdom* (*Fusus al-Hikam*) and *The Meccan Illuminations* (*Al-Futūḥāt al-Makkiyya*). To Ibn Arabi, every prophet corresponds to a reality which he called a *logos* (*[Kalimah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kalimah)*), as an aspect of the unique divine being. In his view the divine being would have for ever remained hidden, had it not been for the prophets, with *logos* providing the link between man and divinity.[^o70]

Ibn Arabi seems to have adopted his version of the *logos* concept from neoplatonic and Christian sources,[^o71] although (writing in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_language) rather than Greek) he used more than twenty different terms when discussing it. For Ibn Arabi, the *logos* or "Universal Man" was a mediating link between individual human beings and the divine essence.[^o72]

Other Sufi writers also show the influence of the neoplatonic *logos*.[^o73] In the 15th century [Abd al-Karīm al-Jīlī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abd_al-Karīm_al-Jīlī) introduced the *Doctrine of Logos and the Perfect Man*. For al-Jīlī, the "perfect man" (associated with the *logos* or the [Prophet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mohammed)) has the power to assume different forms at different times and to appear in different guises.[^o74]

In [Ottoman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ottoman_Empire) Sufism, Şeyh Gâlib (d. 1799) articulates Sühan (*logos*-*Kalima*) in his *Hüsn ü Aşk* (*Beauty and Love*) in parallel to Ibn Arabi's Kalima. In the romance, *Sühan* appears as an embodiment of Kalima as a reference to the Word of God, the Perfect Man, and the Reality of Muhammad.[^o75]

## Rhetoric {#Rhetoric}

Author and professor Jeanne Fahnestock describes *logos* as a "premise". She states that, to find the reason behind a [rhetor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetor)'s backing of a certain position or stance, one must acknowledge the different "premises" that the rhetor applies via their chosen diction.[^o81] The rhetor's success, she argues, will come down to "certain objects of agreement...between arguer and audience".

## Rhema {#Rhema}

The word *logos* has been used in different senses along with *[rhema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhema)*. Both [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) and [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) used the term *logos* along with *rhema* to refer to sentences and propositions.[^o83][^o84]

The [Septuagint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Septuagint) translation of the [Hebrew Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_Bible) into Greek uses the terms *rhema* and *logos* as equivalents and uses both for the [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language) word *[dabar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dabar_%28Hebrew_word%29)*, as the Word of God.[^o85][^o86][^o87]

Some modern usage in [Christian theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_theology) distinguishes *rhema* from *logos* (which here refers to the written scriptures) while *rhema* refers to the revelation received by the reader from the [Holy Spirit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_Spirit_in_Christianity) when the Word (*logos*) is read,[^o88][^o89][^o90][^o91] although this distinction has been criticized.[^o92][^o93]
