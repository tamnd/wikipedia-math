# Paul Halmos

Halmos Pál

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Paul Halmos"}

**Paul Richard Halmos** (Halmos Pál ; 3 March 1916 – 2 October 2006) was a [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary)-born American mathematician and probabilist who made fundamental advances in the areas of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675), [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986), and [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) (in particular, [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932)). He was also recognized as a great mathematical expositor. He has been described as one of [The Martians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Martians_%28scientists%29).[^o3]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Born in the [Kingdom of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary) into a [Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Jews_in_Hungary) family, Halmos immigrated to the United States at age 13. He attended Robert A. Waller High School in Lincoln Park, Chicago, where he enrolled in 1929 and graduated in 1931. He obtained his B.A. from the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois_at_Urbana-Champaign), majoring in mathematics while also fulfilling the requirements for a degree in philosophy. He obtained the degree after only three years, and was 19 years old when he graduated. He then began a Ph.D. in philosophy, still at the [Champaign–Urbana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Champaign–Urbana) campus. However, after failing his masters' oral exams,[^o5] he shifted to mathematics and graduated in 1938. [Joseph L. Doob](wikipedia://en/1547599) supervised his dissertation, titled *Invariants of Certain Stochastic Transformations: The Mathematical Theory of Gambling Systems*.[^o6]

## Career {#Career}

Shortly after his graduation, Halmos left for the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179), lacking both job and grant money. Six months later, he was working under [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), which proved a decisive experience. While at the Institute, Halmos wrote his first book, *Finite Dimensional Vector Spaces*, which immediately established his reputation as a fine expositor of mathematics.[^o8]

From 1967 to 1968 he was the [Donegall Lecturer in Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donegall_Lecturer_in_Mathematics) at [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin).

Halmos taught at [Syracuse University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syracuse_University), the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago) (1946–60), the [University of Michigan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Michigan) (~1961–67), the [University of Hawaiʻi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Hawaiʻi) (1967–68), [Indiana University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiana_University) (1969–85), and the [University of California at Santa Barbara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California_at_Santa_Barbara) (1976–78). From his 1985 retirement from Indiana until his death, he was affiliated with the Mathematics department at [Santa Clara University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Santa_Clara_University) (1985–2006).

## Accomplishments {#Accomplishments}

In a series of papers reprinted in his 1962 *Algebraic Logic*, Halmos devised [polyadic algebras](wikipedia://en/22737733), an algebraic version of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) differing from the better known [cylindric algebras](wikipedia://en/7852887) of [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) and his students. An elementary version of polyadic algebra is described in [monadic Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/1018197).

In addition to his original contributions to mathematics, Halmos was an unusually clear and engaging expositor of university mathematics. He won the [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford_Award) in 1971[^o10] and again in 1977 (shared with W. P. Ziemer, W. H. Wheeler, S. H. Moolgavkar, J. H. Ewing and W. H. Gustafson).[^o11] Halmos chaired the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) committee that wrote the AMS style guide for academic mathematics, published in 1973. In 1983, he received the AMS's [Leroy P. Steele Prize](wikipedia://en/1252550) for exposition.

In an article in the *[American Scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Scientist)*, Halmos argued that mathematics is a creative art, and that mathematicians should be seen as artists, not number crunchers.[^o12] He discussed the division of the field into mathology and mathophysics, further arguing that mathematicians and painters think and work in related ways.

Halmos's 1985 "automathography" *I Want to Be a Mathematician* is an account of what it was like to be an academic mathematician in 20th century America. He called the book "automathography" rather than "autobiography", because its focus is almost entirely on his life as a mathematician, not his personal life. The book contains the following quote on Halmos' view of what doing mathematics means:

Don't just read it; fight it! Ask your own questions, look for your own examples, discover your own proofs. Is the hypothesis necessary? Is the converse true? What happens in the classical special case? What about the degenerate cases? Where does the proof use the hypothesis?[^o13]

What does it take to be \[a mathematician\]? I think I know the answer: you have to be born right, you must continually strive to become perfect, you must love mathematics more than anything else, you must work at it hard and without stop, and you must never give up.

In these memoirs, Halmos claims to have invented the "iff" notation for the words "[if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922)" and to have been the first to use the ["tombstone"](wikipedia://en/819251) notation to signify the [end of a proof](wikipedia://en/25228),[^o15] and this is generally agreed to be the case. The tombstone symbol ∎ ([Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) U+220E) is sometimes called a *halmos*.[^o16]

In 1994, Halmos received the [Deborah and Franklin Haimo Award for Distinguished College or University Teaching of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deborah_and_Franklin_Haimo_Awards_for_Distinguished_College_or_University_Teaching_of_Mathematics).[^o17]

In 2005, Halmos and his wife [Virginia Halmos](wikipedia://en/78544295) funded the [Euler Book Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler_Book_Prize), an annual award given by the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) for a book that is likely to improve the view of mathematics among the public. The first prize was given in 2007, the 300th anniversary of [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902)'s birth, to [John Derbyshire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Derbyshire) for his book about [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) and the [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125): [Prime Obsession](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Obsession).[^o18]

In 2009 [George Csicsery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Csicsery) featured Halmos in a documentary film also called *I Want to Be a Mathematician*.[^o19]
