# Principal bundle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Principal bundle"}

In the [mathematical area](wikipedia://en/18831) of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a **principal bundle**[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5] is a mathematical object that formalizes some of the essential features of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $X \times G$ of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ with a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$, but without requiring a product structure. In the same way as with the Cartesian product, a principal bundle $P$ is equipped with

:::

1. An [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of $G$ on $P$, analogous to $(x, g)h = (x, gh)$ for a [product space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space), where $(x, g)$ is an element of $P$ and $h$ is the group element from $G$ (the group action is conventionally a right action).
1. A projection onto $X$. For a product space, this is just the projection onto the first factor, $(x,g) \mapsto x$.

Unless it is the product space $X \times G$, a principal bundle lacks a preferred choice of identity cross-section; it has no preferred analog of $x \mapsto (x,e)$. Likewise, there is not generally a projection onto $G$ generalizing the projection onto the second factor, $X \times G \to G$ that exists for the Cartesian product. It may also have a complicated [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) that prevents it from being realized as a product space.

An example of a principal bundle is the bundle $\pi:\mathbb R \to S^1 \subseteq \mathbb C$ where $\pi$ is defined by $\pi(t) = \exp(2\pi it)$, in which the fibers are $\mathbb Z$-torsors. Since this bundle looks like a helix, there is no canonical choice of identity cross-section, which should look like a circle.

Another example of a principal bundle is the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) $F(E)$ of a [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) $E$, which consists of all ordered [bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_of_a_vector_space) of the vector space attached to each point. The group $G,$ in this case, is the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564), which acts on the right [in the usual way](wikipedia://en/366550#Definition_and_construction): by [changes of basis](wikipedia://en/1179451). Since there is no natural way to choose an ordered basis of a vector space, a frame bundle lacks a canonical choice of identity cross-section.

Principal bundles have important applications in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and mathematical [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/64324611). They have also found application in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) where they form part of the foundational framework of physical [gauge theories](wikipedia://en/23912155). Important cases are [principal U(1)-bundles](wikipedia://en/80364893) and [principal SU(2)-bundles](wikipedia://en/80364907).

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

A principal $G$-bundle, where $G$ denotes any [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315), is a [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526) $\pi:P \to X$ together with a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29) [right action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) $P \times G \to P$ such that $G$ preserves the fibers of $P$ (i.e. if $y \in P_x$ then $yg \in P_x$ for all $g \in G$) and acts [freely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_action) and [transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transitive_action) (meaning each fiber is a [G-torsor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsor)) on them in such a way that for each $x \in X$ and $y \in P_x$, the map $G \to P_x$ sending $g$ to $yg$ is a homeomorphism. In particular each fiber of the bundle is homeomorphic to the group $G$ itself. Frequently, one requires the base space $X$ to be [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) and possibly [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact).

Conventionally, the group action acts on the right. This convention is chosen in order to be compatible with another convention: a [transition map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_map) acts on the left.

Since the group action preserves the fibers of $\pi:P \to X$ and acts transitively, it follows that the [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of the $G$-action are precisely these fibers and the orbit space $P/G$ is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to the base space $X$. Because the action is free and transitive, the fibers have the structure of G-torsors. A $G$-torsor is a space that is homeomorphic to $G$ but lacks a group structure since there is no preferred choice of an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962).

An equivalent definition of a principal $G$-bundle is as a $G$-bundle $\pi:P \to X$ with fiber $G$ where the structure group acts on the fiber by left multiplication. Since right multiplication by $G$ on the fiber commutes with the action of the structure group, there exists an invariant notion of right multiplication by $G$ on $P$. The fibers of $\pi$ then become right $G$-torsors for this action.

The definitions above are for arbitrary topological spaces. One can also define principal $G$-bundles in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold). Here $\pi:P \to X$ is required to be a [smooth map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map) between smooth manifolds, $G$ is required to be a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), and the corresponding action on $P$ should be smooth.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Trivial bundle and sections {#Trivial_bundle_and_sections}

Over an open ball $U \subset \mathbb{R}^n$, or $\mathbb{R}^n$, with induced coordinates $x_1,\ldots,x_n$, any principal $G$-bundle is isomorphic to a trivial bundle

> $$\pi:U\times G \to U$$

and a smooth section $s \in \Gamma(\pi)$ is equivalently given by a (smooth) function $\hat{s}: U \to G$ since

> $$s(u) = (u,\hat{s}(u)) \in U\times G$$

for some smooth function. For example, if $G=U(2)$, the Lie group of $2\times 2$ [unitary matrices](wikipedia://en/129222), then a section can be constructed by considering four real-valued functions

> $$\phi(x),\psi(x),\Delta(x),\theta(x) : U \to \mathbb{R}$$

and applying them to the parameterization

$$\hat{s}(x) = e^{i\phi(x)}\begin{bmatrix} e^{i\psi(x)} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-i\psi(x)} \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta(x) & \sin \theta(x) \\ -\sin \theta(x) & \cos \theta(x) \\ \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} e^{i\Delta(x)} & 0 \\ 0 & e^{-i\Delta(x)} \end{bmatrix}.$$

This same procedure valids by taking a parameterization of a collection of matrices defining a Lie group $G$ and by considering the set of functions from a patch of the base space $U\subset X$ to $\mathbb{R}$ and inserting them into the parameterization.

### Other examples {#Other_examples}

- The prototypical example of a smooth principal bundle is the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) of a smooth manifold $M$, often denoted $FM$ or $GL(M)$. Here the fiber over a point $x \in M$ is the set of all frames (i.e. ordered bases) for the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) $T_xM$. The [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) $GL(n,\mathbb{R})$ acts freely and transitively on these frames. These fibers can be glued together in a natural way so as to obtain a principal $GL(n,\mathbb{R})$-bundle over $M$.
- Variations on the above example include the [orthonormal frame bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal_frame_bundle) of a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652). Here the frames are required to be [orthonormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal) with respect to the [metric](wikipedia://en/195795). The structure group is the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $O(n)$. The example also works for bundles other than the tangent bundle; if $E$ is any vector bundle of rank $k$ over $M$, then the bundle of frames of $E$ is a principal $GL(k,\mathbb{R})$-bundle, sometimes denoted $F(E)$.
- A normal (regular) [covering space](wikipedia://en/250323) $p:C \to X$ is a principal bundle where the structure group

$$G = \pi_1(X)/p_{*}(\pi_1(C))$$

acts on the fibres of $p$ via the [monodromy action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monodromy_action). Intuitively, *G* is the group of loops in base space that are unliftable. In particular, the [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) of $X$ is a principal bundle over $X$ with structure group $\pi_1(X)$ (since the universal cover is simply connected and thus $\pi_1(C)$ is trivial).

- Let $G$ be a Lie group and let $H$ be a closed subgroup (not necessarily [normal](wikipedia://en/21918)). Then $G$ is a principal $H$-bundle over the (left) [coset space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coset_space) $G/H$. Here the action of $H$ on $G$ is just right multiplication. The fibers are the left cosets of $H$ (in this case there is a distinguished fiber, the one containing the identity, which is naturally isomorphic to $H$).
- *Image: Z2 principal bundle over circle.png.* Non-trivial **Z**/2**Z** principal bundle over the circle. There is no well-defined way to identify which point corresponds to *\+1* or *\-1* in each fibre. This bundle is non-trivial as there is no globally defined section of the projection *π*. There are two possible principal $\mathbb{Z}_2$-bundles over $S^1$. One is the trivial bundle. The other is the [associated bundle](wikipedia://en/398874) of the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817), formed by the projection $\pi:S^1 \to S^1$ given by $z \mapsto z^2$.
- [Projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135) provide some more interesting examples of principal bundles. Recall that the $n$-[sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) $S^n$ is a two-fold covering space of [real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694) $\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^n$. The natural action of $O(1) \simeq \mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$ on $S^n$ gives it the structure of a principal $O(1)$-bundle over $\mathbb{R}\mathbb{P}^n$. Likewise, $S^{2n+1}$ is a principal $U(1)$-bundle over [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977) $\mathbb{C}\mathbb{P}^n$, where note that $U(1)$ is isomorphic to the circle. Likewise, $S^{4n+3}$ is a principal $Sp(1)$-bundle over [quaternionic projective space](wikipedia://en/2808931) $\mathbb{H}\mathbb{P}^n$, where $Sp(1)$ is the group of unit quaternions, isomorphic to the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792), $\operatorname{Spin}(3)$, and $\operatorname{SU}(2)$. We then have a series of principal bundles for each positive $n$:

$$\mbox{O}(1) \to S(\mathbb{R}^{n+1}) \to \mathbb{RP}^n$$

$$\mbox{U}(1) \to S(\mathbb{C}^{n+1}) \to \mathbb{CP}^n$$

$$\mbox{Sp}(1) \to S(\mathbb{H}^{n+1}) \to \mathbb{HP}^n.$$

Here $S(V)$ denotes the unit sphere in $V$ (equipped with the Euclidean metric). For all of these examples the $n = 1$ cases give the so-called [Hopf bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hopf_bundle).

:::

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

### Trivializations and cross sections {#Trivializations_and_cross_sections}

One of the most important questions regarding any fiber bundle is whether or not it is [trivial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_bundle), *i.e.* isomorphic to a product bundle. For principal bundles there is a convenient characterization of triviality:

**Proposition**. *A principal bundle is trivial if and only if it admits a global [section](wikipedia://en/367621).*

The same is not true in general for other fiber bundles. For instance, [vector bundles](wikipedia://en/276524) always have a zero section whether they are trivial or not and [sphere bundles](wikipedia://en/276526#Sphere_bundles) may admit many global sections without being trivial (such as the [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412)).

The same fact applies to local trivializations of principal bundles. Let $\pi : P \to X$ be a principal $G$-bundle. An [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $U$ in $X$ admits a local trivialization if and only if there exists a local section on $U$. Given a local trivialization

$$\Phi : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times G$$

one can define an associated local section

$$s:U \to \pi^{-1}(U);s(x) = \Phi^{-1}(x,e)\,$$

where $e$ is the [identity](wikipedia://en/14962) in $G$. Conversely, given a section $s$ one defines a trivialization $\Phi$ by

$$\Phi^{-1}(x,g) = s(x)\cdot g.$$

The simple transitivity of the $G$ action on the fibers of $P$ guarantees that this map is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942), it is also a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660). The local trivializations defined by local sections are $G$-[equivariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivariant) in the following sense. If we write

$$\Phi : \pi^{-1}(U) \to U \times G$$

in the form

$$\Phi(p) = (\pi(p), \varphi(p)),$$

then the map

$$\varphi : P \to G$$

satisfies

$$\varphi(p\cdot g) = \varphi(p)g.$$

Equivariant trivializations therefore preserve the $G$-torsor structure of the fibers. In terms of the associated local section $s$ the map $\varphi$ is given by

$$\varphi(s(x)\cdot g) = g.$$

The local version of the cross section theorem then states that the equivariant local trivializations of a principal bundle are in one-to-one correspondence with local sections.

Given an equivariant local trivialization $(\{U_{i}\}, \{\Phi_{i}\})$ of $P$, we have local sections $s_{i}$ on each $U_{i}$. On overlaps these must be related by the action of the structure group $G$. In fact, the relationship is provided by the [transition functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transition_map)

$$t_{ij} : U_i \cap U_j \to G\,.$$

By gluing the local trivializations together using these transition functions, one may reconstruct the original principal bundle. This is an example of the [fiber bundle construction theorem](wikipedia://en/7762521). For any $x \in U_{i} \cap U_{j}$ we have

$$s_j(x) = s_i(x)\cdot t_{ij}(x).$$

### Characterization of smooth principal bundles {#Characterization_of_smooth_principal_bundles}

If $\pi : P \to X$ is a smooth principal $G$-bundle then $G$ acts freely and [properly](wikipedia://en/1190262) on $P$ so that the orbit space $P/G$ is [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic) to the base space $X$. It turns out that these properties completely characterize smooth principal bundles. That is, if $P$ is a smooth manifold, $G$ a Lie group and $\mu : P \times G \to P$ a smooth, free, and proper right action then

- $P/G$ is a smooth manifold,
- the natural projection $\pi : P \to P/G$ is a smooth [submersion](wikipedia://en/356413), and
- $P$ is a smooth principal $G$-bundle over $P/G$.

:::

## Use of the notion {#Use_of_the_notion}

### Reduction of the structure group {#Reduction_of_the_structure_group}

Given a subgroup $H$ of **$G$** one may consider the bundle $P/H$ whose fibers are homeomorphic to the [coset space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coset_space) $G/H$. If the new bundle admits a global section, then one says that the section is a **reduction of the structure group from $G$ to $H$**. The reason for this name is that the (fiberwise) inverse image of the values of this section form a subbundle of $P$ that is a principal $H$-bundle. If $H$ is the identity, then a section of $P$ itself is a reduction of the structure group to the identity. Reductions of the structure group do not in general exist.

Many topological questions about the structure of a manifold or the structure of bundles over it that are associated to a principal $G$-bundle may be rephrased as questions about the admissibility of the reduction of the structure group (from $G$ to $H$). For example:

*Image: Mobius frame bundle.png.* The frame bundle $\mathcal{F}(E)$ of the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817) $E$ is a non-trivial principal $\mathbb{Z}/2\mathbb{Z}$-bundle over the circle.

- A $2n$-dimensional real manifold admits an [almost-complex structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost-complex_structure) if the [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) on the manifold, whose fibers are $GL(2n,\mathbb{R})$, can be reduced to the group $\mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{C}) \subseteq \mathrm{GL}(2n,\mathbb{R})$.
- An $n$-dimensional real manifold admits a $k$-plane field if the frame bundle can be reduced to the structure group $\mathrm{GL}(k,\mathbb{R}) \subseteq \mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$.
- A manifold is [orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable) if and only if its frame bundle can be reduced to the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group), $\mathrm{SO}(n) \subseteq \mathrm{GL}(n,\mathbb{R})$.
- A manifold has [spin structure](wikipedia://en/2844303) if and only if its frame bundle can be further reduced from $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ to $\mathrm{Spin}(n)$ the [Spin group](wikipedia://en/411231), which maps to $\mathrm{SO}(n)$ as a double cover.

Also note: an $n$-dimensional manifold admits $n$ vector fields that are linearly independent at each point if and only if its [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550) admits a global section. In this case, the manifold is called [parallelizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallelizable).

### Associated vector bundles and frames {#Associated_vector_bundles_and_frames}

If $P$ is a principal $G$-bundle and $V$ is a [linear representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_representation) of $G$, then one can construct a vector bundle $E=P\times_G V$ with fibre $V$, as the quotient of the product $P$×$V$ by the diagonal action of $G$. This is a special case of the [associated bundle](wikipedia://en/398874) construction, and $E$ is called an [associated vector bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associated_vector_bundle) to $P$. If the representation of $G$ on $V$ is [faithful](wikipedia://en/2617872), so that $G$ is a subgroup of the general linear group GL($V$), then $E$ is a $G$-bundle and $P$ provides a reduction of structure group of the frame bundle of $E$ from $GL(V)$ to $G$. This is the sense in which principal bundles provide an abstract formulation of the theory of frame bundles.

## Classification of principal bundles {#Classification_of_principal_bundles}

Any topological group $G$ admits a **classifying space** $BG$: the quotient by the action of $G$ of some [weakly contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakly_contractible) space, *e.g.*, a topological space with vanishing [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506). The classifying space has the property that any $G$ principal bundle over a [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact) manifold *B* is isomorphic to a [pullback](wikipedia://en/1665392) of the principal bundle $EG \to BG$.[^o20] In fact, more is true, as the set of isomorphism classes of principal $G$ bundles over the base $B$ identifies with the set of homotopy classes of maps $B \to BG$.
