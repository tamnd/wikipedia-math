# Equaliser (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equaliser"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **equaliser** is a set of arguments where two or more [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) have [equal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_%28math%29) values. An equaliser is the [solution set](wikipedia://en/567840) of an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284). In certain contexts, a **difference kernel** is the equaliser of exactly two functions.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Let *X* and *Y* be [sets](wikipedia://en/26691). Let *f* and *g* be [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), both from *X* to *Y*. Then the *equaliser* of *f* and *g* is the set of elements *x* of *X* such that *f*(*x*) equals *g*(*x*) in *Y*. Symbolically:

$$\operatorname{Eq}(f, g) := \{x \in X \mid f(x) = g(x)\}.$$

The equaliser may be denoted Eq(*f*, *g*) or a variation on that theme (such as with lowercase letters "eq"). In informal contexts, the notation {*f* = *g*} is common.

The definition above used two functions *f* and *g*, but there is no need to restrict to only two functions, or even to only [finitely](wikipedia://en/11742) many functions. In general, if **F** is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of functions from *X* to *Y*, then the *equaliser* of the members of **F** is the set of elements *x* of *X* such that, given any two members *f* and *g* of **F**, *f*(*x*) equals *g*(*x*) in *Y*. Symbolically:

$$\operatorname{Eq}(\mathcal{F}) := \{x \in X \mid \forall f,g \in \mathcal{F}, \; f(x) = g(x)\}.$$

This equaliser may be written as Eq(*f*, *g*, *h*, ...) if $\mathcal{F}$ is the set {*f*, *g*, *h*, ...}. In the latter case, one may also find {*f* = *g* = *h* = ···} in informal contexts.

As a [degenerate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_%28math%29) case of the general definition, let **F** be a [singleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_%28set_theory%29) {*f*}. Since *f*(*x*) always equals itself, the equaliser must be the entire domain *X*. As an even more degenerate case, let **F** be the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566). Then the equaliser is again the entire domain *X*, since the [universal quantification](wikipedia://en/76174) in the definition is [vacuously true](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuously_true).

:::

## Difference kernels {#Difference_kernels}

A binary equaliser (that is, an equaliser of just two functions) is also called a *difference kernel*. This may also be denoted DiffKer(*f*, *g*), Ker(*f*, *g*), or Ker(*f* − *g*). The last notation shows where this terminology comes from, and why it is most common in the context of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384): The difference kernel of *f* and *g* is simply the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of the difference *f* − *g*. Furthermore, the kernel of a single function *f* can be reconstructed as the difference kernel Eq(*f*, 0), where 0 is the [constant function](wikipedia://en/320861) with value [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29).

Of course, all of this presumes an algebraic context where the kernel of a function is the [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of zero under that function; that is not true in all situations. However, the terminology "difference kernel" has no other meaning.

## In category theory {#In_category_theory}

Equalisers can be defined by a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245), which allows the notion to be generalised from the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) to arbitrary [categories](wikipedia://en/5869).

In the general context, *X* and *Y* are objects, while *f* and *g* are morphisms from *X* to *Y*. These objects and morphisms form a [diagram](wikipedia://en/138484) in the category in question, and the equaliser is simply the [limit](wikipedia://en/39248) of that diagram.

In more explicit terms, the equaliser consists of an object *E* and a morphism *eq* : *E* → *X* satisfying $f \circ eq = g \circ eq$, and such that, given any object *O* and morphism *m* : *O* → *X*, if $f \circ m = g \circ m$, then there exists a [unique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_%28mathematics%29) morphism *u* : *O* → *E* such that $eq \circ u = m$.

*Image: Equalizer-01.png.*

A morphism $m:O \rightarrow X$ is said to **equalise** $f$ and $g$ if $f \circ m = g \circ m$.[^o7]

In any [universal algebraic](wikipedia://en/45200) category, including the categories where difference kernels are used, as well as the category of sets itself, the object *E* can always be taken to be the ordinary notion of equaliser, and the morphism *eq* can in that case be taken to be the [inclusion function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inclusion_function) of *E* as a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of *X*.

The generalisation of this to more than two morphisms is straightforward; simply use a larger diagram with more morphisms in it. The degenerate case of only one morphism is also straightforward; then *eq* can be any [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) from an object *E* to *X*.

The correct diagram for the degenerate case with *no* morphisms is slightly subtle: one might initially draw the diagram as consisting of the objects *X* and *Y* and no morphisms. This is incorrect, however, since the limit of such a diagram is the [product](wikipedia://en/237868) of *X* and *Y*, rather than the equaliser. (And indeed products and equalisers are different concepts: the set-theoretic definition of product doesn't agree with the set-theoretic definition of the equaliser mentioned above, hence they are actually different.) Instead, the appropriate insight is that every equaliser diagram is fundamentally concerned with *X*, including *Y* only because *Y* is the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) of morphisms which appear in the diagram. With this view, we see that if there are no morphisms involved, *Y* does not make an appearance and the equaliser diagram consists of *X* alone. The limit of this diagram is then any isomorphism between *E* and *X*.

It can be proved that any equaliser in any category is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538). If the [converse](wikipedia://en/155941) holds in a given category, then that category is said to be *regular* (in the sense of monomorphisms). More generally, a [regular monomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_monomorphism) in any category is any morphism *m* that is an equaliser of some set of morphisms. Some authors require more strictly that *m* be a *binary* equaliser, that is an equaliser of exactly two morphisms. However, if the category in question is [complete](wikipedia://en/62781), then both definitions agree.

The notion of difference kernel also makes sense in a category-theoretic context. The terminology "difference kernel" is common throughout category theory for any binary equaliser. In the case of a [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521) (a category [enriched](wikipedia://en/142622) over the category of [Abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974)), the term "difference kernel" may be interpreted literally, since subtraction of morphisms makes sense. That is, Eq(*f*, *g*) = Ker(*f* - *g*), where Ker denotes the [category-theoretic kernel](wikipedia://en/142616).

Any category with [fibre products](wikipedia://en/841689) (pullbacks) and products has equalisers.

### Category of topological spaces (*Top*) {#Category_of_topological_spaces_(Top)}

In [**Top**](wikipedia://en/435051) where objects are [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) and [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) are [continuous maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_maps), the equaliser of two continuous maps $f, g : X \rightarrow Y$ retains the [underlying set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underlying_set) $E = \{x \in X | f(x) = g(x)\}$, but endows it with the subspace topology inherited from $X$. The inclusion $e : E \rightarrow X$ is continuous, and the universal property is preserved: any continuous map $h : Z \rightarrow X$ coalescing with $f$ and $g$ factors uniquely through $e$. This exemplifies the principle that topological structure, when subordinate to the equaliser's defining condition, is inherited without contradiction.[^o9]
