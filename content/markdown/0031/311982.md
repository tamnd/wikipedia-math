# Divine illumination

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Divine illumination"}

According to **divine illumination**, the process of human thought needs to be aided by [divine grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_grace). It is the oldest and most influential alternative to [naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_%28philosophy%29) in the [theory of mind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_mind) and [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology).[^o2] It was an important feature of ancient [Greek philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_philosophy), [Neoplatonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonism), [medieval philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Medieval_philosophy), and the [Illuminationist](wikipedia://en/26117670) school of [Islamic philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_philosophy).

:::

### Early Greek origins {#Early_Greek_origins}

[Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) quotes [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) in *[The Apology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apology_%28Plato%29)* as saying that he had a divine or spiritual sign that began when he was a child. It was a voice that turned him away from something he was about to do, although it never encouraged him to do anything. [Apuleius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apuleius) later suggested the voice was of a friendly [daemon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daemon_%28classical_mythology%29)[^o5] and that Socrates deserved this help as he was the most perfect of human beings.

### St. Augustine {#St._Augustine}

The early Christian philosopher [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine) (354–430) also emphasised the role of divine illumination in human thought, saying that "The mind needs to be enlightened by light from outside itself, so that it can participate in truth, because it is not itself the nature of truth. You will light my lamp, Lord,"[^o7] and "You hear nothing true from me which you have not first told me."[^o8] According to Augustine, God does not give people certain information, but rather gives them insight into the truth of the information they received for themselves.

> If we both see that what you say is true, and we both see that what I say is true, then where do we see that? Not I in you, nor you in me, but both of us in that unalterable truth that is above our minds.[^o9]

*Image: Saint Augustine by Philippe de Champaigne.jpg.* Saint Augustine

Augustine's theory was defended by Christian philosophers of the [later Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Middle_Ages), particularly [Franciscans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscan) such as [Bonaventure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonaventure) and [Matthew of Aquasparta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_of_Aquasparta). According to Bonaventure:

> "Things have existence in the mind, in their own nature (*proprio genere*), and in the eternal art. So the truth of things as they are in the mind or in their own nature – given that both are changeable – is sufficient for the soul to have certain knowledge only if the soul somehow reaches things as they are in the eternal art."[^o10]

The doctrine was criticised by [John Pecham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Pecham) and [Roger Marston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roger_Marston).

### Thomas Aquinas {#Thomas_Aquinas}

[Thomas Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Aquinas) is often seen as a harsh critic of this doctrine; but his position is more nuanced. As Robert Pasnau observes,

> "Thomas Aquinas is often thought of as the figure most responsible for putting an end to the theory of divine illumination. Although there is some truth to this view, as we will see, it seems more accurate to regard Aquinas as one of the last defenders of the theory, as a proponent of innate Aristotelian illumination."[^o2]

Undoubtedly, Aquinas denied that in this life people have divine ideas as an object of thought, and that divine illumination is sufficient on its own, without the senses, for natural knowledge. He also denied that there is a special continuing divine influence on human thought. People have sufficient capacity for thought on their own, without needing "new illumination added onto their natural illumination."[^o12]

But this natural illumination, which Aquinas distinguishes from the supernatural illumination required for knowledge of intelligible things above human strengths (as is the case of faith and of prophecy),[^o13] is, nevertheless, divine illumination, according to Aquinas; indeed, he writes that

> "The material sun sheds its light outside us; but the intelligible Sun, Who is God, shines within us. Hence the natural light bestowed upon the soul is God's enlightenment, whereby we are enlightened to see what pertains to natural knowledge; and for this there is required no further knowledge, but only for such things as surpass natural knowledge."[^o14]

Aquinas asserted also that "the intellectual light that is in us is nothing other than a certain likeness of the uncreated light, obtained through participation, in which the eternal reasons are contained."[^o15] For this reason, he concluded that, in this life, people know things in the divine ideas as in the principle of knowledge. He also claimed that his position was the right interpretation of Augustine's doctrine on divine illumination;[^o16] some scholars, as Lydia Schumacher, maintain that his claim is right.[^n1]

### Henry of Ghent {#Henry_of_Ghent}

On the other hand, [Henry of Ghent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_of_Ghent) defended a different version of the theory, which, according to Henry himself and to various scholars, would be closer to the Augustinian one. Henry argued against Aquinas that [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s theory of [abstraction](wikipedia://en/2972) is not enough to explain how people can acquire infallible knowledge of the truth, and must be supplemented by divine illumination. A thing has two exemplars against which it can be compared. The first is a created exemplar which exists in the soul through abstraction. The second is an exemplar which exists outside the soul, and which is uncreated and eternal. But no comparison to a created exemplar can give one infallible truth. Since the dignity of man requires that people can acquire such truth, it follows that they have access to the exemplar in the divine mind.[^n2]

Henry's defence of divine illumination was strongly criticised by the [Franciscan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franciscans) theologian [Duns Scotus](wikipedia://en/34293405), who argued that Henry's version of the theory led to [scepticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scepticism) and presented his own version, according to which there are "four senses in which the human intellect sees infallible truths in the [divine light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_light). In each sense, the divine light acts not on us but on the objects of our understanding."[^o2]
