# Retract (group theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Retract"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the field of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is termed a **retract** if there is an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569) of the group that maps [surjectively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) to the subgroup and is the [identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_map) on the subgroup. In symbols, $H$ is a retract of $G$ if and only if there is an endomorphism $\sigma: G \to G$ such that $\sigma(h) = h$ for all $h \in H$ and $\sigma(g) \in H$ for all $g \in G$.[^o3][^o2]

:::

The endomorphism $\sigma$ is an [idempotent element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent_element) in the [transformation monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_monoid) of endomorphisms, so it is called an idempotent endomorphism[^o3][^o4] or a retraction.[^o2]

The following is known about retracts:

- A subgroup is a retract if and only if it has a [normal](wikipedia://en/21918) [complement](wikipedia://en/11436087).[^o5] The normal complement, specifically, is the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of the retraction.
- Every [direct factor](wikipedia://en/3706015) is a retract.[^o3] Conversely, any retract which is a normal subgroup is a direct factor.[^o6]
- Every retract has the [congruence extension property](wikipedia://en/4967042).
- Every [regular factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_factor), and in particular, every [free factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_factor), is a retract.
