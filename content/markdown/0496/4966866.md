# Supersolvable group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Supersolvable group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) is **supersolvable** (or **supersoluble**) if it has an invariant [normal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_series) where all the factors are [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327). Supersolvability is stronger than the notion of [solvability](wikipedia://en/105012).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *G* be a [group](wikipedia://en/19447). *G* is supersolvable if there exists a [normal series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_series)

$$\{1\} = H_0 \triangleleft H_1 \triangleleft \cdots \triangleleft H_{s-1} \triangleleft H_s = G$$

such that each [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $H_{i+1}/H_i \;$ is cyclic and each $H_i$ is normal in $G$.

By contrast, for a [solvable group](wikipedia://en/105012) the definition requires each quotient to be [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974). In another direction, a [polycyclic group](wikipedia://en/4000598) must have a [subnormal series](wikipedia://en/1545790#Normal_series,_subnormal_series) with each quotient cyclic, but there is no requirement that each $H_i$ be normal in $G$. As every finite solvable group is polycyclic, this can be seen as one of the key differences between the definitions. For a concrete example, the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) on four points, $A_4$, is solvable but not supersolvable.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic Properties"}

## Basic Properties {#Basic_Properties}

Some facts about supersolvable groups:

- Supersolvable groups are always [polycyclic](wikipedia://en/4000598), and hence [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012).
- Every [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042) [nilpotent group](wikipedia://en/144569) is supersolvable.
- Every [metacyclic group](wikipedia://en/7515561) is supersolvable.
- The [commutator subgroup](wikipedia://en/8847) of a supersolvable group is nilpotent.
- Subgroups and quotient groups of supersolvable groups are supersolvable.
- A finite supersolvable group has an invariant normal series with each factor cyclic of prime order.
- In fact, the primes can be chosen in a nice order: For every prime p, and for *π* the set of primes greater than p, a finite supersolvable group has a unique [Hall *π*-subgroup](wikipedia://en/1735335). Such groups are sometimes called ordered Sylow tower groups.
- Every group of [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525) order, and every group with cyclic Sylow subgroups (a [Z-group](wikipedia://en/15757039#Group_whose_Sylow_subgroups_are_cyclic)), is supersolvable.
- Every [irreducible complex representation](wikipedia://en/874400) of a finite supersolvable group is monomial, that is, induced from a linear character of a subgroup. In other words, every finite supersolvable group is a [monomial group](wikipedia://en/16388251).
- Every [maximal subgroup](wikipedia://en/1472649) in a supersolvable group has prime [index](wikipedia://en/230581).
- A finite group is supersolvable if and only if every maximal subgroup has prime index.
- A finite group is supersolvable if and only if every maximal chain of subgroups has the same length. This is important to those interested in the [lattice of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172) of a group, and is sometimes called the [Jordan–Dedekind chain condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan–Dedekind_chain_condition).
- Moreover, a finite group is supersolvable if and only if its lattice of subgroups is a [supersolvable lattice](wikipedia://en/75629307), a significant strengthening of the Jordan-Dedekind chain condition.
- By [Baum's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baum's_theorem), every supersolvable finite group has a [DFT](wikipedia://en/8811) algorithm running in time *O*(*n* log *n*).
