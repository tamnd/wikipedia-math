# Coarse structure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Coarse structure"}

In the [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) fields of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) and [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a **coarse structure** on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *X* is a collection of [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) *X* × *X* with certain properties which allow the *large-scale structure* of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) and [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) to be defined.

:::

The concern of traditional geometry and topology is with the small-scale structure of the space: properties such as the [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) depend on whether the [inverse images](wikipedia://en/579311) of small [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358), or [neighborhoods](wikipedia://en/1529485), are themselves open. Large-scale properties of a space—such as [boundedness](wikipedia://en/48258), or the [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606) of the space—do not depend on such features. *Coarse geometry* and *coarse topology* provide tools for measuring the large-scale properties of a space, and just as a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) or a [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) contains information on the small-scale structure of a space, a coarse structure contains information on its large-scale properties.

Properly, a coarse structure is not the large-scale analog of a topological structure, but of a [uniform structure](wikipedia://en/32339).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A coarse structure on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $X$ is a collection $\mathbf{E}$ of [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of $X \times X$ (therefore falling under the more general categorization of [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931) on $X$) called controlled sets, and so that $\mathbf{E}$ possesses the [identity relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_relation), is closed under taking subsets, inverses, and finite unions, and is closed under [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170). Explicitly:

1. **Identity/diagonal**:

The [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251) $\Delta = \{(x, x) : x \in X\}$ is a member of $\mathbf{E}$—the identity relation.

1. **Closed under taking subsets**:

If $E \in \mathbf{E}$ and $F \subseteq E,$ then $F \in \mathbf{E}.$

1. **Closed under taking inverses**:

If $E \in \mathbf{E}$ then the **inverse** (or **transpose**) $E^{-1} = \{(y, x) : (x, y) \in E\}$ is a member of $\mathbf{E}$—the inverse relation.

1. **Closed under taking unions**:

If $E, F \in \mathbf{E}$ then their **[union](wikipedia://en/47949)** $E \cup F$ is a member of$\mathbf{E}.$

1. **Closed under composition**:

If $E, F \in \mathbf{E}$ then their **product** $E \circ F = \{(x, y) : \text{ there exists } z \in X \text{ such that } (x, z) \in E \text{ and } (z, y) \in F\}$ is a member of $\mathbf{E}$—the [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170).

A set $X$ endowed with a coarse structure $\mathbf{E}$ is a coarse space.

Let $E \in \mathbf{E}$ a controlled set. For a subset $K$ of $X,$ the set $E[K]$ is defined as $\{x \in X : (x, k) \in E \text{ for some } k \in K\}.$ We define the section of $E$ by $x$ to be the set $E[\{x\}],$ also denoted $E_x.$ The symbol $E^y$ denotes the set $E^{-1}[\{y\}].$ These are forms of [projections](wikipedia://en/2702833).

A subset $B$ of $X$ is said to be a bounded set if $B \times B$ is a controlled set.

### Intuition {#Intuition}

The controlled sets are "small" sets, or "[negligible sets](wikipedia://en/195132)": a set $A$ such that $A \times A$ is controlled is negligible, while a function $f : X \to X$ such that its graph is controlled is "close" to the identity. In the bounded coarse structure, these sets are the bounded sets, and the functions are the ones that are a finite distance from the identity in the [uniform metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_metric).

:::

## Coarse maps {#Coarse_maps}

Given a set $S$ and a coarse structure $X,$ we say that the maps $f : S \to X$ and $g : S \to X$ are close if $\{(f(s), g(s)) : s \in S\}$ is a controlled set.

For coarse structures $X$ and $Y,$ we say that $f : X \to Y$ is a coarse map if for each bounded set $B$ of $Y$ the set $f^{-1}(B)$ is bounded in $X$ and for each controlled set $E$ of $X$ the set $(f \times f)(E)$ is controlled in $Y.$[^o6] $X$ and $Y$ are said to be coarsely equivalent if there exists coarse maps $f : X \to Y$ and $g : Y \to X$ such that $f \circ g$ is close to $\operatorname{id}_Y$ and $g \circ f$ is close to $\operatorname{id}_X.$

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The bounded coarse structure on a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) $(X, d)$ is the collection $\mathbf{E}$ of all [subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsets) $E$ of $X \times X$ such that $\sup_{(x, y) \in E} d(x, y)$ is [finite](wikipedia://en/11742). With this structure, the [integer lattice](wikipedia://en/3333402) $\mathbb{Z}^n$ is coarsely equivalent to $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697).
- A space $X$ where $X \times X$ is controlled is called a bounded space. Such a space is coarsely equivalent to a point. A metric space with the bounded coarse structure is bounded (as a coarse space) if and only if it is bounded (as a metric space).
- The trivial coarse structure only consists of the diagonal and its subsets. In this structure, a map is a coarse equivalence if and only if it is a bijection (of sets).
- The C0 coarse structure on a metric space $(X, d)$ is the collection of all subsets $E$ of $X \times X$ such that for all $\varepsilon > 0$ there is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) set $K$ of $E$ such that $d(x, y) < \varepsilon$ for all $(x, y) \in E \setminus K \times K.$ Alternatively, the collection of all subsets $E$ of $X \times X$ such that $\overline{\{(x, y) \in E : d(x, y) \geq \varepsilon\}}$ is compact.
- The discrete coarse structure on a set $X$ consists of the [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251) $\Delta$ together with subsets $E$ of $X \times X$ which contain only a finite number of points $(x, y)$ off the diagonal.
- If $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) then the indiscrete coarse structure on $X$ consists of all proper subsets of $X \times X,$ meaning all subsets $E$ such that $E[K]$ and $E^{-1}[K]$ are [relatively compact](wikipedia://en/596600) whenever $K$ is relatively compact.

:::

## Bounded sets {#Bounded_sets}

Let $\mathcal{B} = \{B\subseteq X : B\times B\in\mathbf{E}\}$ be the collection of all bounded sets of a coarse space $X$. Say that a coarse structure $\mathbf{E}$ on $X$ is coarsely connected[^o6] if $\{(x, y)\}\in\mathbf{E}$ for all $(x, y)\in X\times X$. Then $\mathcal{B}$ is a [bornology](wikipedia://en/9584635) on $X$ if and only if $\mathbf{E}$ is coarsely connected. For example, if $X$ has at least two points and $\mathbf{E}$ is the trivial coarse structure, then $\mathcal{B}$ is not a bornology. Bounded, discrete and indiscrete coarse structures are coarsely connected.
