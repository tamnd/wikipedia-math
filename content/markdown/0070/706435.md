# Parseval's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Parseval's theorem"}

In mathematics, **Parseval's theorem** usually refers to the result that the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) is [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999); loosely, that the sum (or integral) of the square of a function is equal to the sum (or integral) of the square of its transform.[^o2] It originates from a 1799 theorem about [series](wikipedia://en/15287) by [Marc-Antoine Parseval](wikipedia://en/708363), which was later applied to the [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038). It is also known as **Rayleigh's energy theorem**, or **Rayleigh's identity**, after [John William Strutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_William_Strutt), Lord Rayleigh.[^o3]

:::

Although the term "Parseval's theorem" is often used to describe the unitarity of *any* Fourier transform, especially in [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the most general form of this property is more properly called the [Plancherel theorem](wikipedia://en/454315).[^o4]

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of Parseval's theorem"}

## Statement of Parseval's theorem {#Statement_of_Parseval's_theorem}

Suppose that $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ are two complex-valued functions on $\mathbb{R}$ of [period](wikipedia://en/76848) $2 \pi$ that are [square integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_integrable) (with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)) over intervals of period length, with [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038)

$$A(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty a_ne^{inx}$$

and

$$B(x)=\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty b_ne^{inx}$$

respectively. Then

where $i$ is the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) and horizontal bars indicate [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation).

:::

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

Substituting $A(x)$ and $\overline{B(x)}$ in the integral:

$$\begin{aligned} &\frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \left( \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty a_ne^{inx} \right) \left( \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \overline{b_n}e^{-inx} \right) \, \mathrm{d}x \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \Bigl(a_1e^{i1x} + a_2e^{i2x} + \cdots\Bigr) \Bigl(\overline{b_1}e^{-i1x} + \overline{b_2}e^{-i2x} + \cdots\Bigr) \, \mathrm{d}x \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \left(a_1e^{i1x} \overline{b_1}e^{-i1x} + a_1e^{i1x} \overline{b_2}e^{-i2x} + a_2e^{i2x} \overline{b_1}e^{-i1x} + a_2e^{i2x} \overline{b_2}e^{-i2x} + \cdots \right) \mathrm{d}x \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \left(a_1 \overline{b_1} + a_1 \overline{b_2}e^{-ix} + a_2 \overline{b_1}e^{ix} + a_2 \overline{b_2} + \cdots\right) \mathrm{d}x \end{aligned}$$

As is the case with the middle terms in this example, many terms will integrate to $0$ over a full [period](wikipedia://en/76848) of length $2\pi$ (see [harmonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic)):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{2\pi} \left[a_1 \overline{b_1} x + i a_1 \overline{b_2}e^{-ix} - i a_2 \overline{b_1}e^{ix} + a_2 \overline{b_2} x + \cdots\right] _{-\pi} ^{+\pi} &= \frac{1}{2\pi} \left(2\pi a_1 \overline{b_1} + 0 + 0 + 2\pi a_2 \overline{b_2} + \cdots\right) \\[6pt] &= a_1 \overline{b_1} + a_2 \overline{b_2} + \cdots \\[6pt] &= \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty a_n\overline{b_n} \,. \end{aligned}$$

More generally, if $A(x)$ and $B(x)$ are instead two complex-valued functions on $\mathbb{R}$ of period $P$ that are [square integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_integrable) (with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)) over intervals of period length, with [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038)

$$\begin{aligned} A(x) &= \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty a_n e^{2\pi n i \frac{x}{P}} \\ B(x) &= \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty b_n e^{2\pi n i \frac{x}{P}} \end{aligned}$$

respectively. Then

Even more generally, given an [abelian locally compact group](wikipedia://en/454325) *G* with [Pontryagin dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontryagin_dual) *G^*, Parseval's theorem says the Pontryagin–Fourier transform is a unitary operator between [Hilbert spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert_spaces) *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G*) and *L*<sup>2</sup>(*G^*) (with integration being against the appropriately scaled [Haar measures](wikipedia://en/40645) on the two groups.) When *G* is the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) **T**, *G^* is the integers and this is the case discussed above. When *G* is the real line $\mathbb{R}$, *G^* is also $\mathbb{R}$ and the unitary transform is the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) on the real line. When *G* is the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) **Z**<sub>n</sub>, again it is self-dual and the Pontryagin–Fourier transform is what is called [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) in applied contexts.

Parseval's theorem can also be expressed as follows:

Suppose $f(x)$ is a square-integrable function over $[-\pi, \pi]$ (i.e., $f(x)$ and $f^2(x)$ are integrable on that interval), with the Fourier series

$$f(x) \simeq \tfrac12 a_0 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \bigl(a_n \cos(nx) + b_n \sin(nx)\bigr).$$

Then[^o11][^o12][^o13]

$$\frac{1}{\pi} \int_{-\pi}^{\pi} f^2(x) \,\mathrm{d}x = \tfrac12 a_0^2 + \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \left(a_n^2 + b_n^2 \right).$$

:::

## Notation used in engineering {#Notation_used_in_engineering}

In [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering), Parseval's theorem is often written as:

$$\int_{-\infty}^\infty \bigl| x(t) \bigr|^2 \, \mathrm{d}t = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\infty}^\infty \bigl| X(\omega) \bigr|^2 \, \mathrm{d}\omega = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \bigl| X(2\pi f) \bigr|^2 \, \mathrm{d}f$$

where $X(\omega) = \mathcal{F}_\omega\{ x(t) \}$ represents the [continuous Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_Fourier_transform) (in non-unitary form) of $x(t)$, and $\omega = 2\pi f$ is angular frequency in radians per second.

The interpretation of this form of the theorem is that the total [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_%28signal_processing%29) of a signal can be calculated by summing power-per-sample across time or spectral power across frequency.

For [discrete time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_time) [signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_%28information_theory%29), the theorem becomes:

$$\sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty \bigl| x[n] \bigr|^2 = \frac{1}{2\pi} \int_{-\pi}^\pi \bigl| X_{2\pi}({\phi}) \bigr|^2 \mathrm{d}\phi$$

where $X_{2\pi}$ is the [discrete-time Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/1216914) (DTFT) of $x$ and $\phi$ represents the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) (in [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) per sample) of $x$.

Alternatively, for the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) (DFT), the relation becomes:

$$\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \bigl| x[n] \bigr|^2 = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \bigl| X[k] \bigr|^2$$

where $X[k]$ is the DFT of $x[n]$, both of length $N$.

We show the DFT case below. For the other cases, the proof is similar. By using the definition of inverse DFT of $X[k]$, we can derive

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \bigl| X[k] \bigr|^2 &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X[k]\cdot X^*[k] = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{k=0}^{N-1} \Biggl(\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n]\,\exp\left(-j\frac{2\pi}{N}k\,n\right)\Biggr) \, X^*[k] \\[5mu] &= \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \Biggl(\sum_{k=0}^{N-1} X^*[k]\,\exp\left(-j\frac{2\pi}{N}k\,n\right)\Biggr) = \frac{1}{N} \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x[n] \bigl(N \cdot x^*[n]\bigr) \\[5mu] &= \sum_{n=0}^{N-1} \bigl| x[n] \bigr|^2, \end{aligned}$$

where $*$ represents complex conjugate.
