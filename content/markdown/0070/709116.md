# Singular value

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Singular value"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in particular in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **singular values** of a [compact operator](wikipedia://en/577441) $\, T \! : X \rightarrow Y$ acting between [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) $X$ and $Y$, are the square roots of the (necessarily non-negative) [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of the [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408) $T^* T$ (where $T^*$ denotes the [adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_operator) of $T$).

:::

The singular values are non-negative [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), usually listed in decreasing order $\big( \sigma_{1}(T) \geq \sigma_{2}(T) \geq \dots \big)$. The largest singular value $\sigma_{1}(T)$ is equal to the [operator norm](wikipedia://en/246722) of $T$ (see [Min-max theorem](wikipedia://en/694952#Min-max_principle_for_singular_values)).

*Image: Singular value decomposition.gif.* Visualization of a [singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207) (SVD) of a $2$-dimensional, real shearing matrix $M$. First, we see the [unit disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_disc) in blue together with the two [canonical unit vectors](wikipedia://en/462005). We then see the action of $M$, which distorts the disc to an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277). The SVD decomposes $M$ into three simple transformations: a [rotation](wikipedia://en/856005) $V^{*}$, a [scaling](wikipedia://en/651752) $\Sigma$ along the rotated coordinate axes, and a second rotation $U$. Moreover, $\Sigma$ is a (square, in this example) [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) containing in its diagonal the singular values of $M$, which represent the lengths $\sigma_{1}$ and $\sigma_{2}$ of the [semi-axes](wikipedia://en/9277#Elements_of_an_ellipse) of the ellipse.

If $T$ acts on a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) (for example, $\mathbb{R}^n$), there is a simple geometric interpretation for the singular values: Consider the image by $T$ of the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/39782); this is an [ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/145381), and the lengths of its semi-axes are the singular values of $T$ (the figure provides an example in $\mathbb{R}^2$).

If $A$ is a [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/170366), then its singular values are the absolute values of its [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues). Indeed, in this case, the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) can be applied to obtain unitary diagonalization of $A$ as $A = U \varLambda \, U^*$. Then, by definition, $\textstyle \sqrt{A^* A} = U \sqrt{\varLambda^* \varLambda} ~ U^* = U \, \vert \varLambda \vert ~ U^*$.

Most [norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_linear_space) on Hilbert space operators studied are defined using singular values. For example, the [Ky Fan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ky_Fan) $k$-norm is the sum of the first $k$ singular values, the trace norm is the sum of all singular values, and the [Schatten norm](wikipedia://en/10687767) is the $p$-th root of the sum of the $p$-th powers of the singular values. Note that each norm is defined only on a special class of operators, hence singular values can be useful in classifying different operators.

In the finite-dimensional case, a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) can always be decomposed in the form $U \varSigma \, V^*$, where $U$ and $V^*$ are [unitary matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unitary_matrices) and $\varSigma$ is a [rectangular diagonal matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_diagonal_matrix) with the singular values lying on its diagonal. This is the [singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207).

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

For $A \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}$ and $i = 1,2, \ldots, \min \{ m,n \}$:

[Min-max theorem for singular values](wikipedia://en/694952#Min-max_principle_for_singular_values): Here, $U$ is a subspace of $\mathbb{C}^n$;

$$\sigma_i (A) = \min_{\dim(U)=n-i+1} \max_{\underset{\| x \|_2 = 1}{x \in U}} \left\| Ax \right\|_2.$$

$$\sigma_i (A) = \max_{\dim(U)=i} \min_{\underset{\| x \|_2 = 1}{x \in U}} \left\| Ax \right\|_2.$$

Matrix transpose and conjugate do not alter singular values:

$$\sigma_i (A) = \sigma_i \! \left( A^\textsf{T} \right) = \sigma_i \! \left( A^* \right).$$

For any unitary matrices $U \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times m}$ and $V \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$,

$$\sigma_i (A) = \sigma_i (UAV).$$

Relation to eigenvalues:

$$\sigma_i ^2 (A) = \lambda_i \! \left( A A^* \right) = \lambda_i \! \left( A^* A \right).$$

Relation to [trace](wikipedia://en/43270):

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \sigma_i ^2 = \text{tr} (A^* A).$$

If $A^* A$ has full rank, the product of singular values is $\det \sqrt{A^* A}$.

If $A A^*$ has full rank, the product of singular values is $\det \sqrt{A A^*}$.

If $A$ is square and has full rank, the product of singular values is $\vert \! \det A \vert$.

If $A$ is [normal](wikipedia://en/170366), then $\sigma_i (A) = \left\vert \lambda_i (A) \right\vert$, that is, its singular values are the absolute values of its eigenvalues.

For a generic rectangular $m \times n$ matrix $A$, let $\tilde{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 0 & A \\ A^* & 0 \end{bmatrix}$ be its [augmented](wikipedia://en/3676187) $(m+n) \times (m+n)$ matrix. $2 \min \{ m,n \}$ eigenvalues of $\tilde A$ are $\pm \sigma_i (A)$ (where the $\sigma_i (A)$ are the singular values of $A$), and the $|m-n|$ remaining eigenvalues of $\tilde A$ are zero. Let $A = U \varSigma \, V^*$ be the singular value decomposition of $A$; then the eigenvectors of $\tilde{A}$ for $\pm \sigma_i (A)$ are $\begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{u}_i \\ \pm \mathbf{v}_i \end{bmatrix}$.[^o4]

:::

## The smallest singular value {#The_smallest_singular_value}

The smallest singular value of a matrix $A$ is $\sigma_\mathrm{n}(A)$. For a non-singular matrix $A$, it has the following properties:

- $\left\| A^{-1} \right\|_2 = \sigma_\mathrm{n}^{-1}(A)$.[^o6]
- For all indices $1 \! \leq i, j \leq \! n$, $~$ $\left\vert A_{i,j}^{-1} \right\vert \leq \sigma_\mathrm{n}^{-1}(A)$.[^o6]

Intuitively, if $\sigma_\mathrm{n}(A)$ is small, then the rows of $A$ are "almost" linearly dependent. If it is $\sigma_\mathrm{n}(A) = 0$, then the rows of $A$ are linearly dependent and $A$ is not invertible.

## Inequalities about singular values {#Inequalities_about_singular_values}

See also:[^o8]

### Singular values of sub-matrices {#Singular_values_of_sub-matrices}

For $A \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}$,

1. Let $B$ denote $A$ with one of its rows *or* columns deleted. Then $$\sigma_{i+1}(A) \leq \sigma_i (B) \leq \sigma_i(A)$$
1. Let $B$ denote $A$ with two of its rows *and* columns deleted. Then $$\sigma_{i+2}(A) \leq \sigma_i (B) \leq \sigma_i(A)$$
1. Let $B$ denote an $(m-k)\times(n-\ell)$ submatrix of $A$. Then $$\sigma_{i+k+\ell}(A) \leq \sigma_i (B) \leq \sigma_i(A)$$

### Singular values of $A + B$ {#Singular_values_of}

For $A, B \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}$,

1. $$\sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i(A + B) \leq \sum_{i=1}^k \big( \sigma_i(A) + \sigma_i(B) \big), ~ \text{where} ~ k = \min \{m,n\}.$$
1. $$\sigma_{i+j-1}(A + B) \leq \sigma_i(A) + \sigma_j(B), \quad \forall \, i,j \in \mathbb{N}^*,\ i + j - 1 \leq \min \{m,n\}.$$

### Singular values of $AB$ {#Singular_values_of}

For $A, B \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$,

1. $$\begin{aligned} \prod_{i=n}^{i=n-k+1} \sigma_i(A) \sigma_i(B) &\leq \prod_{i=n}^{i=n-k+1} \sigma_i(AB). \\ \prod_{i=1}^k \sigma_i(AB) &\leq \prod_{i=1}^k \sigma_i(A) \sigma_i(B). \\ \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i^p(AB) &\leq \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i^p(A) \sigma_i^p(B). \end{aligned}$$
1. $$\sigma_n(A) \sigma_i(B) \leq \sigma_i (AB) \leq \sigma_1(A) \sigma_i(B), \quad \forall i = 1, 2, \ldots, n.$$

For $A, B \in \mathbb{C}^{m \times n}$,[^o12]

$$2 \sigma_i(A B^*) \leq \sigma_i \left( A^* A + B^* B \right), \quad \forall i = 1, 2, \ldots, n.$$

### Singular values and eigenvalues {#Singular_values_and_eigenvalues}

For $A \in \mathbb{C}^{n \times n}$,

1. See:[^o14]. $$\lambda_i \left( A + A^* \right) \leq 2 \sigma_i(A), \quad \forall i = 1, 2, \ldots, n.$$
1. Assume $\left| \lambda_1(A) \right| \geq \cdots \geq \left| \lambda_n(A) \right|$. Then for $k = 1, 2, \ldots, n$,
   1. [Weyl's theorem](wikipedia://en/4908214#Weyl's_inequality_in_matrix_theory): $$\prod_{i=1}^k \left| \lambda_i(A) \right| \leq \prod_{i=1}^k \sigma_i(A).$$
   1. For $p>0$, $$\sum_{i=1}^k \left| \lambda_i^p(A) \right| \leq \sum_{i=1}^k \sigma_i^p(A).$$

## History {#History}

This concept was introduced by [Erhard Schmidt](wikipedia://en/672191) in 1907. Schmidt called singular values "eigenvalues" at that time. The name "singular value" was first quoted by Smithies in 1937. In 1957, Allahverdiev proved the following characterization of the $n$-th singular number:[^o16]

$$\sigma_n(T) = \inf \big\{ \, \|T-L\| : L \text{ is an operator of finite rank } < n \, \big\}.$$

This formulation made it possible to extend the notion of singular values to operators in [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989).

Note that there is a more general concept of *[s-numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-numbers)*, which also includes Gelfand and Kolmogorov width.
