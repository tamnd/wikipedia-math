# Canonical coordinates

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Canonical coordinates"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), **canonical coordinates** are sets of [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates) on [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) which can be used to describe a physical system at any given point in time. Canonical coordinates are used in the [Hamiltonian formulation](wikipedia://en/198319) of [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586). A closely related concept also appears in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202); see the [Stone–von Neumann theorem](wikipedia://en/744569) and [canonical commutation relations](wikipedia://en/706295) for details.

:::

As Hamiltonian mechanics are generalized by [symplectic geometry](wikipedia://en/294298) and [canonical transformations](wikipedia://en/514534) are generalized by [contact transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contact_transformation), so the 19th century definition of canonical coordinates in classical mechanics may be generalized to a more abstract 20th century definition of coordinates on the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) (the mathematical notion of phase space).

## Definition in classical mechanics {#Definition_in_classical_mechanics}

In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), **canonical coordinates** are coordinates $q^i$ and $p_i$ in [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) that are used in the [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) formalism. The canonical coordinates satisfy the fundamental [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) relations:

$$\left\{q^i, q^j\right\} = 0 \qquad \left\{p_i, p_j\right\} = 0 \qquad \left\{q^i, p_j\right\} = \delta_{ij}$$

A typical example of canonical coordinates is for $q^i$ to be the usual [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), and $p_i$ to be the components of [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431). Hence in general, the $p_i$ coordinates are referred to as "conjugate momenta".

Canonical coordinates can be obtained from the [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514) of the [Lagrangian](wikipedia://en/23371726) formalism by a [Legendre transformation](wikipedia://en/390273), or from another set of canonical coordinates by a [canonical transformation](wikipedia://en/514534).

## Definition on cotangent bundles {#Definition_on_cotangent_bundles}

Canonical coordinates are defined as a special set of [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinates) on the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600) of a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). They are usually written as a set of $\left(q^i, p_j\right)$ or $\left(x^i, p_j\right)$ with the *x*'s or *q*'s denoting the coordinates on the underlying manifold and the *p*'s denoting the **conjugate momentum**, which are [1-forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-form) in the cotangent bundle at point *q* in the manifold.

A common definition of canonical coordinates is any set of coordinates on the cotangent bundle that allow the [canonical one-form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_one-form) to be written in the form

$$\sum_i p_i\,\mathrm{d}q^i$$

up to a total differential. A change of coordinates that preserves this form is a [canonical transformation](wikipedia://en/514534); these are a special case of a [symplectomorphism](wikipedia://en/312304), which are essentially a change of coordinates on a [symplectic manifold](wikipedia://en/28356).

In the following exposition, we assume that the manifolds are real manifolds, so that cotangent vectors acting on tangent vectors produce real numbers.

## Formal development {#Formal_development}

Given a manifold $Q$, a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) $X$ on $Q$ (a [section](wikipedia://en/367621) of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) $TQ$) can be thought of as a function acting on the [cotangent bundle](wikipedia://en/221600), by the duality between the tangent and cotangent spaces. That is, define a function

$$P_X: T^*Q \to \mathbb{R}$$

such that

$$P_X(q, p) = p(X_q)$$

holds for all cotangent vectors $p$ in $T_q^*Q$. Here, $X_q$ is a vector in $T_qQ$, the tangent space to the manifold $Q$ at point $q$. The function $P_X$ is called the *momentum function* corresponding to $X$.

In [local coordinates](wikipedia://en/47436), the vector field $X$ at point $q$ may be written as

$$X_q = \sum_i X^i(q) \frac{\partial}{\partial q^i}$$

where the $\partial /\partial q^i$ are the coordinate frame on $TQ$. The conjugate momentum then has the expression

$$P_X(q, p) = \sum_i X^i(q)\; p_i$$

where the $p_i$ are defined as the momentum functions corresponding to the vectors $\partial /\partial q^i$:

$$p_i = P_{\partial /\partial q^i}$$

The $q^i$ together with the $p_j$ together form a coordinate system on the cotangent bundle $T^*Q$; these coordinates are called the *canonical coordinates*.

## Generalized coordinates {#Generalized_coordinates}

In [Lagrangian mechanics](wikipedia://en/23371726), a different set of coordinates are used, called the [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514). These are commonly denoted as $\left(q^i, \dot{q}^i\right)$ with $q^i$ called the **generalized position** and $\dot{q}^i$ the **generalized velocity**. When a [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/1818385) is defined on the cotangent bundle, then the generalized coordinates are related to the canonical coordinates by means of the [Hamilton–Jacobi equations](wikipedia://en/897539).
