# Cesàro summation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cesàro summation"}

article In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), **Cesàro summation** assigns values to some [infinite sums](wikipedia://en/15287) that are [not necessarily convergent](wikipedia://en/876428) in the usual sense. The **Cesàro sum**, also known as the **Cesàro mean**[^o2][^o3] or **Cesàro limit**,[^o4] is defined as the limit, as *n* tends to infinity, of the sequence of arithmetic means of the first *n* partial sums of the series.

:::

This special case of a [matrix summability method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_summability_method) is named for the Italian analyst [Ernesto Cesàro](wikipedia://en/605144) (1859–1906).

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ be a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838), and let

$$s_k = a_1 + \cdots + a_k= \sum_{n=1}^k a_n$$

be its $k$th [partial sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum).

The sequence $(a_{n})$ is called **Cesàro summable**, with Cesàro sum $A \in \mathbb{R}$, if, as $n$ tends to infinity, the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of its first *n* partial sums $s_{1}, s_{2}, ..., s_{n}$ tends to $A$:

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=1}^n s_k = A.$$

The value of the resulting limit is called the Cesàro sum of the series $\textstyle\sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n.$ If this series is convergent, then it is Cesàro summable and its Cesàro sum is the usual sum.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### First example {#First_example}

Let $a_{n} = (-1)^{n}$ for $n \ge 0$. That is, $(a_n)_{n=0}^\infty$ is the sequence

$$(1, -1, 1, -1, \ldots).$$

Let $G$ denote the series

$$G = \sum_{n=0}^\infty a_n = 1-1+1-1+1-\cdots$$

The series $G$ is known as [Grandi's series](wikipedia://en/7830441).

Let $(s_k)_{k=0}^\infty$ denote the sequence of partial sums of $G$:

$$\begin{aligned} s_k &= \sum_{n=0}^k a_n \\ (s_k) &= (1, 0, 1, 0, \ldots). \end{aligned}$$

This sequence of partial sums does not converge, so the series $G$ is divergent. However, $G$ is Cesàro summable. Let $(t_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ be the sequence of arithmetic means of the first $n$ partial sums:

$$\begin{aligned} t_n &= \frac{1}{n}\sum_{k=0}^{n-1} s_k \\ (t_n) &= \left(\frac{1}{1}, \frac{1}{2}, \frac{2}{3}, \frac{2}{4}, \frac{3}{5}, \frac{3}{6}, \frac{4}{7}, \frac{4}{8}, \ldots\right). \end{aligned}$$

Then

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} t_n = 1/2,$$

and therefore, the Cesàro sum of the series $G$ is $1/2$.

### Second example {#Second_example}

As another example, let $a_{n} = n$ for $n \ge 1$. That is, $(a_n)_{n=1}^\infty$ is the sequence

$$(1, 2, 3, 4, \ldots).$$

Let $G$ now denote the series

$$G = \sum_{n=1}^\infty a_n = 1+2+3+4+\cdots$$

Then the sequence of partial sums $(s_k)_{k=1}^\infty$ is

$$(1, 3, 6, 10, \ldots).$$

Since the sequence of partial sums grows without bound, the series $G$ diverges to infinity. The sequence $(t_{n})$ of means of partial sums of G is

$$\left(\frac{1}{1}, \frac{4}{2}, \frac{10}{3}, \frac{20}{4}, \ldots\right).$$

This sequence diverges to infinity as well, so $G$ is not Cesàro summable. In fact, for the series of any sequence which diverges to (positive or negative) infinity, the Cesàro method also leads to the series of a sequence that diverges likewise, and hence such a series is not Cesàro summable.

:::

## $(C, \alpha)$ summation {#summation}

In 1890, Ernesto Cesàro stated a broader family of summation methods which have since been called $(C, \alpha)$ for non-negative integers $\alpha$. The $(C, 0)$ method is just ordinary summation, and $(C, 1)$ is Cesàro summation as described above.

The higher-order methods can be described as follows: given a series $\Sigma a_{n}$, define the quantities

$$\begin{aligned} A_n^{-1}&=a_n \\ A_n^\alpha&=\sum_{k=0}^n A_k^{\alpha-1} \end{aligned}$$

(where the upper indices do not denote exponents) and define $E^{\alpha}_{n}$ to be $A^{\alpha}_{n}$ for the series 1 + 0 + 0 + 0 + .... Then the $(C, \alpha)$ sum of $\Sigma a_{n}$ is denoted by $(C, \alpha)-\Sigma a_{n}$ and has the value

$$(\mathrm{C},\alpha)\text{-}\sum_{j=0}^\infty a_j=\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{A_n^\alpha}{E_n^\alpha}$$

if it exists (Shawyer Watson 1994, pp.16-17). This description represents an $\alpha$-times iterated application of the initial summation method and can be restated as

$$\begin{aligned} (\mathrm{C},\alpha)\text{-}\sum_{j=0}^\infty a_j &= \lim_{n\to\infty} \sum_{j=0}^n \frac{\binom{n}{j}}{\binom{n+\alpha}{j}} a_j\\&=\lim_{n\to\infty}\sum_{j=0}^n\frac{\left(n-j+1\right)_\alpha}{\left(n+1\right)_\alpha}a_j\text{.} \end{aligned}$$

Even more generally, for $\alpha \in \mathbb{R} \backslash \mathbb{Z}^{-}$, let $A^{\alpha}_{n}$ be implicitly given by the coefficients of the series

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty A_n^\alpha x^n=\frac{\displaystyle{\sum_{n=0}^\infty a_nx^n}}{(1-x)^{1+\alpha}},$$

and $E^{\alpha}_{n}$ as above. In particular, $E^{\alpha}_{n}$ are the [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668#Newton's_binomial_series) of power $-1 - \alpha$. Then the $(C, \alpha)$ sum of $\Sigma a_{n}$ is defined as above.

If $\Sigma a_{n}$ has a $(C, \alpha)$ sum, then it also has a $(C, \beta)$ sum for every $\beta > \alpha$, and the sums agree; furthermore we have $a_{n} = o(n^{\alpha})$ if $\alpha > -1$ (see [little-$o$ notation](wikipedia://en/44578#Little-o_notation)).

## Cesàro summability of an integral {#Cesàro_summability_of_an_integral}

Let $\alpha \ge 0$. The [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) $\textstyle\int_0^\infty f(x)\,dx$ is $(C, \alpha)$ summable if

$$\lim_{\lambda\to\infty}\int_0^\lambda\left(1-\frac{x}{\lambda}\right)^\alpha f(x)\, dx$$

exists and is finite (Titchmarsh 1948, §1.15). The value of this limit, should it exist, is the $(C, \alpha)$ sum of the integral. Analogously to the case of the sum of a series, if $\alpha = 0$, the result is convergence of the [improper integral](wikipedia://en/346030). In the case $\alpha = 1$, $(C, 1)$ convergence is equivalent to the existence of the limit

$$\lim_{\lambda\to \infty}\frac{1}{\lambda}\int_0^\lambda \int_0^x f(y)\, dy\,dx$$

which is the limit of means of the partial integrals.

As is the case with series, if an integral is $(C, \alpha)$ summable for some value of $\alpha \ge 0$, then it is also $(C, \beta)$ summable for all $\beta > \alpha$, and the value of the resulting limit is the same.
