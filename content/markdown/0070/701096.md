# Rotational symmetry

*Image: The armoured triskelion on the flag of the Isle of Man.svg.* The [triskelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskelion) appearing on the [Isle of Man flag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_of_the_Isle_of_Man) has rotational symmetry because it appears the same when rotated by one third of a full turn about its center. Because its appearance is identical in three distinct orientations, its rotational symmetry is three-fold.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rotational symmetry"}

**Rotational symmetry**, also known as **radial symmetry** in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), is the property a [shape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_%28geometry%29) has when it looks the same after some [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) by a partial [turn](wikipedia://en/855329). An object's degree of rotational symmetry is the number of distinct [orientations](wikipedia://en/2544098) in which it looks exactly the same for each rotation.

:::

Certain geometric objects are partially symmetrical when rotated at certain angles such as [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Squares) rotated 90°, however the only geometric objects that are fully rotationally symmetric at any angle are spheres, circles and other [spheroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spheroids).[^o2][^o3]

## Formal treatment {#Formal_treatment}

Formally the rotational symmetry is [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) with respect to some or all [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789) in $m$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). Rotations are [direct isometries](wikipedia://en/652164#Direct_and_indirect_isometries), i.e., [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) preserving [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29). Therefore, a [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of rotational symmetry is a subgroup of $E^{+}(m)$ (see [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164)).

Symmetry with respect to all rotations about all points implies [translational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetry) with respect to all translations, so space is homogeneous, and the symmetry group is the whole $E(m)$. With the [modified notion of symmetry for vector fields](wikipedia://en/53741#Mathematical_model_for_symmetry) the symmetry group can also be $E^{+}(m)$.

For symmetry with respect to rotations about a point we can take that point as origin. These rotations form the special [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $\operatorname{SO}(m)$, the group of $m \times m$ [orthogonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_matrices) with determinant 1. For $m = 3$ this is the rotation group $\operatorname{SO}(3)$.

In another definition of the word, the rotation group *of an object* is the symmetry group within $E^{+}(n)$, the [group of direct isometries](wikipedia://en/652164); in other words, the intersection of the full symmetry group and the group of direct isometries. For [chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160) objects it is the same as the full symmetry group.

Laws of physics [are SO(3)-invariant](wikipedia://en/14865) if they do not distinguish different directions in space. Because of [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159), the rotational symmetry of a physical system is equivalent to the [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) conservation law.

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Discrete rotational symmetry"}

### Discrete rotational symmetry {#Discrete_rotational_symmetry}

**Rotational symmetry of order $n$**, also called **$n$-fold rotational symmetry**, or **discrete rotational symmetry of the $n$th order**, with respect to a particular point (in 2D) or axis (in 3D) means that rotation by an angle of $\tfrac{360^\circ}{n}$ (180°, 120°, 90°, 72°, 60°, 51 $\tfrac{3}{7}$°, etc.) does not change the object. A "1-fold" symmetry is no symmetry (all objects look alike after a rotation of 360°).

The [notation](wikipedia://en/456410#Overview_of_point_groups_by_crystal_system) for $n$-fold symmetry is $\boldsymbol{C_{n}}$ or simply $n$. The actual [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) is specified by the point or axis of symmetry, together with the $n$. For each point or axis of symmetry, the abstract group type is [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order $n$, $Z_{n}$. Although for the latter also the notation $C_{n}$ is used, the geometric and abstract $C_{n}$ should be distinguished: there are other symmetry groups of the same abstract group type which are geometrically different, see [cyclic symmetry groups in 3D](wikipedia://en/2694525#Cyclic_symmetry_groups_in_3D_cyclic_symmetry_groups).

The [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) is a [sector](wikipedia://en/1967249) of $\tfrac{360^\circ}{n}.$

Examples without additional [reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873):

- $n = 2$, 180°: the *dyad*; letters Z, N, S; the outlines, albeit not the colors, of the [yin and yang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yin_and_yang) symbol; the [Union Jack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_Jack) (as divided along the flag's diagonal and rotated about the flag's center point)
- $n = 3$, 120°: *triad*, [triskelion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triskelion), [Borromean rings](wikipedia://en/873118); sometimes the term *trilateral symmetry* is used;
- $n = 4$, 90°: *tetrad*, [swastika](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swastika)
- $n = 5$, 72°: *pentad,* [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544), regular pentagon; 5-fold symmetry is not possible in periodic crystals.
- $n = 6$, 60°: *hexad*, [Star of David](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_of_David) (this one has additional [reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873))
- $n = 8$, 45°: *octad*, Octagonal [muqarnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqarnas), computer-generated (CG), ceiling

$C_{n}$ is the rotation group of a regular $n$-sided [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) in 2D and of a regular $n$-sided [pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid) in 3D.

If there is e.g. rotational symmetry with respect to an angle of 100°, then also with respect to one of 20°, the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) of 100° and 360°.

A typical 3D object with rotational symmetry (possibly also with perpendicular axes) but no mirror symmetry is a [propeller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propeller).

### Multiple symmetry axes through the same point {#Multiple_symmetry_axes_through_the_same_point}

For [discrete symmetry](wikipedia://en/1372580) with multiple symmetry axes through the same point, there are the following possibilities:

- In addition to an $n$-fold axis, $n$ perpendicular 2-fold axes: the [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267) $D_{n}$ of order $2n$ ($n \ge 2$). This is the rotation group of a regular [prism](wikipedia://en/283007), or regular [bipyramid](wikipedia://en/4153). Although the same notation is used, the geometric and abstract $D_{n}$ should be distinguished: there are other symmetry groups of the same abstract group type which are geometrically different, see [dihedral symmetry groups in 3D](wikipedia://en/100267#Dihedral_symmetry_groups_in_3D).
- 4×3-fold and 3×2-fold axes: the rotation group $T$ of order 12 of a regular [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606). The group is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $A_{4}$.
- 3×4-fold, 4×3-fold, and 6×2-fold axes: the rotation group *O* of order 24 of a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) and a regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635). The group is isomorphic to [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{4}$.
- 6×5-fold, 10×3-fold, and 15×2-fold axes: the rotation group $I$ of order 60 of a [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) and an [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084). The group is isomorphic to alternating group $A_{5}$. The group contains 10 versions of $D_{3}$ and 6 versions of $D_{5}$ (rotational symmetries like prisms and antiprisms).

In the case of the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905), the 2-fold axes are through the midpoints of opposite edges, and the number of them is half the number of edges. The other axes are through opposite vertices and through centers of opposite faces, except in the case of the tetrahedron, where the 3-fold axes are each through one vertex and the center of one face.

### Rotational symmetry with respect to any angle {#Rotational_symmetry_with_respect_to_any_angle}

Rotational symmetry with respect to any angle is, in two dimensions, [circular symmetry](wikipedia://en/2217599). The fundamental domain is a [half-line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29#Ray).

In three dimensions we can distinguish *[cylindrical symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_symmetry)* and *[spherical symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_symmetry)* (no change when rotating about one axis, or for any rotation). That is, no dependence on the angle using [cylindrical coordinates](wikipedia://en/176733) and no dependence on either angle using [spherical coordinates](wikipedia://en/29181). The fundamental domain is a [half-plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Half-plane) through the axis, and a radial half-line, respectively. **Axisymmetric** and **axisymmetrical** are [adjectives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjective) which refer to an object having cylindrical symmetry, or **axisymmetry** (i.e. rotational symmetry with respect to a central axis) like a [doughnut](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doughnut) ([torus](wikipedia://en/74800)). An example of approximate spherical symmetry is the Earth (with respect to density and other physical and chemical properties).

In 4D, continuous or discrete rotational symmetry about a plane corresponds to corresponding 2D rotational symmetry in every perpendicular plane, about the point of intersection. An object can also have rotational symmetry about two perpendicular planes, e.g. if it is the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two rotationally symmetry 2D figures, as in the case of e.g. the [duocylinder](wikipedia://en/2114445) and various regular [duoprisms](wikipedia://en/2114155).

2-fold rotational symmetry together with single [translational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetry) is one of the [Frieze groups](wikipedia://en/375503). A rotocenter is the [fixed, or invariant, point](wikipedia://en/449738) of a rotation.[^o18] There are two rotocenters per [primitive cell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_cell).

Together with double translational symmetry the rotation groups are the following [wallpaper groups](wikipedia://en/386214), with axes per primitive cell:

- p2 (2222): 4×2-fold; rotation group of a [parallelogrammic](wikipedia://en/133496), [rectangular](wikipedia://en/70048), and [rhombic](wikipedia://en/183884) [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246).
- p3 (333): 3×3-fold; *not* the rotation group of any lattice (every lattice is upside-down the same, but that does not apply for this symmetry); it is e.g. the rotation group of the [regular triangular tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tilings_of_regular_polygons#Regular_tilings) with the equilateral triangles alternatingly colored.
- p4 (442): 2×4-fold, 2×2-fold; rotation group of a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29) lattice.
- p6 (632): 1×6-fold, 2×3-fold, 3×2-fold; rotation group of a [hexagonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal) lattice.
- 2-fold rotocenters (including possible 4-fold and 6-fold), if present at all, form the translate of a lattice equal to the translational lattice, scaled by a factor 1/2. In the case translational symmetry in one dimension, a similar property applies, though the term "lattice" does not apply.
- 3-fold rotocenters (including possible 6-fold), if present at all, form a regular hexagonal lattice equal to the translational lattice, rotated by 30° (or equivalently 90°), and scaled by a factor $\tfrac{1}{3} \sqrt {3}$
- 4-fold rotocenters, if present at all, form a regular square lattice equal to the translational lattice, rotated by 45°, and scaled by a factor $\tfrac{1}{2} \sqrt {2}$
- 6-fold rotocenters, if present at all, form a regular hexagonal lattice which is the translate of the translational lattice.

Scaling of a lattice divides the number of points per unit area by the square of the scale factor. Therefore, the number of 2-, 3-, 4-, and 6-fold rotocenters per primitive cell is 4, 3, 2, and 1, respectively, again including 4-fold as a special case of 2-fold, etc.

3-fold rotational symmetry at one point and 2-fold at another one (or ditto in 3D with respect to parallel axes) implies rotation group p6, i.e. double translational symmetry and 6-fold rotational symmetry at some point (or, in 3D, parallel axis). The translation distance for the symmetry generated by one such pair of rotocenters is $2\sqrt {3}$ times their distance.
