# Lotka's law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lotka's law"}

*Image: Lotka\_law\_for\_the\_15\_most\_populated\_categories\_on\_arXiv\_(2023-07).svg.* Lotka law for the 15 most populated categories on [arXiv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ArXiv) (2023-07). It is a log-log plot. The x-axis is the number of publications, and the y-axis is the number of authors with *at least* that many publications.

**Lotka's law**,[^o2] named after [Alfred J. Lotka](wikipedia://en/484120), is one of a variety of special applications of [Zipf's law](wikipedia://en/43218). It describes the frequency of publication by authors in any given field.[^o3][^o4]

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be the number of publications, $Y$ be the number of authors with $X$ publications, and $k$ be a constant depending on the specific field. Lotka's law states that $Y \propto X^{-k}$.

In Lotka's original publication, he claimed $k=2$. Subsequent research showed that $k$ varies depending on the discipline.

Equivalently, Lotka's law can be stated as $Y' \propto X^{-(k-1)}$, where $Y'$ is the number of authors with *at least* $X$ publications. Their equivalence can be proved by taking the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921).

*Image: Lotka plot.png.* Graphical plot of the Lotka function described in the text, with C=1, n=2

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Assume that n=2 in a discipline, then as the number of articles published increases, authors producing that many publications become less frequent. There are 1/4 as many authors publishing two articles within a specified time period as there are single-publication authors, 1/9 as many publishing three articles, 1/16 as many publishing four articles, etc.

And if 100 authors wrote *exactly* one article each over a specific period in the discipline, then:

::: {#o9 .table cue=table}

| Portion of articles written | Number of authors writing that number of articles |
|---|---|
| 10 | 100/10<sup>2</sup> = 1 |
| 9 | 100/9<sup>2</sup> ≈ 1 (1.23) |
| 8 | 100/8<sup>2</sup> ≈ 2 (1.56) |
| 7 | 100/7<sup>2</sup> ≈ 2 (2.04) |
| 6 | 100/6<sup>2</sup> ≈ 3 (2.77) |
| 5 | 100/5<sup>2</sup> = 4 |
| 4 | 100/4<sup>2</sup> ≈ 6 (6.25) |
| 3 | 100/3<sup>2</sup> ≈ 11 (11.111...) |
| 2 | 100/2<sup>2</sup> = 25 |
| 1 | 100 |

:::

That would be a total of 294 articles and 155 writers, with an average of 1.9 articles for each writer.

:::

## Other applications {#Other_applications}

A generalized version of Lotka's Law has been used to model the number of [gold disks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_recording_certification) certified by the [Recording Industry Association of America](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RIAA) from 1958 to 1989, and was found to be an almost perfect fit to the data.[^o11]

## Relationship to Riemann Zeta {#Relationship_to_Riemann_Zeta}

Lotka's law may be described using the [Zeta distribution](wikipedia://en/115742):

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{\zeta (s)} \cdot \frac{1}{x^s}$$

for $x = 1, 2, 3, 4, \dots$ and where

$$\zeta (s) = \sum_{x=1}^\infty \frac{1}{x^s}$$

is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809). It is the limiting case of [Zipf's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zipfs_law) where an individual's maximum number of publications is infinite.
