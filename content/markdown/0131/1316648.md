# Constructive dilemma

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Constructive dilemma"}

**Constructive dilemma.**

If $P$ implies $Q$ and $R$ implies $S$, and either $P$ or $R$ is true, then either $Q$ or $S$ has to be true.

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Constructive dilemma"}

**Constructive dilemma**[^o3][^o4][^o5] is a [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) [rule of inference](wikipedia://en/252311) of [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus). It is the [inference](wikipedia://en/317465) that, if *P* implies *Q* and *R* implies *S* and either *P* or *R* is true, then either *Q or S* has to be true. In sum, if two [conditionals](wikipedia://en/658808) are true and at least one of their antecedents is, then at least one of their consequents must be too. *Constructive dilemma* is the [disjunctive](wikipedia://en/7962) version of *[modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900)*, whereas [destructive dilemma](wikipedia://en/1316878) is the disjunctive version of *[modus tollens](wikipedia://en/18901)*. The constructive dilemma rule can be stated:

:::

$$\frac{(P \to Q), (R \to S), P \lor R}{\therefore Q \lor S}$$

where the rule is that whenever instances of "$P \to Q$", "$R \to S$", and "$P \lor R$" appear on lines of a proof, "$Q \lor S$" can be placed on a subsequent line.

## Formal notation {#Formal_notation}

The *constructive dilemma* rule may be written in [sequent](wikipedia://en/252355) notation:

$$(P \to Q), (R \to S), (P \lor R) \vdash (Q \lor S)$$

where $\vdash$ is a [metalogical](wikipedia://en/387934) symbol meaning that $Q \lor S$ is a [syntactic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) of $P \to Q$, $R \to S$, and $P \lor R$ in some [logical system](wikipedia://en/396102);

and expressed as a [truth-functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth-functional) [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335) or [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of propositional logic:

$$(((P \to Q) \land (R \to S)) \land (P \lor R)) \to (Q \lor S)$$

where $P$, $Q$, $R$ and $S$ are propositions expressed in some [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102).

## Natural language example {#Natural_language_example}

If I win a million dollars, I will donate it to an orphanage.

If my friend wins a million dollars, he will donate it to a wildlife fund.

Either I win a million dollars, or my friend wins a million dollars, or both.

Therefore, either an orphanage will get a million dollars, or a wildlife fund will get a million dollars, or both.

The dilemma derives its name because of the transfer of disjunctive operator.
