# Argumentation theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Argumentation theory"}

*Image: Whatley.png.* Example of an early [argument map](wikipedia://en/6190251), from [Richard Whately](wikipedia://en/207580)'s *Elements of Logic* (1852 edition)

**Argumentation theory** is the [interdisciplinary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary) study of how conclusions can be supported or undermined by [premises](wikipedia://en/7993337) through [logical reasoning](wikipedia://en/637990). With historical origins in [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), [dialectic](wikipedia://en/52813) and [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447), argumentation theory includes the arts and sciences of civil debate, [dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogue), [conversation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conversation) and [persuasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persuasion). It studies rules of [inference](wikipedia://en/317465), [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and procedural rules in both [Artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) and real-world settings.[^o2][^o4]

:::

Argumentation includes various forms of dialogue such as [deliberation](wikipedia://en/151350) and [negotiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negotiation) which are concerned with collaborative [decision-making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision-making) procedures.[^o3] It also encompasses [eristic](wikipedia://en/5725967) dialogue, the branch of social debate in which victory over an opponent is the primary goal, and [didactic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Didactic) dialogue used for teaching.[^o4] This discipline also studies the means by which people can express and rationally resolve or at least manage their disagreements.[^o5]

Argumentation is a daily occurrence, such as in [public debate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_debate), [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science), and [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law).[^o6] For example in [law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law), in [courts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Court) by the [judge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judge), the parties and the [prosecutor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosecutor), in presenting and testing the [validity](wikipedia://en/21304742) of [evidences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence_%28law%29). Also, argumentation scholars study the *post hoc* [rationalizations](wikipedia://en/1706965) by which organizational actors try to justify decisions they have made [irrationally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrationality).

Argumentation is one of four [rhetorical modes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhetorical_modes) (also known as *modes of discourse*), along with [exposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exposition_%28literary_technique%29), [description](wikipedia://en/19759799), and [narration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Narrative_mode).

## Key components of argumentation {#Key_components_of_argumentation}

Some key components of argumentation are:

- Understanding and identifying arguments, either explicit or implied, and the goals of the participants in the different types of dialogue.
- Identifying the [premises](wikipedia://en/7993337) from which conclusions are derived.
- Establishing the "[burden of proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophical_burden_of_proof)" – determining who made the initial claim and is thus responsible for providing evidence why their position merits acceptance.
- For the one carrying the "burden of proof", the advocate, to marshal [evidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evidence) for their position in order to convince or force the opponent's acceptance. The method by which this is accomplished is producing valid, sound, and cogent arguments, devoid of weaknesses, and not easily attacked.
- In a debate, fulfillment of the burden of proof creates a burden of rejoinder. One must try to identify faulty reasoning in the opponent's argument, to attack the reasons/premises of the argument, to provide counterexamples if possible, to identify any [fallacies](wikipedia://en/53986), and to show why a valid conclusion cannot be derived from the reasons provided for their argument.

For example, consider the following exchange, illustrating the [No true Scotsman](wikipedia://en/17694) fallacy:

Argument: "No Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge."

Reply: "But my friend Angus, who is a Scotsman, likes sugar with his porridge."

Rebuttal: "Well perhaps, but no true Scotsman puts sugar on his porridge."

In this dialogue, the proposer first offers a premise, the premise is challenged by the interlocutor, and so the proposer offers a modification of the premise, which is designed only to evade the challenge provided.

## Internal structure of arguments {#Internal_structure_of_arguments}

Typically an argument has an internal structure, comprising the following:

1. a set of assumptions or [premises](wikipedia://en/7993337),
1. a method of reasoning or deduction, and
1. a conclusion or point.

An argument has one or more premises and one conclusion.

Often classical logic is used as the method of reasoning so that the conclusion follows logically from the assumptions or support. One challenge is that if the set of assumptions is inconsistent then anything can follow logically from inconsistency. Therefore, it is common to insist that the set of assumptions be consistent. It is also good practice to require the set of assumptions to be the minimal set, with respect to set inclusion, necessary to infer the consequent. Such arguments are called MINCON arguments, short for minimal consistent. Such argumentation has been applied to the fields of law and medicine.

A non-classical approach to argumentation investigates abstract arguments, where 'argument' is considered a primitive term, so no internal structure of arguments is taken into account.

## Types of dialogue {#Types_of_dialogue}

In its most common form, argumentation involves an individual and an interlocutor or opponent engaged in dialogue, each contending differing positions and trying to persuade each other, but there are various types of dialogue:[^o56]

- [Persuasion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persuasion) dialogue aims to resolve conflicting points of view of different positions.
- [Negotiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negotiation) aims to resolve conflicts of interests by cooperation and dealmaking.
- [Inquiry](wikipedia://en/3657365) aims to resolve general ignorance by the growth of knowledge.
- [Deliberation](wikipedia://en/151350) aims to resolve a need to take action by reaching a decision.
- [Information seeking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_seeking) aims to reduce one party's ignorance by requesting information from another party that is in a position to know something.
- [Eristic](wikipedia://en/5725967) aims to resolve a situation of antagonism through verbal fighting.

## Argumentation and the grounds of knowledge {#Argumentation_and_the_grounds_of_knowledge}

Argumentation theory had its origins in [foundationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundationalism), a theory of knowledge ([epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology)) in the field of [philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy). It sought to find the grounds for claims in the forms (logic) and materials (factual laws) of a universal system of knowledge. The [dialectical](wikipedia://en/52813) method was made famous by [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954) and his use of [Socrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socrates) critically questioning various characters and historical figures. But argument scholars gradually rejected [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s systematic philosophy and the [idealism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealism) in Plato and [Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kant). They questioned and ultimately discarded the idea that argument premises take their soundness from formal philosophical systems. The field thus broadened.[^o11]

One of the original contributors to this trend was the philosopher [Chaïm Perelman](wikipedia://en/3784254), who together with [Lucie Olbrechts-Tyteca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucie_Olbrechts-Tyteca) introduced the French term *la nouvelle rhetorique* in 1958 to describe an approach to argument which is not reduced to application of formal rules of inference. Perelman's view of argumentation is much closer to a [juridical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Juridical) one, in which rules for presenting evidence and rebuttals play an important role.

Karl R. Wallace's seminal essay, "The Substance of Rhetoric: Good Reasons" in the *Quarterly Journal of Speech* (1963) 44, led many scholars to study "marketplace argumentation" – the ordinary arguments of ordinary people. The seminal essay on marketplace argumentation is Ray Lynn Anderson's and C. David Mortensen's "Logic and Marketplace Argumentation" *Quarterly Journal of Speech* 53 (1967): 143–150.[^o12][^o13] This line of thinking led to a natural alliance with late developments in the [sociology of knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_knowledge).[^o14] Some scholars drew connections with recent developments in philosophy, namely the [pragmatism](wikipedia://en/95170) of [John Dewey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Dewey) and [Richard Rorty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Rorty). Rorty has called this shift in emphasis "the [linguistic turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistic_turn)".

In this new hybrid approach argumentation is used with or without [empirical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empirical) evidence to establish convincing conclusions about issues which are moral, scientific, epistemic, or of a nature in which science alone cannot answer. Out of pragmatism and many intellectual developments in the humanities and social sciences, "non-philosophical" argumentation theories grew which located the formal and material grounds of arguments in particular intellectual fields. These theories include [informal logic](wikipedia://en/14122398), [social epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_epistemology), [ethnomethodology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnomethodology), [speech acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech_acts), the sociology of knowledge, the [sociology of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sociology_of_science), and [social psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_psychology). These new theories are not non-logical or anti-logical. They find logical coherence in most communities of discourse. These theories are thus often labeled "sociological" in that they focus on the social grounds of knowledge.

## Kinds of argumentation {#Kinds_of_argumentation}

### Conversational argumentation {#Conversational_argumentation}

The study of naturally occurring conversation arose from the field of sociolinguistics. It is usually called *conversation analysis* (CA). Inspired by ethnomethodology, it was developed in the late 1960s and early 1970s principally by the sociologist [Harvey Sacks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvey_Sacks) and, among others, his close associates [Emanuel Schegloff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emanuel_Schegloff) and [Gail Jefferson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gail_Jefferson). Sacks died early in his career, but his work was championed by others in his field, and CA has now become an established force in sociology, anthropology, linguistics, speech-communication and psychology.[^o17] It is particularly influential in [interactional sociolinguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interactional_sociolinguistics), discourse analysis and discursive psychology, as well as being a coherent discipline in its own right. Recently CA techniques of sequential analysis have been employed by phoneticians to explore the fine [phonetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phonetics) details of speech.

Empirical studies and theoretical formulations by Sally Jackson and Scott Jacobs, and several generations of their students, have described argumentation as a form of managing conversational disagreement within communication contexts and systems that naturally prefer agreement.

### Mathematical argumentation {#Mathematical_argumentation}

The basis of mathematical truth has been the subject of long debate. [Frege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frege) in particular sought to demonstrate (see Gottlob Frege, [The Foundations of Arithmetic](wikipedia://en/8983001), 1884, and *[Begriffsschrift](wikipedia://en/900125)*, 1879) that arithmetical truths can be derived from purely logical axioms and therefore are, in the end, [logical truths](wikipedia://en/3986852).[^o19] The project was developed by [Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) and [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) in their *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)*. If an argument can be cast in the form of sentences in [symbolic logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_logic), then it can be tested by the application of accepted proof procedures. This was carried out for arithmetic using [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005), and the foundation most commonly used for most modern mathematics is [Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zermelo-Fraenkel_set_theory), with or without the [Axiom of Choice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_of_Choice). Be that as it may, an argument in mathematics, as in any other discipline, can be considered valid only if it can be shown that it cannot have true premises and a false conclusion.

### Scientific argumentation {#Scientific_argumentation}

Perhaps the most radical statement of the social grounds of scientific knowledge appears in Alan G.Gross's *The Rhetoric of Science* (Cambridge: Harvard University Press, 1990). Gross holds that science is rhetorical "without remainder",[^o21] meaning that scientific knowledge itself cannot be seen as an idealized ground of knowledge. Scientific knowledge is produced rhetorically, meaning that it has special epistemic authority only insofar as its communal methods of verification are trustworthy. This thinking represents an almost complete rejection of the [foundationalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundationalism) on which argumentation was first based.

### Interpretive argumentation {#Interpretive_argumentation}

Interpretive argumentation is a dialogical process in which participants [explore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divergent_thinking) and/or [resolve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_thinking) interpretations often of a [text](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_%28literary_theory%29) of any medium containing significant [ambiguity](wikipedia://en/677) in meaning.

Interpretive argumentation is pertinent to [the humanities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humanities), [hermeneutics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermeneutics), [literary theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Literary_theory), [linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linguistics), [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107), [pragmatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pragmatics), [semiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotics), [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211) and [aesthetics](wikipedia://en/159211#Aesthetics). Topics in [conceptual interpretation](wikipedia://en/26279594#Conceptual_interpretations) include [aesthetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetic_interpretation), [judicial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_interpretation), [logical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_interpretation) and [religious](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Religious_interpretation) interpretation. Topics in [scientific interpretation](wikipedia://en/26279594#Scientific_interpretation) include [scientific modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_modeling).

### Legal argumentation {#Legal_argumentation}

#### By lawyers {#By_lawyers}

Legal arguments are spoken presentations to a [judge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judge) or [appellate court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Appellate_court) by a [lawyer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawyer), or parties when representing themselves of the legal reasons why they should prevail. Oral argument at the appellate level accompanies written briefs, which also advance the argument of each party in the legal dispute. A closing argument, or summation, is the concluding statement of each party's counsel reiterating the important arguments for the [trier of fact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trier_of_fact), often the jury, in a court case. A closing argument occurs after the presentation of evidence.

#### By judges {#By_judges}

A [judicial opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_opinion) or [legal opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_opinion) is in certain [jurisdictions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction) a written explanation by a [judge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judge) or group of judges that accompanies an order or ruling in a case, laying out the [rationale (justification)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justification_%28epistemology%29) and [legal principles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_doctrine) for the ruling.[^o27] It cites the [decision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Case_law) reached to resolve the dispute. A judicial opinion usually includes the [reasons](wikipedia://en/10822467) behind the decision.[^o27] Where there are three or more judges, it may take the form of a [majority opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Majority_opinion), [minority opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minority_opinion) or a [concurring opinion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concurring_opinion).[^o28]

### Political argumentation {#Political_argumentation}

Political arguments are used by academics, media pundits, candidates for political office and government officials. Political arguments are also used by citizens in ordinary interactions to comment about and understand political events.[^o30] The rationality of the public is a major question in this line of research. Political scientist [Samuel L. Popkin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_L._Popkin) coined the expression "[low information voters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_information_voter)" to describe most voters who know very little about politics or the world in general.

In practice, a "[low information voter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_information_voter)" may not be aware of legislation that their representative has [sponsored](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sponsor_%28legislative%29) in Congress. A low-information voter may base their [ballot box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballot_box) decision on a media sound-bite, or a flier received in the mail. It is possible for a media sound-bite or campaign flier to present a political position for the [incumbent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incumbent) candidate that completely contradicts the legislative action taken in the Capitol on behalf of the constituents. It may only take a small percentage of the overall voting group who base their decision on the inaccurate information to form a voter bloc large enough to swing an overall election result. When this happens, the constituency at large may have been duped or fooled. Nevertheless, the election result is legal and confirmed. Savvy [Political consultants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_consulting) will take advantage of low-information voters and sway their votes with [disinformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disinformation) and [fake news](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fake_news) because it can be easier and sufficiently effective. [Fact checkers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact_checker) have come about in recent years to help counter the effects of such campaign tactics.

## Psychological aspects {#Psychological_aspects}

[Psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) has long studied the non-logical aspects of argumentation. For example, studies have shown that [simple repetition of an idea](wikipedia://en/224553) is often a more effective method of argumentation than appeals to reason. [Propaganda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propaganda) often utilizes repetition.[^o32] "Repeat a lie often enough and it becomes the truth" is a law of propaganda often attributed to the [Nazi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nazism) politician [Joseph Goebbels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Goebbels). Nazi rhetoric has been studied extensively as, inter alia, a repetition campaign.

Empirical studies of communicator credibility and attractiveness, sometimes labeled *charisma,* have also been tied closely to empirically occurring arguments. Such studies bring argumentation within the ambit of persuasion theory and practice. In the moment emotion can have a very rational impact within an argument as many can use values or beliefs to make an emotional appeal changing the possible response the argument may convey.[^o33] Focusing on body movement and vocal delivery is also a major part to using emotions as appeals since a simple body movement can say a lot during a major discussion in the way it conveys the argument.[^o34]

Some psychologists such as William J. McGuire believe that the [syllogism](wikipedia://en/48046) is the basic unit of human reasoning. They have produced a large body of empirical work around McGuire's famous title "A Syllogistic Analysis of Cognitive Relationships".[^o35] A central line of this way of thinking is that logic is contaminated by psychological variables such as "wishful thinking", in which subjects confound the likelihood of predictions with the desirability of the predictions. People hear what they want to hear and see what they expect to see. If planners want something to happen they see it as likely to happen. If they hope something will not happen, they see it as unlikely to happen. Thus smokers think that they personally will avoid cancer, promiscuous people practice unsafe sex, and teenagers drive recklessly. This being said, while using imagery and visuals may be compelling it can often backfire as it may turn legal judgment into a spectacle causing unfairness in the courtroom.[^o36][^o34]

## Theories {#Theories}

### Argument fields {#Argument_fields}

[Stephen Toulmin](wikipedia://en/1543748), [Charles Arthur Willard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Arthur_Willard) and Quintilian[^o39] have championed the idea of argument fields, the former drawing upon [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741)'s notion of [language games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language-game), (Sprachspiel) the latter drawing from communication and argumentation theory, sociology, political science, and social epistemology. Quintilian, a first-century Roman rhetorician and teacher best known for his work *[Institutio Oratoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutio_Oratoria)*, emphasized that persuasive arguments must be adapted to the audience and social context in which they are delivered.[^o39] For Toulmin, the term "field" designates discourses within which arguments and factual claims are grounded.[^o40] For Willard, the term "field" is interchangeable with "community", "audience", or "readership".[^o41] Similarly, G. Thomas Goodnight has studied "spheres" of argument and sparked a large literature created by younger scholars responding to or using his ideas.[^o42] The general tenor of these field theories is that the premises of arguments take their meaning from social communities.[^o43]

### Stephen E. Toulmin's contributions {#Stephen_E._Toulmin's_contributions}

One of the most influential theorists of argumentation was the philosopher and educator [Stephen Toulmin](wikipedia://en/1543748), who is known for creating the [Toulmin model of argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toulmin_model_of_argument). His book *[The Uses of Argument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Uses_of_Argument)* is regarded as a seminal contribution to argumentation theory.[^o45]

### Pragma-dialectics {#Pragma-dialectics}

Scholars at the [University of Amsterdam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Amsterdam) in the Netherlands have pioneered a rigorous modern version of [dialectic](wikipedia://en/52813) under the name *pragma-dialectics*. The intuitive idea is to formulate clear-cut rules that, if followed, will yield reasonable discussion and sound conclusions. [Frans H. van Eemeren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frans_H._van_Eemeren), the late [Rob Grootendorst](wikipedia://en/27220545), and many of their students and co-authors have produced a large body of work expounding this idea.

The dialectical conception of reasonableness is given by ten rules for critical discussion, all being instrumental for achieving a resolution of the difference of opinion (from Van Eemeren, Grootendorst, & Snoeck Henkemans, 2002, p. 182–183). The theory postulates this as an ideal model, and not something one expects to find as an empirical fact. The model can however serve as an important [heuristic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heuristic) and critical tool for testing how reality approximates this ideal and point to where discourse goes wrong, that is, when the rules are violated. Any such violation will constitute a [fallacy](wikipedia://en/53986). Albeit not primarily focused on fallacies, pragma-dialectics provides a systematic approach to deal with them in a coherent way.

Van Eemeren and Grootendorst identified four stages of argumentative dialogue. These stages can be regarded as an argument protocol. In a somewhat loose interpretation, the stages are as follows:

- Confrontation stage: Presentation of the difference of opinion, such as a debate question or a political disagreement.
- Opening stage: Agreement on material and procedural starting points, the mutually acceptable common ground of facts and beliefs, and the rules to be followed during the discussion (such as, how evidence is to be presented, and determination of closing conditions).
- Argumentation stage: Presentation of reasons for and against the standpoint(s) at issue, through application of logical and common-sense principles according to the agreed-upon rules
- Concluding stage: Determining whether the standpoint has withstood reasonable criticism, and accepting it is justified. This occurs when the termination conditions are met (Among these could be, for example, a time limitation or the determination of an arbiter.)

Van Eemeren and Grootendorst provide a detailed list of rules that must be applied at each stage of the protocol. Moreover, in the account of argumentation given by these authors, there are specified roles of protagonist and antagonist in the protocol which are determined by the conditions which set up the need for argument.

### Quintilian Contributions {#Quintilian_Contributions}

[Quintilian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintilian) (Marcus Fabius Quintilian) was a Roman rhetorician, teacher and lawyer to the first century CE who became a very influential person in classical rhetoric. Quintilian's most important work was the *[Insititio Oratoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutio_Oratoria)*, a book that outlines the education of an Orator. Quintilian explains that rhetorical skills and persuasion depend on the morals for effective persuasion. He explains the importance of emotional appeal, arguing that speakers must vividly express emotion to then effectively communicate so the audience can be moved by sympathy, compassion and anger.[^o51]

In Quintilian's book *[Insitutio Oratoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institutio_Oratoria)* he explained his theory on the six rules of emotional appeal that truly make arguments effective.[^o51] Quintilian has six rules:

**Rule One:** the advocate should know the emotions they want to present and what actions they must take to be able to convey them without misunderstanding.[^o52]

**Rule two:** the advocate should be able to feel the emotion themselves.[^o52]

**Rule three:** the advocate must be able to describe what exactly they are speaking of as if they were there.[^o52]

**Rule four:** the advocate must be “awaken” meaning whatever emotion is being used should not over power the argument itself.[^o52]

**Rule five:** the advocate should follow along with what the other person using emotion is doing and saying in order for the emotion to have an effect.[^o52]

**Rule six:** the advocate should always speak not in his own voice but rather with that of the voice he is arguing in. Based on Quintilian by following these rules during your argument it will make it more effective when it comes to appealing the emotion in the court rooms and general arguments.[^o52]

### Walton's logical argumentation method {#Walton's_logical_argumentation_method}

[Douglas N. Walton](wikipedia://en/5725671) developed a distinctive philosophical theory of logical argumentation built around a set of practical methods to help a user identify, analyze and evaluate arguments in everyday conversational discourse and in more structured areas such as debate, law and scientific fields.[^o54] There are four main components: [argumentation schemes](wikipedia://en/61290799),[^o55] dialogue structures, [argument mapping](wikipedia://en/6190251) tools, and formal argumentation systems. The method uses the notion of commitment in dialogue as the fundamental tool for the analysis and evaluation of argumentation rather than the notion of belief.[^o56] Commitments are statements that the agent has expressed or formulated, and has pledged to carry out, or has publicly asserted. According to the commitment model, agents interact with each other in a dialogue in which each takes its turn to contribute speech acts. The dialogue framework uses critical questioning as a way of testing plausible explanations and finding weak points in an argument that raise doubt concerning the acceptability of the argument.

Walton's logical argumentation model took a view of proof and justification different from [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211)'s dominant [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology), which was based on a [justified true belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justified_true_belief) framework.[^o57] In the logical argumentation approach, knowledge is seen as form of belief commitment firmly fixed by an argumentation procedure that tests the evidence on both sides, and uses standards of proof to determine whether a proposition qualifies as knowledge. In this evidence-based approach, knowledge must be seen as [defeasible](wikipedia://en/2628057).

## Artificial intelligence {#Artificial_intelligence}

*Image: Intelligent assistant for argumentative support and arguments inquiry.png.* Structured debates from platforms like [Kialo](wikipedia://en/74010797) could be used for "artificial deliberative agents" (ADAs) or computational reasoning.[^o59][^o60]

*Image: Basic design of artificial deliberative agents (ADAs) for argumentation.png.* Example of an ADA contributing missing information to a debate via crawled Kialo data and selected based [on the prior conversation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing) and crawled [argument weight ratings](wikipedia://en/74010797#collective_determination_of_argument_weights)[^o60]

Efforts have been made within the field of [artificial intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_intelligence) to perform and analyze argumentation with computers. Argumentation has been used to provide a proof-theoretic [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) for [non-monotonic logic](wikipedia://en/341086), starting with the influential work of Dung (1995).[^o61] Computational argumentation systems have found particular application in domains where formal logic and classical [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216) are unable to capture the richness of reasoning, domains such as law and medicine. In *Elements of Argumentation*, Philippe Besnard and Anthony Hunter show how classical logic-based techniques can be used to capture key elements of practical argumentation.[^o62]

In computer science the ArgMAS workshop series (Argumentation in Multi-Agent Systems), the CMNA workshop series, and the COMMA Conference, are annual events that attract participants from every continent.[^o63][^o64] The journal *Argument & Computation* is dedicated to exploring the intersection between argumentation and computer science.[^o65] ArgMining is a workshop series dedicated specifically to the related [argument mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argument_mining) task.[^o66]

Data from the collaborative structured online argumentation platform [Kialo](wikipedia://en/74010797) has been used to train and to evaluate [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing) AI systems like [BERT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BERT_%28language_model%29). This work includes argument extraction, conclusion generation,[^n1] argument form quality assessment,[^o67] machine argumentative debate generation or participation,[^n2][^n3][^n4] surfacing relevant previously overlooked viewpoints or arguments,[^n2][^n3] argumentative writing support[^o68] (including sentence attackability scores),[^o69] automatic real-time evaluation of how truthful or convincing a sentence is (similar to [fact-checking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fact-checking)),[^o69] [language model fine tuning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fine-tuning_%28machine_learning%29)[^o70][^n4] (including for [chatbots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chatbot)),[^o71][^o72] argument impact prediction, argument classification and polarity prediction.[^o73][^o74]
