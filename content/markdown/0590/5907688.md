# Digital topology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Digital topology"}

**Digital topology** deals with properties and features of [two-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional) (2D) or [three-dimensional](wikipedia://en/3054853) (3D) [digital images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_images) that correspond to [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) properties (e.g., [connectedness](wikipedia://en/87141)) or topological features (e.g., [boundaries](wikipedia://en/72824)) of objects.

:::

Concepts and results of digital topology are used to specify and justify important (low-level) [image analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_analysis) algorithms, including algorithms for [thinning](wikipedia://en/35638823), border or surface tracing, counting of components or tunnels, or region-filling.

## History {#History}

Digital topology was first studied in the late 1960s by the [computer image analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_image_analysis) researcher [Azriel Rosenfeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Azriel_Rosenfeld) (1931–2004), whose publications on the subject played a major role in establishing and developing the field. The term "digital topology" was itself invented by Rosenfeld, who used it in a 1973 publication for the first time.

A related work called the [grid cell topology](wikipedia://en/5909220), which could be considered as a link to classic [combinatorial topology](wikipedia://en/363400), appeared in the book of [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245) and [Heinz Hopf](wikipedia://en/543082), Topologie I (1935). Rosenfeld *et al.* proposed digital connectivity such as 4-connectivity and 8-connectivity in two dimensions as well as 6-connectivity and 26-connectivity in three dimensions. The labeling method for inferring a connected component was studied in the 1970s. Theodosios Pavlidis (1982) suggested the use of graph-theoretic algorithms such as the [depth-first search](wikipedia://en/97034) method for finding connected components. [Vladimir A. Kovalevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Antonovich_Kovalevsky) (1989) extended the Alexandrov–Hopf 2D grid cell topology to three and higher dimensions. He also proposed (2008) a more general axiomatic theory of [locally finite topological spaces](wikipedia://en/11611098) and [abstract cell complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_cell_complexes) formerly suggested by [Ernst Steinitz](wikipedia://en/3966685) (1908). It is the [Alexandrov topology](wikipedia://en/542198). The book from 2008 contains new definitions of topological balls and spheres independent of a metric and numerous applications to digital image analysis.

In the early 1980s, [digital surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_surface) were studied. David Morgenthaler and Rosenfeld (1981) gave a mathematical definition of surfaces in three-dimensional digital space. This definition contains a total of nine types of digital surfaces. The [digital manifold](wikipedia://en/27123668) was studied in the 1990s. A recursive definition of the digital k-manifold was proposed intuitively by Chen and Zhang in 1993.[^o3] Many applications were found in image processing and computer vision.

## Basic results {#Basic_results}

A basic (early) result in digital topology says that 2D binary images require the alternative use of 4- or 8-adjacency or "[pixel connectivity](wikipedia://en/21569386)" (for "object" or "non-object" [pixels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixels)) to ensure the basic topological duality of separation and connectedness. This alternative use corresponds to open or closed sets in the 2D [grid cell topology](wikipedia://en/5909220), and the result generalizes to 3D: the alternative use of 6- or 26-adjacency corresponds to open or closed sets in the 3D grid cell topology. Grid cell topology also applies to multilevel (e.g., color) 2D or 3D images, for example based on a total order of possible image values and applying a 'maximum-label rule' (see the book by Klette and Rosenfeld, 2004).

Digital topology is highly related to [combinatorial topology](wikipedia://en/363400). The main differences between them are: (1) digital topology mainly studies digital objects that are formed by grid cells (the cells of integer lattices), rather than more general [cell complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cell_complex), and (2) digital topology also deals with non-Jordan manifolds.

A **combinatorial manifold** is a kind of manifold which is a discretization of a manifold. It usually means a [piecewise linear manifold](wikipedia://en/2174184) made by [simplicial complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_complexes). A [digital manifold](wikipedia://en/27123668) is a special kind of combinatorial manifold which is defined in digital space i.e. grid cell space.

A digital form of the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229) is: Let *M* be a closed digital 2D [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) in direct adjacency (i.e., a (6,26)-surface in 3D). The formula for genus is

$$g = 1 + (M_{5} + 2 M_{6} - M_{3}) / 8,$$

where $M_i$ indicates the set of surface-points each of which has *i* adjacent points on the surface (Chen and Rong, ICPR 2008). If *M* is simply connected, i.e., $g=0$, then $M_3= 8+ M_5+ 2M_6$. (See also [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420).)
