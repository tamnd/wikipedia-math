# Polignac's conjecture

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polignac's conjecture"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Polignac's conjecture** was made by [Alphonse de Polignac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphonse_de_Polignac) in 1849 and states:[^o2]

:::

For any positive [even number](wikipedia://en/143135) *n*, there are infinitely many [prime gaps](wikipedia://en/5075259) of size *n*. In other words: There are infinitely many cases of two consecutive [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) with difference *n*.[^o3]

Although the conjecture has not yet been proven or disproven for any given value of *n*, in 2013 an important breakthrough was made by [Yitang Zhang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zhang_Yitang) who proved that there are infinitely many [prime gaps](wikipedia://en/5075259) of size *n* for some value of *n* \< 70,000,000.[^o4][^o5] Later that year, [James Maynard](wikipedia://en/42512102) announced a related breakthrough which proved that there are infinitely many prime gaps of some size less than or equal to 600.[^o6] As of April 14, 2014, one year after Zhang's announcement, according to the [Polymath project wiki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath_projects), *n* has been reduced to 246.[^o7] Further, assuming the [Elliott–Halberstam conjecture](wikipedia://en/1898427) and its generalized form, the Polymath project wiki states that *n* has been reduced to 12 and 6, respectively.[^o8]

For *n* = 2, it is the [twin prime conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Twin_prime_conjecture). For *n* = 4, it says there are infinitely many [cousin primes](wikipedia://en/343085) (*p*, *p* + 4). For *n* = 6, it says there are infinitely many [sexy primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexy_prime) (*p*, *p* + 6) with no prime between *p* and *p* + 6.

[Dickson's conjecture](wikipedia://en/31611712) generalizes Polignac's conjecture to cover all [prime constellations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_constellations).

In 1966, [Chen Jing-run](wikipedia://en/352985) proved a slightly weaker version of Polignac's conjecture: there are infinitely many primes *p* such that *p+k* is either prime or a [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525) number with at most 2 prime factors (a [semiprime](wikipedia://en/339542)).[^o9]

## Conjectured density {#Conjectured_density}

Let $\pi_n(x)$ for even *n* be the number of prime gaps of size *n* below *x*.

The first [Hardy–Littlewood conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Littlewood_conjecture) says the asymptotic density is of form

$$\pi_n(x) \sim 2 C_n \frac{x}{(\ln x)^2} \sim 2 C_n \int_2^x {dt \over (\ln t)^2}$$

where *C*<sub>*n*</sub> is a function of *n*, and $\sim$ means that the quotient of two expressions [tends to](wikipedia://en/26551602) 1 as *x* approaches infinity.[^o11]

*C*<sub>2</sub> is the twin prime constant

$$C_2 = \prod_{p\ge 3} \frac{p(p-2)}{(p-1)^2} \approx 0.66016 18158 46869 57392 78121 10014\dots$$

where the product extends over all prime numbers *p* ≥ 3.

*C<sub>n</sub>* is *C*<sub>2</sub> multiplied by a number which depends on the odd prime factors *q* of *n*:

$$C_n = C_2 \prod_{q|n} \frac{q-1}{q-2}.$$

For example, *C*<sub>4</sub> = *C*<sub>2</sub> and *C*<sub>6</sub> = 2*C*<sub>2</sub>. Twin primes have the same conjectured density as cousin primes, and half that of sexy primes.

Note that each odd prime factor *q* of *n* increases the conjectured density compared to twin primes by a factor of $\tfrac{q-1}{q-2}$. A [heuristic argument](wikipedia://en/43491) follows. It relies on some unproven assumptions so the conclusion remains a conjecture. The chance of a random odd prime *q* dividing either *a* or *a* + 2 in a random "potential" twin prime pair is $\tfrac{2}{q}$, since *q* divides one of the *q* numbers from *a* to *a* + *q* − 1. Now assume *q* divides *n* and consider a potential prime pair (*a*, *a* + *n*). *q* divides *a* + *n* if and only if *q* divides *a*, and the chance of that is $\tfrac{1}{q}$. The chance of (*a*, *a* + *n*) being free from the factor *q*, divided by the chance that (*a*, *a* + *2*) is free from *q*, then becomes $\tfrac{q-1}{q}$ divided by $\tfrac{q-2}{q}$. This equals $\tfrac{q-1}{q-2}$ which transfers to the conjectured prime density. In the case of *n* = 6, the argument simplifies to: If *a* is a random number then 3 has a probability of 2/3 of dividing *a* or *a* + 2, but only a probability of 1/3 of dividing *a* and *a* + 6, so the latter pair is conjectured twice as likely to both be prime.
