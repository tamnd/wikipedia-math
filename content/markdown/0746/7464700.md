# Theorem of corresponding states

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Theorem of corresponding states"}

According to van der Waals, the **theorem of corresponding states** (or **principle/law of corresponding states**) indicates that all [fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid), when compared at the same [reduced temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_temperature) and [reduced pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_pressure), have approximately the same [compressibility factor](wikipedia://en/2131266) and all deviate from ideal gas behavior to about the same degree.[^o2][^o3]

:::

[Material constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Material_constant) that vary for each type of material are eliminated, in a recast reduced form of a [constitutive equation](wikipedia://en/1034699). The reduced variables are defined in terms of [critical variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_variables).

The principle originated with the work of [Johannes Diderik van der Waals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Diderik_van_der_Waals) in about 1873[^o4] when he used the [critical temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_temperature) and [critical pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_pressure) to derive a universal property of all fluids that follow the [van der Waals equation](wikipedia://en/206064) of state. It predicts a value of $3/8 = 0.375$ that is found to be an overestimate when compared to real gases.

[Edward A. Guggenheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_A._Guggenheim) used the phrase "Principle of Corresponding States" in an oft-cited paper to describe the phenomenon where different systems have very similar behaviors when near a [critical point](wikipedia://en/1951419).[^o5]

There are many examples of non-ideal gas models which satisfy this theorem, such as the van der Waals model, the Dieterici model, and so on, that can be found on [the page on real gases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_gas).

## Compressibility factor at the critical point {#Compressibility_factor_at_the_critical_point}

The compressibility factor at the critical point, which is defined as $Z_c=\frac{P_c v_c \mu}{R T_c}$, where the subscript $c$ indicates physical quantities measured at the [critical point](wikipedia://en/1951419), is predicted to be a constant independent of substance by many equations of state.

The table below for a selection of gases uses the following conventions:

- $T_c$: [critical temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_temperature) \[K\]
- $P_c$: [critical pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_pressure) \[Pa\]
- $v_c$: [critical specific volume](wikipedia://en/1951419) \[m<sup>3</sup>⋅kg<sup>−1</sup>\]
- $R$: [gas constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_constant) (8.314 [J](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joule)⋅[K](wikipedia://en/19593121)<sup>−1</sup>⋅[mol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mole_%28unit%29)<sup>−1</sup>)
- $\mu$: [Molar mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molar_mass) \[kg⋅mol<sup>−1</sup>\]
