# Heresthetic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Heresthetic"}

**Heresthetic** is a theoretical approach within [political science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_science) that examines how political actors strategically [manipulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_manipulation) the structure of political situations, decision-making processes, and [agenda-setting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agenda-setting) to achieve favorable outcomes even when they lack majority support.[^o2] The term was coined by political scientist [William H. Riker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_H._Riker) in the 1980s, combining the Greek words "hairesis" (choosing) and "rhetoric" to describe tactics beyond mere persuasion.[^o3]

:::

Heresthetic belongs to the tradition of [positive political theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_political_theory), incorporating elements from [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924), [public choice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_choice_theory), [rational choice theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_choice_theory), and [social choice theory](wikipedia://en/1745670) to analyze strategic political behavior.[^o4] While [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447) focuses on persuading others to change their preferences, heresthetic involves restructuring the political environment to achieve victory using opponents' existing preferences. Common heresthetic strategies include dimension manipulation, [strategic voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_voting), [agenda control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agenda_control), and the introduction of new alternatives to split opposing coalitions.[^o5] The concept has been influential in understanding political maneuvering in [legislative politics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislative_politics), [electoral systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_systems), and [constitutional design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constitutional_design).

## Description {#Description}

Riker argues that herestheticians win because they compel or persuade others to join them in voting or political coalitions.[^o7] Heresthetic focuses both on the use of [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447) and political strategy. Riker argues that there are three vital components to heresthetic.[^o8]

1. **Agenda control**: political [agents](wikipedia://en/730742) may structure debate in ways that highlight favorable or eliminate undesirable [policy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Policy) alternatives.[^o9] Depending upon the order that policy options are discussed, debate can produce different outcomes, including outcomes that are not preferred by the heresthetician.[^o9] The order of choice matters.[^o10] Riker's concept of agenda control differs from conventional accounts of [agenda-setting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agenda-setting_theory) involving policy-makers, which focus on how policy-makers’ agendas are influenced by [exogenous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exogeny) factors, such as the media and public opinion.[^o11] Riker's focus is on how policy-makers affect the ordering of items on the [political agenda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_agenda), and how that ordering can be manipulated.[^o12]
1. **Strategic voting**: in deliberative settings herestheticians can take advantage of voting procedures to influence outcomes.[^o13] For example, agents may vote against their own interest in the short-term to secure a better long-term position. Another element of strategic voting is [vote trading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vote_trading).[^o14] Policy-makers may informally trade votes with others in exchange for future votes or benefits.[^o15] [Strategic voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tactical_voting) is common in legislative settings because of open rules and sequential voting procedures.
1. **Dimension manipulation**: herestheticians can manipulate the dimensionality of political decision-making by introducing new dimensions or modifying existing framing.[^o16][^o17] Dimension manipulation can either expand a decision-making space by reconfiguring acceptable topics of debate and policy alternatives, or shrink dimensions by eliminating certain topics or alternatives.[^o18] The manipulation of dimensions is important because once an agent manipulates framing, it cannot be removed from debate.[^o19]

These components allow herestheticians to manipulate political outcomes by structuring debate, rhetorically or structurally, to be more advantageous to their preferred position.[^o8]

## Example {#Example}

The [British Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Parliament) is scheduling upcoming referendum votes to determine [Scottish independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scottish_independence) from the [United Kingdom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Kingdom) and if the UK should remain a member of the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union). A [member of parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Member_of_parliament) who has influence on how the votes will be structured, has a preference that Scotland will remain in union with the UK and that the UK will leave the EU. Scottish independence and the UK remaining a member of the EU is an undesirable outcome for the MP. While polling indicates that a majority of Scottish voters do not support independence, voters are more likely to support independence if the UK leaves the EU.[^o22] In order to receive his preferred outcome, the MP seeks for the referendum votes to be held in a sequence in which Scottish independence is determined first, and then UK withdrawal from the EU. This voting sequence demonstrates how herestheticians can manipulate the decision-making process so they can win.
