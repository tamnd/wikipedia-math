# Cyclic quadrilateral

*Image: Cyclic quadrilateral.svg.* Examples of cyclic quadrilaterals

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cyclic quadrilateral"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **cyclic quadrilateral** or **inscribed quadrilateral** is a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) (four-sided [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621)) whose [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) all lie on a single [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), making the sides [chords](wikipedia://en/297947) of the circle. This circle is called the *circumcircle* or *circumscribed circle*, and the vertices are said to be *[concyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concyclic)*. The center of the circle and its radius are called the *circumcenter* and the *circumradius* respectively. Usually the quadrilateral is assumed to be [convex](wikipedia://en/660019), but there are also [crossed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crossed_quadrilateral) cyclic quadrilaterals. The formulas and properties given below are valid in the convex case.

:::

The word cyclic is from the [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) κύκλος (*kuklos*), which means "circle" or "wheel".

All [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) have a [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432), but not all quadrilaterals do. An example of a quadrilateral that cannot be cyclic is a non-square [rhombus](wikipedia://en/183884). The section [characterizations](wikipedia://en/194131#Characterizations) below states what [necessary and sufficient conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient_condition) a quadrilateral must satisfy to have a circumcircle.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

## Special cases {#Special_cases}

Any [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29), [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048), [isosceles trapezoid](wikipedia://en/511691), or [antiparallelogram](wikipedia://en/5817627) is cyclic. A [kite](wikipedia://en/84840) is cyclic [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it has two right angles – a [right kite](wikipedia://en/37263250). A [bicentric quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/23335118) is a cyclic quadrilateral that is also [tangential](wikipedia://en/9176412) and an [ex-bicentric quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/32791638#Ex-bicentric_quadrilateral) is a cyclic quadrilateral that is also [ex-tangential](wikipedia://en/32791638). A [harmonic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/61186948) is a cyclic quadrilateral in which the product of the lengths of opposite sides are equal.

:::

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

*Image: Sehnenviereck.svg.* A cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ with circumcenter $M$

### Circumcenter {#Circumcenter}

A convex quadrilateral is cyclic [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the four [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) bisectors to the sides are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157). This common point is the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter).[^o7]

### Supplementary angles {#Supplementary_angles}

*Image: cyclic\_quadrilateral\_supplementary\_angles\_visual\_proof.svg.* [Proof without words](wikipedia://en/18045517) using the [inscribed angle](wikipedia://en/462730) theorem that opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral are supplementary:$2\theta + 2\varphi = 360^{\circ} \therefore \theta + \varphi = 180^{\circ}$

A convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ is cyclic if and only if its opposite angles are [supplementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplementary_angle), that is[^o7][^o9]

$$\alpha + \gamma = \beta + \delta = \pi \ \text{radians}\ (= 180^{\circ}).$$

The direct theorem was Proposition 22 in Book 3 of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s [*Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107).[^o10] Equivalently, a convex quadrilateral is cyclic if and only if each [exterior angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_angle) is equal to the opposite [interior angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_angle).

In 1836 [Duncan Gregory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duncan_Gregory) generalized this result as follows: Given any convex cyclic $2n$-gon, then the two sums of *alternate* interior angles are each equal to $(n - 1)\pi$.[^o11] This result can be further generalized as follows: lf $A_{1}A_{2}...A_{2n}$ $(n > 1)$ is any cyclic $2n$-gon in which vertex $A_{i} \to A_{i+k}$ (vertex $A_{i}$ is joined to $A_{i+k}$), then the two sums of alternate interior angles are each equal to $m\pi$ (where $m = n - k$ and $k = 1, 2, 3, ...$ is the total turning).[^o12]

Taking the stereographic projection (half-angle tangent) of each angle, this can be re-expressed,

$$\dfrac { \tan{\frac{\alpha}{2}} + \tan{\frac{\gamma}{2}} } { 1 - \tan{\frac{\alpha}{2}} \tan{\frac{\gamma}{2}} } = \dfrac { \tan{\frac{\beta}{2}} + \tan{\frac{\delta}{2}} } { 1 - \tan{\frac{\beta}{2}} \tan{\frac{\delta}{2}} } = \infty.$$

Which implies that[^o13]

$$\tan{\frac{\alpha}{2}} \tan{\frac{\gamma}{2}} = \tan{\frac{\beta}{2}}{\tan \frac{\delta}{2}} = 1$$

### Angles between sides and diagonals {#Angles_between_sides_and_diagonals}

A convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ is cyclic if and only if an angle between a side and a [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251) is equal to the angle between the opposite side and the other diagonal.[^o15] That is, for example,

$$\angle ACB = \angle ADB.$$

### Pascal points {#Pascal_points}

*Image: Cyclic\_quadrilateral\_-\_pascal\_points.png.* $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral. $E$ is the point of intersection of the diagonals and $F$ is the point of intersection of the extensions of sides $BC$ and $AD$. $\omega$ is a circle whose diameter is the segment, $EF$. $P$ and $Q$ are Pascal points formed by the circle $\omega$. Triangles $\triangle FAB$ and $\triangle FCD$ are similar.

Other necessary and sufficient conditions for a convex quadrilateral $ABCD$ to be cyclic are: let $E$ be the point of intersection of the diagonals, let $F$ be the intersection point of the extensions of the sides $AD$ and $BC$, let $\omega$ be a circle whose diameter is the segment, $EF$, and let $P$ and $Q$ be [Pascal points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_points) on sides $AB$ and $CD$ formed by the circle $\omega$.

1. $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral if and only if points $P$ and $Q$ are collinear with the center $O$, of circle $\omega$.
1. $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral if and only if points $P$ and $Q$ are the midpoints of sides $AB$ and $CD$.[^o9]

### Intersection of diagonals {#Intersection_of_diagonals}

If two lines, one containing segment $AC$ and the other containing segment $BD$, intersect at $E$, then the four points $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ are concyclic if and only if[^o18]

$$\displaystyle AE \cdot EC = BE \cdot ED.$$

The intersection $E$ may be internal or external to the circle. In the former case, the cyclic quadrilateral is $ABCD$, and in the latter case, the cyclic quadrilateral is $ABDC$. When the intersection is internal, the equality states that the product of the segment lengths into which $E$ divides one diagonal equals that of the other diagonal. This is known as the *[intersecting chords theorem](wikipedia://en/19359050)* since the diagonals of the cyclic quadrilateral are chords of the circumcircle.

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Ptolemy's theorem"}

### Ptolemy's theorem {#Ptolemy's_theorem}

[Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971) expresses the product of the lengths of the two diagonals $e$ and $f$ of a cyclic quadrilateral as equal to the sum of the products of opposite sides:[^o36][^o9]

$$\displaystyle ef = ac + bd,$$

where $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ are the side lengths in order. The [converse](wikipedia://en/30977#Converse) is also true. That is, if this equation is satisfied in a convex quadrilateral, then a cyclic quadrilateral is formed.

:::

### Diagonal triangle {#Diagonal_triangle}

*Image: Nine-point circle of diagonal triangle.png.* $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral. $\triangle EFG$ is the diagonal triangle of $ABCD$. The point $T$ of intersection of the bimedians of $ABCD$ belongs to the nine-point circle of $\triangle EFG$.

In a convex quadrilateral $ABCD$, let $\triangle EFG$ be the diagonal triangle of $ABCD$ and let $\omega$ be the nine-point circle of $\triangle EFG$. $ABCD$ is cyclic if and only if the point of intersection of the bimedians of $ABCD$ belongs to the nine-point circle $\omega$.[^o22][^o23][^o9]

:::

## Area {#Area}

The [area](wikipedia://en/1209) $K$ of a cyclic quadrilateral with sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ is given by [Brahmagupta's formula](wikipedia://en/194129)[^o36]

$$K=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)} \,$$

where $s = \tfrac12(a + b + c + d)$ is the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617). This is a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) of [Bretschneider's formula](wikipedia://en/5615284) for the general quadrilateral, since opposite angles are supplementary in the cyclic case. If also $d = 0$, the cyclic quadrilateral becomes a triangle and the formula is reduced to [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123).

The cyclic quadrilateral has [maximal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) area among all quadrilaterals having the same side lengths (regardless of sequence). This is another corollary to Bretschneider's formula. It can also be proved using [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176).[^o25]

Four unequal lengths, each less than the sum of the other three, are the sides of each of three non-congruent cyclic quadrilaterals,[^o48] which by Brahmagupta's formula all have the same area. Specifically, for sides $a$, $b$, $c$, and $d$, side $a$ could be opposite any of side $b$, side $c$, or side $d$.

The area of a cyclic quadrilateral with successive sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, angle $A$ between sides $a$ and $d$, and angle $B$ between sides $a$ and $b$ can be expressed as[^o36]

$$K = \tfrac{1}{2}(ab+cd)\sin{B}$$

or

$$K = \tfrac{1}{2}(ad+bc)\sin{A}$$

or[^o36]

$$K = \tfrac{1}{2}(ac+bd)\sin{\theta}$$

where $\theta$ is either angle between the diagonals. Provided $A$ is not a right angle, the area can also be expressed as[^o36]

$$K = \tfrac{1}{4}(a^2-b^2-c^2+d^2)\tan{A}.$$

Another formula is[^o26]

$$\displaystyle K=2R^2\sin{A}\sin{B}\sin{\theta}$$

where $R$ is the radius of the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/161262). As a direct consequence,[^o27]

$$K\le 2R^2$$

where there is equality if and only if the quadrilateral is a square.

## Diagonals {#Diagonals}

In a cyclic quadrilateral with successive vertices $A$, $B$, $C$, $D$ and sides $a = AB$, $b = BC$, $c = CD$, and $d = DA$, the lengths of the diagonals $p = AC$ and $q = BD$ can be expressed in terms of the sides as[^o36][^o29][^o30]

$$\begin{aligned} p &= \sqrt{\frac{(ac+bd)(ad+bc)}{ab+cd}}\,, \\[2pt] q &= \sqrt{\frac{(ac+bd)(ab+cd)}{ad+bc}}\,, \end{aligned}$$

so showing [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971)

$$pq = ac+bd.$$

According to *Ptolemy's second theorem*,[^o36][^o29]

$$\frac {p}{q}= \frac{ad+bc}{ab+cd}$$

using the same notations as above.

For the sum of the diagonals we have the inequality[^o31]

$$p+q\ge 2\sqrt{ac+bd}.$$

Equality holds [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the diagonals have equal length, which can be proved using the [AM-GM inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AM-GM_inequality).

Moreover,[^o31]

$$(p+q)^2 \leq (a+c)^2+(b+d)^2.$$

In any convex quadrilateral, the two diagonals together partition the quadrilateral into four triangles; in a cyclic quadrilateral, opposite pairs of these four triangles are [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) to each other.

If $ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral where $AC$ meets $BD$ at $E$, then[^o32]

$$\frac{AE}{CE} = \frac{AB}{CB} \cdot \frac{AD}{CD}.$$

A set of sides that can form a cyclic quadrilateral can be arranged in any of three distinct sequences each of which can form a cyclic quadrilateral of the same area in the same circumcircle (the areas being the same according to Brahmagupta's area formula). Any two of these cyclic quadrilaterals have one diagonal length in common.[^o30]

## Angle formulas {#Angle_formulas}

For a cyclic quadrilateral with successive sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617) $s$, and angle $A$ between sides $a$ and $d$, the [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367) of $A$ are given by[^o35]

$$\begin{aligned} \cos A &= \frac{a^2-b^2-c^2+d^2}{2(ad+bc)}, \\[2pt] \sin A &= \frac{2\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}}{(ad+bc)}, \\[2pt] \tan \frac{A}{2} &= \sqrt{\frac{(s-a)(s-d)}{(s-b)(s-c)}}. \end{aligned}$$

The angle $\theta$ between the diagonals that is opposite sides $a$ and $c$ satisfies[^o36]

$$\tan \frac{\theta}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{(s-b)(s-d)}{(s-a)(s-c)}}.$$

If the extensions of opposite sides $a$ and $c$ intersect at an angle $\varphi$, then

$$\cos{\frac{\varphi}{2}}=\sqrt{\frac{(s-b)(s-d)(b+d)^2}{(ab+cd)(ad+bc)}}$$

where $s$ is the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617).[^o36]

A generalization of [Mollweide's formula](wikipedia://en/22213676) to cyclic quadrilaterals is given by the following two identities. Let $B$ denote the angle between sides $a$ and $b$, $C$ the angle between $b$ and $c$, and $D$ the angle between $c$ and $d$. If $E$ is the point of intersection of the diagonals, denote $\angle CED = \theta$, then:[^o37]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{a+c}{b+d} &= \frac{\sin\tfrac12(A+B)}{\cos\tfrac12(C-D)}\tan\tfrac12\theta, \\[10mu] \frac{a-c}{b-d} &= \frac{\cos\tfrac12(A+B)}{\sin\tfrac12(D-C)}\cot\tfrac12\theta. \end{aligned}$$

Moreover, a generalization of the [law of tangents](wikipedia://en/319484) for cyclic quadrilaterals is:[^o38]

$$\frac{(a-c)(b-d)}{(a+c)(b+d)}=\frac{\tan\tfrac12(A-B)}{\tan\tfrac12(A+B)}.$$

:::

## Parameshvara's circumradius formula {#Parameshvara's_circumradius_formula}

A cyclic quadrilateral with successive sides $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ and [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617) $s$ has the circumradius (the [radius](wikipedia://en/1780815) of the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/161262)) given by[^o29][^o41]

$$R=\frac{1}{4} \sqrt{\frac{(ab+cd)(ac+bd)(ad+bc)}{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}}.$$

This was derived by the Indian mathematician Vatasseri [Parameshvara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parameshvara) in the 15th century. (Note that the radius is invariant under the interchange of any side lengths.)

Using [Brahmagupta's formula](wikipedia://en/194129), Parameshvara's formula can be restated as

$$4KR=\sqrt{(ab+cd)(ac+bd)(ad+bc)}$$

where $K$ is the area of the cyclic quadrilateral.

:::

## Anticenter and collinearities {#Anticenter_and_collinearities}

Four line segments, each [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to one side of a cyclic quadrilateral and passing through the opposite side's [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979), are [concurrent](wikipedia://en/2206157).[^o43][^o45] These line segments are called the *maltitudes*, which is an abbreviation for midpoint altitude. Their common point is called the *anticenter*. It has the property of being the reflection of the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) in the ["vertex centroid"](wikipedia://en/25278#Remarkable_points_and_lines_in_a_convex_quadrilateral). Thus in a cyclic quadrilateral, the circumcenter, the "vertex centroid", and the anticenter are [collinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collinear_points).[^o45]

If the diagonals of a cyclic quadrilateral intersect at $P$, and the [midpoints](wikipedia://en/437979) of the diagonals are $M$ and $N$, then the anticenter of the quadrilateral is the [orthocenter](wikipedia://en/161244) of [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) $MNP$.

The anticenter of a cyclic quadrilateral is the [Poncelet point](wikipedia://en/6811772) of its vertices.

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

*Image: Japanese theorem 2.svg.* Japanese theorem

- In a cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$, the [incenters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle_and_excircles_of_a_triangle) $M_{1}$, $M_{2}$, $M_{3}$, $M_{4}$ (see the figure to the right) in [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) $\triangle DAB$, $\triangle ABC$, $\triangle BCD$, and $\triangle CDA$ are the vertices of a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048). This is one of the theorems known as the [Japanese theorem](wikipedia://en/13940585). The [orthocenters](wikipedia://en/161244) of the same four triangles are the vertices of a quadrilateral [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) to $ABCD$, and the [centroids](wikipedia://en/187926) in those four triangles are vertices of another cyclic quadrilateral.[^o15]
- In a cyclic quadrilateral $ABCD$ with circumcenter $O$, let $P$ be the point where the diagonals $AC$ and $BD$ intersect. Then angle $\angle APB$ is the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of the angles $\angle AOB$ and $\angle COD$. This is a direct consequence of the [inscribed angle theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_angle_theorem) and the [exterior angle theorem](wikipedia://en/19298354).
- There are no cyclic quadrilaterals with rational area and with unequal rational sides in either [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/168389) or [geometric progression](wikipedia://en/23474670).[^o47]
- If a cyclic quadrilateral has side lengths that form an [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389) the quadrilateral is also [ex-bicentric](wikipedia://en/32791638#Ex-bicentric_quadrilateral).
- If the opposite sides of a cyclic quadrilateral are extended to meet at $E$ and $F$, then the internal [angle bisectors](wikipedia://en/152547#Angle_bisector) of the angles at $E$ and $F$ are perpendicular.[^o48]

## Brahmagupta quadrilaterals {#Brahmagupta_quadrilaterals}

A **Brahmagupta quadrilateral**[^o51] is a cyclic quadrilateral with integer sides, integer diagonals, and integer area. It is a *primitive Brahmagupta quadrilateral* if no smaller [geometrically similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometrically_similar) quadrilateral has all these values as integers. Quadrilaterals whose side lengths, diagonals, and areas are all [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) are called *rational Brahmagupta quadrilaterals* in this article. Every primitive Brahmagupta quadrilateral is a rational Brahmagupta quadrilateral. Conversely, every rational Brahmagupta quadrilateral is geometrically similar to exactly one primitive Brahmagupta quadrilateral.

All primitive Brahmagupta quadrilaterals can be obtained from the following expressions involving rational parameters $t$, $u$, and $v$. The computed side lengths $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$, diagonals $e$, $f$, area $K$, and circumradius $R$ will be rational numbers. These can be scaled to produce a unique primitive Brahmagupta quadrilateral; note that $K$ is an area and will be scaled by the square of the value that multiplies the other quantities. The Brahmagupta quadrilateral will be non-self-intersecting and non-degenerate if $\min(u, v) > t > 0$.

$$\begin{aligned} a &= [t(u+v)+(1-uv)][u+v-t(1-uv)] \\[2pt] b &= (1+u^2)(v-t)(1+tv) \\[2pt] c &= t(1+u^2)(1+v^2) \\[2pt] d &= (1+v^2)(u-t)(1+tu) \\[2pt] e &= u(1+t^2)(1+v^2) \\[2pt] f &= v(1+t^2)(1+u^2) \\[2pt] K &= uv[2t(1-uv)-(u+v)(1-t^2)][2(u+v)t+(1-uv)(1-t^2)] \\[2pt] R &= \tfrac14(1+u^2)(1+v^2)(1+t^2). \\[2pt] \end{aligned}$$

See Concyclic points for a different parameterization of all non-degenerate primitive Brahmagupta quadrilaterals, which depends upon rational numbers, $0 < c_{1} < c_{2} < c_{3}$.

:::

## Orthodiagonal case {#Orthodiagonal_case}

### Circumradius and area {#Circumradius_and_area}

For a cyclic quadrilateral that is also [orthodiagonal](wikipedia://en/30425383) (has perpendicular diagonals), suppose the intersection of the diagonals divides one diagonal into segments of lengths $p_{1}$ and $p_{2}$ and divides the other diagonal into segments of lengths $q_{1}$ and $q_{2}$. Then[^o54] (the first equality is Proposition 11 in [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844)' *[Book of Lemmas](wikipedia://en/17941791)*)

$$D^2=p_1^2+p_2^2+q_1^2+q_2^2=a^2+c^2=b^2+d^2$$

where $D$ is the [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) of the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432). This holds because the diagonals are perpendicular [chords of a circle](wikipedia://en/6220#Chord). These equations imply that the [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) $R$ can be expressed as

$$R=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{p_1^2+p_2^2+q_1^2+q_2^2}$$

or, in terms of the sides of the quadrilateral, as[^o43]

$$R=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{a^2+c^2}=\tfrac{1}{2}\sqrt{b^2+d^2}.$$

It also follows that[^o43]

$$a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2=8R^2.$$

Thus, according to [Euler's quadrilateral theorem](wikipedia://en/25278#Diagonals), the circumradius can be expressed in terms of the diagonals $p$ and $q$, and the distance $x$ between the midpoints of the diagonals as

$$R=\sqrt{\frac{p^2+q^2+4x^2}{8}}.$$

A formula for the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) $K$ of a cyclic orthodiagonal quadrilateral in terms of the four sides is obtained directly when combining [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/194131#Diagonals) and the formula for the [area of an orthodiagonal quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/30425383#Area). The result is[^o55]

$$K=\tfrac{1}{2}(ac+bd).$$

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

- In a cyclic orthodiagonal quadrilateral, the [anticenter](wikipedia://en/194131#Anticenter_and_collinearities) coincides with the point where the diagonals intersect.[^o43]
- [Brahmagupta's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmagupta's_theorem) states that for a cyclic quadrilateral that is also [orthodiagonal](wikipedia://en/30425383), the perpendicular from any side through the point of intersection of the diagonals bisects the opposite side.[^o43]
- If a cyclic quadrilateral is also orthodiagonal, the distance from the [circumcenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumcenter) to any side equals half the length of the opposite side.[^o43]
- In a cyclic orthodiagonal quadrilateral, the distance between the midpoints of the diagonals equals the distance between the circumcenter and the point where the diagonals intersect.[^o43]

## Cyclic spherical quadrilaterals {#Cyclic_spherical_quadrilaterals}

In [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/650405), a spherical quadrilateral formed from four intersecting greater circles is cyclic if and only if the summations of the opposite angles are equal, i.e., α + γ = β + δ for consecutive angles α, β, γ, δ of the quadrilateral.[^o59] One direction of this theorem was proved by [Anders Johan Lexell](wikipedia://en/767959) in 1782.[^o60] Lexell showed that in a spherical quadrilateral inscribed in a small circle of a sphere the sums of opposite angles are equal, and that in the circumscribed quadrilateral the sums of opposite sides are equal. The first of these theorems is the spherical analogue of a plane theorem, and the second theorem is its dual, that is, the result of interchanging great circles and their poles.[^o61] Kiper et al.[^o62] proved a converse of the theorem: If the summations of the opposite sides are equal in a spherical quadrilateral, then there exists an inscribing circle for this quadrilateral.
