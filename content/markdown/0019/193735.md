# Poisson's equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Poisson's equation"}

*Image: Simeon Poisson crop.jpg.* Siméon Denis Poisson

**Poisson's equation** is an [elliptic partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/662787) of broad utility in [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028). For example, the solution to Poisson's equation is the potential field caused by a given electric charge or mass density distribution; with the potential field known, one can then calculate the corresponding electrostatic or gravitational (force) field. It is a generalization of [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941), which is also frequently seen in physics. The equation is named after French mathematician and physicist [Siméon Denis Poisson](wikipedia://en/44670) who published it in 1823.[^o2][^o3]

:::

## Statement of the equation {#Statement_of_the_equation}

Poisson's equation is

$$\Delta\phi = f,$$

where $\Delta$ is the [Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/174706), and $f$ and $\phi$ are [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-valued [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) on a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). Usually, $f$ is given, and $\phi$ is sought. When the manifold is [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), the Laplace operator is often denoted as $\nabla^{2}$ to denote its second-order nature relative to the classical gradient operator, and so Poisson's equation is frequently written as

$$\nabla^2 \phi = f.$$

In three-dimensional [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate), the potential function $\phi(x, y, z)$ satisfies

$$\frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial x^2} + \frac{\partial^2\phi}{\partial y^2} + \frac{\partial^2 \phi}{\partial z^2} = f(x, y, z).$$

When $f = 0$ identically, we obtain [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941).

Poisson's equation is linear inhomogeneous partial differential equation, and invariant under rotations or translations, and may be solved using a [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001) as:

$$\phi(\mathbf{r}) = - \iiint \frac{f(\mathbf{r}')}{4\pi |\mathbf{r} - \mathbf{r}'|}\, \mathrm{d}^3 r',$$

where the integral is over all of space. Note here $r$ is where we observe the field, where we are solving for. And integrating $r'$ is equivalent to integrating all the "sources". In a sense that we integrate all the effects of sources at $r'$ to get what's seen at $r$ (with the addition of point spread of source). A general exposition of the Green's function for Poisson's equation is given in the article on the [screened Poisson equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screened_Poisson_equation). There are various methods for numerical solution, such as the [relaxation method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relaxation_method), an iterative algorithm.

## Solution {#Solution}

The solution to the Poisson equation can be treated on $n$-dimensional space for any $n\geq 2$ in a unified manner. The Poisson equation on the whole space $\mathbb{R}^n$, which is given by

$$-\Delta v = f,$$

with a function $f$ that is twice continuously [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) and has [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) support (that is, it has non-zero values only on a [bounded set](wikipedia://en/48258)), has the solution[^n1]

$$v(x) = \int_{\mathbb{R}^n}\Phi(y-x)f(y)\mathrm{d}y, \qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^n,$$

with the *fundamental solution* or *Newton potential*

$$\Phi(x)=\begin{cases} -\frac{1}{2\pi}\log|x| \\ \frac{1}{n(n-2)\omega_n}|x|^{-n+2} \end{cases} \qquad x\in\mathbb{R}^n\setminus\{0\},$$

where $\omega_n$ denotes the volume of the unit ball in $\mathbb{R}^n$.

In order to solve Poisson's equation on more general domains, a few more specifications are needed: Let $U\subset\mathbb{R}^n$ be a bounded, [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) with $C^1$-boundary and $f,g$ [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) functions on $U$ and its [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) $\partial U$ respectively. Then, Poisson's equation with [Dirichlet boundary conditions](wikipedia://en/423504) is given by

$$\begin{cases} -\Delta u&=f &&\text{in } U \\ \quad u&=g &&\text{on }\partial U \end{cases}$$

for a function $u$ to be determined, which must be twice differentiable in $U$ (in order for $\Delta u$ to be well-defined) and continuous on $\partial U$.

Solutions to the above boundary value problem do not always exist, however, if they do, then they are unique.[^n2] In this case, solutions can be constructed using the fundamental solution $\Phi$. As state above, the function $v$ already fulfills $-\Delta v=f$, however, it does not yet meet the boundary conditions. To this end, a *corrector function* $\phi^x$ is introduced for $x\in U$ as the solution to the equation

$$\begin{cases} \Delta\phi^x(y)&=0 &&y\in U \\ \quad\phi^x(y)&=\Phi(y-x) &&y\in\partial U. \end{cases}$$

Determining $\phi^x$ is typically quite difficult and is feasible only for simple domains such as a ball. With this, the [Green function](wikipedia://en/311001) can be defined via

$$G(x,y) = \Phi(y-x) - \phi^x(y),\qquad x,y\in U,\, x\neq y.$$

Now, if the solution to Poisson's equation exists, then the (unique) solution is given by (also known as the *representation formula*)[^n3]

$$u(x) = \int_U f(y)G(x,y)\mathrm{d}y - \int_{\partial U}g(y)\frac{\partial G}{\partial\nu}(x,y)\mathrm{d}S_{n-1}(y), \qquad x\in U,$$

where $\partial/\partial\nu$ denotes the derivative in direction of the outer normal vector on $\partial U$ and $S_{n-1}$ is the $(n-1)$-dimensional [surface measure](wikipedia://en/504109).

## Applications in physics and engineering {#Applications_in_physics_and_engineering}

### Newtonian gravity {#Newtonian_gravity}

In the case of a gravitational field **g** due to an attracting massive object of density *ρ*, Gauss's law for gravity in differential form can be used to obtain the corresponding Poisson equation for gravity. Gauss's law for gravity is

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{g} = -4\pi G\rho.$$

Since the gravitational field is conservative (and [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational)), it can be expressed in terms of a [scalar potential](wikipedia://en/461654) *ϕ*:

$$\mathbf{g} = -\nabla \phi.$$

Substituting this into Gauss's law,

$$\nabla\cdot(-\nabla \phi) = - 4\pi G \rho,$$

yields **Poisson's equation** for gravity:

$$\nabla^2 \phi = 4\pi G \rho.$$

If the mass density is zero, Poisson's equation reduces to Laplace's equation. The [corresponding Green's function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Green's_function_for_the_three-variable_Laplace_equation) can be used to calculate the potential at distance $r$ from a central point mass $m$ (i.e., the [fundamental solution](wikipedia://en/1820858)). In three dimensions the potential is

$$\phi(r) = \frac{-G m}{r},$$

which is equivalent to [Newton's law of universal gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newton's_law_of_universal_gravitation).

### Electrostatics {#Electrostatics}

Many problems in [electrostatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatics) are governed by the Poisson equation, which relates the [electric potential](wikipedia://en/59615) $\varphi$ to the free charge density $\rho_f$, such as those found in [conductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_conductor).

The mathematical details of Poisson's equation, commonly expressed in [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_units) (as opposed to [Gaussian units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units)), describe how the [distribution of free charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) generates the electrostatic potential in a given [region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Region_%28mathematics%29).

Starting with [Gauss's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_law) for electricity (also one of [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737)) in differential form, one has

$$\mathbf{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{D} = \rho_f,$$

where $\mathbf{\nabla} \cdot$ is the [divergence operator](wikipedia://en/8328), **D** is the [electric displacement field](wikipedia://en/1191101), and *ρ<sub>f</sub>* is the free-[charge density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_density) (describing charges brought from outside).

Assuming the medium is linear, isotropic, and homogeneous (see [polarization density](wikipedia://en/698648)), we have the [constitutive equation](wikipedia://en/1034699#Electromagnetism)

$$\mathbf{D} = \varepsilon \mathbf{E},$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the [permittivity](wikipedia://en/53933) of the medium, and **E** is the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092).

Substituting this into Gauss's law and assuming that $\varepsilon$ is spatially constant in the region of interest yields

$$\mathbf{\nabla} \cdot \mathbf{E} = \frac{\rho_f}{\varepsilon}.$$

In electrostatics, we assume that there is no magnetic field (the argument that follows also holds in the presence of a constant magnetic field).[^o12] Then, we have that

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = 0,$$

where $\nabla\times$ is the [curl operator](wikipedia://en/6123). This equation means that we can write the electric field as the gradient of a scalar function $\varphi$ (called the [electric potential](wikipedia://en/59615)), since the curl of any gradient is zero. Thus we can write

$$\mathbf{E} = -\nabla \varphi,$$

where the minus sign is introduced so that $\varphi$ is identified as the [electric potential energy](wikipedia://en/1975956) per unit charge.[^o13]

The derivation of Poisson's equation under these circumstances is straightforward. Substituting the potential gradient for the electric field,

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{E} = \nabla \cdot (-\nabla \varphi) = -\nabla^2 \varphi = \frac{\rho_f}{\varepsilon},$$

directly produces Poisson's equation for electrostatics, which is

$$\nabla^2 \varphi = -\frac{\rho_f}{\varepsilon}.$$

Specifying the Poisson's equation for the potential requires knowing the charge density distribution. If the charge density is zero, then [Laplace's equation](wikipedia://en/36941) results. If the charge density follows a [Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/4107), then the [Poisson–Boltzmann equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson–Boltzmann_equation) results. The Poisson–Boltzmann equation plays a role in the development of the [Debye–Hückel theory of dilute electrolyte solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debye–Hückel_equation).

Using a Green's function, the potential at distance $r$ from a central point charge $Q$ (i.e., the [fundamental solution](wikipedia://en/1820858)) is

$$\varphi(r) = \frac {Q}{4 \pi \varepsilon r},$$

which is [Coulomb's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law) of electrostatics. (For historical reasons, and unlike gravity's model above, the $4 \pi$ factor appears here and not in Gauss's law.)

The above discussion assumes that the magnetic field is not varying in time. The same Poisson equation arises even if it does vary in time, as long as the [Coulomb gauge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb_gauge) is used. In this more general class of cases, computing $\varphi$ is no longer sufficient to calculate **E**, since **E** also depends on the [magnetic vector potential](wikipedia://en/1191067) **A**, which must be independently computed. See [Maxwell's equation in potential formulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_descriptions_of_the_electromagnetic_field#Maxwell's_equations_in_potential_formulation) for more on $\varphi$ and **A** in Maxwell's equations and how an appropriate Poisson's equation is obtained in this case.

#### Potential of a Gaussian charge density {#Potential_of_a_Gaussian_charge_density}

If there is a static spherically symmetric [Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_distribution) charge density

$$\rho_f(r) = \frac{Q}{\sigma^3\sqrt{2\pi}^3}\,e^{-r^2/(2\sigma^2)},$$

where $Q$ is the total charge, then the solution $\varphi(r)$ of Poisson's equation

$$\nabla^2 \varphi = -\frac{\rho_f}{\varepsilon}$$

is given by

$$\varphi(r) = \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon} \frac{Q}{r} \operatorname{erf}\left(\frac{r}{\sqrt{2}\sigma}\right),$$

where $\operatorname{erf}(x)$ is the [error function](wikipedia://en/177111).[^o15] This solution can be checked explicitly by evaluating $\nabla^{2}\varphi$.

Note that for $r$ much greater than $\sigma$, $\operatorname{erf}(r/\sqrt{2} \sigma)$ approaches unity,[^o16] and the potential $\varphi(r)$ approaches the [point-charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_potential) potential,

$$\varphi \approx \frac{1}{4 \pi \varepsilon} \frac{Q}{r},$$

as one would expect. Furthermore, the error function approaches 1 extremely quickly as its argument increases; in practice, for $r > 3.4\sigma$ the relative error is smaller than one part in a thousand.[^o16]

### Surface reconstruction {#Surface_reconstruction}

Surface reconstruction is an [inverse problem](wikipedia://en/203956). The goal is to digitally reconstruct a smooth surface based on a large number of points *p<sub>i</sub>* (a [point cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_cloud)) where each point also carries an estimate of the local [surface normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_normal) **n**<sub>*i*</sub>.[^o19] Poisson's equation can be utilized to solve this problem with a technique called Poisson surface reconstruction.[^o20]

The goal of this technique is to reconstruct an [implicit function](wikipedia://en/250896) *f* whose value is zero at the points *p<sub>i</sub>* and whose gradient at the points *p<sub>i</sub>* equals the normal vectors **n**<sub>*i*</sub>. The set of (*p<sub>i</sub>*, **n**<sub>*i*</sub>) is thus modeled as a continuous [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) field **V**. The implicit function *f* is found by [integrating](wikipedia://en/15532) the vector field **V**. Since not every vector field is the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of a function, the problem may or may not have a solution: the necessary and sufficient condition for a smooth vector field **V** to be the gradient of a function *f* is that the [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) of **V** must be identically zero. In case this condition is difficult to impose, it is still possible to perform a [least-squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Least-squares) fit to minimize the difference between **V** and the gradient of *f*.

In order to effectively apply Poisson's equation to the problem of surface reconstruction, it is necessary to find a good discretization of the vector field **V**. The basic approach is to bound the data with a [finite-difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-difference) grid. For a function valued at the nodes of such a grid, its gradient can be represented as valued on staggered grids, i.e. on grids whose nodes lie in between the nodes of the original grid. It is convenient to define three staggered grids, each shifted in one and only one direction corresponding to the components of the normal data. On each staggered grid we perform [trilinear interpolation](wikipedia://en/674582) on the set of points. The interpolation weights are then used to distribute the magnitude of the associated component of *n<sub>i</sub>* onto the nodes of the particular staggered grid cell containing *p<sub>i</sub>*. Kazhdan and coauthors give a more accurate method of discretization using an adaptive finite-difference grid, i.e. the cells of the grid are smaller (the grid is more finely divided) where there are more data points.[^o20] They suggest implementing this technique with an adaptive [octree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octree).

:::

### Fluid dynamics {#Fluid_dynamics}

For the incompressible [Navier–Stokes equations](wikipedia://en/48395), given by

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial\mathbf{v}}{\partial t} + (\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla) \mathbf{v} &= -\frac{1}{\rho} \nabla p + \nu\Delta\mathbf{v} + \mathbf{g}, \\ \nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} &= 0. \end{aligned}$$

The equation for the pressure field $p$ is an example of a nonlinear Poisson equation:

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta p &= -\rho \nabla \cdot(\mathbf{v} \cdot \nabla \mathbf{v}) \\ &= -\rho \operatorname{Tr}\big((\nabla\mathbf{v}) (\nabla\mathbf{v})\big). \end{aligned}$$

Notice that the above trace is not sign-definite.

### Thermodynamics {#Thermodynamics}

[Thermal conduction](wikipedia://en/72536) is modelled via the [Heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415). Stationary state heat conduction with a source term is modelled via the following Poisson equation:

$$\nabla^2\vartheta = - \frac{\Phi}{\lambda},$$

where $\vartheta$ is the temperature, $\Phi$ is the heat source term and $\lambda$ is [Thermal conductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_conductivity).
