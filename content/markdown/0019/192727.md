# Luck

*Image: Four-leaf clover.jpg.* A [four-leaf clover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-leaf_clover), a rare variant of the [shamrock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shamrock), is often considered to bestow good luck.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Luck"}

**Luck** is a phenomenon or [belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief) that humans may associate with experiencing [improbable](wikipedia://en/22934) events, especially improbably positive or negative events.[^o2][^o3][^o4] [Philosophical naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_%28philosophy%29), eschewing any supernatural explanations, might suggest that positive or negative events may happen at any time (due to both random and non-random natural and artificial processes), and that even improbable events can happen by [random chance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_chance). In this view, the epithet "lucky" or "unlucky" is a descriptive label that refers to an event's positivity, negativity, or improbability.

:::

[Supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural) interpretations of luck consider it to be an attribute of a person[^o5] or of an object, or the result of a favorable (or unfavorable) view manifested by a [deity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deity)[^o6] towards the lucky (or unlucky) person. These interpretations often *prescribe* how luckiness or unluckiness can be obtained, such as by carrying a lucky charm or offering sacrifices or prayers to a deity. Saying someone is "born lucky" may hold different meanings, depending on the interpretation: it could simply mean that they have been born into a good family or circumstance; or that they habitually experience improbably positive events, due to some inherent property, or due to the lifelong favor of a god or goddess in a [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheistic) or [polytheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytheistic) religion.

Many superstitions are associated with luck,[^o7] Such superstitions are often specific to a given culture[^o8][^o9][^o10] or set of related cultures. For example, [lucky symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucky_symbols) include the number 7 in Christian-influenced cultures and the number 8 in Chinese-influenced cultures. [Unlucky symbols](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unlucky_symbols) and events include entering and leaving a house by different doors or breaking a mirror[^o11] in Greek culture, throwing rocks into a whirlwind in Navajo culture,[^o12] and [ravens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raven) in Western culture. Some of these associations may derive from related facts or desires. For example, in Western culture opening an umbrella indoors might be considered unlucky partly because it could poke someone in the eye, whereas shaking hands with a [chimney sweep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chimney_sweep) might be considered lucky partly because it is a kind but unpleasant thing to do given the dirty nature of their work. In Chinese and Japanese culture, the association of the number 4 as a [homophone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homophone) with the word for death may explain why it is considered unlucky. Extremely complicated and sometimes contradictory systems for prescribing auspicious and inauspicious times and arrangements of things have been devised, for example [feng shui](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feng_shui) in Chinese culture and systems of [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology) in various cultures around the world.

Many polytheistic religions feature specific gods or goddesses who are associated by adherents with luck, either good or bad. Such deities include [Fortuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortuna) and [Felicitas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felicitas) in the [Ancient Roman religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Roman_religion) (the name of the former relating to the words "fortunate" and "unfortunate" in [English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_language)), [Dedun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedun) in Nubian religion, the [Seven Lucky Gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Lucky_Gods) in [Japanese mythology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_mythology), mythical American serviceman turned prosperity-god [John Frum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Frum) in a Vanuatu [cargo-cult](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cargo-cult),[^o13] and the inauspicious [Alakshmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alakshmi) and her auspicious counterpart [Lakshmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lakshmi)[^o14] in Hinduism.

## Etymology and definition {#Etymology_and_definition}

*Image: 1926WhyBeUnlucky.jpg.* 1927 advertisement for lucky jewellery. "Why Be Unlucky?".

The English noun *luck* appears comparatively late, during the 1480s, as a loan from [Low German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_German), [Dutch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_language) or Frisian *luk*, a short form of *gelucke* ([Middle High German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_High_German) *gelücke*). Compare to old Slavic word *lukyj* (*лукый*) - *appointed by destiny* and old Russian *luchaj* (*лучаи*) - *destiny, fortune*. It likely entered English as a [gambling](wikipedia://en/11921) term, and the context of gambling remains detectable in the word's connotations; luck is a way of understanding a personal chance event. Luck has three aspects:[^o16][^o17][^o18]

- Luck is good or bad.[^o19]
- Luck is the result of chance.[^o20]
- Luck applies to a sentient being.

Before the adoption of *luck* at the end of the Middle Ages, Old English and [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English) expressed the notion of "good fortune" with the word *speed* (Middle English *spede*, Old English *spēd*); *speed* besides "good fortune" had the wider meaning of "[prosperity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prosperity), [profit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Profit_%28accounting%29), abundance"; it is not associated with the notion of probability or chance but rather with that of [fate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fate) or divine help; a bestower of success can also be called *speed*, as in "Christ be our speed" (William Robertson, *Phraseologia generalis*, 1693).

The notion of probability was expressed by the Latin loanword *chance*, adopted in Middle English from the late 13th century, literally describing an outcome as a "falling" (as it were of [dice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice)), via Old French *cheance* from Late Latin *cadentia* "falling". [Fortuna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortuna), the Roman goddess of fate or luck, was popular as an allegory in medieval times, and even though it was not strictly reconcilable with Christian theology, it became popular in learned circles of the High Middle Ages to portray her as a servant of God in distributing success or failure in a characteristically "fickle" or unpredictable way, thus introducing the notion of *chance*.

## Interpretations {#Interpretations}

Luck is interpreted and understood in many different ways.

### Lack of control {#Lack_of_control}

Luck refers to that which happens to a person beyond that person's control. This view incorporates [phenomena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomena) that are chance happenings, a person's place of birth for example, but where there is no uncertainty involved, or where the uncertainty is irrelevant. Within this framework, one can differentiate between three different types of luck:

1. Constitutional luck, that is, luck with factors that cannot be changed. Place of birth and genetic constitution are typical examples.
1. Circumstantial luck—with factors that are haphazardly brought on. Accidents and epidemics are typical examples.
1. Ignorance luck, that is, luck with factors one does not know about. Examples can be identified only in [hindsight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindsight_bias).

Circumstantial luck with accidental happenstance of favorable discoveries and/or inventions is [serendipity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serendipity).

### Fallacy {#Fallacy}

Another view holds that "luck is probability taken personally." A [rationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalist) approach to luck includes the application of the rules of [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and an avoidance of [unscientific](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method) beliefs. The [rationalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rationalism) thinks that the belief in luck is a result of poor reasoning or [wishful thinking](wikipedia://en/386062). To a rationalist, a believer in luck who asserts that something has influenced his or her luck commits the "[post hoc ergo propter hoc](wikipedia://en/174725)" [logical fallacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_fallacy): that because two events are connected sequentially, they are connected causally as well. In general, this fallacy is that:

1. A luck-attracting event or action happens;
1. Then a positive outcome happens;
1. Therefore, the event or action influenced the outcome.

More contemporary authors writing on the subject believe that the definition of good destiny is: One who enjoys good health; has the physical and mental capabilities of achieving his goals in life; has good appearance, and; has happiness in mind and is not prone to accidents.[^o24]

In the rationalist perspective, probability is only affected by confirmed causal connections.

The [gambler's fallacy](wikipedia://en/12970) and [inverse gambler's fallacy](wikipedia://en/91388) both explain some reasoning problems in common beliefs in luck. They involve denying the unpredictability of [random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random) events: "I haven't rolled a seven all week, so I'll definitely roll one tonight".

[Philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) [Daniel Dennett](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daniel_Dennett) wrote that "luck is *mere* luck" rather than a property of a person or thing.[^o25]

### Essence {#Essence}

*Image: Maneki neko with 7 Lucky Gods by OiMax in Asakusa, Tokyo.jpg.* [Maneki-neko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maneki-neko) with [Seven Lucky gods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_lucky_gods)

There is also a series of [spiritual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritualism_%28beliefs%29), or [supernatural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernatural) beliefs regarding fortune. These beliefs vary widely from one to another, but most agree that luck can be influenced through spiritual means by performing certain [rituals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ritual) or by avoiding certain circumstances.

Luck can also be a [belief](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belief) in an organization of fortunate and unfortunate events. Luck is a form of [superstition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superstition) which is interpreted differently by different individuals. [Carl Jung](wikipedia://en/42031) coined the term [synchronicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchronicity), which he described as "a meaningful coincidence".

[Abrahamic religions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrahamic_religions) believe [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God) controls future events; belief in luck or fate is criticised in Isaiah:

But you who forsake the Lord, who forget my holy mountain, who set a table for Fortune and fill cups of mixed wine for Destiny, I will destine you to the sword, and all of you shall bow down to the slaughter

Belief in the extent of [Divine Providence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Providence) varies; most acknowledge providence as at least a partial, if not complete influence on luck. [Christianity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity), in its early development, accommodated many traditional practices which at different times, accepted [omens](wikipedia://en/186746) and practiced forms of ritual [sacrifice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sacrifice) in order to divine the will of their supreme being or to influence divine favoritism. The concepts of "[Divine Grace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Grace)" or "[Blessing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blessing)" as they are described by believers closely resemble what is referred to as "luck" by others.

[Mesoamerican](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesoamerica) religions, such as the [Aztecs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aztec), [Mayans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maya_civilization) and [Incas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inca_civilization), had particularly strong beliefs regarding the relationship between rituals and the gods, which could in a similar sense to Abrahamic religions be called luck or providence. In these cultures, human sacrifice (both of willing volunteers and captured enemies), as well as self-sacrifice by means of [bloodletting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bloodletting), could possibly be seen as a way to propitiate the gods and earn favor for the city offering the sacrifice. An alternative interpretation[^o27] would be that the sacrificial blood was considered as a necessary element for the gods to maintain the proper working order of the universe, in the same way that oil would be applied to an automobile to keep it working as designed.

Many traditional [African](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethnic_groups_of_Africa) practices, such as [voodoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_African_Vodun) and [hoodoo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hoodoo_%28folk_magic%29), have a strong belief in superstition. Some of these religions include a belief that third parties can influence an individual's luck. [Shamans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shaman) and [witches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witch) are both respected and feared, based on their ability to cause good or bad fortune for those in villages near them.

### Self-fulfilling prophecy {#Self-fulfilling_prophecy}

Some evidence supports the idea that belief in luck acts like a [placebo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placebo), producing [positive thinking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimism) and improving people's responses to events.

In personality psychology, people reliably differ from each other depending on four key aspects: beliefs in luck, rejection of luck, being lucky, and being unlucky.[^o29] People who believe in good luck are more optimistic, more satisfied with their lives, and have better moods.[^o29] People who believe they are personally unlucky experience more anxiety, and less likely to take advantage of unexpected opportunities.[^o30] One 2010 study found that golfers who were told they were using a "lucky ball" performed better than those who were not.[^o30]

Some people intentionally put themselves in situations that increase the chances of a [serendipitous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serendipity) encounter, such as socializing with people who work in different fields.[^o30]

## Social aspects {#Social_aspects}

*Image: Stultifera-navis - Wheel of fortune (112540531).jpg.* [Wheel of fortune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wheel_of_Fortune_%28Tarot_card%29) as depicted in Sebastian Brant's book, author [Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer)

### Games {#Games}

The philosopher Nicholas Rescher has proposed that the luck of someone's result in a situation of uncertainty is measured by the difference between this party's yield and expectation: λ = Y - E. Thus skill enhances expectation and reduces luck. The extent to which different [games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game) will [depend on luck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Game#Skill,_strategy,_and_chance), rather than skill or effort, varies considerably. For example, [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess) does not involve any random factors (beyond the determination of which player moves first), while the outcome of [Snakes and Ladders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Snakes_and_Ladders) is entirely based on random dice rolls. In [poker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poker), especially games with a communal board, pure luck may decide a winning hand. Luck in games involving chance is defined as the change in a player's equity after a random event such as a die roll or card draw.[^o33] Luck is positive (good luck) if the player's position is improved and negative (bad luck) if it is worsened. A poker player who is doing well (playing successfully, winning) is said to be "running good".[^o34]

Almost all sports contain elements of luck. A statistical analysis in the book *[The Success Equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Success_Equation)* attempted to elucidate the differing balance between skill and luck with respect to how teams finished in the [major North American sports leagues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Major_professional_sports_leagues_in_the_United_States_and_Canada). This analysis concluded that, on a luck-skill continuum, the [NBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Basketball_Association) had the most skill-dependent result while that of the [NHL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Hockey_League) was most luck-dependent.[^o35]

### Lotteries {#Lotteries}

A defining feature of a lottery is that winners are selected purely by chance. Marketing and other discussions regarding lotteries often mention luck but tend to underplay the actual prospects of winning, which are usually millions to one against.[^o37]

### Means of resolving issues {#Means_of_resolving_issues}

"Leaving it to chance" is sometimes a way of resolving issues for example, where there are two possible outcomes, flipping a coin may determine the outcome. This practice has gone on for thousands of years, a common contemporary example is the coin toss at the start of a [sporting event](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporting_event) which may determine who goes first.[^o39]

### Numerology {#Numerology}

Most cultures consider some numbers to be lucky or unlucky. This is found to be particularly strong in [Asian cultures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Culture_of_Asia), where the obtaining of "lucky" [telephone numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telephone_number), automobile [license plate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/License_plate) numbers, and [household addresses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Address_%28geography%29) (such as those which [contain the digit 6 or 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_numerology)) are actively sought, sometimes at great [monetary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Money) expense.

[Numerology](wikipedia://en/21137), as it relates to luck, is closer to an art than to a science, yet numerologists, astrologists or psychics may disagree. It is interrelated to [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), and to some degree to [parapsychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parapsychology) and [spirituality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spirituality) and is based on converting virtually anything material into a [pure number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_number), using that number in an attempt to detect something meaningful about reality, and trying to predict or calculate the future based on lucky numbers. Numerology is [folkloric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Folkloric) by nature and started when humans first learned to count. Through human history it was, and still is, practiced by many cultures of the world from traditional [fortune-telling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortune-telling) to on-line [psychic reading](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychic_reading). Dudley describes numerology as, "the delusion that numbers have power over events."[^o41]

### Science {#Science}

Different thinkers like [Thomas Kuhn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Kuhn) have discussed the [role of chance in scientific discoveries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Role_of_chance_in_scientific_discoveries). [Richard Wiseman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Wiseman) did a ten-year scientific study into the nature of luck that has revealed that, to a large extent, people make their own good and bad fortune. His research revealed that "Lucky people generate their own good fortune via four basic principles. They are skilled at creating and noticing chance opportunities, making lucky decisions by listening to their intuition, creating self-fulfilling prophecies via positive expectations, and adopting a resilient attitude that transforms bad luck into good."[^o43] Researchers have suggested that good luck and good mood often co-occur (Duong & Ohtsuka, 2000)[^o44] and that people who believe themselves to be lucky are often comparatively happy and optimistic whereas people who believe themselves to be unlucky may feel comparatively anxious and depressed (Day & Maltby, 2003; Wiseman, 2003).[^o43][^o45] Luck can also correlate with superstitious behaviors that increase opportunities of good fortune occurring like avoiding walking under ladders or blowing left and right for safe travels before crossing train tracks, which inadvertently increases your chances of seeing an oncoming train.[^o46][^o47]

Although previous studies have explored the antecedents and consequences of luck using attribution theory (e. g., Fischoff, 1976; Weiner et al., 1987),[^o48][^o49] personality variables (Darke & Freedman, 1997a;b),[^o50] and more recently a cognitive priming approach (DeMarree et al., 2005; Kramer & Block, 2008) research on the underlying mechanism of how luck influences consumer judgment and behavior has been noticeably absent in the extant literature. Moreover, in much of this previous work, luck is manipulated in a way that is very likely to elicit positive affect as well. Thus, it is difficult to articulate whether the observed effects of luck are due to chronic beliefs about luck, temporary changes in how lucky people feel, or because of changes caused by the positive affect that is experienced. Their research showed that priming participants subliminally with luck-related stimuli made them feel luckier and happier. It was also found that the effects of priming luck using [subliminal messages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subliminal_messages) increased participants' estimates of the likelihood of favorable events, their participation in lotteries, the amount of money they invested in relatively risky financial options and these effects appeared to be mediated by temporary changes in perceptions of luck rather than by affect.[^o51][^o52]

## In religion and mythology {#In_religion_and_mythology}

### Buddhism {#Buddhism}

[Gautama Buddha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gautama_Buddha), the founder of [Buddhism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buddhism), taught his followers not to believe in luck. He taught that all things which happen must have a cause, either material or spiritual, and do not occur due to luck, chance or fate. The idea of moral causality, [karma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma) ([Pali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pali): kamma), is central in Buddhism. In the [Sutta Nipata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sutta_Nipata), the Buddha is recorded as having said the following about selling luck:

> Whereas some religious men, while living of food provided by the faithful make their living by such low arts, such wrong means of livelihood as palmistry, divining by signs, interpreting dreams ... bringing good or bad luck ... invoking the goodness of luck ... picking the lucky site for a building, the monk Gautama refrains from such low arts, such wrong means of livelihood. D.I, 9–12[^o55]

Belief in luck is prevalent in many predominantly Buddhist countries. In [Thailand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thailand), Buddhists may wear verses (takrut) or lucky amulets which have been blessed by monks for protection against harm.[^o56]

### Christianity and Judaism {#Christianity_and_Judaism}

Simply put, luck, as other religious worldviews consider it, is non-existent in Judeo-Christian theology. The [book of Proverbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Proverbs) 16:33 states that "the lot is cast into the lap, but its every decision is from the Lord" and [Ecclesiastes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastes) 9:11 states (in a passage that describes events with different outcomes - e.g. a race, a battle, the gaining of wealth and favour) that "time and chance happeneth to them all". Whilst the latter passage from Ecclesiastes suggests from the author's intentionally earthly perspective that no things in human affairs are certain, the extract from Proverbs indicates that the outcome of something as apparently random as the rolling of dice or the tossing of a coin remains subject to God's will or sovereignty and thus His control as well. In his book *God, Chance and Purpose: Can God have it both ways?*,[^o58] Bartholomew argues that chance is part of God's creation but that not even God can accurately predict what chance will do.[^o59] [God's sovereignty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sovereignty_of_God_in_Christianity) involves two aspects. God's active will or sovereignty would involve something God causes to happen such as the leading of wicked King Ahab into battle (2 Chronicles 18:18-19). Ahab's death was not merely the result of a randomly shot arrow, but as 2 Chronicles 18 reveals, God actively directed the events that led Ahab into battle and used that randomly shot arrow to accomplish his intended will for Ahab that day.[^o60] God's passive will involves God allowing, rather than causing, something to happen. Chapter 1 of the book of Job illustrates this in what God allowed Satan to do in the life of Job.[^o61] It is also involved in the evil that God allowed Joseph's brothers to do to Joseph in order to accomplish a greater good, a good not apparent to Joseph until years later (Genesis 50:20).[^o62]

### Hinduism {#Hinduism}

In [Hinduism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinduism) the [Bhagavad-Gita](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhagavad_Gita) values "Purushartha" more than mere Luck or Fate.

The Gita says; "Set thy heart upon selfless performance of thy natural duty (Svakarma, Svadharma), but never on its reward. Work not for a reward; but never cease to do thy work" (Gita, 2.47).[^o64]

There are five causes of all actions as given in the Samkhya wisdom; says Gita—

"The physical body (adhisthana), the lower 'I am' (karta), the means of perception (karanam), the varied multiple efforts (vividhasch pruthak cheshta) and the Cosmic Wheel of Action (daivam), these five sources together are responsible for success or failure of any action" ( Gita, 18 . 14–15 ).[^o65]

Here the word "daivam" does not mean luck, fate, fortune, providence, or destiny. None of these English words are the exact synonym for the Sanskrit word "Daivam" here. "Daivam" is the Cosmic Wheel of Action (Kshara-gati, Apara-Prakriti, Maya) that keeps the perfect account of our past and present actions.

A man who says "Nothing is impossible for me; I am the only hero of all these achievements; who else is there like me?" He is a man of clouded vision and he does not see the Truth.

## Belief measurement {#Belief_measurement}

*Image: Horseshoe lucky on door.jpg.* A horseshoe on a door is regarded a protective [talisman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Talisman) in some cultures.

Darke and Freedman (1997)[^o71] were the first researchers systematically to address directly both the concept and the measurement of belief in luck as a [deterministic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic) and personal attribute. They define luck belief as the perception that good luck is "a somewhat stable characteristic that consistently favors some people but not others".[^o71] They define disbelief in luck as "a tendency to agree with the rational view of luck as random and unreliable" (p. 490). To capture their unidimensional definition of irrational luck belief, Darke and Freedman developed a 12-item measure.[^o71] Unfortunately, they found their measure "does not seem particularly good at distinguishing between people who \[say\] they \[are\] typically lucky from those who \[say\] they \[are\] typically unlucky".[^o71] They also found factor analyses of their measure produced a multi-component solution,[^o71] as did Prendergast and Thompson (2008).[^o72]

André (2006)[^o73] proposed a model of luck-related perceptions that includes separate positive and negative beliefs. She found the positive and negative components of personal luck beliefs correlate highly, suggesting they are conceptually very close or in fact the same. Maltby et al. (2008)[^o74] proposed a six-dimensional model of beliefs around luck, but empirical analyses supported only a four-dimensional model: belief in being personally lucky; belief in being personally unlucky; general belief in luck; and rejection of belief in luck.

*Image: BarnstarProud.jpg.* A [barnstar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barnstar), mounted on a wall, is seen as a lucky symbol in some parts of North America.

Thompson and Prendergast (2013)[^o75] clarified the concepts of belief in luck and belief in personal luckiness. They addressed the logical problem that nobody who disbelieves in luck could consider themselves lucky by differentiating between belief in luck as a deterministic phenomenon that affects the future, on one hand, and on the other, belief in personal luckiness as an appraisal of how fortunately or otherwise chance events in the past might have turned out. They developed and validated an internationally applicable scale to measure, respectively, belief in luck and personal luckiness constructs. They found no correlation between the constructs and no evidence of a distinction between positive and negative aspects of each, suggesting they represent two discrete and unidimensional constructs. Belief in luck and personal luckiness were also found to correlate differently with personality and psychological variables, such as the Big Five and affect.
