# Function composition

the mathematical concept

∘

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Function composition"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **composition operator** $\circ$ takes two [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), $f$ and $g$, and returns a new function $f \circ g$. When the composite function $f \circ g$ (pronounced "$f$ of $g$") is evaluated at an input $x$, the result is $(f \circ g)(x) = f(g(x))$. That is, the function $f$ is [applied](wikipedia://en/20576545) after applying $g$ to $x$.[^o2]

:::

The composition of functions is a special case of the [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170), sometimes also denoted by $\circ$. As a result, all properties of composition of relations are true of composition of functions,[^o28] such as associativity.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Composition of functions on a finite [set](wikipedia://en/26691): If $f = \{(1, 1), (2, 3), (3, 1), (4, 2)\}$, and $g = \{(1, 2), (2, 3), (3, 1), (4, 2)\}$, then $g \circ f = \{(1, 2), (2, 1), (3, 2), (4, 3)\}$, as shown in the figure.
- Composition of functions on an [infinite set](wikipedia://en/142679): If $f: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ (where $\mathbf{R}$ is the set of all [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)) is given by $f(x) = 2x + 4$ and $g: \mathbf{R} \to \mathbf{R}$ is given by $g(x) = x^{3}$, then:
- If an airplane's altitude at time $t$ is $a(t)$, and the air pressure at altitude $x$ is $p(x)$, then $(p \circ a)(t)$ is the pressure around the plane at time $t$.
- Function defined on finite sets which change the order of their elements such as [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) can be composed on the same set, this being composition of permutations.

## Properties {#Properties}

The composition of functions is always [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative)—a property inherited from the [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170).[^o28] That is, if $f$, $g$, and $h$ are composable, then $f \circ (g\,\circ\,h) = (f\,\circ\,g) \circ h$.[^o7] Since the parentheses do not change the result, they are generally omitted.

In a strict sense, the composition $g\,\circ\,f$ is only meaningful if the codomain of $f$ equals the domain of $g$; in a wider sense, it is sufficient that the former be an improper [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the latter.[^o24] Moreover, it is often convenient to tacitly restrict the domain of $f$, such that $f$ produces only values in the domain of $g$. For example, the composition $g\,\circ\,f$ of the functions $f\,: \mathbf{R} \to (-\infty,+9]$ defined by $f(x) = 9 - x^{2}$ and $g\,: [0,+\infty) \to \mathbf{R}$ defined by $g(x) = \sqrt x$ can be defined on the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[-3,+3]$.

*Image: Absolute value composition.svg.* Compositions of two [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) functions, the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) and a [cubic function](wikipedia://en/243709), in different orders, show a non-commutativity of composition.

The functions $g$ and $f$ are said to [commute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) with each other if $g\,\circ\,f = f\,\circ\,g$. Commutativity is a special property, attained only by particular functions, and often in special circumstances. For example, $\lvert x\rvert + 3 = \lvert x + 3\rvert$ only when $x \ge 0$. The picture shows another example.

The composition of [one-to-one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-to-one_function) (injective) functions is always one-to-one. Similarly, the composition of [onto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onto_function) (surjective) functions is always onto. It follows that the composition of two [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942) is also a bijection. The [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) of a composition (assumed invertible) has the property that $(f\,\circ\,g)^{-1} = g^{-1}\circ f^{-1}$.[^o29]

[Derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) of compositions involving differentiable functions can be found using the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113). [Higher derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_derivative) of such functions are given by [Faà di Bruno's formula](wikipedia://en/425943).[^o7]

Composition of functions is sometimes described as a kind of [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) on a function space, but has very different properties from [pointwise](wikipedia://en/4786797) multiplication of functions (e.g. composition is not [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390)).[^o8]

:::

## Composition monoids {#Composition_monoids}

Suppose one has two (or more) functions $f: X \to X,$ $g: X \to X$ having the same domain and codomain; these are often called *[transformations](wikipedia://en/1904373)*. Then one can form chains of transformations composed together, such as $f \circ f \circ g \circ f$. Such chains have the [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) of a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652), called a *[transformation monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_monoid)* or (much more seldom) a *composition monoid*. In general, transformation monoids can have remarkably complicated structure. One particular notable example is the [de Rham curve](wikipedia://en/4539673). The set of *all* functions $f: X \to X$ is called the [full transformation semigroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_transformation_semigroup)[^o30] or *symmetric semigroup*[^o31] on $X$. (One can actually define two semigroups depending how one defines the semigroup operation as the left or right composition of functions.[^o32])

*Image: SVG skew and rotation.svg.* Composition of a [shear mapping](wikipedia://en/799405) (red) and a clockwise rotation by 45° (green). On the left is the original object. Above is shear, then rotate. Below is rotate, then shear.

If the given transformations are [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) (and thus invertible), then the set of all possible combinations of these functions forms a [transformation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformation_group) (also known as a [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634)); and one says that the group is [generated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_generator) by these functions.

The set of all bijective functions $f: X \to X$ (called [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027)) forms a group with respect to function composition. This is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901), also sometimes called the *composition group*. A fundamental result in group theory, [Cayley's theorem](wikipedia://en/105967), essentially says that any group is in fact just a subgroup of a symmetric group ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) isomorphism).[^o33]

In the symmetric semigroup (of all transformations) one also finds a weaker, non-unique notion of inverse (called a pseudoinverse) because the symmetric semigroup is a [regular semigroup](wikipedia://en/6797677).[^o34]

## Functional powers {#Functional_powers}

If $Y \subseteq X$, then $f:X\to Y$ may compose with itself; this is sometimes denoted as $f^2$. That is:

More generally, for any [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n \ge 2$, the $n$th **functional [power](wikipedia://en/99491)** can be defined inductively by $f\,^{n} = f \circ f\,^{n-1} = f\,^{n-1} \circ f$, a notation introduced by [Hans Heinrich Bürmann](wikipedia://en/19714107)[^o45][^o43] and [John Frederick William Herschel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Frederick_William_Herschel).[^o44][^o45][^o47][^o43] Repeated composition of such a function with itself is called **[function iteration](wikipedia://en/1448702)**.

- By convention, $f\,^{0}$ is defined as the identity map on $f\,$'s domain, $\operatorname{id}_{X}$.
- If $Y = X$ and $f: X \to X$ admits an [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) $f\,^{-1}$, negative functional powers $f\,^{-n}$ are defined for $n > 0$ as the [negated](wikipedia://en/228312) power of the inverse function: $f\,^{-n} = (f\,^{-1})^{n}$.[^o44][^o45][^o43]

If $f$ takes its values in a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) (in particular for real or complex-valued $f\,$), there is a risk of confusion, as $f\,^{n}$ could also stand for the $n$-fold product of $f$, e.g. $f\,^{2}(x) = f(x) \cdot f(x)$.[^o43] For trigonometric functions, usually the latter is meant, at least for positive exponents.[^o43] For example, in [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), this superscript notation represents standard [exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491) when used with [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367):

$$\sin^{2}(x) = \sin(x) \cdot \sin(x).$$

However, for negative exponents (especially −1), it nevertheless usually refers to the inverse function, e.g., $\tan^{-1} = \arctan \ne 1/\tan$.

In some cases, when, for a given function $f$, the equation $g \circ g = f$ has a unique solution $g$, that function can be defined as the [functional square root](wikipedia://en/7207519) of $f$, then written as $g = f\,^{1/2}$.

More generally, when $g^{n} = f$ has a unique solution for some natural number $n > 0$, then $f\,^{m/n}$ can be defined as $g^{m}$.

Under additional restrictions, this idea can be generalized so that the [iteration count](wikipedia://en/1448702) becomes a continuous parameter; in this case, such a system is called a [flow](wikipedia://en/2619735), specified through solutions of [Schröder's equation](wikipedia://en/5834541). Iterated functions and flows occur naturally in the study of [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals) and [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems).

To avoid ambiguity, some mathematicians choose to use $\circ$ to denote the compositional meaning, writing $f^{\circ n}(x)$ for the $n$-th iterate of the function $f(x)$, as in, for example, $f^{\circ3}(x)$ meaning $f(f(f(x)))$. For the same purpose, $f^{[n]}(x)$ was used by [Benjamin Peirce](wikipedia://en/364270)[^o46][^o43] whereas [Alfred Pringsheim](wikipedia://en/1068126) and [Jules Molk](wikipedia://en/35227735) suggested ${}^{n}f(x)$ instead.[^o48][^o43][^o25]

:::

## Alternative notations {#Alternative_notations}

Many mathematicians, particularly in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), omit the composition symbol, writing $gf$ for $g \circ f$.[^o35]

During the mid-20th century, some mathematicians adopted [postfix notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postfix_notation), writing $xf\,$ for $f(x)$ and $(xf)g$ for $g(f(x))$.[^o36] This can be more natural than [prefix notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_notation) in many cases, such as in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) when $x$ is a [row vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_vector) and $f$ and $g$ denote [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) and the composition is by [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). The order is important because function composition is not necessarily commutative. Having successive transformations applying and composing to the right agrees with the left-to-right reading sequence.

Mathematicians who use postfix notation may write "$fg$", meaning first apply $f$ and then apply $g$, in keeping with the order the symbols occur in postfix notation, thus making the notation "$fg$" ambiguous. Computer scientists may write "$f ; g$" for this,[^o37] thereby disambiguating the order of composition. To distinguish the left composition operator from a text semicolon, in the [Z notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_notation) the ⨾ character is used for left [relation composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relation_composition).[^o38] Since all functions are [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931#Special_types_of_binary_relations), it is correct to use the \[fat\] semicolon for function composition as well (see the article on [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170) for further details on this notation).

## Composition operator {#Composition_operator}

Given a function $g$, the **composition operator** $C_{g}$ is defined as the [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) which maps functions to functions as

$$C_g f = f \circ g.$$

Composition operators are studied in the field of [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675).

## Multivariate functions {#Multivariate_functions}

Partial composition is possible for [multivariate functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_function). The function resulting when some argument $x_{i}$ of the function $f$ is replaced by the function $g$ is called a composition of $f$ and $g$ in some computer engineering contexts, and is denoted $f |_{x_{i} = g}$

$$f|_{x_i = g} = f (x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}, g(x_1, x_2, \ldots, x_n), x_{i+1}, \ldots, x_n).$$

When $g$ is a simple constant $b$, composition degenerates into a (partial) valuation, whose result is also known as [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) or *co-factor*.[^o39]

$$f|_{x_i = b} = f (x_1, \ldots, x_{i-1}, b, x_{i+1}, \ldots, x_n).$$

In general, the composition of multivariate functions may involve several other functions as arguments, as in the definition of [primitive recursive function](wikipedia://en/24829). Given $f$, a $n$-ary function, and $n$ $m$-ary functions $g_{1}, ..., g_{n}$, the composition of $f$ with $g_{1}, ..., g_{n}$, is the $m$-ary function

$$h(x_1,\ldots,x_m) = f(g_1(x_1,\ldots,x_m),\ldots,g_n(x_1,\ldots,x_m)).$$

This is sometimes called the **generalized composite** or **superposition** of *f* with $g_{1}, ..., g_{n}$.[^o27] The partial composition in only one argument mentioned previously can be instantiated from this more general scheme by setting all argument functions except one to be suitably chosen [projection functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_function). Here $g_{1}, ..., g_{n}$ can be seen as a single vector/[tuple](wikipedia://en/132729)-valued function in this generalized scheme, in which case this is precisely the standard definition of function composition.[^o40]

A set of finitary [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245) on some base set *X* is called a [clone](wikipedia://en/15535840) if it contains all projections and is closed under generalized composition. A clone generally contains operations of various [arities](wikipedia://en/42301).[^o27] The notion of commutation also finds an interesting generalization in the multivariate case; a function *f* of arity *n* is said to commute with a function *g* of arity *m* if *f* is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) preserving *g*, and vice versa, that is:[^o27]

$$f(g(a_{11},\ldots,a_{1m}),\ldots,g(a_{n1},\ldots,a_{nm})) = g(f(a_{11},\ldots,a_{n1}),\ldots,f(a_{1m},\ldots,a_{nm})).$$

A unary operation always commutes with itself, but this is not necessarily the case for a binary (or higher arity) operation. A binary (or higher arity) operation that commutes with itself is called [medial or entropic](wikipedia://en/502296).[^o27]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

[Composition](wikipedia://en/7472170) can be generalized to arbitrary [binary relations](wikipedia://en/3931). If $R \subseteq X \times Y$ and $S \subseteq Y \times Z$ are two binary relations, then their composition amounts to

$$R \circ S = \{(x,z) \in X \times Z: (\exists y \in Y)((x,y) \in R\,\land\,(y,z) \in S)\}.$$

Considering a function as a special case of a binary relation (namely [functional relations](wikipedia://en/3931#Specific_types_of_binary_relations)), function composition satisfies the definition for relation composition. A small circle $R\circ S$ has been used for the [infix notation of composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170#Notational_variations), as well as functions. When used to represent composition of functions $(g \circ f)(x) \ = \ g(f(x))$ however, the text sequence is reversed to illustrate the different operation sequences accordingly.

The composition is defined in the same way for [partial functions](wikipedia://en/23577) and Cayley's theorem has its analogue called the [Wagner–Preston theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wagner–Preston_theorem).[^o41]

The [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) with functions as [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) is the prototypical [category](wikipedia://en/53759). The axioms of a category are in fact inspired from the properties (and also the definition) of function composition.[^o42] The structures given by composition are axiomatized and generalized in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869) with the concept of [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) as the category-theoretical replacement of functions. The reversed order of composition in the formula $(f\,\circ\,g)^{-1} = (g^{-1} \circ f\,^{-1})$ applies for [composition of relations](wikipedia://en/7472170) using [converse relations](wikipedia://en/3941020), and thus in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890). These structures form [dagger categories](wikipedia://en/7152070).

> *The standard "foundation" for mathematics starts with [sets and their elements](wikipedia://en/27553). It is possible to start differently, by axiomatising not elements of sets but functions between sets. This can be done by using the language of categories and universal constructions.*
>
> *. . . the membership relation for sets can often be replaced by the composition operation for functions. This leads to an alternative foundation for Mathematics upon categories -- specifically, on the category of all functions. Now much of Mathematics is dynamic, in that it deals with morphisms of an object into another object of the same kind. Such morphisms* (*like functions*) *form categories, and so the approach via categories fits well with the objective of organizing and understanding Mathematics. That, in truth, should be the goal of a proper philosophy of Mathematics.*
>
> \- [Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574), *[Mathematics: Form and Function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics,_Form_and_Function)*[^o19]
