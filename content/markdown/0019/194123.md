# Heron's formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Heron's formula"}

*Image: Triangle with notations 2 without points.svg.* A triangle with sides $a$, $b$, and $c$

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **Heron's formula** (or **Hero's formula**) gives the [area of a triangle](wikipedia://en/9194858) in terms of the three side lengths $a,$ $b,$ $c.$ Letting $s$ be the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617) of the triangle, $s = \tfrac12(a + b + c)$, the area $A$ is[^o2]

$$A = \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}.$$

:::

It is named after first-century engineer [Heron of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heron_of_Alexandria) (or Hero) who proved it in his work *Metrica*, though it was probably known centuries earlier.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

{|- class="wikitable floatleft" style="margin: 0;" ! colspan=2 style="padding: 0 1em" | Area calculator |- | || |- | || |- | || |- | $s$ || |- | Area[^o5] |style="max-width: 4em" | |}

Let $\triangle ABC$ be the triangle with sides $a = 4$, $b = 13$, and $c = 15$. This triangle's semiperimeter is $s = \tfrac12(a+b+c)= {}$$\tfrac12(4+13+15) = 16$ therefore $s-a = 12$, $s-b =3$, $s-c =1$, and the area is

$$\begin{aligned} A &= {\textstyle \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}} \\[3mu] &= {\textstyle \sqrt{16 \cdot 12 \cdot 3 \cdot 1 \vphantom)} } \\[3mu] &= 24. \end{aligned}$$

In this example, the triangle's side lengths and area are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), making it a [Heronian triangle](wikipedia://en/731959). However, Heron's formula works equally well when the side lengths are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438). As long as they obey the strict [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941), they define a triangle in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) whose area is a positive real number.

:::

## Alternative expressions {#Alternative_expressions}

Heron's formula can also be written in terms of just the side lengths instead of using the semiperimeter, in several ways,

$$\begin{aligned} A &=\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{(a+b+c)(-a+b+c)(a-b+c)(a+b-c)} \\[6mu] &=\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{2\bigl(a^2 b^2+a^2c^2+b^2c^2\bigr)-\bigl(a^4+b^4+c^4\bigr)} \\[6mu] &=\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{\bigl(a^2+b^2+c^2\bigr)\vphantom)^2-2\bigl(a^4+b^4+c^4\bigr)} \\[6mu] &=\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{4\bigl(a^2b^2+a^2c^2+b^2c^2\bigr)-\bigl(a^2+b^2+c^2\bigr)\vphantom)^2} \\[6mu] &=\tfrac{1}{4}\sqrt{4a^2b^2-\bigl(a^2+b^2-c^2\bigr)\vphantom)^2}. \end{aligned}$$

After expansion, the expression under the square root is a [quadratic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial) of the squared side lengths $\textstyle a^2$, $\textstyle b^2$, $\textstyle c^2$.

The same relation can be expressed using the [Cayley–Menger determinant](wikipedia://en/2826108),[^o7]

$$-16A^2 = \begin{vmatrix} 0 & a^2 & b^2 & 1 \\ a^2 & 0 & c^2 & 1 \\ b^2 & c^2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix}.$$

## History {#History}

The formula is credited to [Heron (or Hero) of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/68274) ( 60 AD),[^o9] and a proof can be found in his book *Metrica*. Mathematical historian [Thomas Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Heath_%28classicist%29) suggested that [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) knew the formula over two centuries earlier,[^o10] and since *Metrica* is a collection of the mathematical knowledge available in the ancient world, it is possible that the formula predates the reference given in that work.

A formula equivalent to Heron's was discovered by Chinese mathematician [Qin Jiushao](wikipedia://en/612837):

$$A = \frac1{2}\sqrt{a^2 c^2 - \left(\frac{a^2 + c^2 - b^2}{2}\right)^2},$$

published in *[Mathematical Treatise in Nine Sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_Treatise_in_Nine_Sections)* in 1247.[^o12]

## Proofs {#Proofs}

There are many ways to prove Heron's formula, for example using [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261) as below, or the [incenter](wikipedia://en/316837) and one [excircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excircle) of the triangle,[^o15] or as a special case of [De Gua's theorem](wikipedia://en/6469973) (for the particular case of acute triangles),[^o16] or as a special case of [Brahmagupta's formula](wikipedia://en/194129) (for the case of a degenerate cyclic quadrilateral).

::: {#o18 .proof cue=named-heading name="Trigonometric proof using the law of cosines"}

### Trigonometric proof using the law of cosines {#Trigonometric_proof_using_the_law_of_cosines}

A modern proof, which uses [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and is quite different from the one provided by Heron, follows.[^o19] Let $a,$ $b,$ $c$ be the sides of the triangle and $\alpha,$ $\beta,$ $\gamma$ the [angles](wikipedia://en/1196) opposite those sides. Applying the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890) we get

$$\cos \gamma = \frac{a^2+b^2-c^2}{2ab}$$

*Image: Triangle with notations 2 without points.svg.* A triangle with sides $a$, $b$ and $c$

From this proof, we get the algebraic statement that

$$\sin \gamma = \sqrt{1-\cos^2 \gamma} = \frac{\sqrt{4a^2 b^2 -\bigl(a^2 +b^2 -c^2\bigr)\vphantom)^2 }}{2ab}.$$

The [altitude](wikipedia://en/161241) of the triangle on base $a$ has length $b\sin\gamma$, and it follows

$$\begin{aligned} A &= \tfrac12 (\mbox{base}) (\mbox{altitude}) \\[6mu] &= \tfrac12 ab\sin \gamma \\[6mu] &= \frac{ab}{4ab}\sqrt{4a^2 b^2 -\bigl(a^2 +b^2 -c^2\bigr)\vphantom)^2} \\[6mu] &= \tfrac14\sqrt{-a^4 - b^4 - c^4 + 2 a^2 b^2 + 2 a^2 c^2 + 2 b^2 c^2} \\[6mu] &= \tfrac14\sqrt{(a + b + c)(- a + b + c)(a - b + c)(a + b - c)} \\[6mu] &= \sqrt{ \left(\frac{a + b + c}{2}\right) \left(\frac{- a + b + c}{2}\right) \left(\frac{a - b + c}{2}\right) \left(\frac{a + b - c}{2}\right)} \\[6mu] &= \sqrt{s(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}. \end{aligned}$$

:::

::: {#o21 .proof cue=named-heading name="Algebraic proof using the Pythagorean theorem"}

### Algebraic proof using the Pythagorean theorem {#Algebraic_proof_using_the_Pythagorean_theorem}

*Image: Triangle with notations 3.svg.* Triangle with altitude $h$ cutting base $c$ into $d + (c - d)$

The following proof is very similar to one given by Raifaizen.[^o22] By the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) we have $b^2 = h^2 + d^2$ and $a^2 = h^2 + (c - d)^2$ according to the figure at the right. Subtracting these yields $a^2 - b^2 = c^2 - 2cd.$ This equation allows us to express $d$ in terms of the sides of the triangle:

$$d = \frac{-a^2 + b^2 + c^2}{2c}.$$

For the height of the triangle we have that $h^2 = b^2 - d^2.$ By replacing $d$ with the formula given above and applying the [difference of squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_of_squares) identity we get

$$\begin{aligned} h^2 &= b^2-\left(\frac{-a^2 + b^2 + c^2}{2c}\right)^2 \\ &= \frac{(2bc - a^2 + b^2 + c^2)(2bc + a^2 - b^2 - c^2)}{4c^2} \\ &= \frac{\big((b + c)^2 - a^2\big)\big(a^2 - (b - c)^2\big)}{4c^2} \\ &= \frac{(b + c - a)(b + c + a)(a + b - c)(a - b + c)}{4c^2} \\ &= \frac{2(s - a) \cdot 2s \cdot 2(s - c) \cdot 2(s - b)}{4c^2} \\ &= \frac{4s(s - a)(s - b)(s -c )}{c^2}. \end{aligned}$$

We now apply this result to the formula that calculates the area of a triangle from its height:

$$\begin{aligned} A &= \frac{ch}{2} \\ &= \sqrt{\frac{c^2}{4} \cdot \frac{4s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}{c^2}} \\ &= \sqrt{s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c)}. \end{aligned}$$

:::

::: {#o24 .proof cue=named-heading name="Trigonometric proof using the law of cotangents"}

### Trigonometric proof using the law of cotangents {#Trigonometric_proof_using_the_law_of_cotangents}

*Image: Herontriangle2greek.svg.* Geometrical significance of $s - a$, $s - b$, and $s - c$. See the [law of cotangents](wikipedia://en/32376357) for the reasoning behind this.

If $r$ is the radius of the [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) of the triangle, then the triangle can be broken into three triangles of equal altitude $r$ and bases $a,$ $b,$ and $c.$ Their combined area is

$$A = \tfrac12ar + \tfrac12br + \tfrac12cr = rs,$$

where $s = \tfrac12(a + b + c)$ is the semiperimeter.

The triangle can alternately be broken into six triangles (in congruent pairs) of altitude $r$ and bases $s - a,$ $s - b,$ and $s - c$ of combined area (see [law of cotangents](wikipedia://en/32376357))

$$\begin{aligned} A &= r(s-a) + r(s-b) + r(s-c) \\[2mu] &= r^2\left(\frac{s - a}{r} + \frac{s - b}{r} + \frac{s - c}{r}\right) \\[2mu] &= r^2\left(\cot{\frac{\alpha}{2}} + \cot{\frac{\beta}{2}} + \cot{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\right) \\[3mu] &= r^2\left(\cot{\frac{\alpha}{2}} \cot{\frac{\beta}{2}} \cot{\frac{\gamma}{2}}\right)\\[3mu] &= r^2\left(\frac{s - a}{r} \cdot \frac{s - b}{r} \cdot \frac{s - c}{r}\right) \\[3mu] &= \frac{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)}{r}. \end{aligned}$$

The middle step above is $\cot{\tfrac{\alpha}{2}} + \cot{\tfrac{\beta}{2}} + \cot{\tfrac{\gamma}{2}} = {}$$\cot{\tfrac{\alpha}{2}}\cot{\tfrac{\beta}{2}}\cot{\tfrac{\gamma}{2}}$, the [triple cotangent identity](wikipedia://en/7527818#Miscellaneous_–_the_triple_cotangent_identity), which applies because the sum of half-angles is $\tfrac\alpha2 + \tfrac\beta2 + \tfrac\gamma2 = \tfrac\pi2.$

Combining the two, we get

$$A^2 = s(s - a)(s - b)(s - c),$$

from which the result follows.

:::

:::

## Numerical stability {#Numerical_stability}

Heron's formula as given above is [numerically unstable](wikipedia://en/233807) for triangles with a very small angle, causing [round-off error](wikipedia://en/432450) when computing with limited precision such as when using [floating-point arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11376). Such triangles have one or two sides whose length is very close to the semiperimeter, leading to [catastrophic cancellation](wikipedia://en/19985542). A stable alternative involves arranging the lengths of the sides so that $a \ge b \ge c$ and computing[^o26]

$$A = \tfrac14 \sqrt{\big(a + (b + c)\big) \big(c - (a - b)\big) \big(c + (a - b)\big) \big(a + (b - c)\big)}.$$

The extra parentheses indicate the order of operations required to achieve numerical stability in the evaluation.

## Similar triangle-area formulae {#Similar_triangle-area_formulae}

Three other formulae for the area of a general triangle have a similar structure as Heron's formula, expressed in terms of different variables.

First, if $m_a,$ $m_b,$ and $m_c$ are the [medians](wikipedia://en/1072006) from sides $a,$ $b,$ and $c$ respectively, and their semi-sum is $\sigma = \tfrac12(m_a + m_b + m_c)$, then[^o28]

$$A = \tfrac43 \sqrt{\sigma (\sigma - m_a)(\sigma - m_b)(\sigma - m_c)}.$$

Next, if $h_a$, $h_b$, and $h_c$ are the [altitudes](wikipedia://en/161241) from sides $a,$ $b,$ and $c$ respectively, and semi-sum of their reciprocals is $\textstyle H = \tfrac12\bigl(h_a^{-1} + h_b^{-1} + h_c^{-1}\bigr)$, then[^o29]

$$A^{-1} = 4 \sqrt{H\bigl(H-h_a^{-1}\bigr)\bigl(H-h_b^{-1}\bigr)\bigl(H-h_c^{-1}\bigr)}.$$

Finally, if $\alpha,$ $\beta,$ and $\gamma$ are the three angle measures of the triangle, and the semi-sum of their [sines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) is $S = \tfrac12(\sin\alpha + \sin\beta + \sin\gamma)$, then[^o30][^o31]

$$\begin{aligned} A &= D^{2} \sqrt{S(S-\sin \alpha)(S-\sin \beta)(S-\sin \gamma)} \\[5mu] &= \tfrac12 D^{2} \sin \alpha\,\sin \beta\,\sin \gamma, \end{aligned}$$

where $D$ is the diameter of the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432), $D = a/{\sin \alpha} = b/{\sin \beta} = c/{\sin \gamma}.$ This last formula coincides with the standard Heron formula when the circumcircle has unit diameter.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

*Image: Cyclicquadrilateral.png.* Cyclic quadrilateral

Heron's formula is a special case of [Brahmagupta's formula](wikipedia://en/194129) for the area of a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131). Heron's formula and Brahmagupta's formula are both special cases of [Bretschneider's formula](wikipedia://en/5615284) for the area of a [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278). Heron's formula can be obtained from Brahmagupta's formula or Bretschneider's formula by setting one of the sides of the quadrilateral to zero. Brahmagupta's formula gives the area $K$ of a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131) whose sides have lengths $a,$ $b,$ $c,$ $d$ as

$$K=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}$$

where $s = \tfrac12(a + b + c + d)$ is the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617).

Heron's formula is also a special case of the formula for the [area of a trapezoid or trapezium](wikipedia://en/201826#Area) based only on its sides. Heron's formula is obtained by setting the smaller parallel side to zero.

Expressing Heron's formula with a [Cayley–Menger determinant](wikipedia://en/2826108) in terms of the squares of the [distances](wikipedia://en/39378) between the three given vertices,

$$A = \frac{1}{4} \sqrt{- \begin{vmatrix} 0 & a^2 & b^2 & 1 \\ a^2 & 0 & c^2 & 1 \\ b^2 & c^2 & 0 & 1 \\ 1 & 1 & 1 & 0 \end{vmatrix} }$$

illustrates its similarity to [Tartaglia's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tartaglia's_formula) for the [volume](wikipedia://en/32498) of a [three-simplex](wikipedia://en/39781).

Another generalization of Heron's formula to pentagons and hexagons inscribed in a circle was discovered by [David P. Robbins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_P._Robbins).[^o34]

### Degenerate and imaginary triangles {#Degenerate_and_imaginary_triangles}

If one of three given lengths is equal to the sum of the other two, the three sides determine a [degenerate triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degenerate_triangle), a line segment with zero area. In this case, the semiperimeter will equal the longest side, causing Heron's formula to equal zero.

If one of three given lengths is greater than the sum of the other two, then they violate the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) and do not describe the sides of a Euclidean triangle. In this case, Heron's formula gives an [imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) result. For example if $a=3$ and $b=c=1$, then $\textstyle A = \tfrac {3\sqrt5}4i$. This can be interpreted using a triangle in the [complex coordinate plane](wikipedia://en/39125683) $\mathbb{C}^2$, where "area" can be a complex-valued quantity, or as a triangle lying in a [pseudo-Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/10962546) with one space-like dimension and one time-like dimension.[^o36]

### Volume of a tetrahedron {#Volume_of_a_tetrahedron}

If $U,$ $V,$ $W,$ $u,$ $v,$ $w$ are lengths of edges of the tetrahedron (first three form a triangle; $u$ opposite to $U$ and so on), then[^o38]

$$\text{volume} = \frac {\sqrt {\,( - a + b + c + d)\,(a - b + c + d)\,(a + b - c + d)\,(a + b + c - d)}} {192\,u\,v\,w}$$

*Image: tetrahedron\_volume.svg.* Tetrahedron with base sides $U$, $V$, $W$

where

$$\begin{aligned} a &= \sqrt {\vphantom{y}xYZ}, \qquad b = \sqrt {XyZ}, & c &= \sqrt {\vphantom{y}XYz}, \qquad d = \sqrt {\vphantom{X}xyz}, \\[4mu] X &= (-U + v + w)\,(U + v + w), & x &= (U - v + w)\,(U + v - w), \\ Y &= (-V + w + u)\,(V + w + u), & y &= (V - w + u)\,(V + w - u), \\ Z &= (-W + u + v)\,(W + u + v), & z &= (W - u + v)\,(W + u - v). \end{aligned}$$

### Spherical and hyperbolic geometry {#Spherical_and_hyperbolic_geometry}

[L'Huilier's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L'Huilier's_formula) relates the area of a triangle in [spherical geometry](wikipedia://en/174026) to its side lengths. For a [spherical triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_triangle) with side lengths $a,$ $b,$ and $c$, semiperimeter $s=\tfrac12(a+b+c)$, and area $S$,[^o40]

$$\tan^2 \frac S 4 = \tan \frac s2 \tan\frac{s-a}2 \tan\frac{s-b}2 \tan\frac{s-c}2$$

For a triangle in [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) the analogous formula is

$$\tan^2 \frac S 4 = \tanh \frac s2 \tanh\frac{s-a}2 \tanh\frac{s-b}2 \tanh\frac{s-c}2.$$
