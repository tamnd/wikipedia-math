# Chemical equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chemical equation"}

A **chemical equation** is the symbolic representation ([notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notation)) of a [chemical reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction) in the form of symbols and [chemical formulas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_formula). The [reactant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reactant) entities are given on the left-hand side, and the [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_%28chemistry%29) entities are on the right-hand side with a plus sign between the entities in both the reactants and the products, and an arrow that points towards the products to show the direction of the reaction. The chemical formulas may be symbolic, [structural](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_formula) (pictorial diagrams), or intermixed. The coefficients next to the symbols and formulas of entities are the absolute values of the [stoichiometric numbers](wikipedia://en/199040#Structure). The first chemical equation was diagrammed by [Jean Beguin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Beguin) in 1615.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Stoichiometric coefficient"}

## Structure {#Structure}

A chemical equation (see an example below) consists of a list of reactants (the starting substances) on the left-hand side, an [arrow symbol](wikipedia://en/4912832), and a list of products (substances formed in the chemical reaction) on the right-hand side. Each substance is specified by its [chemical formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_formula), optionally preceded by a number called **stoichiometric coefficient**. The coefficient specifies how many entities (e.g. [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule)) of that substance are involved in the reaction on a molecular basis. If not written explicitly, the coefficient is equal to 1. Multiple substances on any side of the equation are separated from each other by a [plus sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plus_sign).

As an example, the equation for the reaction of [hydrochloric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrochloric_acid) with [sodium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium) can be denoted:

2HCl + 2Na -> 2NaCl + H2

Given the formulas are fairly simple, this equation could be read as "two H-C-L plus two N-A yields two N-A-C-L and H two." Alternately, and in general for equations involving complex chemicals, the chemical formulas are read using [IUPAC nomenclature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IUPAC_nomenclature), which could [verbalise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Verbalisation) this equation as "two hydrochloric acid molecules and two sodium atoms react to form two [formula units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formula_unit) of [sodium chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sodium_chloride) and a [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) gas molecule."

### State of matter {#State_of_matter}

To indicate [physical state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_state) of a chemical, a symbol in parentheses may be appended to its formula: (s) for a solid, (l) for a liquid, (g) for a gas, and (aq) for an [aqueous solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solution).[^o8] This is especially done when one wishes to emphasize the states or changes thereof. For example, the reaction of aqueous hydrochloric acid with solid (metallic) sodium to form aqueous sodium chloride and hydrogen gas would be written like this:

2HCl(aq) + 2Na(s) -> 2NaCl(aq) + H2(g)

That reaction would have different [thermodynamic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic) and [kinetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_kinetics) properties if gaseous [hydrogen chloride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_chloride) were to replace the hydrochloric acid as a reactant:

2HCl(g) + 2Na(s) -> 2NaCl(s) + H2(g)

Alternately, an arrow without parentheses is used in some cases to indicate formation of a gas ↑ or precipitate ↓. This is especially useful if only one such species is formed. Here is an example indicating that hydrogen gas is formed:

2HCl + 2Na -> 2 NaCl + H2 ↑

### Catalysis and other conditions {#Catalysis_and_other_conditions}

*Image: Baker-Venkataraman-Rearrangement V.1.svg.* The [Baker–Venkataraman rearrangement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baker–Venkataraman_rearrangement) needs a [base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_%28chemistry%29) as a [catalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalyst).

If the reaction requires energy, it is indicated above the arrow. A capital Greek letter [delta (Δ)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delta_%28letter%29) or a [triangle (△)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/△) is put on the reaction arrow to show that energy in the form of heat is added to the reaction. The expression $h\nu$ is used as a symbol for the addition of energy in the form of light. Other symbols are used for other specific types of energy or radiation.

Similarly, if a reaction requires a certain medium with certain specific characteristics, then the name of the acid or base that is used as a medium may be placed on top of the arrow. If no specific acid or base is required, another way of denoting the use of an acidic or basic medium is to write H<sup>\+</sup> or OH<sup>−</sup> (or even "acid" or "base") on top of the arrow. Specific conditions of the temperature and pressure, as well as the presence of catalysts, may be indicated in the same way.

### Notation variants {#Notation_variants}

*Image: Acid-CatAmideHydrolMarch.png.* This illustration of a mechanism for acid-catalyzed [hydrolysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrolysis) of an [amide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amide) puts some reactants and products above the arrows and to their branches to allow chaining of the chemical "equations".

The standard notation for chemical equations only permits all reactants on one side, all products on the other, and all stoichiometric coefficients positive. For example, the usual form of the equation for [dehydration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dehydration_reaction) of [methanol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methanol) to [dimethylether](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimethylether) is:

2 CH3OH -> CH3OCH3 + H2O

Sometimes an extension is used, where some substances with their stoichiometric coefficients are moved above or below the arrow, preceded by a plus sign or nothing for a reactant, and by a minus sign for a product. Then the same equation can look like this:

$$\ce{2 CH3OH ->[\overset{}\ce{-H2O}] CH3OCH3}$$

Such notation serves to hide less important substances from the sides of the equation, to make the type of reaction at hand more obvious, and to facilitate chaining of chemical equations. This is very useful in illustrating multi-step [reaction mechanisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction_mechanism). Note that the substances above or below the arrows are not [catalysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catalyst) in this case, because they are consumed or produced in the reaction like ordinary reactants or products.

Another extension used in reaction mechanisms moves some substances to branches of the arrow. Both extensions are used in the example illustration of a mechanism.

Use of negative stoichiometric coefficients at either side of the equation (like in the example below) is not widely adopted and is often discouraged.[^o11]

$$\ce{2 CH3OH \;-\; H2O -> CH3OCH3}$$

:::

## Balancing chemical equations {#Balancing_chemical_equations}

Because no [nuclear reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_reaction) take place in a chemical reaction, the [chemical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_element) pass through the reaction unchanged. Thus, each side of the chemical equation must represent the same number of atoms of any particular element (or [nuclide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclide), if different [isotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope) are taken into account). The same holds for the total [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804), as stated by the [charge conservation](wikipedia://en/3094621) law. An equation adhering to these requirements is said to be **balanced**.

A chemical equation is balanced by assigning suitable values to the stoichiometric coefficients. Simple equations can be balanced by inspection, that is, by trial and error. Another technique involves solving a [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087).

Balanced equations are usually written with smallest [natural-number](wikipedia://en/21474) coefficients. Yet sometimes it may be advantageous to accept a fractional coefficient, if it simplifies the other coefficients. The introductory example can thus be rewritten as

$$\ce{HCl + Na -> NaCl + 1/2 H2}$$

In some circumstances the fractional coefficients are even inevitable. For example, the reaction corresponding to the [standard enthalpy of formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_enthalpy_of_formation) must be written such that one molecule of a single product is formed. This will often require that some reactant coefficients be fractional, as is the case with the formation of [lithium fluoride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithium_fluoride):

$$\ce{Li(s) + 1/2F2(g) -> LiF(s)}$$

### Inspection method {#Inspection_method}

*Image: Combustion reaction of methane.jpg.* As seen from the equation CH + 2 O → CO + 2 H, a coefficient of 2 must be placed before the [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) gas on the reactants side and before the [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properties_of_water) on the products side in order for, as per the law of conservation of mass, the quantity of each element does not change during the reaction

*Image: H3PO4 balancing chemical equation phosphorus pentoxide and water becomes phosphoric acid.gif.* [P<sub>4</sub>O<sub>10</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphorus_pentoxide) + 6 [H<sub>2</sub>O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) → 4 [H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid)This chemical equation is being balanced by first multiplying H<sub>3</sub>PO<sub>4</sub> by four to match the number of P atoms, and then multiplying H<sub>2</sub>O by six to match the numbers of H and O atoms.

The method of inspection can be outlined as setting the most complex substance's stoichiometric coefficient to 1 and assigning values to other coefficients step by step such that both sides of the equation end up with the same number of atoms for each element. If any [fractional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) coefficients arise during this process, the presence of fractions may be eliminated (at any time) by multiplying all coefficients by their [lowest common denominator](wikipedia://en/378200).

::: {#o14 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**

Balancing of the chemical equation for the complete [combustion](wikipedia://en/5638) of [methane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane)

$$\ce{\mathord{?}\,{CH4} + \mathord{?}\,{O2} -> \mathord{?}\,{CO2} + \mathord{?}\,{H2O}}$$

is achieved as follows:

1. A coefficient of 1 is placed in front of the most complex formula ([CH<sub>4</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Methane)):

$$\ce{1 {CH4} + \mathord{?}\,{O2} -> \mathord{?}\,{CO2} + \mathord{?}\,{H2O}}$$

1. The left-hand side has 1 [carbon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon) atom, so 1 molecule of [CO<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) will balance it. The left-hand side also has 4 [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) atoms, which will be balanced by 2 molecules of [H<sub>2</sub>O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water):

$$\ce{1 {CH4} + \mathord{?}\,{O2} -> 1 {CO2} + 2 H2O}$$

1. Balancing the 4 [oxygen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxygen) atoms of the right-hand side by 2 molecules of [O<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dioxygen) yields the equation

$$\ce{1 CH4 + 2 O2 -> 1 CO2 + 2 H2O}$$

1. The coefficients equal to 1 are omitted, as they do not need to be specified explicitly:

$$\ce{CH4 + 2 O2 -> CO2 + 2 H2O}$$

1. It is wise to check that the final equation is balanced, i.e. that for each element there is the same number of atoms on the left- and right-hand side: 1 carbon, 4 hydrogen, and 4 oxygen.

:::

### System of linear equations {#System_of_linear_equations}

For each chemical element (or nuclide or unchanged [moiety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moiety_%28chemistry%29) or charge) $i$, its conservation requirement can be expressed by the [mathematical equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equation_%28mathematics%29)

$$\sum_{j\,\in\,\text{reactants}} \!\!\!\!\! a_{ij} s_j \ = \!\!\!\!\! \sum_{j\,\in\,\text{products}} \!\!\!\!\! a_{ij} s_j$$

where

$a_{ij}$ is the number of atoms of element $i$ in a molecule of substance $j$ (per formula in the chemical equation), and

$s_{j}$ is the stoichiometric coefficient for the substance $j$.

This results in a [homogeneous system of linear equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_system_of_linear_equations), which are readily solved using mathematical methods. Such system always has the all-zeros [trivial solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_solution), which we are not interested in, but if there are any additional solutions, there will be infinite number of them. Any non-trivial solution will balance the chemical equation. A "preferred" solution is one with [whole-number](wikipedia://en/14563), mostly positive stoichiometric coefficients $s_{j}$ with [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) equal to one.

::: {#o17 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

#### Example {#Example}

Let us assign variables to stoichiometric coefficients of the chemical equation from the previous section and write the corresponding linear equations:

$$\ce{\mathit{s}_1 {CH4} + \mathit{s}_2 {O2} -> \mathit{s}_3 {CO2} + \mathit{s}_4 {H2O}}$$

$$\quad\;\;\; \begin{aligned} \text{C:} && s_1 & = s_3 \\ \text{H:} && 4s_1 & = 2s_4 \\ \text{O:} && 2s_2 & = 2s_3 + s_4 \end{aligned}$$

All solutions to this system of linear equations are of the following form, where $r$ is any [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438):

$$\begin{aligned} s_1 & = r \\ s_2 & = 2r \\ s_3 & = r \\ s_4 & = 2r \end{aligned}$$

The choice of $r$ = 1 yields the preferred solution,

$$\begin{aligned} s_1 & = 1 \\ s_2 & = 2 \\ s_3 & = 1 \\ s_4 & = 2 \end{aligned}$$

which corresponds to the balanced chemical equation:

$$\ce{CH4 + 2 O2 -> CO2 + 2 H2O}$$

:::

### Matrix method {#Matrix_method}

The system of linear equations introduced in the previous section can also be written using an efficient [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) formalism. First, to unify the reactant and product stoichiometric coefficients $s_{j}$, let us introduce the quantity

$$\nu_j = \begin{cases} -s_j & \text{for a reactant} \\ +s_j & \text{for a product} \end{cases}$$

called [stoichiometric number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stoichiometric_number), which simplifies the linear equations to

$$\sum_{j=1}^J a_{ij} \nu_j=0$$

where $J$ is the total number of reactant and product substances (formulas) in the chemical equation.

Placement of the values $a_{ij}$ at row $i$ and column $j$ of the composition matrix

$\mathbf{A} =$$\begin{bmatrix} a_{1,1} & a_{1,2} & \cdots & a_{1,J} \\ a_{2,1} & a_{2,2} & \cdots & a_{2,J} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \end{bmatrix}$

and arrangement of the stoichiometric numbers into the stoichiometric vector

$\mathbf{\nu} =$$\begin{bmatrix} \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ \vdots \\ \nu_J \end{bmatrix}$

allows the system of equations to be expressed as a single [matrix equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_equation):

$$\mathbf{A\nu} = \mathbf{0}$$

Like previously, any nonzero stoichiometric vector $\mathbf{\nu}$, which solves the matrix equation, will balance the chemical equation.

The set of solutions to the matrix equation is a [linear space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_space) called the [kernel](wikipedia://en/1072915) of the matrix $\mathbf{A}$. For this space to contain nonzero vectors $\mathbf{\nu}$, i.e. to have a positive [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) $J$<sub>N</sub>, the columns of the composition matrix $\mathbf{A}$ must not be [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863). The problem of balancing a chemical equation then becomes the problem of determining the $J$<sub>N</sub>-dimensional kernel of the composition matrix. Only for $J$<sub>N</sub> = 1 will there be a unique preferred solution to the balancing problem. For $J$<sub>N</sub> > 1 there will be an infinite number of preferred solutions with $J$<sub>N</sub> of them linearly independent. If $J$<sub>N</sub> = 0, there will be only the unusable trivial solution, the zero vector.

Techniques have been developed[^o19][^o20] to quickly calculate a set of $J$<sub>N</sub> independent solutions to the balancing problem, which are superior to the inspection and in that they are determinative and yield all solutions to the balancing problem.

::: {#o21 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**

Using the same chemical equation again, write the corresponding matrix equation:

$$\ce{\mathit{s}_1 {CH4} + \mathit{s}_2 {O2} -> \mathit{s}_3 {CO2} + \mathit{s}_4 {H2O}}$$

$$\begin{matrix} \text{C:} \\ \text{H:} \\ \text{O:} \end{matrix} \quad \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 1 & 0 \\ 4 & 0 & 0 & 2 \\ 0 & 2 & 2 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ \nu_3 \\ \nu_4 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{0}$$

Its solutions are of the following form, where $r$ is any real number:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \nu_1 \\ \nu_2 \\ \nu_3 \\ \nu_4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -s_1 \\ -s_2 \\ s_3 \\ s_4 \end{bmatrix} = r \begin{bmatrix} -1 \\ -2 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

The choice of $r$ = 1 and a [sign](wikipedia://en/7951270)-flip of the first two rows yields the preferred solution to the balancing problem:

$$\begin{bmatrix} -\nu_1 \\ -\nu_2 \\ \nu_3 \\ \nu_4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} s_1 \\ s_2 \\ s_3 \\ s_4 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \\ 2 \\ 1 \\ 2 \end{bmatrix}$$

:::

## Ionic equations {#Ionic_equations}

An ionic equation is a chemical equation in which [electrolytes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrolyte) are written as dissociated [ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ion). Ionic equations are used for [single](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_displacement_reaction) and [double displacement reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_displacement_reaction) that occur in [aqueous solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueous_solution).

For example, in the following precipitation reaction:

$$\ce{CaCl2 + 2AgNO3 -> Ca(NO3)2 + 2 AgCl(v)}$$

the full ionic equation is:

$$\ce{Ca^2+ + 2Cl^- + 2Ag+ + 2NO3^- -> Ca^2+ + 2NO3^- + 2AgCl(v)}$$

or, with all physical states included:

$$\ce{Ca^2+(aq) + 2Cl^{-}(aq) + 2Ag+(aq) + 2NO3^{-}(aq) -> Ca^2+(aq) + 2NO3^{-}(aq) + 2AgCl(v)}$$

In this reaction, the Ca<sup>2+</sup> and the NO<sub>3</sub><sup>−</sup> ions remain in solution and are not part of the reaction. That is, these ions are identical on both the reactant and product side of the chemical equation. Because such ions do not participate in the reaction, they are called [spectator ions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectator_ion). A *net ionic* equation is the full ionic equation from which the spectator ions have been removed.[^o24] The net ionic equation of the proceeding reactions is:

$$\ce{2Cl^- + 2Ag+ -> 2AgCl(v)}$$

or, in *reduced* balanced form,

$$\ce{Ag+ + Cl^- -> AgCl(v)}$$

In a [neutralization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutralization_%28chemistry%29) or [acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acid)/[base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_%28chemistry%29) reaction, the net ionic equation will usually be:

$$\ce{H+ (aq) + OH^{-}(aq) -> H2O(l)}$$

There are a few acid/base reactions that produce a precipitate in addition to the water molecule shown above. An example is the reaction of [barium hydroxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_hydroxide) with [phosphoric acid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phosphoric_acid), which produces not only water but also the insoluble salt [barium phosphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barium_phosphate). In this reaction, there are no spectator ions, so the net ionic equation is the same as the full ionic equation.

$$\ce{3Ba(OH)2 + 2H3PO4 -> 6H2O + Ba3(PO4)2(v)}$$

$$\ce{{3Ba^2+} + {6OH^-} + {6H+}} + \underbrace\ce{2PO4^3-}_\ce{phosphate} \ce{-> {6H2O} + \underbrace{Ba3(PO4)2(v)}_{barium~phosphate}}$$

Double displacement reactions that feature a [carbonate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbonate) reacting with an acid have the net ionic equation:

$$\ce{2H+} + \underbrace\ce{CO3^2-}_\ce{carbonate} \ce{-> H2O + CO2 (^)}$$

If every ion is a "spectator ion" then there was no reaction, and the net ionic equation is null.

Generally, if *z<sub>j</sub>* is the multiple of elementary charge on the *j-th* molecule, charge neutrality may be written as:

$$\sum_{j=1}^J z_j \nu_j=0$$

where the *ν<sub>j</sub>* are the stoichiometric coefficients described above. The *z<sub>j</sub>* may be incorporated[^o19][^o20] as an additional row in the *a<sub>ij</sub>* matrix described above, and a properly balanced ionic equation will then also obey:

$$\sum_{j=1}^J a_{ij} \nu_j=0$$

:::
