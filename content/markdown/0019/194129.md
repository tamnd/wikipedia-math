# Brahmagupta's formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brahmagupta's formula"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), **Brahmagupta's formula**, named after the 7th century [Indian mathematician](wikipedia://en/297206), is used to find the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of any convex [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131) (one that can be inscribed in a circle) given the lengths of the sides. Its generalized version, *[Bretschneider's formula](wikipedia://en/5615284)*, can be used with non-cyclic quadrilateral. *[Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123)* can be thought as a special case of the Brahmagupta's formula for triangles.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

Brahmagupta's formula gives the area $K$ of a convex [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131) whose sides have lengths $a$, $b$, $c$, $d$ as

$$K=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}$$

where $s$, the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617), is defined to be

$$s=\frac{a+b+c+d}{2}.$$

This formula generalizes [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123) for the area of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654). A triangle may be regarded as a quadrilateral with one side of length zero. From this perspective, as $d$ (or any one side) approaches zero, a cyclic quadrilateral converges into a cyclic triangle (all triangles are cyclic), and Brahmagupta's formula simplifies to Heron's formula.

If the semiperimeter is not used, Brahmagupta's formula is

$$K=\frac{1}{4}\sqrt{(-a+b+c+d)(a-b+c+d)(a+b-c+d)(a+b+c-d)}.$$

Another equivalent version is

$$K=\frac{\sqrt{(a^2+b^2+c^2+d^2)^2+8abcd-2(a^4+b^4+c^4+d^4)}}{4}\cdot$$

## Proof {#Proof}

### Trigonometric proof {#Trigonometric_proof}

Here the notations in the figure to the right are used. The area $K$ of the convex cyclic quadrilateral equals the sum of the areas of $\triangle ADB$ and $\triangle BDC$:

$$K = \frac{1}{2}pq\sin A + \frac{1}{2}rs\sin C.$$

But since $\square ABCD$ is a cyclic quadrilateral, $\angle DAB = 180^{\circ} - \angle DCB$. Hence $\sin A = \sin C$. Therefore,

$$K = \frac{1}{2}pq\sin A + \frac{1}{2}rs\sin A$$

$$K^2 = \frac{1}{4} (pq + rs)^2 \sin^2 A$$

$$4K^2 = (pq + rs)^2 (1 - \cos^2 A) = (pq + rs)^2 - ((pq + rs)\cos A)^2$$

(using the [trigonometric identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identity)).

Solving for common side $DB$, in $\triangle ADB$ and $\triangle BDC$, the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890) gives

$$p^2 + q^2 - 2pq\cos A = r^2 + s^2 - 2rs\cos C.$$

Substituting $\cos C = -\cos A$ (since angles $A$ and $C$ are [supplementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplementary_angles)) and rearranging, we have

$$(pq + rs) \cos A = \frac{1}{2}(p^2 + q^2 - r^2 - s^2).$$

Substituting this in the equation for the area,

$$4K^2 = (pq + rs)^2 - \frac{1}{4}(p^2 + q^2 - r^2 - s^2)^2$$

$$16K^2 = 4(pq + rs)^2 - (p^2 + q^2 - r^2 - s^2)^2.$$

The right-hand side is of the form $a^{2} - b^{2} = (a - b)(a + b)$ and hence can be written as

$$[2(pq + rs)) - p^2 - q^2 + r^2 +s^2][2(pq + rs) + p^2 + q^2 -r^2 - s^2]$$

which, upon rearranging the terms in the square brackets, yields

$$16K^2= [ (r+s)^2 - (p-q)^2 ][ (p+q)^2 - (r-s)^2 ]$$

that can be factored again into

$$16K^2=(q+r+s-p)(p+r+s-q)(p+q+s-r)(p+q+r-s).$$

Introducing the [semiperimeter](wikipedia://en/886617) $S = \tfrac{p + q + r + s}{2}$ yields

$$16K^2 = 16(S-p)(S-q)(S-r)(S-s).$$

Taking the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208), we get

$$K = \sqrt{(S-p)(S-q)(S-r)(S-s)}.$$

### Non-trigonometric proof {#Non-trigonometric_proof}

An alternative, non-trigonometric proof utilizes two applications of Heron's triangle area formula on [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) triangles.[^o8]

:::

## Extension to non-cyclic quadrilaterals {#Extension_to_non-cyclic_quadrilaterals}

In the case of non-cyclic quadrilaterals, Brahmagupta's formula can be extended by considering the measures of two opposite angles of the quadrilateral:

$$K=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)-abcd\cos^2\theta}$$

where $\theta$ is half the sum of any two opposite angles. (The choice of which pair of opposite angles is irrelevant: if the other two angles are taken, half their sum is $180^{\circ} - \theta$. Since $\cos(180^{\circ} - \theta) = -\cos \theta$, we have $\cos^{2}(180^{\circ} - \theta) = \cos^{2} \theta$.) This more general formula is known as [Bretschneider's formula](wikipedia://en/5615284).

It is a property of [cyclic quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/194131) (and ultimately of [inscribed angles](wikipedia://en/462730)) that opposite angles of a quadrilateral sum to 180°. Consequently, in the case of an inscribed quadrilateral, $\theta$ is 90°, whence the term

$$abcd\cos^2\theta=abcd\cos^2 \left(90^\circ\right)=abcd\cdot0=0,$$

giving the basic form of Brahmagupta's formula. It follows from the latter equation that the area of a cyclic quadrilateral is the maximum possible area for any quadrilateral with the given side lengths.

A related formula, which was proved by [Coolidge](wikipedia://en/4379392), also gives the area of a general convex quadrilateral. It is[^o10]

$$K=\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)-\textstyle{1\over4}(ac+bd+pq)(ac+bd-pq)}$$

where $p$ and $q$ are the lengths of the diagonals of the quadrilateral. In a [cyclic quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/194131), $pq = ac + bd$ according to [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971), and the formula of Coolidge reduces to Brahmagupta's formula.

## Related theorems {#Related_theorems}

- [Heron's formula](wikipedia://en/194123) for the area of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) is the special case obtained by taking $d = 0$.
- The relationship between the general and extended form of Brahmagupta's formula is similar to how the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890) extends the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).
- Increasingly complicated closed-form formulas exist for the area of general polygons on circles, as described by Maley et al.[^o13]
