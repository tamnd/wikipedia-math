# Orthonormal basis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orthonormal basis"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), an **orthonormal basis** for an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) $V$ with finite [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28linear_algebra%29) is a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for $V$ whose vectors are [orthonormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal), that is, they are all [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) and [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/71675950) to each other.[^o2][^o3][^o4] For example, the [standard basis](wikipedia://en/462005) for a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ is an orthonormal basis, where the relevant inner product is the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of vectors. The [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of the standard basis under a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) or [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) (or any [orthogonal transformation](wikipedia://en/1382381)) is also orthonormal, and every orthonormal basis for $\mathbb{R}^n$ arises in this fashion. An orthonormal basis can be derived from an [orthogonal basis](wikipedia://en/755400) via [normalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normalize_%28linear_algebra%29). The choice of an [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) and an orthonormal basis forms a [coordinate frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_frame) known as an ***orthonormal frame***.

:::

For a general inner product space $V,$ an orthonormal basis can be used to define normalized [orthogonal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5006088) on $V.$ Under these coordinates, the inner product becomes a dot product of vectors. Thus the presence of an orthonormal basis reduces the study of a [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) inner product space to the study of $\mathbb{R}^n$ under the dot product. Every finite-dimensional inner product space has an orthonormal basis, which may be obtained from an arbitrary basis using the [Gram–Schmidt process](wikipedia://en/82361).

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the concept of an orthonormal basis can be generalized to arbitrary (infinite-dimensional) [inner product spaces](wikipedia://en/14856).[^o5] Given a pre-Hilbert space $H,$ an *orthonormal basis* for $H$ is an orthonormal set of vectors with the property that every vector in $H$ can be written as an [infinite linear combination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_linear_combination) of the vectors in the basis. In this case, the orthonormal basis is sometimes called a **Hilbert basis** for $H.$ Note that an orthonormal basis in this sense is not generally a [Hamel basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_basis), since infinite linear combinations are required.[^n1] Specifically, the [linear span](wikipedia://en/56353) of the basis must be [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $H,$ although not necessarily the entire space.

If we go on to [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932), a non-orthonormal set of vectors having the same linear span as an orthonormal basis may not be a basis at all. For instance, any [square-integrable function](wikipedia://en/28680558) on the interval $[-1,1]$ can be expressed ([almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688)) as an infinite sum of [Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/100349) (an orthonormal basis), but not necessarily as an infinite sum of the [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) $x^n.$

A different generalisation is to pseudo-inner product spaces, finite-dimensional vector spaces $M$ equipped with a non-degenerate [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) known as the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795). In such a basis, the metric takes the form $\text{diag}(+1,\cdots,+1,-1,\cdots,-1)$ with $p$ positive ones and $q$ negative ones.

## Examples {#Examples}

- For $\mathbb{R}^3$, the set of vectors $\left\{ \mathbf{e_1} = \begin{pmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \ , \ \mathbf{e_2} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & 0 \end{pmatrix} \ , \ \mathbf{e_3} = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 0 & 1 \end{pmatrix} \right\},$ is called the **standard basis** and forms an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$ with respect to the standard dot product. Note that both the standard basis and standard dot product rely on viewing $\mathbb{R}^3$ as the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}\times\mathbb{R}$

**Proof:** A straightforward computation shows that the inner products of these vectors equals zero, $\left\langle \mathbf{e_1}, \mathbf{e_2} \right\rangle = \left\langle \mathbf{e_1}, \mathbf{e_3} \right\rangle = \left\langle \mathbf{e_2}, \mathbf{e_3} \right\rangle = 0$ and that each of their magnitudes equals one, $\left\|\mathbf{e_1}\right\| = \left\|\mathbf{e_2}\right\| = \left\|\mathbf{e_3}\right\| = 1.$ This means that $\left\{\mathbf{e_1}, \mathbf{e_2}, \mathbf{e_3}\right\}$ is an orthonormal set. All vectors $(\mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y}, \mathbf{z}) \in \mathbb{R}^3$ can be expressed as a sum of the basis vectors scaled

$$(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{y},\mathbf{z}) = \mathbf{x e_1} + \mathbf{y e_2} + \mathbf{z e_3},$$

so $\left\{\mathbf{e_1}, \mathbf{e_2}, \mathbf{e_3}\right\}$ spans $\mathbb{R}^3$ and hence must be a basis. It may also be shown that the standard basis rotated about an axis through the origin or reflected in a plane through the origin also forms an orthonormal basis of $\mathbb{R}^3$.

- For $\mathbb{R}^n$, the standard basis and inner product are similarly defined. Any other orthonormal basis is related to the standard basis by an [orthogonal transformation](wikipedia://en/1382381) in the group O(n).
- For [pseudo-Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/10962546) $\mathbb{R}^{p,q},$, an orthogonal basis $\{e_\mu\}$ with metric $\eta$ instead satisfies $\eta(e_\mu,e_\nu) = 0$ if $\mu\neq \nu$, $\eta(e_\mu,e_\mu) = +1$ if $1\leq\mu\leq p$, and $\eta(e_\mu,e_\mu) =-1$ if $p+1\leq\mu\leq p+q$. Any two orthonormal bases are related by a pseudo-orthogonal transformation. In the case $(p,q) = (1,3)$, these are Lorentz transformations.
- The set $\left\{f_n : n \in \mathbb{Z}\right\}$ with $f_n(x) = \exp(2 \pi inx),$ where $\exp$ denotes the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678), forms an orthonormal basis of the space of functions with finite Lebesgue integrals, $L^2([0,1]),$ with respect to the [2-norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-norm). This is fundamental to the study of [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038).
- The set $\left\{e_b : b \in B\right\}$ with $e_b(c) = 1$ if $b = c$ and $e_b(c) = 0$ otherwise forms an orthonormal basis of $\ell^2(B).$
- [Eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601) of a [Sturm–Liouville eigenproblem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm–Liouville_eigenproblem).
- The [column vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vectors) of an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620) form an orthonormal set.

:::

## Basic formula {#Basic_formula}

If $B$ is an orthogonal basis of $H,$ then every element $x \in H$ may be written as

$$x = \sum_{b\in B} \frac{\langle x,b\rangle}{\lVert b\rVert^2} b.$$

When $B$ is orthonormal, this simplifies to

$$x = \sum_{b\in B}\langle x,b\rangle b$$

and the square of the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) of $x$ can be given by

$$\|x\|^2 = \sum_{b\in B}|\langle x,b\rangle |^2.$$

Even if $B$ is [uncountable](wikipedia://en/32248), only countably many terms in this sum will be non-zero, and the expression is therefore well-defined. This sum is also called the *[Fourier expansion](wikipedia://en/245530)* of $x,$ and the formula is usually known as [Parseval's identity](wikipedia://en/418615).

If $B$ is an orthonormal basis of $H,$ then $H$ is *isomorphic* to $\ell^2(B)$ in the following sense: there exists a [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) map $\Phi : H \to \ell^2(B)$ such that

$$\langle\Phi(x),\Phi(y)\rangle=\langle x,y\rangle \ \ \forall \ x, y \in H.$$

:::

## Orthonormal system {#Orthonormal_system}

A set $S$ of mutually orthonormal vectors in a Hilbert space $H$ is called an orthonormal system. An orthonormal basis is an orthonormal system with the additional property that the linear span of $S$ is dense in $H$.[^n2] Alternatively, the set $S$ can be regarded as either *complete* or *incomplete* with respect to $H$. That is, we can take the smallest closed linear subspace $V \subseteq H$ containing $S.$ Then $S$ will be an orthonormal basis of $V;$ which may of course be smaller than $H$ itself, being an *incomplete* orthonormal set, or be $H,$ when it is a *complete* orthonormal set.

## Existence {#Existence}

Using [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442) and the [Gram–Schmidt process](wikipedia://en/82361) (or more simply well-ordering and transfinite recursion), one can show that *every* Hilbert space admits an orthonormal basis;[^o14] furthermore, any two orthonormal bases of the same space have the same [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6173) (this can be proven in a manner akin to that of the proof of the usual [dimension theorem for vector spaces](wikipedia://en/346992), with separate cases depending on whether the larger basis candidate is countable or not). A Hilbert space is [separable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_metric_space) if and only if it admits a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) orthonormal basis. (One can prove this last statement without using the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840). However, one would have to use the [axiom of countable choice](wikipedia://en/421086).)

## Choice of basis as a choice of isomorphism {#Choice_of_basis_as_a_choice_of_isomorphism}

For concreteness we discuss orthonormal bases for a real, $n$-dimensional vector space $V$ with a positive definite symmetric bilinear form $\phi=\langle\cdot,\cdot\rangle$.

One way to view an orthonormal basis with respect to $\phi$ is as a set of vectors $\mathcal{B} = \{e_i\}$, which allow us to write $v = v^ie_i \ \ \forall \ v \in V$ , and $v^i\in \mathbb{R}$ or $(v^i) \in \mathbb{R}^n$. With respect to this basis, the components of $\phi$ are particularly simple: $\phi(e_i,e_j) = \delta_{ij}$ (where $\delta_{ij}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890)).

We can now view the basis as a map $\psi_\mathcal{B}:V\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^n$ which is an isomorphism of inner product spaces: to make this more explicit we can write

$$\psi_\mathcal{B}:(V,\phi)\rightarrow (\mathbb{R}^n,\delta_{ij}).$$

Explicitly we can write $(\psi_\mathcal{B}(v))^i = e^i(v) = \phi(e_i,v)$ where $e^i$ is the dual basis element to $e_i$.

The inverse is a component map

$$C_\mathcal{B}:\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow V, (v^i)\mapsto \sum_{i=1}^n v^ie_i.$$

These definitions make it manifest that there is a bijection

$$\{\text{Space of orthogonal bases } \mathcal{B}\}\leftrightarrow \{\text{Space of isomorphisms }V\leftrightarrow \mathbb{R}^n\}.$$

The space of isomorphisms admits actions of orthogonal groups at either the $V$ side or the $\mathbb{R}^n$ side. For concreteness we fix the isomorphisms to point in the direction $\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow V$, and consider the space of such maps, $\text{Iso}(\mathbb{R}^n\rightarrow V)$.

This space admits a left action by the group of isometries of $V$, that is, $R\in \text{GL}(V)$ such that $\phi(\cdot,\cdot) = \phi(R\cdot,R\cdot)$, with the action given by composition: $R*C=R\circ C.$

This space also admits a right action by the group of isometries of $\mathbb{R}^n$, that is, $R_{ij} \in \text{O}(n)\subset \text{Mat}_{n\times n}(\mathbb{R})$, with the action again given by composition: $C*R_{ij} = C\circ R_{ij}$.

## As a principal homogeneous space {#As_a_principal_homogeneous_space}

The set of orthonormal bases for $\mathbb{R}^n$ with the standard inner product is a [principal homogeneous space](wikipedia://en/363441) or G-torsor for the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $G = \text{O}(n),$ and is called the [Stiefel manifold](wikipedia://en/1392495) $V_n(\mathbb{R}^n)$ of orthonormal [$n$-frames](wikipedia://en/22256304).[^o17]

In other words, the space of orthonormal bases is like the orthogonal group, but without a choice of base point: given the space of orthonormal bases, there is no natural choice of orthonormal basis, but once one is given one, there is a one-to-one correspondence between bases and the orthogonal group. Concretely, a linear map is determined by where it sends a given basis: just as an invertible map can take any basis to any other basis, an orthogonal map can take any *orthogonal* basis to any other *orthogonal* basis.

The other Stiefel manifolds $V_k(\mathbb{R}^n)$ for $k < n$ of *incomplete* orthonormal bases (orthonormal $k$-frames) are still homogeneous spaces for the orthogonal group, but not *principal* homogeneous spaces: any $k$-frame can be taken to any other $k$-frame by an orthogonal map, but this map is not uniquely determined.

- The set of orthonormal bases for $\mathbb{R}^{p,q}$ is a G-torsor for $G = \text{O}(p,q)$.
- The set of orthonormal bases for $\mathbb{C}^n$ is a G-torsor for $G = \text{U}(n)$.
- The set of orthonormal bases for $\mathbb{C}^{p,q}$ is a G-torsor for $G = \text{U}(p,q)$.
- The set of right-handed orthonormal bases for $\mathbb{R}^n$ is a G-torsor for $G = \text{SO}(n)$
