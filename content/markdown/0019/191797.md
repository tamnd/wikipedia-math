# Kolmogorov's zero–one law

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kolmogorov's zero–one law"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), **Kolmogorov's zero–one law**, named in honor of [Andrey Nikolaevich Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrey_Nikolaevich_Kolmogorov), specifies that a certain type of [event](wikipedia://en/22961), namely a *tail event of independent [σ-algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra)*, will either [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) happen or almost surely not happen; that is, the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) of such an event occurring is zero or one.

:::

Tail events are defined in terms of [countably infinite](wikipedia://en/6026) families of σ-algebras. For illustrative purposes, we present here the special case in which each sigma algebra is generated by a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X_k$ for $k\in\mathbb N$. Let $\mathcal{F}$ be the sigma-algebra generated jointly by all of the $X_k$. Then, a **tail event** $F \in \mathcal{F}$ is an event the occurrence of which cannot depend on the outcome of a finite subfamily of these random variables. (Note: $F$ belonging to $\mathcal{F}$ implies that membership in $F$ is uniquely determined by the values of the $X_k$, but the latter condition is strictly weaker and does not suffice to prove the zero-one law.) For example, the event that the sequence of the $X_k$ [converges](wikipedia://en/50723), and the event that its sum converges are both tail events. If the $X_k$ are, for example, all [Bernoulli-distributed](wikipedia://en/199189), then the event that there are infinitely many $k\in\mathbb N$ such that $X_k=X_{k+1}=\dots=X_{k+100}=1$ is a tail event. If each $X_k$ models the outcome of the $k^{th}$ coin toss in a modeled, infinite sequence of coin tosses, this means that a sequence of 100 consecutive heads occurring infinitely many times is a tail event in this model.

Tail events are precisely those events whose occurrence can still be determined if an arbitrarily large but finite initial segment of the $X_k$ is removed.

In many situations, it can be easy to apply Kolmogorov's zero–one law to show that some event has probability 0 or 1, but surprisingly hard to determine *which* of these two extreme values is the correct one.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

A more general statement of Kolmogorov's zero–one law holds for sequences of independent σ-algebras. Let $(\Omega, F, P)$ be a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) and let $F_n$ be a sequence of σ-algebras contained in $F$. Let

$$G_n=\sigma\bigg(\bigcup_{k=n}^\infty F_k\bigg)$$

be the smallest $\sigma$-algebra containing $F_n, F_{n+1}, ...$. Then the *terminal $\sigma$-algebra* of the $F_n$ is defined as $\mathcal T((F_n)_{n\in\mathbb N})=\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty G_n$.

Kolmogorov's zero–one law asserts that, if the *$F_n$* are [stochastically independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastically_independent), then for any event $E\in \mathcal T((F_n)_{n\in\mathbb N})$, one has either $P(E) = 0$ or $P(E) = 1$.

The statement of the law in terms of random variables is obtained from the latter by taking each *$F_n$* to be the σ-algebra generated by the random variable $X_n$. A tail event is then by definition an event which is [measurable](wikipedia://en/19873) with respect to the σ-algebra generated by all *$X_n$,* but which is [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) of any finite number of *$X_n$.* That is, a tail event is precisely an element of the terminal σ-algebra $\textstyle{\bigcap_{n=1}^\infty G_n}$.

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

1. Let $(X,\mathcal{B},\mu)$ be a [standard probability space](wikipedia://en/10406190), and let $T$ be an invertible, [measure-preserving transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure-preserving_transformation). Then $T$ is called a [Kolmogorov automorphism](wikipedia://en/36563911) or *K*-automorphism, *K*-transform or *K*-shift, if there exists a sub-[sigma algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra) $\mathcal{K} \subset \mathcal{B}$ such that the following three properties hold: $$\mbox{(1) }\mathcal{K}\subset T\mathcal{K}$$ $$\mbox{(2) }\bigvee_{n=0}^\infty T^n \mathcal{K}=\mathcal{B}$$ $$\mbox{(3) }\bigcap_{n=0}^\infty T^{-n} \mathcal{K} = \{X,\varnothing\}$$ Here, the symbol $\lor$ is the [join of sigma algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Join_%28sigma_algebra%29), while $\cap$ is [set intersection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_intersection). The equality should be understood as holding [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688), that is, differing at most on a set of [measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero). A *K*-automorphism by construction necessarily obeys Kolmogorov's 0-1 Law. It can be further shown that all [Bernoulli automorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_automorphism) are *K*-automorphisms, but not *vice versa*.
1. The presence of an infinite cluster in the context of [percolation theory](wikipedia://en/187805) also obeys the 0-1 law.
1. Let $\{X_n\}_n$ be a sequence of independent random variables, then the event $E$ defined below is a tail event: $$E = \left\{\lim _{n \rightarrow \infty} \sum_{k=1}^n X_k \text { exists }\right\}$$ Thus by Kolmogorov's 0-1 law, it has either probability 0 or 1 to happen. Note that independence is required for the tail event condition to hold. Without independence we can consider a sequence that's either $(0,0,0,\dots)$ or $(1,1,1,\dots)$ with probability $\frac{1}{2}$ each. In this case the sum converges with probability $\frac{1}{2}$.

:::

## Possible confusion with fat tails {#Possible_confusion_with_fat_tails}

The "tail events" in the Kolmogorov zero-one law are not related to the "tail events" in the sense of [fat-tailed distributions](wikipedia://en/4357461) (or the closely related [long tails](wikipedia://en/1385393) and [tail risks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tail_risk)). The events we consider in this article are things like [convergence/non-convergence of sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergent_sequences), while the latter type of event means something like "[outliers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Outliers)".
