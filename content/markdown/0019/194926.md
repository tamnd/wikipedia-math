# Petersen graph

::: {#o2 .problem cue=unsolved}

**Conjecture:** Every [bridgeless graph](wikipedia://en/1305071) has a cycle-continuous mapping to the Petersen graph.

:::

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Petersen graph"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the **Petersen graph** is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) with 10 [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) and 15 [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29). It is a small graph that serves as a useful example and [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) for many problems in graph theory. The Petersen graph is named after [Julius Petersen](wikipedia://en/76861), who in 1898 constructed it to be the smallest [bridgeless](wikipedia://en/1305071) [cubic graph](wikipedia://en/980508) with no three-[edge-coloring](wikipedia://en/690647).[^o4][^o5]

:::

Although the graph is generally credited to Petersen, it had in fact first appeared 12 years earlier, in a paper by (txt). Kempe observed that its vertices can represent the ten lines of the [Desargues configuration](wikipedia://en/7209841), and its edges represent pairs of lines that do not meet at one of the ten points of the configuration.[^o7]

[Donald Knuth](wikipedia://en/8095) states that the Petersen graph is "a remarkable configuration that serves as a counterexample to many optimistic predictions about what might be true for graphs in general."[^o8]

The Petersen graph also makes an appearance in [tropical geometry](wikipedia://en/867041). The cone over the Petersen graph is naturally identified with the [moduli space](wikipedia://en/361609) of five-pointed rational tropical curves.[^o9]

::: {#o11 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Constructions"}

## Constructions {#Constructions}

*Image: Kneser graph KG(5,2).svg.* Petersen graph as Kneser graph $KG_{5,2}$

The Petersen graph is the [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649) of the [line graph](wikipedia://en/675231) of $K_{5}$. It is also the [Kneser graph](wikipedia://en/2724154) $KG_{5,2}$; this means that it has one vertex for each 2-element [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of a 5-element set, and two vertices are connected by an edge if and only if the corresponding 2-element subsets are disjoint from each other. As a Kneser graph of the form $KG_{2n-1,n-1}$ it is an example of an [odd graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_graph).

Geometrically, the Petersen graph is the graph formed by the vertices and edges of the [hemi-dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/10393833), that is, a [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) with opposite points, lines and faces identified together.

:::

## Embeddings {#Embeddings}

The Petersen graph is [nonplanar](wikipedia://en/24314). Any nonplanar graph has as [minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_%28graph_theory%29) either the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{5}$, or the [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320) $K_{3,3}$, but the Petersen graph has both as minors. The $K_{5}$ minor can be formed by contracting the edges of a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031), for instance the five short edges in the first picture. The $K_{3,3}$ minor can be formed by deleting one vertex (for instance the central vertex of the 3-symmetric drawing) and contracting an edge incident to each neighbor of the deleted vertex.

*Image: Petersen graph, two crossings.svg.* The Petersen graph has [crossing number](wikipedia://en/18298594) 2 and is [1-planar](wikipedia://en/38249632).[^o13]

The most common and symmetric plane drawing of the Petersen graph, as a pentagram within a pentagon, has five crossings. However, this is not the best drawing for minimizing crossings; there exists another drawing (shown in the figure) with only two crossings. Because it is nonplanar, it has at least one crossing in any drawing, and if a crossing edge is removed from any drawing it remains nonplanar and has another crossing; therefore, its crossing number is exactly 2. Each edge in this drawing is crossed at most once, so the Petersen graph is [1-planar](wikipedia://en/38249632). On a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) the Petersen graph can be drawn without edge crossings; it therefore has [orientable genus](wikipedia://en/61891) 1.

*Image: Petersen graph, unit distance.svg.* The Petersen graph is a [unit distance graph](wikipedia://en/7158665): it can be drawn in the plane with each edge having unit length.

The Petersen graph can also be drawn (with crossings) in the plane in such a way that all the edges have equal length. That is, it is a [unit distance graph](wikipedia://en/7158665).

The simplest [non-orientable surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) on which the Petersen graph can be embedded without crossings is the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). This is the embedding given by the [hemi-dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/10393833) construction of the Petersen graph (shown in the figure). The projective plane embedding can also be formed from the standard pentagonal drawing of the Petersen graph by placing a [cross-cap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-cap) within the five-point star at the center of the drawing, and routing the star edges through this cross-cap; the resulting drawing has six pentagonal faces. This construction forms a [regular map](wikipedia://en/21533914) and shows that the Petersen graph has [non-orientable genus](wikipedia://en/61891) 1.

*Image: Petersen-graph.png.* The Petersen graph and associated map embedded in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350). Opposite points on the circle are identified, yielding a closed surface of non-orientable genus 1.

## Symmetries {#Symmetries}

The Petersen graph is [strongly regular](wikipedia://en/1399856) (with signature $\operatorname{srg}(10,3,0,1)$). It is also [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1577896), meaning that it is [edge transitive](wikipedia://en/584406) and [vertex transitive](wikipedia://en/581175). More strongly, it is 3-arc-transitive: every directed three-edge path in the Petersen graph can be transformed into every other such path by a symmetry of the graph.[^o16] It is one of only 13 cubic [distance-regular graphs](wikipedia://en/7768943).[^o20]

The [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of the Petersen graph is the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{5}$; the action of $S_{5}$ on the Petersen graph follows from its construction as a [Kneser graph](wikipedia://en/2724154). The Petersen graph is a [core](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_%28graph_theory%29): every [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/676328) of the Petersen graph to itself is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/15094186).[^o17] As shown in the figures, the drawings of the Petersen graph may exhibit five-way or three-way symmetry, but it is not possible to draw the Petersen graph in the plane in such a way that the drawing exhibits the full symmetry group of the graph.

Despite its high degree of symmetry, the Petersen graph is not a [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277). It is the smallest vertex-transitive graph that is not a Cayley graph.

:::

## Hamiltonian paths and cycles {#Hamiltonian_paths_and_cycles}

*Image: Petersen2 tiny.svg.* The Petersen graph is hypo-Hamiltonian: by deleting any vertex, such as the center vertex in the drawing, the remaining graph is Hamiltonian. This drawing with order-3 symmetry is the one given by (Kempe 1886).

The Petersen graph has a [Hamiltonian path](wikipedia://en/244437) but no [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle). It is the smallest bridgeless cubic graph with no Hamiltonian cycle. It is [hypohamiltonian](wikipedia://en/18301304), meaning that although it has no Hamiltonian cycle, deleting any vertex makes it Hamiltonian, and is the smallest hypohamiltonian graph.

As a finite [connected](wikipedia://en/1614492) vertex-transitive graph that does not have a Hamiltonian cycle, the Petersen graph is a counterexample to a variant of the [Lovász conjecture](wikipedia://en/4367424), but the canonical formulation of the conjecture asks for a Hamiltonian path and is verified by the Petersen graph.

Only five connected vertex-transitive graphs with no Hamiltonian cycles are known: the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{2}$, the Petersen graph, the [Coxeter graph](wikipedia://en/5358570) and two graphs derived from the Petersen and Coxeter graphs by replacing each vertex with a triangle.[^o20] If $G$ is a 2-connected, $r$-regular graph with at most $3r + 1$ vertices, then $G$ is Hamiltonian or $G$ is the Petersen graph.[^o21]

To see that the Petersen graph has no Hamiltonian cycle, consider the edges in the cut disconnecting the inner 5-cycle from the outer one. If there is a Hamiltonian cycle $C$, it must contain an even number of these edges. If it contains only two of them, their end-vertices must be adjacent in the two 5-cycles, which is not possible. Hence, it contains exactly four of them. Assume that the top edge of the cut is not contained in $C$ (all the other cases are the same by symmetry). Of the five edges in the outer cycle, the two top edges must be in $C$, the two side edges must not be in $C$, and hence the bottom edge must be in $C$. The top two edges in the inner cycle must be in $C$, but this completes a non-spanning cycle, which cannot be part of a Hamiltonian cycle. Alternatively, we can also describe the ten-vertex [3-regular graphs](wikipedia://en/85821) that do have a Hamiltonian cycle and show that none of them is the Petersen graph, by finding a cycle in each of them that is shorter than any cycle in the Petersen graph. Any ten-vertex Hamiltonian 3-regular graph consists of a ten-vertex cycle $C$ plus five chords. If any chord connects two vertices at distance two or three along $C$ from each other, the graph has a 3-cycle or 4-cycle, and therefore cannot be the Petersen graph. If two chords connect opposite vertices of $C$ to vertices at distance four along $C$, there is again a 4-cycle. The only remaining case is a [Möbius ladder](wikipedia://en/7355278) formed by connecting each pair of opposite vertices by a chord, which again has a 4-cycle. Since the Petersen graph has girth five, it cannot be formed in this way and has no Hamiltonian cycle.

## Coloring {#Coloring}

*Image: PetersenBarveniHran.svg.* A 4-coloring of the Petersen graph's edges

*Image: Petersen graph 3-coloring.svg.* A 3-coloring of the Petersen graph's vertices

The Petersen graph has [chromatic number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_number) 3, meaning that its vertices can be [colored](wikipedia://en/426743) with three colors — but not with two — such that no edge connects vertices of the same color. It has a [list coloring](wikipedia://en/690669) with 3 colors, by Brooks's theorem for list colorings.

The Petersen graph has [chromatic index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chromatic_index) 4; coloring the edges requires four colors. As a connected bridgeless cubic graph with chromatic index four, the Petersen graph is a [snark](wikipedia://en/727811). It is the smallest possible snark, and was the only known snark from 1898 until 1946. The [snark theorem](wikipedia://en/727811), a result conjectured by [W. T. Tutte](wikipedia://en/52021) and announced in 2001 by Robertson, Sanders, Seymour, and Thomas,[^o23] states that every snark has the Petersen graph as a [minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_%28graph_theory%29).

Additionally, the graph has [fractional chromatic index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_chromatic_index) 3, proving that the difference between the chromatic index and fractional chromatic index can be as large as 1. The long-standing [Goldberg-Seymour Conjecture](wikipedia://en/31056132) proposes that this is the largest gap possible.

The [Thue number](wikipedia://en/7385565) (a variant of the chromatic index) of the Petersen graph is 5.

The Petersen graph requires at least three colors in any (possibly improper) coloring that breaks all of its symmetries; that is, its [distinguishing number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distinguishing_number) is three. Except for the complete graphs, it is the only Kneser graph whose distinguishing number is not two.[^o24]

## Other properties {#Other_properties}

The Petersen graph:

- is 3-connected and hence 3-edge-connected and bridgeless. See the [glossary](wikipedia://en/325802#Connectivity).
- has independence number 4 and is 3-partite. See the [glossary](wikipedia://en/325802#Independence).
- is [cubic](wikipedia://en/980508), has [domination number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domination_number) 3, and has a [perfect matching](wikipedia://en/44031) and a [2-factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-factor).
- has 6 distinct perfect matchings.
- is the smallest cubic graph of [girth](wikipedia://en/12013) 5. (It is the unique (3,5)-[cage](wikipedia://en/7735022). In fact, since it has only 10 vertices, it is the unique (3,5)-[Moore graph](wikipedia://en/4112719).)[^o26]
- every cubic bridgeless graph without Petersen minor has a cycle double cover.[^o27]
- is the smallest cubic graph with [Colin de Verdière graph invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colin_de_Verdière_graph_invariant) $\mu = 5$.[^o28]
- is the smallest graph of [cop number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cop_number) 3. In fact, it is the unique 10 vertex graph with cop number 3.[^o29][^o30]
- has [radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_%28graph_theory%29) 2 and [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) 2. It is the largest cubic graph with diameter 2.
- has 2000 [spanning trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanning_tree_%28mathematics%29), the most of any 10-vertex cubic graph.[^o31][^o32]
- has [chromatic polynomial](wikipedia://en/1460126) $t(t - 1)(t - 2)(t^{7} - 12t^{6} + 67t^{5} - 230t^{4} + 529t^{3} - 814t^{2} + 775t - 352)$.[^o33]
- has [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) $(t - 1)^{5}(t + 2)^{4}(t - 3)$, making it an [integral graph](wikipedia://en/24845923)—a graph whose [spectrum](wikipedia://en/534914) consists entirely of integers.
- is [$H$-saturated](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturation_%28graph_theory%29) when $H$ is $K_{3}$.

## Petersen coloring conjecture {#Petersen_coloring_conjecture}

An *Eulerian subgraph* of a graph $G$ is a subgraph consisting of a subset of the edges of $G$, touching every vertex of $G$ an even number of times. These subgraphs are the elements of the [cycle space](wikipedia://en/262243) of $G$ and are sometimes called cycles. If $G$ and $H$ are any two graphs, a function from the edges of $G$ to the edges of $H$ is defined to be *cycle-continuous* if the pre-image of every cycle of $H$ is a cycle of $G$. A conjecture of Jaeger asserts that every bridgeless graph has a cycle-continuous mapping to the Petersen graph. Jaeger showed this conjecture implies the 5-[cycle-double-cover](wikipedia://en/21486302) conjecture and the Berge-Fulkerson conjecture."[^o36]

:::

## Related graphs {#Related_graphs}

*Image: Petersen family.svg.* The [Petersen family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersen_family).

The [generalized Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/23716451) $G(n,k)$ is formed by connecting the vertices of a [regular $n$-gon](wikipedia://en/333306) to the corresponding vertices of a [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303) with [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) $\{n/k\}$.[^o38][^o39] For instance, in this notation, the Petersen graph is $G(5,2)$: it can be formed by connecting corresponding vertices of a pentagon and five-point star, and the edges in the star connect every second vertex. The generalized Petersen graphs also include the $n$-prism $G(n,1)$, the [Dürer graph](wikipedia://en/23453065) $G(6,2)$, the [Möbius–Kantor graph](wikipedia://en/13396591) $G(8,3)$, the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) $G(10,2)$, the [Desargues graph](wikipedia://en/1578059) $G(10,3)$, and the [Nauru graph](wikipedia://en/24052345) $G(12,5)$.

The [Petersen family](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petersen_family) consists of the seven graphs that can be formed from the Petersen graph by zero or more applications of [Δ-Y or Y-Δ transforms](wikipedia://en/268145). The [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{6}$ is also in the Petersen family. These graphs form the [forbidden minors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forbidden_minor) for [linklessly embeddable graphs](wikipedia://en/772031), graphs that can be embedded into three-dimensional space in such a way that no two cycles in the graph are [linked](wikipedia://en/1161784).[^o40]

The [Clebsch graph](wikipedia://en/23970662) contains many copies of the Petersen graph as [induced subgraphs](wikipedia://en/1655188): for each vertex $v$ of the Clebsch graph, the ten non-neighbors of $v$ induce a copy of the Petersen graph.
