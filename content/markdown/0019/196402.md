# Legendre function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Legendre function"}

In physical science and mathematics, the **Legendre functions** $P_{\lambda}$, $Q_{\lambda}$ and **associated Legendre functions** $P^{\mu}_{\lambda}$, $Q^{\mu}_{\lambda}$, and **Legendre functions of the second kind**, $Q_{n}$, are all solutions of Legendre's differential equation. The [Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/100349) and the [associated Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/1062015) are also solutions of the differential equation in special cases, which, by virtue of being polynomials, have a large number of additional properties, mathematical structure, and applications. For these polynomial solutions, see the separate Wikipedia articles.

:::

*Image: Associated Legendre Poly.svg.* Associated Legendre polynomial curves for $\lambda = l = 5$.

## Legendre's differential equation {#Legendre's_differential_equation}

The **general Legendre equation** reads

$$\left(1 - x^2\right) y'' - 2xy' + \left[\lambda(\lambda+1) - \frac{\mu^2}{1-x^2}\right] y = 0,$$

where the numbers $\lambda$ and $\mu$ may be complex, and are called the degree and order of the relevant function, respectively. The polynomial solutions when $\lambda$ is an integer (denoted $n$), and $\mu = 0$ are the Legendre polynomials $P_{n}$; and when $\lambda$ is an integer (denoted $n$), and $\mu = m$ is also an integer with $\lvert m\rvert < n$ are the associated Legendre polynomials. All other cases of $\lambda$ and $\mu$ can be discussed as one, and the solutions are written $P^{\mu}_{\lambda}$, $Q^{\mu}_{\lambda}$. If $\mu = 0$, the superscript is omitted, and one writes just $P_{\lambda}$, $Q_{\lambda}$. However, the solution $Q_{\lambda}$ when $\lambda$ is an integer is often discussed separately as Legendre's function of the second kind, and denoted $Q_{n}$.

This is a second order linear equation with three regular singular points (at $1$, $-1$, and $\infty$). Like all such equations, it can be converted into a [hypergeometric differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypergeometric_differential_equation) by a change of variable, and its solutions can be expressed using [hypergeometric functions](wikipedia://en/2055223).

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Legendre function of the first kind"}

## Solutions of the differential equation {#Solutions_of_the_differential_equation}

Since the differential equation is linear, homogeneous (the right hand side =zero) and of second order, it has two linearly independent solutions, which can both be expressed in terms of the [hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/2055223), $_2F_1$. With $\Gamma$ being the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316), the first solution is

$$P_{\lambda}^{\mu}(z) = \frac{1}{\Gamma(1-\mu)} \left[\frac{z+1}{z-1}\right]^{\mu/2} \,_2F_1 \left(-\lambda, \lambda+1; 1-\mu; \frac{1-z}{2}\right),\qquad \text{for } \ |1-z|<2,$$

and the second is

$$Q_{\lambda}^{\mu}(z) = \frac{\sqrt{\pi}\ \Gamma(\lambda+\mu+1)}{2^{\lambda+1}\Gamma(\lambda+3/2)}\frac{e^{i\mu\pi}(z^2-1)^{\mu/2}}{z^{\lambda+\mu+1}} \,_2F_1 \left(\frac{\lambda+\mu+1}{2}, \frac{\lambda+\mu+2}{2}; \lambda+\frac{3}{2}; \frac{1}{z^2}\right),\qquad \text{for}\ \ |z|>1.$$

*Image: Plot of the Legendre function of the second kind Q n(x) with n=0.5 in the complex plane from -2-2i to 2+2i with colors created with Mathematica 13.1 function ComplexPlot3D.svg.* Plot of the Legendre function of the second kind Q n(x) with n=0.5 in the complex plane from -2-2i to 2+2i with colors created with Mathematica 13.1 function ComplexPlot3D

These are generally known as Legendre functions of the first and second kind of noninteger degree, with the additional qualifier 'associated' if $\mu$ is non-zero. A useful relation between the $P$ and $Q$ solutions is [Whipple's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whipple_formulae).

### Positive integer order {#Positive_integer_order}

For positive integer $\mu = m \in \mathbb{N}^+$ the evaluation of $P^\mu_\lambda$ above involves cancellation of singular terms. We can find the limit valid for $m \in \mathbb{N}_0$ as[^o6]

$$P^m_\lambda(z) = \lim_{\mu \to m} P^\mu_\lambda (z) = \frac{(-\lambda )_m (\lambda + 1)_m}{m!} \left[\frac{1-z}{1+z}\right]^{m/2} \,_2F_1 \left(-\lambda, \lambda+1; 1+m; \frac{1-z}{2}\right),$$

with $(\lambda)_{n}$ the (rising) [Pochhammer symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pochhammer_symbol).

:::

## Legendre functions of the second kind ($Q_{n}$) {#Legendre_functions_of_the_second_kind_()}

*Image: Mplwp\_legendreQ04.svg.* Plot of the first five Legendre functions of the second kind.

The nonpolynomial solution for the special case of integer degree $\lambda = n \in \mathbb{N}_0$, and $\mu = 0$, is often discussed separately. It is given by

$$Q_n(x)=\frac{n!}{1\cdot3\cdots(2n+1)}\left(x^{-(n+1)}+\frac{(n+1)(n+2)}{2(2n+3)}x^{-(n+3)}+\frac{(n+1)(n+2)(n+3)(n+4)}{2\cdot4(2n+3)(2n+5)}x^{-(n+5)}+\cdots\right)$$

This solution is necessarily [singular](wikipedia://en/54240) when $x = \pm 1$.

The Legendre functions of the second kind can also be defined recursively via [Bonnet's recursion formula](wikipedia://en/100349#Definition_via_generating_function)

$$Q_n(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2} \log \frac{1+x}{1-x} & n = 0 \\ P_1(x) Q_0(x) - 1 & n = 1 \\ \frac{2n-1}{n} x Q_{n-1}(x) - \frac{n-1}{n} Q_{n-2}(x) & n \geq 2 \,. \end{cases}$$

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Associated Legendre function of the second kind"}

## Associated Legendre functions of the second kind {#Associated_Legendre_functions_of_the_second_kind}

The nonpolynomial solution for the special case of integer degree $\lambda = n \in \mathbb{N}_0$, and $\mu = m \in \mathbb{N}_0$ is given by

$$Q_n^{m}(x) = (-1)^m (1-x^2)^\frac{m}{2} \frac{d^m}{dx^m}Q_n(x)\,.$$

:::

## Integral representations {#Integral_representations}

The Legendre functions can be written as contour integrals. For example,

$$P_\lambda(z) =P^0_\lambda(z) = \frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{1,z} \frac{(t^2-1)^\lambda}{2^\lambda(t-z)^{\lambda+1}}dt$$

where the contour winds around the points $1$ and $z$ in the positive direction and does not wind around $-1$. For real $x$, we have

$$P_s(x) = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi}^{\pi}\left(x+\sqrt{x^2-1}\cos\theta\right)^s d\theta = \frac{1}{\pi}\int_0^1\left(x+\sqrt{x^2-1}(2t-1)\right)^s\frac{dt}{\sqrt{t(1-t)}},\qquad s\in\mathbb{C}$$

## Legendre function as characters {#Legendre_function_as_characters}

The real integral representation of $P_s$ are very useful in the study of [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147) on $L^1(G//K)$ where $G//K$ is the [double coset space](wikipedia://en/363325) of $SL(2,\mathbb{R})$ (see [Zonal spherical function](wikipedia://en/17708915)). Actually the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) on $L^1(G//K)$ is given by

$$L^1(G//K)\ni f\mapsto \hat{f}$$

where

$$\hat{f}(s)=\int_1^\infty f(x)P_s(x)dx,\qquad -1\leq\Re(s)\leq 0$$

## Singularities of Legendre functions of the first kind ($P_{\lambda}$) as a consequence of symmetry {#Singularities_of_Legendre_functions_of_the_first_kind_()_as_a_consequence_of_symmetry}

Legendre functions $P_{\lambda}$ of non-integer degree are unbounded at the interval \[-1, 1\] . In applications in physics, this often provides a selection criterion. Indeed, because Legendre functions $Q_{\lambda}$ of the second kind are always unbounded, in order to have a bounded solution of Legendre's equation at all, the degree *must* be integer valued: *only* for integer degree, Legendre functions of the first kind reduce to Legendre polynomials, which are bounded on \[-1, 1\]. It can be shown[^o13] that the singularity of the Legendre functions $P_{\lambda}$ for non-integer degree is a consequence of the mirror symmetry of Legendre's equation. Thus there is a symmetry under the selection rule just mentioned.
