# Integration by substitution

Integral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Integration by substitution"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176), **integration by substitution**, also known as ***u*-substitution**, **reverse chain rule** or **change of variables**,[^o2] is a method for evaluating [integrals](wikipedia://en/15532) and [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823). It is the counterpart to the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) for [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921), and can loosely be thought of as using the chain rule "backwards." This involves [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms).

:::

## Substitution for a single variable {#Substitution_for_a_single_variable}

### Introduction (indefinite integrals) {#Introduction_(indefinite_integrals)}

Before stating the result [rigorously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_rigor), consider a simple case using [indefinite integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integral).

Compute $\int(2x^3+1)^7(x^2)\,dx.$[^o5]

Set $u=2x^3+1.$ This means $\frac{du}{dx}=6x^2,$ or as a [differential form](wikipedia://en/221519), $du=6x^2\,dx.$ Now consider $\int u^7du.$: $\begin{aligned} \int(2x^3 +1)^7(x^2)\,dx \\ = \frac{1}{6}\int\underbrace{(2x^3+1)^{7}}_{u^{7}}\underbrace{(6x^2)\,dx}_{du} \\ = \frac{1}{6}\int u^{7}\,du = \frac{1}{6}\left(\frac{1}{8}u^{8}\right)+C \\ = \frac{1}{48}(2x^3+1)^{8}+C, \end{aligned}$

where $C$ is an arbitrary [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939).

This procedure is frequently used, but not all integrals are of a form that permits its use. In any event, the result should be verified by differentiating and comparing to the original integrand.

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{d}{dx}\left[\frac{1}{48}(2x^3+1)^{8}+C\right] = \frac{1}{6}(2x^3+1)^{7}(6x^2) = (2x^3+1)^7(x^2). \end{aligned}$$

For definite integrals, the limits of integration must also be adjusted, but the procedure is mostly the same.

### Statement for definite integrals {#Statement_for_definite_integrals}

Let $g:[a,b]\to I$ be a [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) with a [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) derivative, where $I \subset \mathbb{R}$ is an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172). Suppose that $f:I\to\mathbb{R}$ is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122). Then:[^o7]

$$\int_a^b f(g(x))\cdot g'(x)\, dx = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\ du.$$

In Leibniz notation, the substitution $u=g(x)$ yields:

$$\frac{du}{dx} = g'(x).$$

Working heuristically with [infinitesimals](wikipedia://en/160990) yields the equation

$$du = g'(x)\,dx,$$

which suggests the substitution formula above. (This equation may be put on a rigorous foundation by interpreting it as a statement about [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519).) One may view the method of integration by substitution as a partial justification of [Leibniz's notation](wikipedia://en/410712) for integrals and derivatives.

The formula is used to transform one integral into another integral that is easier to compute. Thus, the formula can be read from left to right or from right to left in order to simplify a given integral. When used in the former manner, it is sometimes known as ***u*-substitution** or ***w*-substitution** in which a new variable is defined to be a function of the original variable found inside the [composite](wikipedia://en/195947) function multiplied by the derivative of the inner function. The latter manner is commonly used in [trigonometric substitution](wikipedia://en/375033), replacing the original variable with a [trigonometric function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) of a new variable and the original [differential](wikipedia://en/23997203) with the differential of the trigonometric function.

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

Integration by substitution can be derived from the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696) as follows. Let $f$ and $g$ be two functions satisfying the above hypothesis that $f$ is continuous on $I$ and $g'$ is integrable on the closed interval $[a,b]$. Then the function $f(g(x))\cdot g'(x)$ is also integrable on $[a,b]$. Hence the integrals

$$\int_a^b f(g(x))\cdot g'(x)\ dx$$

and

$$\int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\ du$$

in fact exist, and it remains to show that they are equal.

Since $f$ is continuous, it has an [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823) $F$. The [composite function](wikipedia://en/195947) $F \circ g$ is then defined. Since $g$ is differentiable, combining the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) and the definition of an antiderivative gives:

$$(F \circ g)'(x) = F'(g(x)) \cdot g'(x) = f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x).$$

Applying the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696) twice gives:

$$\begin{aligned} \int_a^b f(g(x)) \cdot g'(x)\ dx &= \int_a^b (F \circ g)'(x)\ dx \\ &= (F \circ g)(b) - (F \circ g)(a) \\ &= F(g(b)) - F(g(a)) \\ &= \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} F'(u)\, du = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)} f(u)\, du, \end{aligned}$$

which is the substitution rule.

:::

### Examples: Antiderivatives (indefinite integrals) {#Examples:_Antiderivatives_(indefinite_integrals)}

Substitution can be used to determine [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823). One chooses a relation between $x$ and $u,$ determines the corresponding relation between $dx$ and $du$ by differentiating, and performs the substitutions. An antiderivative for the substituted function can hopefully be determined; the original substitution between $x$ and $u$ is then undone.

#### Example 1 {#Example_1}

Consider the integral:

$$\int x \cos(x^2+1)\ dx.$$

Make the substitution $u = x^{2} + 1$ to obtain $du = 2x\ dx,$ meaning $x\ dx = \frac{1}{2}\ du.$ Therefore:

$$\begin{aligned} \int x \cos(x^2+1) \,dx &= \frac{1}{2} \int 2x \cos(x^2+1) \,dx \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2} \int\cos u\,du \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2}\sin u + C \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2}\sin(x^2+1) + C, \end{aligned}$$

where $C$ is an arbitrary [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939).

#### Example 2: Antiderivatives of tangent and cotangent {#Example_2:_Antiderivatives_of_tangent_and_cotangent}

The [tangent function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_function) can be integrated using substitution by expressing it in terms of the sine and cosine: $\tan x = \tfrac{\sin x}{\cos x}$.

Using the substitution $u = \cos x$ gives $du = -\sin x\,dx$ and

$$\begin{aligned} \int \tan x \,dx &= \int \frac{\sin x}{\cos x} \,dx \\ &= \int -\frac{du}{u} \\ &= -\ln \left|u\right| + C \\ &= -\ln \left|\cos x\right| + C \\ &= \ln \left|\sec x\right| + C. \end{aligned}$$

The [cotangent function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotangent_function) can be integrated similarly by expressing it as $\cot x = \tfrac{\cos x}{\sin x}$ and using the substitution $u = \sin{x}, du = \cos{x}\,dx$:

$$\begin{aligned} \int \cot x \,dx &= \int \frac{\cos x}{\sin x} \,dx \\ &= \int \frac{du}{u} \\ &= \ln \left|u\right| + C \\ &= \ln \left|\sin x\right| + C. \end{aligned}$$

### Examples: Definite integrals {#Examples:_Definite_integrals}

When evaluating definite integrals by substitution, one may calculate the antiderivative fully first, then apply the boundary conditions. In that case, there is no need to transform the boundary terms. Alternatively, one may fully evaluate the indefinite integral ([see above](wikipedia://en/193748#Examples:_Antiderivatives)) first then apply the boundary conditions. This becomes especially handy when multiple substitutions are used.

#### Example 1 {#Example_1}

Consider the integral:

$$\int_0^2 \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}} dx.$$

Make the substitution $u = x^{2} + 1$ to obtain $du = 2x\ dx,$ meaning $x\ dx = \frac{1}{2}\ du.$ Therefore:

$$\begin{aligned} \int_{x=0}^{x=2} \frac{x}{\sqrt{x^2+1}} \ dx &= \frac{1}{2} \int_{u=1}^{u=5} \frac{du}{\sqrt{u}} \\[6pt] &= \frac{1}{2} \left(2\sqrt{5}-2\sqrt{1}\right) \\[6pt] &= \sqrt{5}-1. \end{aligned}$$

Since the lower limit $x = 0$ was replaced with $u = 1,$ and the upper limit $x = 2$ with $2^{2} + 1 = 5,$ a transformation back into terms of $x$ was unnecessary.

#### Example 2: [Trigonometric substitution](wikipedia://en/375033) {#Example_2:_Trigonometric_substitution}

For the integral

$$\int_0^1 \sqrt{1-x^2}\,dx,$$

a variation of the above procedure is needed. The substitution $x = \sin u$ implying $dx = \cos u \,du$ is useful because $\sqrt{1-\sin^2 u} = \cos u.$ We thus have:

$$\begin{aligned} \int_0^1 \sqrt{1-x^2}\ dx &= \int_0^{\pi/2} \sqrt{1-\sin^2 u} \cos u\ du \\[6pt] &= \int_0^{\pi/2} \cos^2 u\ du \\[6pt] &= \left[\frac{u}{2} + \frac{\sin(2u)}{4}\right]_0^{\pi/2} \\[6pt] &= \frac{\pi}{4} + 0 \\[6pt] &= \frac{\pi}{4}. \end{aligned}$$

The resulting integral can be computed using [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) or a [double angle formula](wikipedia://en/25804104#Multiple-angle_and_half-angle_formulae), $2\cos^{2} u = 1 + \cos (2u),$ followed by one more substitution. One can also note that the function being integrated is the upper right quarter of a circle with a radius of one, and hence integrating the upper right quarter from zero to one is the geometric equivalent to the area of one quarter of the unit circle, or $\tfrac \pi 4.$

## Substitution for multiple variables {#Substitution_for_multiple_variables}

One may also use substitution when integrating [functions of several variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_function).

Here, the substitution function $(v_{1},...,v_{n}) = \varphi(u_{1}, ..., u_{n})$ needs to be [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) and continuously differentiable, and the differentials transform as:

$$dv_1 \cdots dv_n = \left|\det(D\varphi)(u_1, \ldots, u_n)\right| \, du_1 \cdots du_n,$$

where $\det(D\varphi)(u_{1}, ..., u_{n})$ denotes the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) of [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) of $\varphi$ at the point $(u_{1}, ..., u_{n})$. This formula expresses the fact that the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the determinant of a matrix equals the volume of the [parallelotope](wikipedia://en/23975#Parallelotope) spanned by its columns or rows.

More precisely, the *[change of variables](wikipedia://en/2146848)* formula is stated in the next theorem:

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let $U$ be an open set in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ and $\varphi : U \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ an [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) differentiable function with continuous partial derivatives, the Jacobian of which is nonzero for every $x$ in $U$. Then for any real-valued, compactly supported, continuous function $f$, with support contained in $\varphi(U)$:

$$\int_{\varphi(U)} f(\mathbf{v})\, d\mathbf{v} = \int_U f(\varphi(\mathbf{u})) \left|\det(D\varphi)(\mathbf{u})\right|\,d\mathbf{u}.$$

:::

The conditions on the theorem can be weakened in various ways. First, the requirement that $\varphi$ be continuously differentiable can be replaced by the weaker assumption that $\varphi$ be merely differentiable and have a continuous inverse.[^o18] This is guaranteed to hold if $\varphi$ is continuously differentiable by the [inverse function theorem](wikipedia://en/287229). Alternatively, the requirement that $\det(D\varphi) \ne 0$ can be eliminated by applying [Sard's theorem](wikipedia://en/914901).[^o19]

For Lebesgue measurable functions, the theorem can be stated in the following form:[^o20]

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let $U$ be a measurable subset of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ and $\varphi : U \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ an [injective function](wikipedia://en/45196), and suppose for every $x$ in $U$ there exists $\varphi'(x)$ in $\mathbf{R}^{n,n}$ such that $\varphi(y) = \varphi(x) + \varphi'(x)(y - x) + o(\lVert y - x\rVert)$ as $y \to x$ (here $o$ is [little-*o* notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landau_symbol#Related_asymptotic_notations)). Then $\varphi(U)$ is measurable, and for any real-valued function $f$ defined on $\varphi(U)$:

$$\int_{\varphi(U)} f(v)\, dv = \int_U f(\varphi(u)) \left|\det \varphi'(u)\right| \,du$$

in the sense that if either integral exists (including the possibility of being properly infinite), then so does the other one, and they have the same value.

:::

Another very general version in [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) is the following:[^o22]

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let $X$ be a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) equipped with a finite [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933) $\mu$, and let $Y$ be a [σ-compact](wikipedia://en/346681) Hausdorff space with a [σ-finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_finite_measure) Radon measure $\rho$. Let $\varphi : X \to Y$ be an [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous) function (where the latter means that $\rho(\varphi(E)) = 0$ whenever $\mu(E) = 0$). Then there exists a real-valued [Borel measurable function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_algebra) $w$ on $X$ such that for every [Lebesgue integrable](wikipedia://en/26064288) function $f : Y \to \mathbf{R}$, the function $(f \circ \varphi) \cdot w$ is Lebesgue integrable on $X$, and

$$\int_Y f(y)\,d\rho(y) = \int_X (f\circ \varphi)(x)\,w(x)\,d\mu(x).$$

Furthermore, it is possible to write

$$w(x) = (g\circ \varphi)(x)$$

for some Borel measurable function $g$ on $Y$.

:::

In [geometric measure theory](wikipedia://en/12169694), integration by substitution is used with [Lipschitz functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_function). A bi-Lipschitz function is a Lipschitz function $\varphi : U \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ which is injective and whose inverse function $\varphi^{-1} : \varphi(U) \to U$ is also Lipschitz. By [Rademacher's theorem](wikipedia://en/7764195), a bi-Lipschitz mapping is differentiable [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688). In particular, the Jacobian determinant of a bi-Lipschitz mapping $\det D\varphi$ is well-defined almost everywhere. The following result then holds:

::: {#o24 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let $U$ be an open subset of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ and $\varphi : U \to \mathbf{R}^{n}$ be a bi-Lipschitz mapping. Let $f : \varphi(U) \to \mathbf{R}$ be measurable. Then

$$\int_{\varphi(U)} f(x)\,dx = \int_U (f\circ \varphi)(x) \left|\det D\varphi(x)\right|\,dx$$

in the sense that if either integral exists (or is properly infinite), then so does the other one, and they have the same value.

:::

The above theorem was first proposed by [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler) when he developed the notion of [double integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_integrals) in 1769. Although generalized to triple integrals by [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange) in 1773, and used by [Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695), [Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace), and [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss), and first generalized to $n$ variables by [Mikhail Ostrogradsky](wikipedia://en/176917) in 1836, it resisted a fully rigorous formal proof for a surprisingly long time, and was first satisfactorily resolved 125 years later, by [Élie Cartan](wikipedia://en/386274) in a series of papers beginning in the mid-1890s.[^o25][^o26]

## Application in probability {#Application_in_probability}

Substitution can be used to answer the following important question in probability: given a random variable $X$ with probability density $p_{X}$ and another random variable $Y$ such that $Y= \phi(X)$ for [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) (one-to-one) $\phi,$ what is the probability density for $Y$?

It is easiest to answer this question by first answering a slightly different question: what is the probability that $Y$ takes a value in some particular subset $S$? Denote this probability $P(Y \in S).$ Of course, if $Y$ has probability density $p_{Y}$, then the answer is:

$$P(Y \in S) = \int_S p_Y(y)\,dy,$$

but this is not really useful because we do not know $p_{Y};$ it is what we are trying to find. We can make progress by considering the problem in the variable $X$. $Y$ takes a value in $S$ whenever $X$ takes a value in $\phi^{-1}(S),$ so:

$$P(Y \in S) = P(X \in \phi^{-1}(S)) = \int_{\phi^{-1}(S)} p_X(x)\,dx.$$

Changing from variable $x$ to $y$ gives:

$$P(Y \in S) = \int_{\phi^{-1}(S)} p_X(x)\,dx = \int_S p_X(\phi^{-1}(y)) \left|\frac{d\phi^{-1}}{dy}\right|\,dy.$$

Combining this with our first equation gives:

$$\int_S p_Y(y)\,dy = \int_S p_X(\phi^{-1}(y)) \left|\frac{d\phi^{-1}}{dy}\right|\,dy,$$

so:

$$p_Y(y) = p_X(\phi^{-1}(y)) \left|\frac{d\phi^{-1}}{dy}\right|.$$

In the case where $X$ and $Y$ depend on several uncorrelated variables (i.e., $p_X=p_X(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ and $y=\phi(x)$), $p_Y$can be found by substitution in several variables discussed above. The result is:

$$p_Y(y) = p_X(\phi^{-1}(y)) \left|\det D\phi ^{-1}(y) \right|.$$
