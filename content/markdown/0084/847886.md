# Fermat polygonal number theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fermat polygonal number theorem"}

In [additive number theory](wikipedia://en/4460333), the **Fermat polygonal number theorem** states that every positive integer is a sum of at most $n$ [$n$-gonal numbers](wikipedia://en/213996). That is, every positive integer can be written as the sum of three or fewer [triangular numbers](wikipedia://en/213915), and as the sum of four or fewer [square numbers](wikipedia://en/213919), and as the sum of five or fewer [pentagonal numbers](wikipedia://en/504786), and so on. That is, the $n$-gonal numbers form an [additive basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_basis) of order $n$.

:::

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Three such representations of the number 17, for example, are shown below:

- 17 = 10 + 6 + 1 (*triangular numbers*)
- 17 = 16 + 1 (*square numbers*)
- 17 = 12 + 5 (*pentagonal numbers*).

:::

## History {#History}

*Image: Eureka Gauss.png.* [Gauss's diary](wikipedia://en/33670194) entry related to sum of triangular numbers (1796)

The theorem is named after [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966), who stated it, in 1638, without proof, promising to write it in a separate work that never appeared.[^o6] [Joseph Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange) proved the [square case](wikipedia://en/326395) in 1770, which states that every positive number can be represented as a sum of four squares, for example, 7 = 4 + 1 + 1 + 1.[^o6] [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) proved the triangular case in 1796, commemorating the occasion by writing in [his diary](wikipedia://en/33670194) the line "[ΕΥΡΗΚΑ!](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eureka_%28word%29) num = Δ + Δ + Δ",[^o7] and published a proof in his book *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)*. For this reason, Gauss's result is sometimes known as the **Eureka theorem**.[^o8] The full polygonal number theorem was not resolved until it was finally proven by [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy) in 1813.[^o6] The proof of (Nathanson 1987) is based on the following lemma due to Cauchy:

For odd positive integers $a$ and $b$ such that $b^{2} < 4a$ and $3a < b^{2} + 2b + 4$ we can find nonnegative integers $s$, $t$, $u$, and $v$ such that $a = s^{2} + t^{2} + u^{2} + v^{2}$ and $b = s + t + u + v$.
