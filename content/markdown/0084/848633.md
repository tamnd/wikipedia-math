# Discrete valuation ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discrete valuation ring"}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **discrete valuation ring** (**DVR**) is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039) (PID) with exactly one non-zero [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164).

:::

This means a DVR is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) $R$ that satisfies any and all of the following equivalent conditions:

1. $R$ is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391), a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039), and not a [field](wikipedia://en/10603).
1. $R$ is a [valuation ring](wikipedia://en/966856) with a value group isomorphic to the integers under addition.
1. $R$ is a local ring, a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132), and not a field.
1. $R$ is [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) and a [local domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_domain) whose unique maximal [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) is principal, and not a field.[^o2]
1. $R$ is [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209), Noetherian, and a local ring with [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) one.
1. $R$ is a principal ideal domain with a unique non-zero [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928).
1. $R$ is a principal ideal domain with a unique [irreducible element](wikipedia://en/60693) ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) multiplication by [units](wikipedia://en/289666)).
1. $R$ is a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) with a unique irreducible element (up to multiplication by units).
1. $R$ is Noetherian, not a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), and every nonzero [fractional ideal](wikipedia://en/460700) of *R* is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/22260381) in the sense that it cannot be written as a finite intersection of fractional ideals properly containing it.
1. There is some [discrete valuation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_valuation#Discrete_valuation_rings_and_valuations_on_fields) $\nu$ on the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) $K$ of $R$ such that $R=\{0\}\cup\{x\in K: \nu(x)\geq 0\}$.
1. $R$ is a Noetherian local ring whose unique maximal ideal is generated by a single non-[nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) element.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Algebraic {#Algebraic}

#### Localization of Dedekind rings {#Localization_of_Dedekind_rings}

Let $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$ be the [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) of $\mathbb{Z}$ at the ideal generated by 2. Formally,

$$\mathbb{Z}_{(2)} = \{ z/n : z,n\in\mathbb{Z},\,\, n\text{ is odd}\}.$$

The field of fractions of $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$ is $\mathbb{Q}$. For any nonzero element $r$ of $\mathbb{Q}$, we can apply [unique factorization](wikipedia://en/11556) to the numerator and denominator of $r$ to write $r$ as $\tfrac{2^kz}{n}$ where $z$, $n$, and $k$ are integers with $z$ and $n$ odd. In this case, we define $\nu(r)=k$.

Then $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$ is the discrete valuation ring corresponding to $\nu$. The maximal ideal of $\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$ is the principal ideal generated by 2; i.e., $2\mathbb{Z}_{(2)}$, and the "unique" irreducible element (up to units) is 2 (also known as a uniformizing parameter).

More generally, any [localization](wikipedia://en/252216) of a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) at a non-zero [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) is a discrete valuation ring; in practice, this is frequently how discrete valuation rings arise. In particular, we can define [rings](wikipedia://en/48404)

$$\mathbb Z_{(p)}:=\{z/n : z,n\in\mathbb Z,\,p\nmid n\}$$

for any [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$ in complete analogy.

#### *p*-adic integers {#p-adic_integers}

The [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $\mathbb{Z}_p$ of [*p*-adic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_integer) is a DVR, for any [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$. Here $p$ is an [irreducible element](wikipedia://en/60693); the [valuation](wikipedia://en/347049) assigns to each $p$-adic integer $x$ the largest [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $k$ such that $p^k$ divides $x$.

#### Formal power series {#Formal_power_series}

Another important example of a DVR is the [ring of formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) $R = k[[T]]$ in one variable $T$ over some field $k$. The "unique" irreducible element is $T$, the maximal ideal of $R$ is the principal ideal generated by $T$, and the valuation $\nu$ assigns to each power series the index (i.e. degree) of the first non-zero coefficient.

If we restrict ourselves to [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) coefficients, we can consider the ring of power series in one variable that *converge* in a neighborhood of 0 (with the neighborhood depending on the power series). This is a discrete valuation ring. This is useful for building intuition with the [valuative criterion of properness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valuative_criterion_of_properness).

#### Ring in function field {#Ring_in_function_field}

For an example more geometrical in nature, take the ring

$$R=\{f/g:f,g\in\mathbb{R}[x],\,g(0)\neq 0\},$$

considered as a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of the field of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) $\mathbb{R}(x)$. $R$ can be identified with the ring of all real-valued rational functions defined (i.e., finite) on a [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28topology%29) of 0 on the real axis (with the neighborhood depending on the function). It is a discrete valuation ring; the "unique" irreducible element is $x$ and the valuation assigns to each function $f$ the order (possibly 0) of the zero of $f$ at 0. This example provides the template for studying general [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260) near non-[singular points](wikipedia://en/4230456), the algebraic curve in this case being the real line.

### Scheme-theoretic {#Scheme-theoretic}

#### Henselian trait {#Henselian_trait}

For a DVR $R$ it is common to write the fraction field as $K = \text{Frac}(R)$ and $\kappa = R/\mathfrak{m}$ the [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680). These correspond to the [generic](wikipedia://en/3043978) and [closed](wikipedia://en/33086408) points of $S=\text{Spec}(R).$ For example, the closed point of $\text{Spec}(\mathbb{Z}_p)$ is $\mathbb{F}_p$ and the generic point is $\mathbb{Q}_p$. Sometimes this is denoted as

$$\eta \to S \leftarrow s$$

where $\eta$ is the generic point and $s$ is the closed point.

#### Localization of a point {#Localization_of_a_point}

Given an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) $(X,\mathcal{O}_X)$, the [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) $\mathcal{O}_{X,\mathfrak{p}}$ at a smooth (closed) point $\mathfrak{p}$ is a discrete valuation ring, because it is a principal valuation ring. Note because the point $\mathfrak{p}$ is smooth, the [completion](wikipedia://en/9477975) of the [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to the completion of the [localization](wikipedia://en/252216) of $\mathbb{A}^1$ at some point $\mathfrak{q}$.

For any nonsingular variety, one can similarly consider the local ring at a point of codimension one. More generally, on a (locally) [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/2783891) [normal scheme](wikipedia://en/2629423), the local ring at the generic point of a [prime Weil divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Weil_divisor) is a discrete valuation ring, with the discrete valuation giving the order of vanishing of a regular function along the divisor.[^o13]

## Uniformizing parameter {#Uniformizing_parameter}

Given a DVR $R$, any irreducible element of $R$ is a generator for the unique maximal ideal of $R$ and vice versa. Such an element is also called a **uniformizing parameter** of $R$ (or a **uniformizing element**, a **uniformizer**, or a **prime element**).

If we fix a uniformizing parameter $t$, then $\mathfrak{m}=(t)$ is the unique maximal ideal of $R$, and every other non-zero ideal is a power of $\mathfrak{m}$; i.e. has the form $(t^k)$ for some $k\geq 0$. All the powers of $t$ are distinct, and so are the powers of $\mathfrak{m}$. Every non-zero element $x$ of $R$ can be written in the form $\alpha t^k$ with $\alpha$ a unit in $R$ and $k\geq 0$, both uniquely determined by $x$. The valuation is given by $\nu(x)=k\nu(t)$. Thus, to understand the ring completely, one needs to know the group of units of $R$ and how the units interact additively with the powers of $t$.

The function $\nu$ also makes any discrete valuation ring into a [Euclidean domain](wikipedia://en/10376).

:::

## Topology {#Topology}

Every discrete valuation ring, being a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391), carries a natural ([adic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adic_topology)) topology and is a [topological ring](wikipedia://en/60097). It also admits a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) structure where the distance between two elements $x$ and $y$ can be measured as follows:

$$|x-y| = b^{-\nu(x-y)},$$

where $b\in\mathbb{R}_{>1}$. Intuitively, an element $z$ is "small" and "close to 0" [iff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iff) its [valuation](wikipedia://en/347049) $\nu(z)$ is large. The above metric, along with the condition $|0|=0$, is the restriction of an [absolute value](wikipedia://en/5042667) defined on the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of the discrete valuation ring.

A DVR $R$ with maximal ideal $\mathfrak{m}$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) if and only if it is [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) and its [residue field](wikipedia://en/10789680) $R/\mathfrak{m}$ is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615).

Examples of [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) DVRs include the ring of $p$-adic integers and the ring of formal power series over any field.

For a given DVR, one often passes to its [completion](wikipedia://en/9477975), a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) DVR containing the given ring that is often easier to study. This [completion](wikipedia://en/9477975) procedure can be thought of in a geometrical way as passing from [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) to [power series](wikipedia://en/59958), or from [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) to the [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438).

The ring of all formal power series in one variable with real coefficients is the completion of the ring of rational functions defined in a neighborhood of 0 on the real line; it is also the completion of the ring of all real power series that converge near 0. The completion of $\mathbb{Z}_{(p)}=\mathbb{Q} \cap \mathbb{Z}_p$ (which can be seen as the set of all rational numbers that are $p$-adic integers) is the ring of all $p$-adic integers $\mathbb{Z}_p$.
