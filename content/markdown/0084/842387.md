# Evolute

*Image: Evolute-parab-1-e.svg.* The blue parabola is the **involute** of the red curve. The red curve is the **evolute** of the blue parabola, and can be constructed as the locus of all centers of curvature of the blue parabola.

*Image: Evolute1.gif.* The evolute of a curve (in this case, an ellipse) is the envelope of its normals.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Evolute"}

In the [differential geometry of curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_geometry_of_curves), the **evolute** of a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) is the [locus](wikipedia://en/542465) of all its [centers of curvature](wikipedia://en/5816420). That is to say that when the center of curvature of each point on a curve is drawn, the resultant shape will be the evolute of that curve. The evolute of a circle is therefore a single point at its center. Equivalently, an evolute is the [envelope](wikipedia://en/880235) of the [normals](wikipedia://en/76944) to a curve.

:::

The evolute of a curve, a surface, or more generally a [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095), is the [caustic](wikipedia://en/880406) of the normal map. Let $M$ be a smooth, regular submanifold in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. For each point $p$ in $M$ and each vector $\mathbf{v}$, based at $p$ and normal to $M$, we associate the point $p + \mathbf{v}$. This defines a [Lagrangian map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_map), called the normal map. The caustic of the normal map is the evolute of $M$.[^o3]

Evolutes are closely connected to [involutes](wikipedia://en/840704): A curve is the evolute of any of its involutes.

## History {#History}

[Apollonius](wikipedia://en/257242) (c. 200 BC) discussed evolutes in Book V of his *Conics*. However, [Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127) is sometimes credited with being the first to study them (1673). Huygens formulated his theory of evolutes sometime around 1659 to help solve the problem of finding the [tautochrone curve](wikipedia://en/171878), which in turn helped him construct an isochronous pendulum. This was because the tautochrone curve is a [cycloid](wikipedia://en/41638), and the cycloid has the unique property that its evolute is also a cycloid. The theory of evolutes, in fact, allowed Huygens to achieve many results that would later be found using calculus.[^o5]

## Evolute of a parametric curve {#Evolute_of_a_parametric_curve}

If $\vec x= \vec c(t),\; t\in [t_1, t_2]$ is the parametric representation of a [regular curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_curve) in the plane with its curvature nowhere 0 and $\rho(t)$ its curvature radius and $\vec n(t)$ the unit normal pointing to the curvature center, then

$$\vec E(t) = \vec c(t) + \rho (t) \vec n(t)$$

describes the **evolute** of the given curve.

For $\vec c(t)=(x(t),y(t))^\mathsf{T}$ and $\vec E=(X,Y)^\mathsf{T}$ one gets

$$X(t) = x(t) - \frac{y'(t) \Big(x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2\Big)}{x'(t) y''(t) - x''(t) y'(t)}$$

and

$$Y(t) = y(t) + \frac{x'(t) \Big(x'(t)^2+y'(t)^2\Big)}{x'(t) y''(t) - x''(t) y'(t)}.$$

## Evolute of an implicit curve {#Evolute_of_an_implicit_curve}

For a curve $\Gamma$ expressed implicitly in the form $f(x, y) = 0$, the Evolute can be determined through a system of equations that relates the original curve to its normal lines. At each point on the curve $\Gamma$, the direction of the normal vector is given by:

$$\left( -\frac{f_y}{f_x}, \, \frac{x f_y - y f_x}{f_x} \right)$$

where $f_x = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x}$ and $f_y = \frac{\partial f}{\partial y}$. These components describe the direction of the normal vector at each point on the curve.

The Evolute can be obtained by solving the following system (see [^o12] for details):

$$\begin{aligned} 0 &= f(x, y), \\ u &= -\frac{f_y}{f_x}, \\ v &= \frac{x f_y - y f_x}{f_x}, \end{aligned}$$

where $u$ and $v$ represent the components of the normal vector. Eliminating the variables $x$ and $y$ from this system yields a single algebraic equation in terms of $u$ and $v$, which defines the evolute of the curve.

## Properties of the evolute {#Properties_of_the_evolute}

*Image: Evolute-parab-2-e.svg.* The normal at point P is the tangent at the curvature center C.

In order to derive properties of a regular curve it is advantageous to use the [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) $s$ of the given curve as its parameter, because of $\left|\vec c'\right| = 1$ and $\vec n' = -\vec c'/\rho$ (see [Frenet–Serret formulas](wikipedia://en/666987)). Hence the tangent vector of the evolute $\vec E=\vec c +\rho \vec n$ is:

$$\vec E' = \vec c' +\rho'\vec n + \rho\vec n' = \rho'\vec n\ .$$

From this equation one gets the following properties of the evolute:

- At points with $\rho' = 0$ the evolute is *not regular*. That means: at points with maximal or minimal curvature ([vertices](wikipedia://en/6865890) of the given curve) the evolute has *cusps*. (See the diagrams of the evolutes of the parabola, the ellipse, the cycloid and the nephroid.)
- For any arc of the evolute that does not include a cusp, the length of the arc equals the difference between the radii of curvature at its endpoints. This fact leads to an easy proof of the [Tait–Kneser theorem](wikipedia://en/62530331) on nesting of [osculating circles](wikipedia://en/1896705).[^o10]
- The normals of the given curve at points of nonzero curvature are tangents to the evolute, and the normals of the curve at points of zero curvature are asymptotes to the evolute. Hence: the evolute is the *envelope of the normals* of the given curve.
- At sections of the curve with $\rho' > 0$ or $\rho' < 0$ the curve is an *involute* of its evolute. (In the diagram: The blue parabola is an involute of the red semicubic parabola, which is actually the evolute of the blue parabola.)

*Proof* of the last property: Let be $\rho' > 0$ at the section of consideration. An [involute](wikipedia://en/840704) of the evolute can be described as follows:

$$\vec C_0=\vec E -\frac{\vec E'}{\left|\vec E'\right|} \left(\int_0^s\left|\vec E'(w)\right| \mathrm dw + l_0 \right) ,$$

where $l_0$ is a fixed string extension (see [Involute of a parameterized curve](wikipedia://en/840704#Involute_of_a_parameterized_curve) ). With $\vec E=\vec c +\rho\vec n\; ,\; \vec E'=\rho'\vec n$ and $\rho'>0$ one gets

$$\vec C_0 = \vec c +\rho\vec n-\vec n \left(\int_0^s \rho'(w) \; \mathrm dw \;+l_0\right)= \vec c + (\rho(0) - l_0)\; \vec n\, .$$

That means: For the string extension $l_0=\rho(0)$ the given curve is reproduced.

- *Parallel curves* have the same evolute.

*Proof:* A parallel curve with distance $d$ off the given curve has the parametric representation $\vec c_d = \vec c + d\vec n$ and the radius of curvature $\rho_d=\rho -d$ (see [parallel curve](wikipedia://en/912825#Parallel_curve_of_a_parametrically_given_curve)). Hence the evolute of the parallel curve is

$$\vec E_d = \vec c_d +\rho_d \vec n =\vec c +d\vec n +(\rho -d)\vec n=\vec c +\rho \vec n = \vec E\; .$$

### Real algebraic properties and Singularities {#Real_algebraic_properties_and_Singularities}

From the perspective of singularity theory, evolutes are envelopes of smooth families of lines and can exhibit typical singularities such as [cusps](wikipedia://en/6851758). These singularities correspond to critical points of curvature and degeneracies in the family of normals. Evolutes are classic examples of [caustics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caustics) in Lagrangian and [symplectic geometry](wikipedia://en/294298). [Ragni Piene](wikipedia://en/24884377), Cordian Riener, and Boris Shapiro conducted a detailed study of the evolutes of plane real-algebraic curves, focusing on their real and complex geometric properties and bounds on the possible singularities that can arise.[^o12] They show that for the real setup

- The maximal number of times a real line can intersect the evolute of a real-algebraic curve of degree $d$ is at least $d(d-2)$.
- The maximal number of real cusps (vertices) on such an evolute is at least $d(2d - 3)$.
- The maximal number of crunodes (real transverse self-intersections) on the evolute and on its dual curve were also studied, with polynomial lower bounds in terms of $d$.[^o12]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Evolute of a parabola {#Evolute_of_a_parabola}

For the parabola with the parametric representation $(t,t^2)$ one gets from the formulae above the equations:

$$X=\cdots=-4t^3$$

$$Y=\cdots=\frac{1}{2} + 3t^2 \, ,$$

which describes a [semicubic parabola](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicubic_parabola)

*Image: Evolute-elli.svg.* Evolute (red) of an ellipse

### Evolute of an ellipse {#Evolute_of_an_ellipse}

For the ellipse with the parametric representation $(a\cos t, b\sin t)$ one gets:[^o17]

$$X= \cdots = \frac{a^2-b^2}{a}\cos ^3t$$

$$Y= \cdots = \frac{b^2-a^2}{b}\sin ^3t \; .$$

These are the equations of a non symmetric [astroid](wikipedia://en/769163). Eliminating parameter $t$ leads to the implicit representation

$$(aX)^{\tfrac{2}{3}} +(bY)^{\tfrac{2}{3}} = (a^2-b^2)^{\tfrac{2}{3}}\ .$$

*Image: Cycloid osculating circle evolute 2.gif.* Cycloid (blue), its osculating circle (red) and evolute (green).

### Evolute of a cycloid {#Evolute_of_a_cycloid}

For the [cycloid](wikipedia://en/41638) with the parametric representation $(r(t - \sin t), r(1 - \cos t))$ the evolute will be:

$$X=\cdots=r(t + \sin t)$$

$$Y=\cdots=r(\cos t - 1)$$

which describes a transposed replica of itself.

*Image: Nephroide-evolute-e.svg.* The evolute of the large nephroid (blue) is the small nephroid (red).

### Evolute of log-aesthetic curves {#Evolute_of_log-aesthetic_curves}

The evolute of a [log-aesthetic curve](wikipedia://en/11129054) is another log-aesthetic curve.[^o21] One instance of this relation is that the evolute of an [Euler spiral](wikipedia://en/19107713) is a spiral with [Cesàro equation](wikipedia://en/11129054) $\kappa(s) = -s^{-3}$.[^o22]

:::

## Evolutes of some curves {#Evolutes_of_some_curves}

The evolute

- of a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) is a semicubic parabola (see above),
- of an [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) is a non symmetric astroid (see above),
- of a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) is an [ideal point](wikipedia://en/4995922),
- of a [nephroid](wikipedia://en/843192) is a nephroid (half as large, see diagram),
- of an [astroid](wikipedia://en/769163) is an astroid (twice as large),
- of a [cardioid](wikipedia://en/642101) is a cardioid (one third as large),
- of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) is its center,
- of a [deltoid](wikipedia://en/769176) is a deltoid (three times as large),
- of a [cycloid](wikipedia://en/41638) is a congruent cycloid,
- of a [logarithmic spiral](wikipedia://en/42373) is the same logarithmic spiral,
- of a [tractrix](wikipedia://en/841685) is a catenary.

## Radial curve {#Radial_curve}

A curve with a similar definition is the **radial** of a given curve. For each point on the curve take the vector from the point to the center of curvature and translate it so that it begins at the origin. Then the locus of points at the end of such vectors is called the radial of the curve. The equation for the radial is obtained by removing the $x$ and $y$ terms from the equation of the evolute. This produces

$$(X, Y)= \left(-y'\frac{{x'}^2+{y'}^2}{x'y''-x''y'}\; ,\; x'\frac{{x'}^2+{y'}^2}{x'y''-x''y'}\right) .$$

:::
