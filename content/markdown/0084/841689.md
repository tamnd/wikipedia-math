# Pullback (category theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pullback"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **pullback** (also called a **fiber product**, **fibre product**, **fibered product** or **Cartesian square**) is the [limit](wikipedia://en/39248) of a [diagram](wikipedia://en/7651625) consisting of two [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) $f : X \to Z$ and $g : Y \to Z$ with a common codomain. The pullback is written

:::

$$P = X \times_{f, Z, g} Y.$$

Usually the morphisms $f$ and $g$ are omitted from the notation, and then the pullback is written

$$P = X \times_{Z} Y.$$

The pullback comes equipped with two natural morphisms $P \to X$ and $P \to Y$. The pullback of two morphisms $f$ and $g$ need not exist, but if it does, it is essentially uniquely defined by the two morphisms. In many situations, $X \times_{Z} Y$ may intuitively be thought of as consisting of pairs of elements $(x, y)$ with $x$ in $X$, $y$ in $Y$, and $f(x) = g(y)$. For the general definition, a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) is used, which essentially expresses the fact that the pullback is the "most general" way to complete the two given morphisms to a [commutative square](wikipedia://en/138484).

The [dual concept](wikipedia://en/310885) of the pullback is the *[pushout](wikipedia://en/582024)*.

## Universal property {#Universal_property}

Explicitly, a pullback of the morphisms $f$ and $g$ consists of an [object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) $P$ and two morphisms $p_1:P\rightarrow X$ and $p_2:P\rightarrow Y$ for which the diagram

*Image: Categorical pullback.svg.*

[commutes](wikipedia://en/138484). Moreover, the pullback $(P, p_{1}, p_{2})$ must be [universal](wikipedia://en/32245) with respect to this diagram.[^o3] That is, for any other such triple $(Q, q_{1}, q_{2})$ where $q_{1} : Q \to X$ and $q_{2} : Q \to Y$ are morphisms with $f q_{1} = g q_{2}$, there must exist a unique $u : Q \to P$ such that

$$p_1 \circ u=q_1, \qquad p_2\circ u=q_2.$$

This situation is illustrated in the following commutative diagram.

*Image: Categorical pullback (expanded).svg.*

As with all universal constructions, a pullback, if it exists, is unique up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). In fact, given two pullbacks $(A, a_{1}, a_{2})$ and $(B, b_{1}, b_{2})$ of the same [cospan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cospan) $X \to Z \leftarrow Y$, there is a unique isomorphism between $A$ and $B$ respecting the pullback structure.

## Pullback and product {#Pullback_and_product}

The pullback is similar to the [product](wikipedia://en/237868), but not the same. The ordinary binary product is constructed as the limit of a diagram containing only the two objects $X$ and $Y$, and no arrows between them (a [discrete category](wikipedia://en/808519)), thus "forgetting" the morphisms $f$ and $g$ and the object $Z$ from the definition of the pullback. Alternatively, one can also "trivialize" them by specializing $Z$ to be the [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object), when it exists. $f$ and $g$ are then uniquely determined and thus carry no information, and the pullback of this cospan can be seen to be the product of $X$ and $Y$.

Conversely, the pullback can be thought of as the ordinary (Cartesian) product, but with additional structure; formally, the pullback is exactly the binary product in the [slice category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slice_category) over $Z$.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Commutative rings {#Commutative_rings}

*Image: Pullback commutative rings.svg.* The category of commutative rings admits pullbacks.

In the [category of commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_commutative_rings) (with identity), the pullback is called the fibered product. Let $A$, $B$, and $C$ be [commutative rings](wikipedia://en/61346) (with identity) and $\alpha : A \to C$ and $\beta : B \to C$ (identity preserving) [ring homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/26411). Then the pullback of this diagram exists and is given by the [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of the [product ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_ring) $A \times B$ defined by

$$A \times_{C} B = \left\{(a,b) \in A \times B \; \big| \; \alpha(a) = \beta(b) \right\}$$

along with the morphisms

$$\beta' \colon A \times_{C} B \to A, \qquad \alpha'\colon A \times_{C} B \to B$$

given by $\beta'(a, b) = a$ and $\alpha'(a, b) = b$ for all $(a, b) \in A \times_C B$. We then have

$$\alpha \circ \beta' = \beta \circ \alpha'.$$

### Groups and modules {#Groups_and_modules}

In complete analogy to the example of commutative rings above, one can show that all pullbacks exist in the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092) and in the [category of modules](wikipedia://en/11231812) over some fixed ring.

### Sets {#Sets}

In the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), the pullback of functions $f : X \to Z$ and $g : Y \to Z$ always exists and is given by the set

$$X\times_Z Y = \{(x, y) \in X \times Y \; \big| \; f(x) = g(y)\} = \bigcup_{z \in f(X) \cap g(Y)} f^{-1}[\{z\}] \times g^{-1}[\{z\}] ,$$

together with the [restrictions](wikipedia://en/1589032) of the [projection maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projection_map) $\pi_{1}$ and $\pi_{2}$ to $X \times_{Z} Y$.

Alternatively one may view the pullback in $\mathbf{Set}$ asymmetrically:

$$X\times_Z Y \cong \coprod_{x\in X} g^{-1}[\{f(x)\}] \cong \coprod_{y\in Y} f^{-1}[\{g(y)\}]$$

where $\coprod$ is the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946) of sets (the involved sets are not disjoint on their own unless $f$ resp. $g$ is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective)). In the first case, the projection $\pi_{1}$ extracts the $x$ index while $\pi_{2}$ forgets the index, leaving elements of $Y$.

This example motivates another way of characterizing the pullback: as the [equalizer](wikipedia://en/310767) of the morphisms $f \circ p_{1}, g \circ p_{2} : X \times Y \to Z$ where $X \times Y$ is the [binary product](wikipedia://en/237868) of $X$ and $Y$ and $p_{1}$ and $p_{2}$ are the natural projections. This shows that pullbacks exist in any category with binary products and equalizers. In fact, by the [existence theorem for limits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Existence_theorem_for_limits), all finite limits exist in a category with binary products and equalizers; equivalently, all finite limits exist in a category with terminal object and pullbacks (by the fact that a binary product is equal to a pullback on the terminal object, and that an equalizer is a pullback involving a binary product).

#### Graphs of functions {#Graphs_of_functions}

A specific example of a pullback is given by the graph of a function. Suppose that $f \colon X \to Y$ is a function. The *graph* of $f$ is the set

$$\Gamma_f = \{(x, f(x)) \colon x \in X\} \subseteq X \times Y.$$

The graph can be reformulated as the pullback of $f$ and the identity function on $Y$. By definition, this pullback is

$$X \times_{f,Y,1_Y} Y = \{(x, y) \colon f(x) = 1_Y(y)\} = \{(x, y) \colon f(x) = y\} \subseteq X \times Y,$$

and this equals $\Gamma_f$.

### Fiber bundles {#Fiber_bundles}

Another example of a pullback comes from the theory of [fiber bundles](wikipedia://en/276526): given a bundle map $\pi : E \to B$ and a [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map) $f : X \to B$, the pullback (formed in the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) with [continuous maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29)) $X \times_{B} E$ is a fiber bundle over $X$ called the [pullback bundle](wikipedia://en/1665392). The associated commutative diagram is a morphism of fiber bundles. A special case is the pullback of two fiber bundles $E_{1}, E_{2} \to B$. In this case $E_{1} \times E_{2}$ is a fiber bundle over $B \times B$, and pulling back along the diagonal map $B \to B \times B$ gives a space homeomorphic (diffeomorphic) to $E_{1} \times_{B} E_{2}$, which is a fiber bundle over $B$. All statements here hold true for differentiable [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2119219) as well. Differentiable maps $f : M \to N$ and $g : P \to N$ are [transverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29) if and only if their product$M \times P \to N \times N$ is transverse to the diagonal of $N$.[^o12] Thus, the pullback of two transverse differentiable maps into the same [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) is also a differentiable manifold, and the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) of the pullback is the pullback of the tangent spaces along the differential maps.

### Preimages and intersections {#Preimages_and_intersections}

[Preimages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of sets under functions can be described as pullbacks as follows:

Suppose $f : A \to B$, $B_{0} \subseteq B$. Let $g$ be the [inclusion map](wikipedia://en/492516) $B_{0} \hookrightarrow B$. Then a pullback of $f$ and $g$ (in $\mathbf{Set}$) is given by the preimage $f^{-1}[B_{0}]$ together with the inclusion of the preimage in $A$

$$f^{-1}[B_{0}] \hookrightarrow A$$

and the restriction of $f$ to $f^{-1}[B_{0}]$

$$f^{-1}[B_{0}] \to B_{0}.$$

Because of this example, in a general category the pullback of a morphism $f$ and a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) $g$ can be thought of as the "preimage" under $f$ of the [subobject](wikipedia://en/540476) specified by $g$. Similarly, pullbacks of two monomorphisms can be thought of as the "intersection" of the two subobjects.

### Least common multiple {#Least_common_multiple}

Consider the multiplicative [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) of positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $\mathbf{Z}_{+}$ as a category with one object. In this category, the pullback of two positive integers $m$ and $n$ is just the pair $\left(\frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{m}, \frac{\operatorname{lcm}(m,n)}{n}\right)$, where the numerators are both the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961) of $m$ and $n$. The same pair is also the pushout.

### Schemes {#Schemes}

Since the pushout in the category of rings, i.e., the coproduct in a category of [algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring) over a ring *R*, is given by the [tensor product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_rings) over *R*, and [Spec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_spectrum) is a contravariant functor, the pullback of two affine schemes Spec(*A*) and Spec(*B*) over Spec(*R*), usually called fiber product, is given by Spec(*A* ⊗<sub>*R*</sub> *B*). By [gluing schemes](wikipedia://en/49016437), one can then construct the fiber product for any two [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) over an affine base, and from there show that it exists over an arbitrary base scheme.[^o16]

Fiber products of schemes are important in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) because they provide a notion of base change, for instance from a [variety](wikipedia://en/248808) over a field to a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634), as well as allowing to define [scheme-theoretic intersections](wikipedia://en/52580781) and fibers of a [morphism](wikipedia://en/7482797).

## Properties {#Properties}

- In any category with a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object) $T$, the pullback $X \times_{T} Y$ is just the ordinary [product](wikipedia://en/237868) $X \times Y$.[^o19]
- [Monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59538) are stable under pullback: if the arrow $f$ in the diagram is monic, then so is the arrow $p_{2}$. Similarly, if $g$ is monic, then so is $p_{1}$.[^o20]
- [Isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) are also stable, and hence, for example, $X \times_{X} Y \cong Y$ for any map $Y \to X$ (where the implied map $X \to X$ is the identity).
- In an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) all pullbacks exist,[^o21] and they preserve [kernels](wikipedia://en/142616), in the following sense: if

*Image: Categorical pullback.svg.*

is a pullback diagram, then the induced morphism $\ker(p_{2}) \to \ker(f)$ is an isomorphism,[^o22] and so is the induced morphism $\ker(p_{1}) \to \ker(g)$. Every pullback diagram thus gives rise to a commutative diagram of the following form, where all rows and columns are [exact](wikipedia://en/138215):

$$\begin{array}{ccccccc} &&&&0&&0\\ &&&&\downarrow&&\downarrow\\ &&&&L&=&L\\ &&&&\downarrow&&\downarrow\\ 0&\rightarrow&K&\rightarrow&P&\rightarrow&Y \\ &&\parallel&&\downarrow& & \downarrow\\ 0&\rightarrow&K&\rightarrow&X&\rightarrow&Z \end{array}$$

Furthermore, in an abelian category, if $X \to Z$ is an epimorphism, then so is its pullback $P \to Y$, and symmetrically: if $Y \to Z$ is an epimorphism, then so is its pullback $P \to X$.[^o23] In these situations, the pullback square is also a pushout square.[^o24]

- There is a natural isomorphism (*A*×<sub>*C*</sub>*B*)×<sub>*B*</sub> *D* ≅ *A*×<sub>*C*</sub>*D*. Explicitly, this means:
  - if maps *f* : *A* → *C*, *g* : *B* → *C* and *h* : *D* → *B* are given and
  - the pullback of *f* and *g* is given by *r* : *P* → *A* and *s* : *P* → *B*, and
  - the pullback of *s* and *h* is given by *t* : *Q* → *P* and *u* : *Q* → *D* ,
  - then the pullback of *f* and *gh* is given by *rt* : *Q* → *A* and *u* : *Q* → *D*.

Graphically this means that two pullback squares, placed side by side and sharing one morphism, form a larger pullback square when ignoring the inner shared morphism.

$$\begin{array}{ccccc} Q&\xrightarrow{t}&P& \xrightarrow{r} & A \\ \downarrow_{u} & & \downarrow_{s} & &\downarrow_{f}\\ D & \xrightarrow{h} & B &\xrightarrow{g} & C \end{array}$$

- Any category with pullbacks and products has equalizers.

## Weak pullbacks {#Weak_pullbacks}

A **weak pullback** of a [cospan](wikipedia://en/10318351) $X \to Z \leftarrow Y$ is a [cone](wikipedia://en/7533645) over the cospan that is only [weakly universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakly_universal_property), that is, the mediating morphism $u : Q \to P$ above is not required to be unique.
