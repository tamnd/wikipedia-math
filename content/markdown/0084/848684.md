# Cup product

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cup product"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), the **cup product** is a method of adjoining two [cocycles](wikipedia://en/2870898) of degree $p$ and $q$ to form a composite cocycle of degree $p+q$. This defines an associative (and distributive) graded commutative product operation in cohomology, turning the cohomology of a space $X$ into a [graded ring](wikipedia://en/103094), $H^*(X)$, called the [cohomology ring](wikipedia://en/1069723). The cup product was introduced in work of [J. W. Alexander](wikipedia://en/9848523), [Eduard Čech](wikipedia://en/859830) and [Hassler Whitney](wikipedia://en/391794) from 1935–1938, and, in full generality, by [Samuel Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501) in 1944.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In [singular cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_cohomology), the **cup product** is a construction giving a product on the [graded](wikipedia://en/103094) [cohomology ring](wikipedia://en/1069723) $H^*(X)$ of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$.

The construction starts with a product of [cochains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochain_%28algebraic_topology%29): if $\alpha^p$ is a $p$-cochain and $\beta^q$ is a $q$-cochain, then

$$(\alpha^p \smile \beta^q)(\sigma) = \alpha^p(\sigma \circ \iota_{0,1, ... p}) \cdot \beta^q(\sigma \circ \iota_{p,p+1, ..., p + q})$$

where $\sigma$ is a [singular](wikipedia://en/431041) $(p+q)$-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) and $\iota_S , S \subset \{0,1,...,p+q \}$ is the canonical [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) of the simplex spanned by $S$ into the $(p+q)$-simplex whose vertices are indexed by $\{0,...,p+q \}$.

Informally, $\sigma \circ \iota_{0,1, ..., p}$ is the $p$-th **front face** and $\sigma \circ \iota_{p, p+1, ..., p + q}$ is the $q$-th **back face** of $\sigma$, respectively.

The [coboundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coboundary) of the cup product of cochains $\alpha^p$ and $\beta^q$ is given by

$$\delta(\alpha^p \smile \beta^q) = \delta{\alpha^p} \smile \beta^q + (-1)^p(\alpha^p \smile \delta{\beta^q}).$$

The cup product of two cocycles is again a cocycle, and the product of a coboundary with a cocycle (in either order) is a coboundary. The cup product operation induces a bilinear operation on cohomology,

$$H^p(X) \times H^q(X) \to H^{p+q}(X).$$

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The cup product operation in cohomology satisfies the identity

$$\alpha^p \smile \beta^q = (-1)^{pq}(\beta^q \smile \alpha^p)$$

so that the corresponding multiplication is [graded-commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercommutative).

The cup product is [functorial](wikipedia://en/10987), in the following sense: if

$$f\colon X\to Y$$

is a continuous function, and

$$f^*\colon H^*(Y)\to H^*(X)$$

is the [induced homomorphism](wikipedia://en/50783621) in cohomology, then

$$f^*(\alpha \smile \beta) =f^*(\alpha) \smile f^*(\beta),$$

for all classes $\alpha,\beta$ in $H^*(Y)$. In other words, $f^*$ is a (graded) [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411).

:::

## Interpretation {#Interpretation}

It is possible to view the cup product $\smile \colon H^p(X) \times H^q(X) \to H^{p+q}(X)$ as a mapping induced from the following composition:

$$\displaystyle C^\bullet(X) \times C^\bullet(X) \to C^\bullet(X \times X) \overset{\Delta^*}{\to} C^\bullet(X)$$

in terms of the [chain complexes](wikipedia://en/138404) of $X$ and $X \times X$, where the first map is the [Künneth map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Künneth_formula) and the second is the map induced by the [diagonal](wikipedia://en/4606683) $\Delta \colon X \to X \times X$.

This composition passes to the quotient to give a well-defined map in terms of cohomology, and this is the cup product. This approach explains the existence of a cup product for cohomology but not for homology: $\Delta \colon X \to X \times X$ induces a map $\Delta^* \colon H^\bullet(X \times X) \to H^\bullet(X)$ but would also induce a map $\Delta_* \colon H_\bullet(X) \to H_\bullet(X \times X)$, which goes the wrong way round to allow us to define a product. This is however of use in defining the [cap product](wikipedia://en/5230810).

Bilinearity follows from this presentation of cup product, i.e. $(u_1 + u_2) \smile v = u_1 \smile v + u_2 \smile v$ and $u \smile (v_1 + v_2) = u \smile v_1 + u \smile v_2.$

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Cup products may be used to distinguish [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) from [wedges](wikipedia://en/684143) of spaces with identical cohomology groups. The space $X:= S^2\vee S^1\vee S^1$ has the same cohomology groups as the torus *T*, but with a different cup product. In the case of *X* the multiplication of the [cochains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cochain) associated to the copies of $S^1$ is degenerate, whereas in *T* multiplication in the first cohomology group can be used to decompose the torus as a 2-cell diagram, thus having product equal to **Z** (more generally *M* where this is the base module).

:::

## Other definitions {#Other_definitions}

### Cup product and differential forms {#Cup_product_and_differential_forms}

In [de Rham cohomology](wikipedia://en/221530), the cup product of [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms) is induced by the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product). In other words, the wedge product of two [closed](wikipedia://en/404181) differential forms belongs to the de Rham class of the cup product of the two original de Rham classes.

### Cup product and geometric intersections {#Cup_product_and_geometric_intersections}

*Image: Linking Number 1.svg.* The [linking number](wikipedia://en/451999) can be defined in terms of a non-vanishing cup product on the complement of a link. The complement of these two linked circles in $\mathbb{R}^3$ [deformation retracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract) to a wedge sum of a torus and 2-sphere, which has a non-vanishing cup product in degree 1.

For [oriented manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriented_manifold), there is a geometric heuristic that "the cup product is dual to intersections."[^o12][^o13]

Indeed, let $M$ be an oriented [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) of dimension $n$. If two submanifolds $A,B$ of codimension $i$ and $j$ intersect [transversely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversality_%28mathematics%29), then their intersection $A \cap B$ is again a submanifold of codimension $i+j$. By taking the images of the fundamental homology classes of these manifolds under inclusion, one can obtain a bilinear product on homology. This product is [Poincaré dual](wikipedia://en/540732) to the cup product, in the sense that taking the Poincaré pairings $[A]^*, [B]^* \in H^{i},H^{j}$ then there is the following equality:

$[A]^* \smile [B]^*=[A \cap B]^* \in H^{i+j}(X, \mathbb Z)$.[^o12]

Similarly, the [linking number](wikipedia://en/451999) can be defined in terms of intersections, shifting dimensions by 1, or alternatively in terms of a non-vanishing cup product on the complement of a link.

## Massey products {#Massey_products}

*Image: BorromeanRings.svg.* [Massey products](wikipedia://en/2676948) generalize cup product, allowing one to define "higher order linking numbers", the [Milnor invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor_invariants).

The cup product is a binary (2-ary) operation; one can define a ternary (3-ary) and higher order operation called the [Massey product](wikipedia://en/2676948), which generalizes the cup product. This is a higher order [cohomology operation](wikipedia://en/1226719), which is only partly defined (only defined for some triples).
