# Second-countable space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Second-countable space"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a **second-countable space**, also called a **completely separable space**, is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) whose topology has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [base](wikipedia://en/59220). More explicitly, a topological space $T$ is second-countable if there exists some countable collection $\mathcal{U} = \{U_i\}_{i=1}^{\infty}$ of [open](wikipedia://en/39358) subsets of $T$ such that any open subset of $T$ can be written as a union of elements of some subfamily of $\mathcal{U}$. A second-countable space is said to satisfy the **second axiom of countability**. Like other [countability axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countability_axiom), the property of being second-countable restricts the number of open subsets that a space can have.

:::

Many "[well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved)" spaces in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) are second-countable. For example, [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) (**R**<sup>*n*</sup>) with its usual topology is second-countable. Although the usual base of [open balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) is [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable), one can restrict this to the collection of all open balls with [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) radii and whose centers have rational coordinates. This restricted collection is countable and still forms a basis.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Second-countability is a stronger notion than [first-countability](wikipedia://en/904940). A space is first-countable if each point has a countable [local base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_base). Given a base for a topology and a point *x*, the set of all basis sets containing *x* forms a local base at *x*. Thus, if one has a countable base for a topology then one has a countable local base at every point, and hence every second-countable space is also a first-countable space. However any uncountable [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061) is first-countable but not second-countable.

Second-countability implies certain other topological properties. Specifically, every second-countable space is [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) (has a countable [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29) subset) and [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480) (every [open cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cover) has a countable subcover). The reverse implications do not hold. For example, the [lower limit topology](wikipedia://en/310517) on the real line is first-countable, separable, and Lindelöf, but not second-countable. For [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018), however, the properties of being second-countable, separable, and Lindelöf are all equivalent.[^o4] Therefore, the lower limit topology on the real line is not metrizable.

In second-countable spaces—as in metric spaces—[compactness](wikipedia://en/6042), sequential compactness, and countable compactness are all equivalent properties.

[Urysohn's metrization theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urysohn's_metrization_theorem) states that every second-countable, [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [regular space](wikipedia://en/53058) is [metrizable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrizable). It follows that every such space is [completely normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_normal_space) as well as [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact). Second-countability is therefore a rather restrictive property on a topological space, requiring only a separation axiom to imply metrizability.

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

- A continuous, [open](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map) [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a second-countable space is second-countable.
- Every [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of a second-countable space is second-countable.
- [Quotients](wikipedia://en/237213) of second-countable spaces need not be second-countable; however, *open* quotients always are.
- Any countable [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space) of a second-countable space is second-countable, although uncountable products need not be.
- The topology of a second-countable T<sub>1</sub> space has [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) less than or equal to *c* (the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901)).
- Any base for a second-countable space has a countable subfamily which is still a base.
- Every collection of disjoint open sets in a second-countable space is countable.
- A metric space that has every uncountable subset also have a limit point is second-countable.

## Examples {#Examples}

- Consider the disjoint countable union $X = [0,1] \cup [2,3] \cup [4,5] \cup \dots \cup [2k, 2k+1] \cup \dotsb$. Define an equivalence relation and a [quotient topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_topology) by identifying the left ends of the intervals - that is, identify 0 ~ 2 ~ 4 ~ … ~ 2k and so on. *X* is second-countable, as a countable union of second-countable spaces. However, *X*/~ is not first-countable at the coset of the identified points and hence also not second-countable.
- The above space is not homeomorphic to the same set of equivalence classes endowed with the obvious metric: i.e. regular Euclidean distance for two points in the same interval, and the sum of the distances to the left hand point for points not in the same interval -- yielding a strictly coarser topology than the above space. It is a separable metric space (consider the set of rational points), and hence is second-countable.
- The [long line](wikipedia://en/338403) is not second-countable, but is first-countable.
