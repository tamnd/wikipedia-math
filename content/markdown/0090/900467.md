# Kazimierz Ajdukiewicz

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kazimierz Ajdukiewicz"}

**Kazimierz Ajdukiewicz** (; 12 December 1890 – 12 April 1963) was a Polish [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosopher) and [logician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logician), a prominent figure in the [Lwów–Warsaw school of logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lwów–Warsaw_school_of_logic). He taught at the [University of Lwów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Lviv), [Adam Mickiewicz University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Mickiewicz_University), and the [University of Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Warsaw).

:::

He originated many novel ideas in [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107). Among these was [categorial grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorial_grammar), a highly flexible framework for the analysis of [natural language](wikipedia://en/21173) [syntax](wikipedia://en/26860) and, indirectly, semantics that remains a major influence on work in [formal linguistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_linguistics). Ajdukiewicz's fields of research centered on [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858) and the [philosophy of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy_of_science).

## Life and career {#Life_and_career}

Ajdukiewicz was born on 12 December in 1890 in [Tarnopol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tarnopol) (now [Ternopil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ternopil) in [Ukraine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukraine)), then part of the [Kingdom of Galicia and Lodomeria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Galicia_and_Lodomeria), within [Austria-Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austria-Hungary). His father, Bronisław, was a senior civil servant, his mother, Magdalena, hailed from [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866).[^o4] He started his education in [Kraków](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kraków) but his family soon relocated to [Lwów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lwów). In 1908, Ajdukiewicz graduated from the C.K. Franciszek Józef [Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28school%29) in Lwów.[^o5][^o4]

In the same year, Ajdukiewicz matriculated at the [Jan Kazimierz University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Kazimierz_University) (now the [University of Lviv](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Lviv)) to study philosophy, physics and mathematics. His teachers included [Kazimierz Twardowski](wikipedia://en/1022980), [Wacław Sierpiński](wikipedia://en/34122), [Marian Smoluchowski](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marian_Smoluchowski), [Jan Łukasiewicz](wikipedia://en/305064) and [Władysław Witwicki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Władysław_Witwicki).[^o6] In that time, an intellectual cirlce formed around Twardowski, later known as the [Lwów–Warsaw school of logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lwów–Warsaw_school_of_logic), and it was within this environment that Ajdukiewicz matured intellectually.[^o4]

In 1912, he earned his [doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctorate) under Twardowski based on his thesis on [Immanuel Kant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanuel_Kant)'s philosophy of space entitled *O stosunku aprioryzmu przestrzeni u Kanta do kwestii genezy wyobrażenia przestrzeni* (On the Relation Between Kant’s Apriorism of Space and the Question of the Origin of the Representation of Space).[^o4] In 1913–1914, Ajdukiewicz continued his education at the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen) where he attended lectures in philosophy by [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl) and [Leonard Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Nelson), and in mathematics and logic by [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302).[^o4]

After the outbreak of [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I), Ajdukiewciz fought in the [Austro-Hungarian Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austro-Hungarian_Army) on the Italian front. In 1918, when Poland regained its independence, he enlisted in the [Polish Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Armed_Forces) and took part in the [Polish-Soviet War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish-Soviet_War) (1919–1921) as an artillery officer. In 1920, he left the army to pursue his scientific career.[^o7] In 1921, he obtained his [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation) at the [University of Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Warsaw) and from 1928 until 1939, he taught at his alma mater, the Jan Kazimierz University.[^o7] In 1923, he participated in the First Congress of Polish Philosophy held in Lwów.[^o8] In 1934, Ajdukiewicz obtained the title of professor.[^o4] In 1938–1939, he served as the Chairman of the [Polish Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Philosophical_Society) ([Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language): *Polskie Towarzystwo Filozoficzne*).[^o9]

Under [German occupation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invasion_of_Poland) during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), Ajdukiewicz avoided falling victim to the [Massacre of Lwów professors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massacre_of_Lwów_professors), and until the end of the war, he worked as an accountant simultaneously conducting clandestine teaching for Polish youth.[^o9] In 1945, he assumed the Chair of the Theory and Methodology of Science (later renamed the Chair of Logic) at the [Adam Mickiewicz University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_Mickiewicz_University) in [Poznań](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poznań). Between 1948 and 1952, he served as the rector of that university.[^o4] As a scientific official, Ajdukiewicz objected to the restriction of self-governance of universities and the centralization of the management of Polish science.[^o7]

In 1954, Ajdukiewicz moved to Warsaw where he taught logic at the [University of Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Warsaw). In 1957–1961, he assumed the Chair of Logic in the Department of Philosophy of the University of Warsaw. He also co-organized the Department of Logic at the Institute of Philosophy and Sociology of the [Polish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Academy_of_Sciences).[^o4] He was one of the founders of the journal *[Studia Logica](wikipedia://en/27777526)* in its current form, editing it from 1953 until his death.[^o10]

Ajdukiewicz published around 200 scientific papers, including 17 books, and supervised 8 doctoral students. In 1962, he received an [honorary doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_doctorate) from the [University of Clermont-Ferrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Clermont-Ferrand).[^o10][^o4] He died suddenly of a heart-related condition on 12 April in 1963 and was buried in Warsaw at the [Powązki Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powązki_Cemetery).[^o11]

## Work {#Work}

In his early [epistemological system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology), which he called [Radical Conventionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventionalism#Epistemology), Ajdukiewicz analyzed any [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language) as a set of expressions or sentences, with inferential *rules of meaning* that specify the relation of one expression to another, or to external data. There are three kinds of rules: Axiomatic, Deductive, and Empirical. Following the rules, one can map all knowable sentences of a language. However, some languages' vocabularies can produce *disconnected* sentences, which are only partly mapped by the *meaning rules*. Therefore, when one uses a language, even scientific one, a *conceptual apparatus*, an untranslatable set of meanings, is needed too, and with it a choice of the problems to be settled. For this reason the theory is a form of [conventionalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conventionalism), and it is radical because even simple experiential reports are exposed to these language-wide considerations. Ajdukiewicz discarded this theory as the 1930s progressed.

Perhaps the most important contribution of Ajdukiewicz was the introduction of [categorial grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Categorial_grammar), which he developed in the 1930s. It comprises a group of [syntactic](wikipedia://en/26860) [formalisms](wikipedia://en/18020716) for [natural languages](wikipedia://en/21173) that rest on the core idea that syntactic units combine through [function](wikipedia://en/185427)–[argument relations](wikipedia://en/32842052). Ajdukiewicz’s formal treatment of syntax drew on [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl)’s concept of [pure logical grammar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_grammar), later given a formal structure by [Rudolph Carnap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolph_Carnap). It reflects an evolution of the longstanding notion that a universal logical grammar underlies all languages, and its central idea is that syntactic categories can be interchanged — which gives categorial grammar its name.[^o13]

Ajdukiewicz, alongside [Tadeusz Kotarbiński](wikipedia://en/1022909), is also considered one of the leading scholars in [semiotics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotics) and the [methodology of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method), as well as in the application of logic to philosophical inquiry.[^o4]
