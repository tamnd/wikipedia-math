# Begriffsschrift

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Begriffsschrift"}

***Begriffsschrift*** (German for, roughly, concept-writing) is a book on [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) by [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416), published in 1879, and the [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102) set out in that book.

:::

*Begriffsschrift* is usually translated as *concept writing* or *concept notation*; the full title of the book identifies it as "a [formula](wikipedia://en/164040) [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language), modeled on that of [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), for pure [thought](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thought)." Frege's motivation for developing his formal approach to logic resembled [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281)'s motivation for his *[calculus ratiocinator](wikipedia://en/876255)*. (Despite that, in the foreword Frege clearly denied that he achieved this aim, and also that his main aim would be constructing an ideal language like Leibniz's, which Frege declared to be a quite hard and idealistic—though not impossible—task.) Frege went on to employ his logical calculus in his research on the [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358), carried out over the next quarter-century. This is the first work in [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211), a field that British and American philosophers later further developed.

## Notation and the system {#Notation_and_the_system}

The calculus contains the first appearance of quantified variables, and is essentially classical bivalent [second-order logic](wikipedia://en/341428) with identity. It is bivalent in that sentences or formulas denote either True or False; second-order because it includes relation variables in addition to object variables and allows quantification over both. The modifier "with identity" specifies that the language includes the identity relation, =. Frege stated that his book was his version of a [characteristica universalis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristica_universalis), a Leibnizian concept that would be applied in mathematics.[^n1]

In the first chapter, Frege defines basic ideas and notation: *judgement*, *[conditionality](wikipedia://en/658808)*, *[negation](wikipedia://en/161019)*, *identity of content*, *functions* and *[generality](wikipedia://en/76174)*.

Frege presents his calculus in an idiosyncratic[^n2] two-dimensional [notation](wikipedia://en/277184), based on [negation](wikipedia://en/161019), [material conditional](wikipedia://en/658808) and [universal quantification](wikipedia://en/76174). Other [connectives](wikipedia://en/18153) and [existential quantification](wikipedia://en/91420) are provided as definitions. Parentheses are not needed.

The conditional ($B \to A$) is expressed by

*Image: Begriffsschrift connective2.svg.*

. Regarding its meaning Frege wrote:[^n3]

"If A and B stand for contents that can become judgments, there are the following four possibilities:

1. A is affirmed and B is affirmed;
1. A is affirmed and B is denied;
1. A is denied and B is affirmed;
1. A is denied and B is denied.

Now

*Image: BS-05-Kondicionaliskis-svg.svg.*

stands for the judgment that *the third of those possibilities does not take place, but one of the three others does.*"

In hindsight, one can say that in *Begriffsschrift*, formulas are represented by their [parse trees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parse_tree).

::: {#o15 .example cue=inline-head name="Example"}

**Example**

Proposition 59[^n4] is written in modern notation as[^n5]

$$\vdash g \left(b \right) \to \left(\lnot f \left(b\right) \to \lnot \left(\forall a\right)\left(g \left(a\right) \to f \left(a\right)\right)\right).$$

The [parse tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parse_tree) is

```
     →
    / \
g(b)   →
      / \
     ¬   ¬
     |   |
  f(b)   ∀a
         |
         →
        / \
    g(a)   f(a)
```

... in left-to-right horizontal layout

```
→ ─── → ─── ¬ ─── ∀a ─── → ─── f(a)
 \     \                  \
  g(b)  ¬                  g(a)
         \
          f(b)
```

In *Begriffsschrift*, proposition 59 is represented as[^n4]

```
├─┬─┬─┬─a̲─┬─── f(a)
  │ │     └─── g(a)
  │ └──────┬── f(b)
  └─────────── g(b)
```

:::

## The calculus in Frege's work {#The_calculus_in_Frege's_work}

In the second chapter, Frege declared nine of his propositions to be [axioms](wikipedia://en/928), and justified them by arguing informally that, given their intended meanings, they express self-evident truths. Re-expressed in contemporary notation, these axioms are:

1. $$\vdash A \to (B \to A)$$
1. $$\vdash [ A \to (B \to C) ] \to [ (A \to B) \to (A \to C) ]$$
1. $$\vdash [ D \to (B \to A) ] \to [ B \to (D \to A) ]$$
1. $$\vdash (B \to A) \to (\lnot A \to \lnot B)$$
1. $$\vdash \lnot \lnot A \to A$$
1. $$\vdash A \to \lnot \lnot A$$
1. $$\vdash (c = d) \to (f(c) = f(d))$$
1. $$\vdash c = c$$
1. $$\vdash \forall a \, f(a) \to f(c)$$

These are propositions 1, 2, 8, 28, 31, 41, 52, 54, and 58 in the *Begriffschrifft*. (1)–(3) govern [material implication](wikipedia://en/658808), (4)–(6) [negation](wikipedia://en/161019), (7) and (8) identity, and (9) the [universal quantifier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_quantifier). (7) expresses [Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281)'s [indiscernibility of identicals](wikipedia://en/630398), and (8) asserts that identity is a [reflexive relation](wikipedia://en/200458).

All other propositions are deduced from (1)–(9) by invoking any of the following [inference rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference_rule):

- [Modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900) allows to infer $\vdash B$ from $\vdash A \to B$ and $\vdash A$;
- The [rule of generalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalization_%28logic%29) allows to infer $\vdash P \to \forall x A(x)$ from $\vdash P \to A(x)$ if *x* does not occur in *P*;
- The [rule of substitution](wikipedia://en/10983#Rules_of_inference), which Frege does not state explicitly. This rule is much harder to articulate precisely than the two preceding rules, and Frege invokes it in ways that are not obviously legitimate.

The main results of the third chapter, titled "Parts from a general series theory," concern what is now called the [ancestral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancestral_relation) of a relation *R*. "*a* is an *R*-ancestor of *b*" is written "*aR*\**b*".

Frege applied the results from the *Begriffsschrift*, including those on the ancestral of a relation, in his later work *[The Foundations of Arithmetic](wikipedia://en/8983001)*. Thus, if we take *xRy* to be the relation *y* = *x* + 1, then 0*R*\**y* is the predicate "*y* is a natural number." (133) says that if *x*, *y*, and *z* are [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474), then one of the following must hold: *x* \< *y*, *x* = *y*, or *y* \< *x*. This is the so-called "law of [trichotomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichotomy_%28mathematics%29)".

## Influence on other works {#Influence_on_other_works}

For a careful recent study of how the *Begriffsschrift* was reviewed in the German mathematical literature, see Vilko (1998).[^n6] Some reviewers, especially [Ernst Schröder](wikipedia://en/448015), were on the whole favorable. All work in formal logic subsequent to the *Begriffsschrift* is indebted to it, because its second-order logic was the first formal logic capable of representing a fair bit of mathematics and natural language.

Some vestige of Frege's notation survives in the "[turnstile](wikipedia://en/7954712)" symbol $\vdash$ derived from his "Urteilsstrich" (*judging/inferring stroke*) │ and "Inhaltsstrich" (i.e. *content stroke*) ──. Frege used these symbols in the *Begriffsschrift* in the unified form ├─ for declaring that a proposition is true. In his later "Grundgesetze" he revises slightly his interpretation of the ├─ symbol.

In "Begriffsschrift" the "Definitionsdoppelstrich" (i.e. *definition double stroke*) │├─ indicates that a proposition is a definition. Furthermore, the negation sign $\neg$ can be read as a combination of the horizontal *Inhaltsstrich* with a vertical negation stroke. This negation symbol was reintroduced by [Arend Heyting](wikipedia://en/1180911)[^n7] in 1930 to distinguish [intuitionistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intuitionistic) from classical negation. It also appears in [Gerhard Gentzen](wikipedia://en/46027)'s doctoral dissertation.

In the *[Tractatus Logico Philosophicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tractatus_Logico_Philosophicus)*, [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741) pays homage to Frege by employing the term *Begriffsschrift* as a synonym for logical formalism.

Frege's 1892 essay, "[On Sense and Reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_Sense_and_Reference)," recants some of the conclusions of the *Begriffsschrifft* about identity (denoted in mathematics by the "=" sign). In particular, he rejects the "Begriffsschrift" view that the identity predicate expresses a relationship between names, in favor of the conclusion that it expresses [a relationship between the objects](wikipedia://en/90446) that are [denoted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denotation_%28semiotics%29) by those names.
