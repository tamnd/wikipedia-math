# First-countable space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="First-countable space"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **first-countable space** is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) satisfying the "first [axiom of countability](wikipedia://en/346611)". Specifically, a space $X$ is said to be first-countable if each point has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [neighbourhood basis](wikipedia://en/1714290#neighbourhood_basis) (local base). That is, for each point $x$ in $X$ there exists a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $N_1, N_2, \ldots$ of [neighbourhoods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood_%28topology%29) of $x$ such that for any neighbourhood $N$ of $x$ there exists an integer $i$ with $N_i$ [contained in](wikipedia://en/27631) $N.$ Since every neighborhood of any point contains an [open](wikipedia://en/39358) neighborhood of that point, the [neighbourhood basis](wikipedia://en/1714290) can be chosen [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) to consist of open neighborhoods.

:::

## Examples and counterexamples {#Examples_and_counterexamples}

The majority of 'everyday' spaces in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) are first-countable. In particular, every [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) is first-countable. To see this, note that the set of [open balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) centered at $x$ with radius $2^{-n}$ for all natural numbers $n$ form a countable local base at $x$.

An example of a space that is not first-countable is the [cofinite topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofinite_topology) on an [uncountable set](wikipedia://en/32248) (such as the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line)). More generally, the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) on an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) over an uncountable field is not first-countable.

Another counterexample is the [ordinal space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_space) $\omega_1 + 1 = \left[0, \omega_1\right]$ where $\omega_1$ is the [first uncountable ordinal](wikipedia://en/20860540) number. The element $\omega_1$ is a [limit point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of the subset $\left[0, \omega_1\right)$ even though no sequence of elements in $\left[0, \omega_1\right)$ has the element $\omega_1$ as its limit. In particular, the point $\omega_1$ in the space $\omega_1 + 1 = \left[0, \omega_1\right]$ does not have a countable local base. Since $\omega_1$ is the only such point, however, the subspace $\omega_1 = \left[0, \omega_1\right)$ is first-countable.

The [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) $\mathbb{R} / \mathbb{N}$ where the natural numbers on the real line are identified as a single point is not first countable.[^o4] However, this space has the property that for any subset $A$ and every element $x$ in the closure of $A,$ there is a sequence in $A$ converging to $x.$ A space with this sequence property is sometimes called a [Fréchet–Urysohn space](wikipedia://en/28480560).

First-countability is strictly weaker than [second-countability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second-countability). Every [second-countable space](wikipedia://en/901459) is first-countable, but any uncountable [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061) is first-countable but not second-countable.

## Properties {#Properties}

One of the most important properties of first-countable spaces is that given a subset $A,$ a point $x$ lies in the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of $A$ if and only if there exists a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $\left(x_n\right)_{n=1}^{\infty}$ in $A$ that [converges](wikipedia://en/285773) to $x.$ (In other words, every first-countable space is a [Fréchet-Urysohn space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fréchet-Urysohn_space) and thus also a [sequential space](wikipedia://en/4668395).) This has consequences for [limits](wikipedia://en/285759) and [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28topology%29). In particular, if $f$ is a function on a first-countable space, then $f$ has a limit $L$ at the point $x$ if and only if for every sequence $x_n \to x,$ where $x_n \neq x$ for all $n,$ we have $f\left(x_n\right) \to L.$ Also, if $f$ is a function on a first-countable space, then $f$ is continuous if and only if whenever $x_n \to x,$ then $f\left(x_n\right) \to f(x).$

In first-countable spaces, [sequential compactness](wikipedia://en/8293820) and [countable compactness](wikipedia://en/8293816) are equivalent properties. However, there exist examples of sequentially compact, first-countable spaces that are not compact (these are necessarily not metrizable spaces). One such space is the [ordinal space](wikipedia://en/213650) $\left[0, \omega_1\right).$ Every first-countable space is [compactly generated](wikipedia://en/899382).

Every [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of a first-countable space is first-countable. Any countable [product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_space) of a first-countable space is first-countable, although uncountable products need not be.
