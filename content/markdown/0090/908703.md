# Inverse probability

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Inverse probability"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), **inverse probability** is an old term for the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of an unobserved variable.

:::

Today, the problem of determining an unobserved variable (by whatever method) is called [inferential statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferential_statistics). The method of inverse probability (assigning a probability distribution to an unobserved variable) is called [Bayesian probability](wikipedia://en/4890), the distribution of data given the unobserved variable is the [likelihood function](wikipedia://en/44968) (which does not by itself give a probability distribution for the parameter), and the distribution of an unobserved variable, given both data and a [prior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prior_distribution), is the [posterior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_distribution). The development of the field and terminology from "inverse probability" to "Bayesian probability" is described by (Fienberg 2006).

*Image: Youngronaldfisher2.JPG.* Ronald Fisher

The term "inverse probability" appears in an 1837 paper of [De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289), in reference to [Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace)'s method of probability (developed in a 1774 paper, which independently discovered and popularized Bayesian methods, and an 1812 book), though the term "inverse probability" does not occur in these.[^n1] Fisher uses the term in (Fisher 1922), referring to "the fundamental paradox of inverse probability" as the source of the confusion between statistical terms that refer to the true value to be estimated, and the actual value arrived at by the estimation method, which is subject to error. Later Jeffreys uses the term in his defense of the methods of Bayes and Laplace, in (Jeffreys 1939). The term "Bayesian", which displaced "inverse probability", was introduced by [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) in 1950.[^n2] Inverse probability, variously interpreted, was the dominant approach to statistics until the development of [frequentism](wikipedia://en/10869) in the early 20th century by [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824), [Jerzy Neyman](wikipedia://en/2386106) and [Egon Pearson](wikipedia://en/1643759).[^n3] Following the development of frequentism, the terms [frequentist](wikipedia://en/15537745) and [Bayesian](wikipedia://en/404412) developed to contrast these approaches, and became common in the 1950s.

## Details {#Details}

In modern terms, given a probability distribution *p*(*x*|θ) for an observable quantity *x* conditional on an unobserved variable θ, the "inverse probability" is the [posterior distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posterior_distribution) *p*(θ|*x*), which depends both on the likelihood function (the inversion of the probability distribution) and a prior distribution. The distribution *p*(*x*|θ) itself is called the **direct probability**.

The *inverse probability problem* (in the 18th and 19th centuries) was the problem of estimating a parameter from experimental data in the experimental sciences, especially [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) and [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology). A simple example would be the problem of estimating the position of a star in the sky (at a certain time on a certain date) for purposes of [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation). Given the data, one must estimate the true position (probably by averaging). This problem would now be considered one of [inferential statistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inferential_statistics).

The terms "direct probability" and "inverse probability" were in use until the middle part of the 20th century, when the terms "[likelihood function](wikipedia://en/44968)" and "posterior distribution" became prevalent.
