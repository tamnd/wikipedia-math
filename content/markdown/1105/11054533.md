# Archard equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Archard equation"}

The **Archard wear equation** is a simple [model](wikipedia://en/20590) used to describe sliding [wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wear) and is based on the theory of [asperity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asperity_%28materials_science%29) contact. The Archard equation was developed much later than **Reye's hypothesis** (sometimes also known as **energy dissipative hypothesis**), though both came to the [same physical conclusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_discovery), that the volume of the removed debris due to [wear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wear) is proportional to the work done by friction forces. [Theodor Reye](wikipedia://en/27942014)'s model[^o7][^o8] became popular in Europe and it is still taught in university courses of [applied mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_mechanics).[^o16] Until recently, Reye's theory of 1860 has, however, been totally ignored in English and American literature[^o16] where subsequent works by [Ragnar Holm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragnar_Holm)[^o9][^o11][^o12] and [John Frederick Archard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Frederick_Archard) are usually cited.[^o17] In 1960, Mikhail Mikhailovich Khrushchov and Mikhail Alekseevich Babichev published a [similar model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_discovery) as well.[^o14] In modern literature, the relation is therefore also known as **Reye–Archard–Khrushchov wear law**. In 2022, the steady-state Archard wear equation was extended into the running-in regime using the [bearing ratio curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbott-Firestone_curve) representing the initial [surface topography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_topography).[^o18]

:::

## Equation {#Equation}

$$Q = \frac {KWL}H$$

where:[^o10]

*Q* is the total volume of wear debris produced

*K* is a dimensionless constant

*W* is the total normal load

*L* is the sliding distance

*H* is the [hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardness) of the softest contacting surfaces

Note that $WL$ is proportional to the work done by the friction forces as described by Reye's hypothesis.

Also, K is obtained from experimental results and depends on several parameters. Among them are surface quality, [chemical affinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_affinity) between the material of two surfaces, surface hardness process, [heat transfer](wikipedia://en/184726) between two surfaces and others.

## Derivation {#Derivation}

The equation can be derived by first examining the behavior of a single asperity.

The local load $\, \delta W$, supported by an asperity, assumed to have a circular cross-section with a radius $\, a$, is:[^o6]

$$\delta W = P \pi {a^2} \,\!$$

where *P* is the yield pressure for the asperity, assumed to be deforming plastically. *P* will be close to the indentation [hardness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardness), *H*, of the asperity.

If the volume of wear debris, $\, \delta V$, for a particular asperity is a hemisphere sheared off from the asperity, it follows that:

$$\delta V = \frac 2 3 \pi a^3$$

This fragment is formed by the material having slid a distance 2*a*

Hence, $\, \delta Q$, the wear volume of material produced from this asperity per unit distance moved is:

$\delta Q = \frac {\delta V} {2a} = \frac {\pi a^2} 3 \equiv \frac {\delta W} {3P} \approx \frac {\delta W} {3H}$ making the approximation that $\,P \approx H$

However, not all asperities will have had material removed when sliding distance 2*a*. Therefore, the total wear debris produced per unit distance moved, $\, Q$ will be lower than the ratio of *W* to *3H*. This is accounted for by the addition of a dimensionless constant *K*, which also incorporates the factor 3 above. These operations produce the Archard equation as given above. Archard interpreted *K* factor as a probability of forming wear debris from asperity encounters.[^o13] Typically for 'mild' wear, *K* ≈ 10<sup>−8</sup>, whereas for 'severe' wear, *K* ≈ 10<sup>−2</sup>. Recently,[^o15] it has been shown that there exists a critical [length scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_scale) that controls the wear debris formation at the asperity level. This length scale defines a critical junction size, where bigger junctions produce debris, while smaller ones deform plastically.
