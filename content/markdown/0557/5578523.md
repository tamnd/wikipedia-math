# Witt group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Witt group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Witt group** of a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), named after [Ernst Witt](wikipedia://en/2619911), is an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) whose elements are represented by [symmetric](wikipedia://en/1803375) [bilinear forms](wikipedia://en/449477) over the field.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Fix a field *k* of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) not equal to 2. All [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) will be assumed to be finite-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267). Two spaces equipped with [symmetric bilinear forms](wikipedia://en/1803375) are **equivalent** if one can be obtained from the other by adding a [metabolic quadratic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metabolic_quadratic_space), that is, zero or more copies of a [hyperbolic plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_plane_%28quadratic_forms%29), the non-degenerate two-dimensional symmetric bilinear form with a norm 0 vector.[^o4] Each class is represented by the [core form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Core_form) of a [Witt decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Witt_decomposition).[^o5]

The **Witt group of *k*** is the abelian group *W*(*k*) of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of non-degenerate symmetric bilinear forms, with the group operation corresponding to the [orthogonal direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_direct_sum) of forms. It is additively generated by the classes of one-dimensional forms.[^o6] Although classes may contain spaces of different dimension, the parity of the dimension is constant across a class and so rk: *W*(*k*) → **Z**/2**Z** is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396).[^o14]

The elements of finite [order](wikipedia://en/493527) in the Witt group have order a power of 2;[^o7][^o8] the [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) is the [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28group_theory%29) of the [functorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functorial) map from *W*(*k*) to *W*(*k*<sup>py</sup>), where *k*<sup>py</sup> is the [Pythagorean closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_closure) of *k*;[^o9] it is generated by the [Pfister forms](wikipedia://en/7204602) $\langle\!\langle w \rangle\!\rangle = \langle 1, -w \rangle$ with $w$ a non-zero sum of squares.[^o10] If *k* is not [formally real](wikipedia://en/1067223), then the Witt group is [torsion](wikipedia://en/3568695), with [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent_%28group_theory%29) a power of 2.[^o16] The **height** of the field *k* is the exponent of the torsion in the Witt group, if this is finite, or ∞ otherwise.[^o11]

:::

::: {#o13 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Witt ring (forms)"}

## Ring structure {#Ring_structure}

The Witt group of *k* can be given a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) structure, by using the [tensor product of quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/10137896) to define the ring product. This is sometimes called the **Witt ring** *W*(*k*), though the term "Witt ring" is often also used for a completely different ring of [Witt vectors](wikipedia://en/5573710).

To discuss the structure of this ring one assumes that *k* is of characteristic not equal to 2, so that one may identify symmetric bilinear forms and quadratic forms.

The kernel of the rank mod 2 homomorphism is a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928), *I*, of the Witt ring[^o14] termed the *fundamental ideal*.[^o15] The [ring homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/26411) from *W*(*k*) to **Z** correspond to the [field orderings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_ordering) of *k*, by taking [signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_%28quadratic_form%29) with respective to the ordering.[^o15] The Witt ring is a [Jacobson ring](wikipedia://en/4607512).[^o16] It is a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) if and only if there are finitely many [square classes](wikipedia://en/36362488); that is, if the squares in *k* form a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of finite [index](wikipedia://en/230581) in the multiplicative group of *k*.[^o17]

If *k* is not formally real, the fundamental ideal is the only prime ideal of *W*[^o18] and consists precisely of the [nilpotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_element);[^o16] *W* is a [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) and has [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) 0.[^o19]

If *k* is real, then the nilpotent elements are precisely those of finite additive order, and these in turn are the forms all of whose signatures are 0;[^o20] *W* has Krull dimension 1.[^o19]

If *k* is a real [Pythagorean field](wikipedia://en/31032774) then the [zero-divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-divisor) of *W* are the elements for which some signature is 0; otherwise, the zero-divisors are exactly the fundamental ideal.[^o7][^o21]

If *k* is an ordered field with positive cone *P* then [Sylvester's law of inertia](wikipedia://en/1127460) holds for quadratic forms over *k* and the *signature* defines a ring homomorphism from *W*(*k*) to **Z**, with kernel a prime ideal *K*<sub>*P*</sub>. These prime ideals are in [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) with the orderings *X<sub>k</sub>* of *k* and constitute the minimal prime ideal [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) MinSpec *W*(*k*) of *W*(*k*). The bijection is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) between MinSpec *W*(*k*) with the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) and the set of orderings *X*<sub>*k*</sub> with the [Harrison topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harrison_topology).[^o22]

The *n*-th power of the fundamental ideal is additively generated by the *n*-fold [Pfister forms](wikipedia://en/7204602).[^o23]

## Examples {#Examples}

- The Witt ring of **C**, and indeed any [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) or [quadratically closed field](wikipedia://en/36823185), is **Z**/2**Z**.[^o26]
- The Witt ring of **R** is **Z**.[^o26]
- The Witt ring of a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) **F**<sub>*q*</sub> with *q* [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) is **Z**/4**Z** if *q* ≡ 3 mod 4 and [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) (**Z**/2**Z**)\[*F\**/*F\**<sup>2</sup>\] if *q* ≡ 1 mod 4.[^o27]
- The Witt ring of a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) with [maximal ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_ideal_of_a_valuation) of [norm](wikipedia://en/460672) congruent to 1 modulo 4 is isomorphic to the group ring (**Z**/2**Z**)\[*V*\] where *V* is the [Klein 4-group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klein_4-group).[^o28]
- The Witt ring of a local field with maximal ideal of norm congruent to 3 modulo 4 is (**Z**/4**Z**)\[*C*<sub>2</sub>\] where *C*<sub>2</sub> is a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 2.[^o29]
- The Witt ring of **Q**<sub>2</sub> is of order 32 and is given by[^o30]

$$\mathbf{Z}_8[s,t]/\langle2s,2t,s^2,t^2,st-4\rangle.$$

:::

## Invariants {#Invariants}

Certain invariants of a quadratic form can be regarded as functions on Witt classes. Dimension mod 2 is a function on classes: the [discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discriminant_of_a_quadratic_form) is also well-defined. The [Hasse invariant of a quadratic form](wikipedia://en/3028413) is again, a well-defined function on Witt classes with values in the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) of the field of definition.[^o32]

### Rank and discriminant {#Rank_and_discriminant}

A ring is defined over *K*, *Q*(*K*), as a set of pairs (*d*, *e*) with *d* in *K\**/*K\**<sup>hair2</sup> and *e* in **Z**/2**Z**. Addition and multiplication are defined by:

$$(d_1,e_1) + (d_2,e_2) = ((-1)^{e_1e_2}d_1d_2, e_1+e_2)$$

$$(d_1,e_1)\cdot(d_2,e_2)=(d_1^{e_2}d_2^{e_1},e_1e_2).$$

Then there is a [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) ring homomorphism from *W*(*K*) to this obtained by mapping a class to discriminant and rank mod 2. The kernel is *I*<sup>2</sup>.[^o34] The elements of *Q* may be regarded as classifying graded quadratic extensions of *K*.[^o35]

### Brauer–Wall group {#Brauer–Wall_group}

The triple of discriminant, rank mod 2 and Hasse invariant defines a map from *W*(*K*) to the [Brauer–Wall group](wikipedia://en/36842500) BW(*K*).[^o37]

## Witt ring of a local field {#Witt_ring_of_a_local_field}

Let *K* be a complete [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) with valuation *v*, uniformiser π and residue field *k* of characteristic not equal to 2. There is an [injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) *W*(*k*) → *W*(*K*) which lifts the diagonal form ⟨*a*<sub>1</sub>,...*a*<sub>*n*</sub>⟩ to ⟨*u*<sub>1</sub>,...*u*<sub>*n*</sub>⟩ where *u*<sub>*i*</sub> is a unit of *K* with image *a*<sub>*i*</sub> in *k*. This yields

$$W(K)=W(k)\oplus\langle\pi\rangle\cdot W(k)$$

identifying *W*(*k*) with its image in *W*(*K*).[^o39]

::: {#o41 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Symbol ring"}

## Witt ring of a number field {#Witt_ring_of_a_number_field}

Let *K* be a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field). For quadratic forms over *K*, there is a [Hasse invariant](wikipedia://en/3028413) ±1 for every [finite place](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_place) corresponding to the [Hilbert symbols](wikipedia://en/5924217). The invariants of a form over a number field are precisely the dimension, discriminant, all local Hasse invariants and the [signatures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature_of_a_quadratic_form) coming from real embeddings.[^o42]

The **symbol ring** is defined over *K*, Sym(*K*), as a set of triples (*d*, *e*, *f* ) with *d* in *K\**/*K\**<sup>hair2</sup>, *e* in *Z*/2 and *f* a sequence of elements ±1 indexed by the places of *K*, subject to the condition that all but finitely many terms of *f* are +1, that the value on acomplex places is +1 and that the product of all the terms in *f* is +1. Let \[*a*, *b*\] be the sequence of Hilbert symbols: it satisfies the conditions on *f* just stated.[^o43]

Addition and multiplication is defined as follows:

$$(d_1,e_1,f_1) + (d_2,e_2,f_2) = ((-1)^{e_1e_2}d_1d_2, e_1+e_2, [d_1,d_2][-d_1d_2,(-1)^{e_1e_2}]f_1f_2)$$

$$(d_1,e_1,f_1) \cdot (d_2,e_2,f_2) = (d_1^{e_2}d_2^{e_1}, e_1e_2, [d_1,d_2]^{1+e_1e_2}f_1^{e_2}f_2^{e_1}).$$

Then there is a surjective ring homomorphism from *W*(*K*) to Sym(*K*) obtained by mapping a class to discriminant, rank mod 2, and the sequence of Hasse invariants. The kernel is *I*<sup>3</sup>.[^o44]

The symbol ring is a realisation of the Brauer-Wall group.[^o45]

### Witt ring of the rationals {#Witt_ring_of_the_rationals}

The [Hasse–Minkowski theorem](wikipedia://en/1109126) implies that there is an injection[^o47]

$$W(\mathbf{Q})\rightarrow W(\mathbf{R})\oplus\prod_p W(\mathbf{Q}_p).$$

One can make this concrete and compute the image by using the "second residue homomorphism" W(**Q**<sub>*p*</sub>) → W(**F**<sub>*p*</sub>). Composed with the map W(**Q**) → W(**Q**<sub>*p*</sub>), one obtains a group homomorphism ∂<sub>*p*</sub>: W(**Q**) → W(**F**<sub>*p*</sub>) (for *p* = 2, ∂<sub>2</sub> is defined to be the 2-adic valuation of the discriminant, taken mod 2).

One will then have a [split exact sequence](wikipedia://en/1531404)[^o48]

$$0 \rightarrow \mathbf{Z} \rightarrow W(\mathbf{Q}) \rightarrow \mathbf{Z}/2 \oplus \bigoplus_{p\ne2} W(\mathbf{F}_p) \rightarrow 0 \$$

which can be written as an isomorphism

$$W(\mathbf{Q})\cong\mathbf{Z}\oplus\mathbf{Z}/2\oplus\bigoplus_{p\ne2}W(\mathbf{F}_p)$$

where the first component is the signature.[^o49]

:::

## Witt ring and Milnor's K-theory {#Witt_ring_and_Milnor's_K-theory}

Let *k* be a field of characteristic not equal to 2. The powers of the ideal *I* of forms of even dimension ("fundamental ideal") in $W(k)$ form a descending [filtration](wikipedia://en/726014) and one may consider the associated [graded ring](wikipedia://en/103094), that is the direct sum of quotients $I^n/I^{n+1}$. Let $\langle a\rangle$ be the quadratic form $ax^2$ considered as an element of the Witt ring. Then $\langle a\rangle-\langle 1\rangle$ is an element of *I* and correspondingly a product of the form

$$\langle\langle a_1,\ldots ,a_n\rangle\rangle = (\langle a_1\rangle - \langle 1\rangle)\cdots (\langle a_n\rangle - \langle 1\rangle)$$

is an element of $I^n$. [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632) in a 1970 paper[^o51] proved that the mapping from $(k^*)^n$ to $I^n/I^{n+1}$ that sends $(a_1,\ldots ,a_n)$ to $\langle\langle a_1,\ldots ,a_n\rangle\rangle$ is [multilinear](wikipedia://en/221525) and maps Steinberg elements (elements such that for some $i$ and $j$ such that $i\ne j$ one has $a_i+a_j=1$) to 0. This means that this mapping defines a homomorphism from the [Milnor ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milnor_ring) of *k* to the graded Witt ring. Milnor showed also that this homomorphism sends elements divisible by 2 to 0 and that it is surjective. In the same paper, he made a conjecture that this homomorphism is an isomorphism for all fields *k* (of characteristic different from 2). This became known as the Milnor conjecture on quadratic forms.

The conjecture was proved by Dmitry Orlov, Alexander Vishik, and [Vladimir Voevodsky](wikipedia://en/104482)[^o52] in 1996 (published in 2007) for the case $\textrm{char}(k)=0$, leading to increased understanding of the structure of quadratic forms over arbitrary fields.

## Grothendieck–Witt ring {#Grothendieck–Witt_ring}

The **Grothendieck–Witt ring** *GW* is a related construction generated by isometry classes of nonsingular quadratic spaces with addition given by orthogonal sum and multiplication given by tensor product. Since two spaces that differ by a hyperbolic plane are not identified in *GW*, the inverse for the addition needs to be introduced formally through the construction that was discovered by Grothendieck (see [Grothendieck group](wikipedia://en/1634790)). There is a natural homomorphism *GW* → **Z** given by dimension: a field is [quadratically closed](wikipedia://en/36823185) if and only if this is an isomorphism.[^o54] The hyperbolic spaces generate an ideal in *GW* and the Witt ring *W* is the quotient.[^o55] The [exterior power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_power) gives the Grothendieck–Witt ring the additional structure of a [λ-ring](wikipedia://en/32055176).[^o56]

::: {#o58 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- The Grothendieck–Witt ring of **C**, and indeed any [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) or [quadratically closed field](wikipedia://en/36823185), is **Z**.[^o54]
- The Grothendieck–Witt ring of **R** is isomorphic to the group ring **Z**\[*C*<sub>2</sub>\], where *C*<sub>2</sub> is a cyclic group of order 2.[^o54]
- The Grothendieck–Witt ring of any finite field of odd characteristic is **Z** ⊕ **Z**/2**Z** with trivial multiplication in the second component.[^o59] The element (1, 0) corresponds to the quadratic form ⟨1⟩.
- The Grothendieck–Witt ring of a local field with maximal ideal of norm congruent to 1 modulo 4 is isomorphic to **Z** ⊕ (**Z**/2**Z**)<sup>3</sup>.[^o29]
- The Grothendieck–Witt ring of a local field with maximal ideal of norm congruent to 3 modulo 4 is **Z** ***⊕*** **Z**/4**Z** ⊕ **Z**/2**Z**.[^o29]

:::

## Grothendieck–Witt ring and motivic stable homotopy groups of spheres {#Grothendieck–Witt_ring_and_motivic_stable_homotopy_groups_of_spheres}

[Fabien Morel](wikipedia://en/28977644)[^o61][^o62] showed that the Grothendieck–Witt ring of a [perfect field](wikipedia://en/505088) is isomorphic to the motivic stable homotopy group of spheres π<sub>0,0</sub>(S<sup>0,0</sup>) (see "[A¹ homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/17346948)").

::: {#o64 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Reciprocity equivalence of fields"}

## Witt equivalence {#Witt_equivalence}

Two fields are said to be **Witt equivalent** if their Witt rings are isomorphic.

For global fields there is a local-to-global principle: two global fields are Witt equivalent if and only if there is a bijection between their places such that the corresponding local fields are Witt equivalent.[^o65] In particular, two number fields *K* and *L* are Witt equivalent if and only if there is a bijection *T* between the places of *K* and the places of *L* and a group isomorphism *t* between their [square-class groups](wikipedia://en/36362488), preserving degree 2 Hilbert symbols. In this case the pair (*T*, *t*) is called a **reciprocity equivalence** or a **degree 2 Hilbert symbol equivalence**.[^o66] Some variations and extensions of this condition, such as "tame degree *l* Hilbert symbol equivalence", have also been studied.[^o67]

:::

::: {#o69 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Witt groups can also be defined in the same way for [skew-symmetric forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew-symmetric_form), and for [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478), and more generally [ε-quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/13979549), over any [\*-ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/*-ring) *R*.

The resulting groups (and generalizations thereof) are known as the even-dimensional symmetric [*L*-groups](wikipedia://en/696568) *L*<sup>2*k*</sup>(*R*) and even-dimensional quadratic *L*-groups *L*<sub>2*k*</sub>(*R*). The quadratic *L*-groups are 4-periodic, with *L*<sub>0</sub>(*R*) being the Witt group of (1)-quadratic forms (symmetric), and *L*<sub>2</sub>(*R*) being the Witt group of (−1)-quadratic forms (skew-symmetric); symmetric *L*-groups are not 4-periodic for all rings, hence they provide a less exact generalization.

*L*-groups are central objects in [surgery theory](wikipedia://en/5961115), forming one of the three terms of the [surgery exact sequence](wikipedia://en/21298455).
