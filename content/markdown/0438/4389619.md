# Abrikosov vortex

*Image: YBCO vortices.jpg.* Vortices in a 200-nm-thick [YBCO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/YBCO) film imaged by [scanning SQUID microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scanning_SQUID_microscopy)[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Abrikosov vortex"}

In [superconductivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity), an **Abrikosov vortex** (also called **quantum vortex** or a **fluxon[^o3]**) is a vortex of [supercurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercurrent) in a [type-II superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-II_superconductor), used by Soviet physicist [Alexei Abrikosov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexei_Alexeyevich_Abrikosov) to explain magnetic behavior of type-II superconductors in 1957.[^o4] Abrikosov vortices occur generically in the [Ginzburg–Landau theory](wikipedia://en/396286) of superconductivity.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

The solution is a combination of fluxon solution by [Fritz London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fritz_London),[^o8][^o9] combined with a concept of core of quantum vortex by [Lars Onsager](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lars_Onsager).[^o6][^o7]

In the quantum vortex<sub>,</sub> [supercurrent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercurrent) circulates around the normal (i.e. non-superconducting) core of the vortex. The core has a size $\sim\xi$ — the [superconducting coherence length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconducting_coherence_length) (parameter of a [Ginzburg–Landau theory](wikipedia://en/396286)). The supercurrents decay on the distance about $\lambda$ ([London penetration depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_penetration_depth)) from the core. Note that in [type-II superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-II_superconductors) $\lambda>\xi/\sqrt{2}$. The circulating [supercurrents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercurrent) induce magnetic fields with the total flux equal to a single [flux quantum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_flux_quantum) $\Phi_0$. Therefore, an Abrikosov vortex is often called a [fluxon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluxon).

The magnetic field distribution of a single vortex far from its core can be described by the same equation as in the London's fluxoid [^o8][^o9] $B(r) = \frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi\lambda^2}K_0\left(\frac{r}{\lambda}\right) \approx \sqrt{\frac{\lambda}{r}} \exp\left(-\frac{r}{\lambda}\right),$[^o10] where $K_0(z)$ is a zeroth-order [Bessel function](wikipedia://en/4700). Note that, according to the above formula, at $r \to 0$ the magnetic field $B(r)\propto\ln(\lambda/r)$, i.e. logarithmically diverges. In reality, for $r\lesssim\xi$ the field is simply given by

$B(0)\approx \frac{\Phi_0}{2\pi\lambda^2}\ln\kappa,$ where *κ* = *λ/ξ* is known as the Ginzburg–Landau parameter, which must be $\kappa>1/\sqrt{2}$ in [type-II superconductors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-II_superconductor).

Abrikosov vortices can be trapped in a [type-II superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-II_superconductor) by chance, on defects, etc. Even if initially [type-II superconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type-II_superconductor) contains no vortices, and one applies a magnetic field $H$ larger than the [lower critical field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_critical_field#Lower_critical_field) $H_{c1}$ (but smaller than the [upper critical field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_critical_field) $H_{c2}$), the field penetrates into superconductor in terms of Abrikosov vortices. Each vortex obeys London's magnetic flux quantization and carries one quantum of magnetic flux $\Phi_0$.[^o8][^o9] Abrikosov vortices form a lattice, usually triangular (of [hexagonal symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexagonal_symmetry)), with the average vortex density (flux density) approximately equal to the externally applied magnetic field. As with other lattices, defects may form as dislocations.
