# Refactorable number

*Image: Refactorable number Cuisenaire rods 12.png.* Demonstration, with [Cuisenaire rods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuisenaire_rods), that 1, 2, 8, 9, and 12 are refactorable

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Refactorable number"}

A **refactorable number** or **tau number** is an integer *n* that is divisible by the count of its [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492), or to put it algebraically, *n* is such that $\tau(n)\mid n$ with $\tau(n)=\sigma_0(n)=\prod_{i=1}^{n}(e_i+1)$ for $n=\prod_{i=1}^np_i^{e_i}$. For example, 18 has 6 divisors (1 and 18, 2 and 9, 3 and 6) and is divisible by 6. There are infinitely many refactorable numbers.

## Properties {#Properties}

Cooper and Kennedy proved that refactorable numbers have [natural density](wikipedia://en/1819983) zero. Zelinsky proved that no three consecutive integers can all be refactorable.[^o4] Colton proved that no refactorable number is [perfect](wikipedia://en/23670). The equation $\gcd(n,x) = \tau(n)$ has solutions only if $n$ is a refactorable number, where $\gcd$ is the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) function.

Let $T(x)$ be the number of refactorable numbers which are at most $x$. The problem of determining an asymptotic for $T(x)$ is open. [Spiro](wikipedia://en/80571535) has proven that $T(x) = \frac{x}{\sqrt{\log x} (\log \log x)^{1-o(1)}}$[^o5]

There are still unsolved problems regarding refactorable numbers. Colton asked if there are arbitrarily large $n$ such that both $n$ and $n + 1$ are refactorable. Zelinsky wondered if there exists a refactorable number $n_0 \equiv a \mod m$, does there necessarily exist $n > n_0$ such that $n$ is refactorable and $n \equiv a \mod m$.

:::

## History {#History}

First defined by [Curtis Cooper](wikipedia://en/12572471) and Robert E. Kennedy[^o7] where they showed that the tau numbers have [natural density](wikipedia://en/1819983) zero, they were later rediscovered by [Simon Colton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Colton) using a computer program he wrote ("HR") which invents and judges definitions from a variety of areas of mathematics such as [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) and [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401).[^o8] Colton called such numbers "refactorable". While computer programs had discovered proofs before, this discovery was one of the first times that a computer program had discovered a new or previously obscure idea. Colton proved many results about refactorable numbers, showing that there were infinitely many and proving a variety of congruence restrictions on their distribution. Colton was only later alerted that Kennedy and Cooper had previously investigated the topic.
