# Unitary divisor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Unitary divisor"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $a$ is a **unitary divisor** (or **Hall divisor**) of a number $b$ if $a$ is a [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of $b$ and if $a$ and $b / a$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime), having no common factor other than 1. Equivalently, a divisor $a$ of $b$ is a unitary divisor [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) every [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) factor of $a$ has the same [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790) in $a$ as it has in $b$.

:::

The concept of a unitary divisor originates from [R. Vaidyanathaswamy](wikipedia://en/11498709) (1931),[^o2] who used the term **block divisor**.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The integer 5 is a unitary divisor of 60, because 5 and $\frac{60}{5}=12$ have only 1 as a common factor.

On the contrary, 6 is a divisor but not a unitary divisor of 60, as 6 and $\frac{60}{6}=10$ have a common factor other than 1, namely 2.

:::

## Sum of unitary divisors {#Sum_of_unitary_divisors}

The sum-of-unitary-divisors function is denoted by the lowercase Greek letter sigma thus: $\sigma^*(n)$. The sum of the $k$-th [powers](wikipedia://en/99491) of the unitary divisors is denoted by $\sigma_k^*(n)$:

$$\sigma_k^*(n) = \sum_{d \,\mid\, n \atop \gcd(d,\,n/d)=1} \!\! d^k.$$

It is a [multiplicative function](wikipedia://en/20059). If the [proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_divisor) unitary divisors of a given number add up to that number, then that number is called a [unitary perfect number](wikipedia://en/553027).

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The number $1$ is a unitary divisor of every natural number.

The number of unitary divisors of a number $n$ is $2^{k}$, where $k$ is the number of distinct prime factors of $n$. This is because each [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $N > 1$ is the product of positive powers $p^{r_p}$ of distinct prime numbers $p$. Thus every unitary divisor of $N$ is the product, over a given subset $S$ of the prime divisors $\{p$} of $N$, of the prime powers $p^{r_p}$ for $p \in S$. If there are $k$ prime factors, then there are exactly $2^{k}$ subsets $S$, and the statement follows.

The sum of the unitary divisors of $n$ is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) if $n$ is a [power of 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_2) (including $1$), and [even](wikipedia://en/143135) otherwise.

Both the count and the sum of the unitary divisors of $n$ are [multiplicative functions](wikipedia://en/20059) of $n$ that are not [completely multiplicative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_multiplicative). The [Dirichlet generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet_generating_function) is

$$\frac{\zeta(s)\zeta(s-k)}{\zeta(2s-k)} = \sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{\sigma_k^*(n)}{n^s}.$$

Every divisor of $n$ is unitary if and only if $n$ is [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525).

The set of all unitary divisors of $n$ forms a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959) with meet given by the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) and join by the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961). Equivalently, the set of unitary divisors of $n$ forms a Boolean ring, where the addition and multiplication are given by

$$a\oplus b = \frac{ab}{(a,b)^2},\qquad a\odot b=(a,b)$$

where $(a,b)$ denotes the greatest common divisor of $a$ and $b$. [^o8]

:::

## Odd unitary divisors {#Odd_unitary_divisors}

The sum of the *k*-th powers of the odd unitary divisors is

$$\sigma_k^{(o)*}(n) = \sum_{{d \,\mid\, n \atop d \equiv 1 \pmod 2} \atop \gcd(d,n/d)=1} \!\! d^k.$$

It is also multiplicative, with Dirichlet generating function

$$\frac{\zeta(s)\zeta(s-k)(1-2^{k-s})}{\zeta(2s-k)(1-2^{k-2s})} = \sum_{n\ge 1}\frac{\sigma_k^{(o)*}(n)}{n^s}.$$

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Bi-unitary divisor"}

## Bi-unitary divisors {#Bi-unitary_divisors}

A divisor $d$ of $n$ is a **bi-unitary divisor** if the greatest common unitary divisor of $d$ and $n / d$ is $1$. This concept originates from D. Suryanarayana (1972). \[The number of bi-unitary divisors of an integer, in The Theory of Arithmetic Functions, *[Lecture Notes in Mathematics](wikipedia://en/47931235)* 251: 273–282, New York, Springer–Verlag\].

The number of bi-unitary divisors of $n$ is a multiplicative function of $n$ with [average order](wikipedia://en/18760872) $A \log x$ where[^o12]

$$A = \prod_p\left({1 - \frac{p-1}{p^2(p+1)} }\right) \ = 0.8073308216\cdots .$$

A **bi-unitary perfect number** is one equal to the sum of its bi-unitary aliquot divisors. The only such numbers are $6$, $60$ and $90$.[^o13]

:::
