# Differentiation in Fréchet spaces

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Differentiation in Fréchet spaces"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in particular in [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and [nonlinear analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_analysis), it is possible to define the [derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28generalizations%29) of a function between two [Fréchet spaces](wikipedia://en/233801). This notion of differentiation, as it is [Gateaux derivative](wikipedia://en/2253139) between Fréchet spaces, is significantly weaker than the [derivative in a Banach space](wikipedia://en/2735727), even between general [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752). Nevertheless, it is the weakest notion of differentiation for which many of the familiar theorems from [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) hold. In particular, the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) is true. With some additional constraints on the Fréchet spaces and functions involved, there is an analog of the [inverse function theorem](wikipedia://en/287229) called the [Nash–Moser inverse function theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash–Moser_inverse_function_theorem), having wide applications in nonlinear analysis and [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625).

:::

## Mathematical details {#Mathematical_details}

Formally, the definition of differentiation is identical to the [Gateaux derivative](wikipedia://en/2253139). Specifically, let $X$ and $Y$ be Fréchet spaces, $U \subseteq X$ be an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358), and $F : U \to Y$ be a function. The directional derivative of $F$ in the direction $v \in X$ is defined by

$$DF(u)v = \lim_{\tau \to 0} \frac{F(u + v \tau) - F(u)}{\tau}$$

if the limit exists. One says that $F$ is continuously differentiable, or $C^1$, if $F$ is [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_%28topology%29), the directional derivative $DF(u)v$ exists for all $u\in U$ and $v \in X$ and the mapping

$$DF : U \times X \to Y$$

is continuous.

Higher order derivatives are defined inductively via

$$D^{k+1}F(u) \left\{v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_{k+1}\right\} = \lim_{\tau \to 0} \frac{D^kF(u+\tau v_{k+1}) \left\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\right\} - D^kF(u) \left\{v_1, \ldots, v_k\right\}}{\tau}.$$

A function is said to be $C^k$ if $D^k F : U \times X \times X \times \cdots \times X \to Y$ is continuous. It is $C^{\infty},$ or **smooth** if it is $C^k$ for every $k.$

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Let $X, Y,$ and $Z$ be Fréchet spaces. Suppose that $U$ is an open subset of $X,$ $V$ is an open subset of $Y,$ and $F : U \to V,$ $G : V \to Z$ are a pair of $C^1$ functions. Then the following properties hold:

- **[Fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696)**. If the line segment from $a$ to $b$ lies entirely within $U,$ then $$F(b) - F(a) = \int_0^1 DF(a + (b - a) t) \cdot (b - a) dt.$$
- **The [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113)**. For all $u \in U$ and $x \in X,$ $$D(G \circ F)(u) x = DG(F(u)) DF(u) x$$
- **[Linearity](wikipedia://en/91591)**. $DF(u) x$ is linear in $x.$ More generally, if $F$ is $C^k,$ then $DF(u) \left\{ x_1, \ldots, x_k \right\}$ is [multilinear](wikipedia://en/832212) in the $x$'s.
- **Taylor's theorem with remainder**. Suppose that the line segment between $u \in U$ and $u + h$ lies entirely within $U.$ If $F$ is $C^k$ then $$F(u+h) = F(u) + DF(u)h + \frac{1}{2!} D^2F(u) \{h,h\} + \cdots + \frac{1}{(k-1)!} D^{k-1}F(u) \{h, h, \ldots, h\} + R_k$$ where the remainder term is given by $$R_k(u,h) = \frac{1}{(k-1)!} \int_0^1(1-t)^{k-1} D^kF(u+th) \{h,h,\ldots,h\}dt$$
- **Commutativity of directional derivatives**. If $F$ is $C^k,$ then $$D^kF(u) \left\{h_1, \ldots, h_k\right\} = D^kF(u) \left\{h_{\sigma(1)}, \ldots, h_{\sigma(k)}\right\}$$ for every [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) σ of $\{ 1, 2, \ldots, k \}.$

The proofs of many of these properties rely fundamentally on the fact that it is possible to define the [Riemann integral](wikipedia://en/26390) of continuous curves in a Fréchet space.

:::

## Smooth mappings {#Smooth_mappings}

Surprisingly, a mapping between open subset of Fréchet spaces is smooth (infinitely often differentiable) if it maps smooth curves to smooth curves; see [Convenient analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convenient_analysis). Moreover, smooth curves in spaces of smooth functions are just smooth functions of one variable more.

## Consequences in differential geometry {#Consequences_in_differential_geometry}

The existence of a chain rule allows for the definition of a [manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manifold_%28mathematics%29) modeled on a Fréchet space: a [Fréchet manifold](wikipedia://en/3873038). Furthermore, the linearity of the derivative implies that there is an analog of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) for Fréchet manifolds.

## Tame Fréchet spaces {#Tame_Fréchet_spaces}

Frequently the Fréchet spaces that arise in practical applications of the derivative enjoy an additional property: they are **tame**. Roughly speaking, a tame Fréchet space is one which is almost a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989). On tame spaces, it is possible to define a preferred class of mappings, known as tame maps. On the category of tame spaces under tame maps, the underlying topology is strong enough to support a fully fledged theory of [differential topology](wikipedia://en/8562). Within this context, many more techniques from calculus hold. In particular, there are versions of the inverse and implicit function theorems.
