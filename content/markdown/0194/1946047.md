# Eisenstein's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eisenstein's theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Eisenstein's theorem**, named after the German mathematician [Gotthold Eisenstein](wikipedia://en/1452482), applies to the coefficients of any [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) which is an [algebraic function](wikipedia://en/974169) with [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) coefficients. Through the theorem, it is readily demonstrable, for example, that the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) must be a [transcendental function](wikipedia://en/297021).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

Suppose that

$$\sum_{}^{} a_n t^n$$

is a [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) with rational coefficients *a*<sub>*n*</sub>, which has a non-zero [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), and within it represents an [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478) that is in fact an algebraic function. Then Eisenstein's theorem states that there exists a non-zero integer *A*, such that *A*<sup>*n+1*</sup>*a*<sub>*n*</sub> are all integers.

This has an interpretation in terms of [p-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423): with an appropriate extension of the idea, the *p*-adic radius of convergence of the series is at least 1, for [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) *p* (i.e., the primes outside a finite set *S*). In fact that statement is a little weaker, in that it disregards any initial [partial sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum) of the series, in a way that may *vary* according to *p*. For the other primes the radius is non-zero.

:::

## History {#History}

Eisenstein's original paper is the short communication *Über eine allgemeine Eigenschaft der Reihen-Entwicklungen aller algebraischen Funktionen* (1852), reproduced in Mathematische Gesammelte Werke, Band II, Chelsea Publishing Co., New York, 1975, p. 765–767.

More recently, many authors have investigated precise and effective bounds quantifying the above [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309). See, e.g., Sections 11.4 and 11.55 of the book by E. Bombieri & W. Gubler.
