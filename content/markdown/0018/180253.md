# Stellation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stellation"}

*Image: Academ\_Stellated\_dodecagon.svg.* Construction of a stellated [dodecagon](wikipedia://en/508486): [a regular](wikipedia://en/333306) polygon with [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) {12/5}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **stellation** is the process of extending a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) in two [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398), a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) in three dimensions, or, in general, a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) in *n* dimensions to form a new figure. Starting with an original figure, the process extends specific elements such as its [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) or [face](wikipedia://en/149926) planes, usually in a symmetrical way, until they meet each other again to form the closed boundary of a new figure. The new figure is a stellation of the original. The word *stellation* comes from the Latin *stellātus*, "starred", which in turn comes from the Latin *stella*, "star". Stellation is the reciprocal or dual process to *[faceting](wikipedia://en/8613537)*.

:::

## Kepler's definition {#Kepler's_definition}

In 1619 [Kepler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler) defined stellation for polygons as the process of extending edges until they meet to form a new polygon.

For polyhedra he distinguished two types of stellation:

- an *echinus* ([Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for [hedgehog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedgehog) or [sea urchin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_urchin)), now called edge-stellated, made by extending non-adjacent edges until they intersect;
- and *ostrea* (Latin for [oyster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oyster)), now called face-stellated, made by extending faces until they intersect.[^o3]

He (edge-) stellated the regular [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) to obtain the [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843) and the regular [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084) to obtain the [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878). He also (face-) stellated the regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635) to obtain the [stella octangula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stella_octangula), a regular [compound of two tetrahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_of_two_tetrahedra).

## Stellating polygons {#Stellating_polygons}

*Image: regular star polygons.svg.* Regular convex and star polygons with 3 to 12 vertices labelled with their Schläfli symbols

Stellating a regular polygon symmetrically creates a regular [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303) or [polygonal compound](wikipedia://en/313303#Star_figures). These polygons are characterised by the number of times *m* that the polygonal boundary winds around the centre of the figure. Like all regular polygons, their vertices lie on a circle. *m* also corresponds to the number of vertices around the circle to get from one end of a given edge to the other, starting at 1.

A regular star polygon is represented by its [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) {*n*/*m*}, where *n* is the number of vertices, *m* is the *step* used in sequencing the edges around it, and *m* and *n* are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) (have no common [factor](wikipedia://en/49492)). The case *m* = 1 gives the [convex polygon](wikipedia://en/660019) {*n*}. *m* also must be less than half of *n*; otherwise the lines will either be parallel or diverge, preventing the figure from ever closing.

If *n* and *m* do have a common factor, then the figure is a regular compound. For example {6/2} is the regular compound of two triangles {3} or [hexagram](wikipedia://en/302441), while {10/4} is a compound of two pentagrams {5/2}.

Some authors use the Schläfli symbol for such regular compounds. Others regard the symbol as indicating a single path which is wound *m* times around $\tfrac{n}{m}$ vertex points, such that one edge is superimposed upon another and each vertex point is visited *m* times. In this case a modified symbol may be used for the compound, for example 2{3} for the hexagram and 2{5/2} for the regular compound of two pentagrams.

A regular *n*-gon has $\tfrac{n - 4}{2}$ stellations if *n* is [even](wikipedia://en/143135) (assuming compounds of multiple degenerate [digons](wikipedia://en/5442728) are not considered), and $\tfrac{n - 3}{2}$ stellations if *n* is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135).

Like the [heptagon](wikipedia://en/365349), the [octagon](wikipedia://en/314575) also has two [octagrammic](wikipedia://en/2667613) stellations, one, {8/3} being a [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303), and the other, {8/2}, being the compound of two [squares](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29).

## Stellating polyhedra {#Stellating_polyhedra}

A polyhedron is stellated by extending the edges or face planes of a polyhedron until they meet again to form a new polyhedron or compound.

The method by extending the edges of a polyhedron rarely leads to a new polyhedron because at least 3 extended edges need to intersect at the same point. Examples of edge-stellated polyhedra are the [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843) (edge-stellation of the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407)) and the [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878) (edge-stellation of the [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084)).

Face-stellated polyhedra are much more common and the following is only about face-stellated polyhedra.

A polyhedron is face-stellated by extending the face planes of a polyhedron until they meet again to form a new polyhedron or compound. When the face planes of the polyhedron are extended indefinitely the space around the polyhedron is divided into unbounded sub spaces and often a number of bounded polyhedrons or cells. Different sets of cells yield different stellations.

For a symmetrical polyhedron, these cells will fall into groups, or sets, of congruent cells – we say that the cells in such a congruent set are of the same type. A common method of finding stellations involves selecting one or more cell types.

A set of cells forming a closed layer around its core is called a shell. A shell may be made up of one or more cell types. A stellation where only complete shells are considered are called **main-line stellations.**

The stellation where all cells are included is called the **complete or final stellation** of the polyhedron, for example the [final stellation of the icosahedron](wikipedia://en/2968303), the [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878) (the final stellation of the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407)) and the fourth stellation of the [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631).

Face-stellation can lead to a huge number of different possible polyhedra, so further criteria are often imposed to reduce the set to those stellations that are properly significant and distinct in some way.

Based on such ideas, several restrictive categories of interest have been identified:

- **Main-line stellations.** Adding successive shells to the core polyhedron leads to the set of main-line stellations.
- **Fully supported stellations.** The underside faces of a cell can appear externally as an "overhang." In a fully supported stellation there are no such overhangs, and all visible parts of a face are seen from the same side.
- **Monoacral stellations.** Where there is only one kind of peak, or vertex, in a stellation (i.e. all vertices are congruent within a single symmetry orbit), the stellation is monoacral, literally "single-peaked". All such stellations are fully supported.
- **Primary stellations.** Where a polyhedron has planes of mirror symmetry, edges falling in these planes are said to lie in primary lines. If all edges lie in primary lines, the stellation is primary. All primary stellations are fully supported.
- **Miller stellations.** In *[The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra](wikipedia://en/13181245)*, [Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328), Du Val, Flather and Petrie record five rules suggested by [Miller](wikipedia://en/20706909). Although these rules specifically refer to the stellations of the icosahedron, they have been adapted to work for arbitrary polyhedra. These rules ensure, among other things, that the rotational symmetry of the original polyhedron is preserved, and that each stellation is different in outward appearance. The four kinds of stellation just defined are all subsets of the Miller stellations.

We can also identify some other categories:

- A **partial stellation** is one where not all elements of a given dimensionality are extended.
- A **sub-symmetric stellation** is one where not all elements are extended symmetrically.

The [Archimedean solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_solids) and their duals can also be stellated. Here we usually add the rule that all of the original face planes must be present in the stellation, i.e. we do not consider partial stellations. For example the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) is not usually considered a stellation of the [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280).

Generalising Miller's rules there are:

- 4 stellations of the [rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631)
- 187 stellations of the [triakis tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/722548)
- 358,833,097 stellations of the [rhombic triacontahedron](wikipedia://en/665025)
- 17 stellations of the [cuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/6280) (4 are shown in [Wenninger](wikipedia://en/2915428#Stellations:_models_W19_to_W66)'s *Polyhedron Models*)
- An unknown number of stellations of the [icosidodecahedron](wikipedia://en/15023); there are 7,071,671 non-[chiral](wikipedia://en/1170160) stellations, but the number of chiral stellations is unknown. (20 are shown in [Wenninger](wikipedia://en/2915428#Stellations_of_icosidodecahedron)'s *Polyhedron Models*)

Seventeen of the nonconvex [uniform polyhedra](wikipedia://en/2537223) are stellations of Archimedean solids.

### Miller's rules {#Miller's_rules}

In the book *[The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_fifty_nine_icosahedra)*, J.C.P. Miller proposed a [set of rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_fifty_nine_icosahedra#Miller's_rules) for defining which stellation forms should be considered "properly significant and distinct".

These rules have been adapted for use with stellations of many other polyhedra. Under Miller's rules we find:

- There are no stellations of the [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), because all faces are adjacent.
- There are no stellations of the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285), because non-adjacent faces are parallel and thus cannot be extended to meet in new edges.
- There is 1 stellation of the [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), the [stella octangula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stella_octangula).
- There are 3 stellations of the [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407): the [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843), the [great dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/2860441) and the [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878), all of which are Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra.
- There are 58 stellations of the [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084), including the [great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898) (one of the Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra), and the [second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_stellation_of_icosahedron) and [final](wikipedia://en/2968303) stellations of the icosahedron. The 59th model in *The fifty nine icosahedra* is the original icosahedron itself.

Many "Miller stellations" cannot be obtained directly by using Kepler's method. For example many have hollow centres where the original faces and edges of the core polyhedron are entirely missing: there is nothing left to be stellated. On the other hand, Kepler's method also yields stellations which are forbidden by Miller's rules since their cells are edge- or vertex-connected, even though their faces are single polygons. This discrepancy received no real attention until Inchbald (2002).

:::

### Other rules for stellation {#Other_rules_for_stellation}

Miller's rules by no means represent the "correct" way to enumerate stellations. They are based on combining parts within the [stellation diagram](wikipedia://en/18696734) in certain ways, and don't take into account the topology of the resulting faces. As such there are some quite reasonable stellations of the icosahedron that are not part of their list – one was identified by James Bridge in 1974, while some "Miller stellations" are questionable as to whether they should be regarded as stellations at all – one of the icosahedral set (index 16 **f<sub>2</sub>** ) comprises several disconnected cells floating symmetrically in space.

As yet an alternative set of rules that takes this into account has not been fully developed. Most progress has been made based on the notion that stellation is the reciprocal or dual process to [facetting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facetting), whereby parts are removed from a polyhedron without creating any new vertices. For every stellation of some polyhedron, there is a [dual](wikipedia://en/609737) facetting of the [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815), and vice versa. By studying facettings of the dual, we gain insights into the stellations of the original. Bridge found his new stellation of the icosahedron by studying the facettings of its dual, the dodecahedron.

Some polyhedronists take the view that stellation is a two-way process, such that any two polyhedra sharing the same face planes are stellations of each other. This is understandable if one is devising a general algorithm suitable for use in a computer program, but is otherwise not particularly helpful.

Many examples of stellations can be found in the [list of Wenninger's stellation models](wikipedia://en/2915428#Stellations:_models_W19_to_W66).

## Stellating polytopes {#Stellating_polytopes}

The stellation process can be applied to higher dimensional polytopes as well. A [stellation diagram](wikipedia://en/18696734) of an *n*-polytope exists in an (*n* − 1)-dimensional [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) of a given [facet](wikipedia://en/7186647).

For example, in 4-space, the [great grand stellated 120-cell](wikipedia://en/8604513) is the final stellation of the [regular 4-polytope](wikipedia://en/18801624) [120-cell](wikipedia://en/717358).

## Naming stellations {#Naming_stellations}

The first systematic naming of stellated polyhedra was [Cayley](wikipedia://en/311034)'s naming of the regular star polyhedra (nowadays known as the [Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler–Poinsot_polyhedra)). This system was widely, but not always systematically, adopted for other polyhedra and higher polytopes.

[John Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) devised a terminology for stellated [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621), [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) and [polychora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polychoron) (Coxeter 1974). In this system the process of extending edges to create a new figure is called *stellation*, that of extending faces is called *greatening* and that of extending cells is called *aggrandizement* (this last does not apply to polyhedra). This allows a systematic use of words such as 'stellated', 'great', and 'grand' in devising names for the resulting figures. For example Conway proposed some minor variations to the names of the [Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler–Poinsot_polyhedra#History).

## Stellation to infinity {#Stellation_to_infinity}

Wenninger noticed that some polyhedra, such as the cube, do not have any finite stellations. However stellation cells can be constructed as prisms which extend to infinity. The figure comprising these prisms may be called a **stellation to infinity**. By most definitions of a polyhedron, however, these stellations are not strictly polyhedra.

Wenninger's figures occurred as [duals of the uniform hemipolyhedra](wikipedia://en/24084383#Duals_of_the_hemipolyhedra), where the faces that pass through the center are sent to vertices "at infinity".

## From mathematics to art {#From_mathematics_to_art}

*Image: Magnus Wenninger polyhedral models.jpg.* Magnus Wenninger with some of his models of stellated polyhedra in 2009

Alongside from his contributions to mathematics, [Magnus Wenninger](wikipedia://en/8864685) is described in the context of the relationship of [mathematics and art](wikipedia://en/22468661) as making "especially beautiful" models of complex stellated polyhedra.[^o17]

*Image: Marble floor mosaic Basilica of St Mark Vencice.jpg.* Marble floor [mosaic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mosaic) by [Paolo Uccello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Uccello), [Basilica of St Mark, Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_St_Mark,_Venice), c. 1430

The [Italian Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_Renaissance) artist [Paolo Uccello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Uccello) created a floor mosaic showing a small stellated dodecahedron in the [Basilica of St Mark, Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_St_Mark,_Venice), c. 1430. Uccello's depiction was used as the symbol for the [Venice Biennale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice_Biennale) in 1986 on the topic of "Art and Science".[^o18] The same stellation is central to two [lithographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithograph) by [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127): *Contrast (Order and Chaos)*, 1950, and *[Gravitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitation_%28M._C._Escher%29)*, 1952.[^o19]
