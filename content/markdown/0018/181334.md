# Discrete logarithm

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discrete logarithm"}

*Image: Discrete logarithm mod 5.svg.* Discrete logarithms modulo 5 to base 2. Each arrow represents multiplication by 2 modulo 5. Starting at 1, the discrete logarithm of a nonzero residue is the number of arrows needed to reach it, modulo 4.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), for given [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $a$ and $b$, the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) $\log_b(a)$ is a number $x$ such that $b^x = a$. The discrete logarithm is an analogous concept in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890). In any [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$, powers $b^k$ can be defined for all [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $k$, and the **discrete logarithm** $\log_b(a)$ is an integer $k$ such that $b^k = a$. In the special case of [arithmetic modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) an integer $m$, the more commonly used term is **index**: One can write $k = \operatorname{ind}_b a \!\!\!\! \pmod{m}$ when $b^k \equiv a \!\!\!\! \pmod{m}$.

:::

Discrete logarithms are quickly computable in a few special cases, but no efficient method is known for computing them in general. Several [cryptographic](wikipedia://en/18934432) systems, including [Diffie–Hellman](wikipedia://en/3237741) and [ElGamal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ElGamal_cryptosystem), base their security on the [hardness assumption](wikipedia://en/6158383) that the discrete logarithm problem over carefully chosen groups has no efficient solution.[^o2] In general, there is no [subexponential time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subexponential_time) solution for [black box](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_box) groups.[^o3]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $G$ be any group. Denote its [group operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_operation) by multiplication and its [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) by $1$. Let $b$ be any element of $G$. For any positive integer $k$, the expression $b^k$ denotes the product of $b$ with itself $k$ times:[^o6]

$$b^k = \underbrace{b \cdot b \cdot \ldots \cdot b}_{k \; \text{factors}}.$$

Similarly, let $b^{-k}$ denote the product of $b^{-1}$ with itself $k$ times. For $k=0$, the $k$<sup>th</sup> power is the identity: $b^0=1$.

Let $a$ also be an element of $G$. An integer $k$ that solves the equation $b^k=a$ is termed a **discrete logarithm** (or simply **logarithm**, in this context) of $a$ to the base $b$. One writes $k=\log_b a$.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Powers of 10 {#Powers_of_10}

The [powers of 10](wikipedia://en/1161104) are

$$\ldots, 0.001, 0.01, 0.1, 1, 10, 100, 1000, \ldots.$$

For any number $a$ in this list, one can compute $\log_{10}a$. For example, $\log_{10}{10000}=4$, and $\log_{10}{0.001}=-3$. These are instances of the discrete logarithm problem.

Other base-10 logarithms in the real numbers are not instances of the discrete logarithm problem, because they involve non-integer exponents. For example, the equation $\log_{10}{53}=1.724276\ldots$ means that $10^{1.724276\ldots}=53$. While integer exponents can be defined in any group using products and inverses, arbitrary real exponents, such as this 1.724276…, require other concepts such as the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678).

In [group-theoretic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group-theoretic) terms, the powers of 10 form a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $G$ under multiplication, and 10 is a [generator](wikipedia://en/52327) for this group. The discrete logarithm $\log_{10}a$ is defined for any $a$ in $G$.

### Powers of a fixed real number {#Powers_of_a_fixed_real_number}

A similar example holds for any non-zero real number $b$. The powers form a multiplicative [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $G = \{\ldots , b^{-2}, b^{-1}, 1, b^{1}, b^{2}, \ldots \}$ of the non-zero real numbers. For any element $a$ of $G$, one can compute $\log_b a$.

### Modular arithmetic {#Modular_arithmetic}

Let $p$ be a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), and let $\mathbf{Z}_{p}^{\times}$ be the group [multiplicative group of integers modulo $p$](wikipedia://en/1195234). Its elements are non-zero [congruence classes](wikipedia://en/20087#Congruence_class) modulo $p$, which can be written $1, 2, 3, \ldots, p - 1$. The group product of two elements is obtained by ordinary integer multiplication followed by reduction modulo $p$.

The $k$<sup>th</sup> [power](wikipedia://en/99491) of one of the numbers in this group may be computed by finding its $k$<sup>th</sup> power as an integer and then finding the remainder after division by $p$. When the numbers involved are large, it is more efficient to reduce modulo $p$ multiple times during the computation. Regardless of the specific algorithm used, this operation is called [modular exponentiation](wikipedia://en/903032). For example, consider $\mathbf{Z}_{17}^{\times}$. To compute $3^4$ in this group, compute $3^4=81$, and then divide $81$ by $17$, obtaining a remainder of $13$. Thus $3^4=13$ in the group $\mathbf{Z}_{17}^{\times}$.

The discrete logarithm is just the inverse operation. For example, consider the equation

$$3^k \equiv 13 \pmod{17}.$$

From the example above, one solution is $k=4$, and therefore $\log_3 13 = 4$ in $\mathbf{Z}_{17}^{\times}$. However, $4$ is not the only solution. Because

$$3^{16}\equiv 1 \pmod{17},$$

it follows that if $n$ is any integer then

$$3^{4+16n}\equiv 3^4\cdot (3^{16})^n \equiv 3^4 \cdot 1^n \equiv 3^4 \equiv 13 \pmod{17}.$$

Hence the equation has infinitely many solutions of the form $4+16n$. Moreover, because $16$ is the smallest positive integer $m$ satisfying $3^m\equiv 1 \pmod{17}$, these are the only solutions. Equivalently, the set of all possible solutions can be expressed by the constraint that $k \equiv 4 \pmod{16}$.

::: {#o13 .statement cue=named-heading name="Powers of the identity"}

### Powers of the identity {#Powers_of_the_identity}

In the special case where $b$ is the identity element $1$ of the group $G$, the discrete logarithm $\log_ba$ is undefined for $a$ other than $1$, and every integer $k$ is a discrete logarithm for $a=1$.

:::

### Elliptic curve {#Elliptic_curve}

Let $C$ be a [Weierstrass normal form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_plane_curve#Weierstrass_normal_form) [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225) in the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $F$. Let $O$ be the point at infinity on $C$. For any points $P$ and $Q$ of $C$, let $P \# Q$ denote the unique third point of $C$, where the line through $P$ and $Q$ intersects $C$. (If $P = Q$, then the line in question is the line tangent to $C$ at $P$. If $P = Q = O$, then $P \# Q = O$.) Define an "addition" operation on $C$ by

$$P + Q = (P \; \# \; Q) \; \# \; O.$$

This addition operation makes $C$ a commutative group with identity element $O$. For any point $P$ of $C$, let

$$k P = P + P + \cdots + P$$

denote the sum of $k$ copies of $P$. In this context, the discrete logarithm problem is: Given points $P$ and $Q$, find $k$ such that $Q = k P$. When the underlying field $F$ is a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615), this problem has cryptographic applications.[^o15]

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Powers obey the usual algebraic identity $b^{k+l}=b^k\cdot b^l$.[^o6] In other words, the [function](wikipedia://en/185427)

$$f \colon \mathbf{Z} \to G$$

defined by $f(k)=b^k$ is a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from the group of integers $\mathbf{Z}$ under addition [onto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection) the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $H$ of $G$ [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by $b$. For all $a$ in $H$, $\log_b a$ exists. [Conversely](wikipedia://en/155941), $\log_b a$ does not exist for $a$ that are not in $H$.

If $H$ is [infinite](wikipedia://en/4587030), then $\log_b a$ is also unique, and the discrete logarithm amounts to a [group isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397)

$$\log_b \colon H \to \mathbf{Z}.$$

On the other hand, if $H$ is [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) $n$, then $\log_b a$ is unique only up to [congruence modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $n$, and the discrete logarithm amounts to a group isomorphism

$$\log_b\colon H \to \mathbf{Z}_n,$$

where $\mathbf{Z}_n$ denotes the additive group of integers modulo $n$.

The familiar base change formula for ordinary logarithms remains valid: If $c$ is another generator of $H$, then

$$\log_c a = \log_c b \cdot \log_b a.$$

:::

## Algorithms {#Algorithms}

::: {#o20 .problem cue=unsolved}

Can the discrete logarithm be computed in polynomial time on a classical computer?

:::

The discrete logarithm problem is considered to be computationally intractable. For a classical (e.g., non-[quantum](wikipedia://en/25220)) computer, no efficient ([polynomial](wikipedia://en/658550)-time) algorithm is yet known for computing discrete logarithms in general.

A general algorithm for computing $\log_b a$ in finite groups $G$ is to raise $b$ to larger and larger powers $k$ until the desired $a$ is found. This algorithm is sometimes called *trial multiplication*. It requires [running time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Running_time) [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) in the size of the group $G$ and thus [exponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_time) in the number of digits in the size of the group. Therefore, it is an exponential-time algorithm, practical only for small groups $G$.

More sophisticated algorithms exist, usually inspired by similar algorithms for [integer factorization](wikipedia://en/15491). These algorithms run faster than the naïve algorithm, some of them proportional to the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of the size of the group, and thus exponential in half the number of digits in the size of the group. However, none of them runs in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) (in the number of digits in the size of the group).

- [Baby-step giant-step](wikipedia://en/563928)
- [Function field sieve](wikipedia://en/22407581)
- [Index calculus algorithm](wikipedia://en/1549595)
- [Number field sieve](wikipedia://en/152734)
- [Pohlig–Hellman algorithm](wikipedia://en/1995624)
- [Pollard's rho algorithm for logarithms](wikipedia://en/1630817)
- [Pollard's kangaroo algorithm](wikipedia://en/12928899) (aka Pollard's lambda algorithm)

There is an efficient [quantum algorithm](wikipedia://en/42674) due to [Peter Shor](wikipedia://en/68338).[^o21]

Efficient classical algorithms also exist in certain special cases. For example, in the group of the integers modulo $p$ under addition, the power $b^k$ becomes a product $b \cdot k$, and equality means congruence modulo $p$ in the integers. The [extended Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/99438) finds $k$ quickly.

With [Diffie–Hellman](wikipedia://en/7903), a cyclic group modulo a prime $p$ is used, allowing an efficient computation of the discrete logarithm with Pohlig–Hellman if the order of the group (being $p-1$) is sufficiently [smooth](wikipedia://en/1035039), i.e. has no large [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor).

:::

## Comparison with integer factorization {#Comparison_with_integer_factorization}

While computing discrete logarithms and integer factorization are distinct problems, they share some properties:

- both are special cases of the [hidden subgroup problem](wikipedia://en/3728000) for [finite abelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_abelian_group),
- both problems seem to be difficult (no efficient algorithms are known for non-[quantum computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computer)),
- for both problems efficient algorithms on quantum computers are known,
- algorithms from one problem are often adapted to the other, and
- the difficulty of both problems has been used to construct various [cryptographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptographic) systems.

::: {#o24 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Discrete logarithm cryptography"}

## Cryptography {#Cryptography}

There exist groups for which computing discrete logarithms is apparently difficult. In some cases (e.g. large prime order subgroups of groups $\mathbf{Z}_p^\times$) there is not only no efficient algorithm known for the worst case, but the [average-case complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average-case_complexity) can be shown to be about as hard as the worst case using [random self-reducibility](wikipedia://en/3087266).[^o25]

At the same time, the inverse problem of discrete exponentiation is not difficult (it can be computed efficiently using [exponentiation by squaring](wikipedia://en/10237), for example). This asymmetry is analogous to the one between integer factorization and integer multiplication. Both asymmetries (and other possibly [one-way functions](wikipedia://en/363890)) have been exploited in the construction of cryptographic systems.

Popular choices for the group $G$ in discrete logarithm cryptography (DLC) are the cyclic groups $\mathbf{Z}_p^\times$ (e.g. [ElGamal encryption](wikipedia://en/59458), [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903), and the [Digital Signature Algorithm](wikipedia://en/59470)) and cyclic subgroups of [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) (*see* [Elliptic curve cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_cryptography)).

While there is no publicly known algorithm for solving the discrete logarithm problem in general, the first three steps of the [number field sieve](wikipedia://en/152734) algorithm only depend on the group $G$, not on the specific elements of $G$ whose finite $\log$ is desired. By [precomputing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precomputing) these three steps for a specific group, one need only carry out the last step, which is much less computationally expensive than the first three, to obtain a specific logarithm in that group.[^o27]

It turns out that much [internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet) traffic uses one of a handful of groups that are of order 1024 bits or less, e.g. cyclic groups with order of the Oakley primes specified in RFC 2409.[^o26] The [Logjam](wikipedia://en/46759583) attack used this vulnerability to compromise a variety of internet services that allowed the use of groups whose order was a 512-bit prime number, so called [export grade](wikipedia://en/25872370).[^o27]

The authors of the Logjam attack estimate that the much more difficult precomputation needed to solve the discrete log problem for a 1024-bit prime would be within the budget of a large national [intelligence agency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intelligence_agency) such as the U.S. [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) (NSA). The Logjam authors speculate that precomputation against widely reused 1024 DH primes is behind claims in [leaked NSA documents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_surveillance_disclosures_%282013–present%29) that NSA is able to break much of current cryptography.[^o27]
