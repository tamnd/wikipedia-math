# Adolphe Quetelet

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Adolphe Quetelet"}

**Lambert Adolphe Jacques Quetelet** (; 22 February 1796 – 17 February 1874)[^o3] was a Belgian astronomer, mathematician, statistician, and sociologist who founded and directed the [Brussels Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Observatory_of_Belgium) and was influential in introducing statistical methods to the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science). His name is sometimes spelled with an accent as *Quételet*.[^o4]

:::

He also founded the science of [anthropometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropometry) and developed the [body mass index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_mass_index) (BMI) scale, originally called the Quetelet Index.[^o6] His work on measuring human characteristic to determine the ideal *l'homme moyen* ("the average man") played a key role in the origins of [eugenics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eugenics).

## Life {#Life}

Adolphe was born in [Ghent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghent) (which, at the time, was part of the new [French Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_French_Republic)). He was the son of François-Augustin-Jacques-Henri Quetelet, a Frenchman, and Anne Françoise Vandervelde, a Flemish woman. His father was born at [Ham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ham,_France), Picardy, and being of a somewhat adventurous spirit, he crossed the English Channel and became a British citizen and the secretary of a Scottish nobleman. In that capacity, he traveled with his employer on the Continent, particularly spending time in Italy. At about 31, he settled in Ghent and was employed by the city, where Adolphe was born, the fifth of nine children, several of whom died in childhood.

Francois died when Adolphe was only seven years old. Adolphe studied at the Ghent Lycée, where he afterwards started teaching mathematics in 1815 at the age of 19. In 1819, he moved to the Athenaeum in Brussels and in the same year he completed his [dissertation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thesis) (*De quibusdam locis geometricis, necnon de curva focal – Of some new properties of the focal distance and some other curves*).

Quetelet received a doctorate in mathematics in 1819 from the [University of Ghent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Ghent). Shortly thereafter, the young man set out to convince government officials and private donors to build an astronomical [observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observatory) in Brussels; he succeeded in 1828. He became a member of the [Royal Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Academy) in 1820. He lectured at the museum for sciences and letters and at the Belgian Military School. In 1825, he became a correspondent of the Royal Institute of the Netherlands, in 1827 he became a member. In 1839, he was elected as a member of the [American Philosophical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Philosophical_Society).[^o8] From 1841 to 1851, he was a supernumerary associate in the institute, and when it became [Royal Netherlands Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Netherlands_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) he became foreign member.[^o9] In 1850, he was elected a foreign member of the [Royal Swedish Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Swedish_Academy_of_Sciences).

In 1825, he married Cécile-Virginie Curtet.[^o10]

Quetelet also founded several statistical journals and societies, and was especially interested in creating international cooperation among statisticians. He encouraged the creation of a statistical section of the British Association for the Advancement of Science (BA), which later became the [Royal Statistical Society](wikipedia://en/552664), of which he became the first overseas member. In 1853 he chaired both the [International Maritime Conference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Meteorological_Organization) and the [first International Statistical Congress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_International_Statistical_Congress). He was a founding member of the first *[Société des douze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Société_des_douze)*.

In 1855, Quetelet developed [apoplexy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apoplexy), which diminished but did not end his scientific activity.

He died in [Brussels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels) on 17 February 1874, and is buried in the [Brussels Cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brussels_Cemetery).

## Work {#Work}

His scientific research encompassed a wide range of different scientific disciplines: [meteorology](wikipedia://en/19904), astronomy, mathematics, statistics, [demography](wikipedia://en/45642), sociology, [criminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminology) and history of science. He made significant contributions to scientific development, but he also wrote several [monographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph) directed to the general public. He founded the [Royal Observatory of Belgium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Observatory_of_Belgium), founded or co-founded several national and international statistical societies and scientific journals, and presided over the first series of the International Statistical Congresses. Quetelet was a liberal and an [anticlerical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-clericalism), but not an [atheist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atheism) or [materialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materialism) nor a [socialist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Socialism).

### Social physics {#Social_physics}

The new science of [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and statistics was mainly used in astronomy at the time, where it was essential to account for measurement [errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Errors_and_residuals_in_statistics) around means. This was done using the method of [least squares](wikipedia://en/82359). Quetelet was among the first to apply statistics to social science, planning what he called a "[social physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_physics)". He was keenly aware of the overwhelming complexity of social phenomena and of the many variables that needed measurement. His goal was to understand the statistical laws underlying such phenomena as crime rates, marriage rates, or [suicide rates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_countries_by_suicide_rate). He wanted to explain the values of these variables by other social factors. These ideas were rather controversial among other scientists at the time who held that it contradicted the concept of freedom of choice.

His most influential book was *Sur l'homme et le développement de ses facultés, ou Essai de physique sociale*, published in 1835 (In English translation, it is titled *Treatise on Man*, but a literal translation would be "On Man and the Development of his Faculties, or Essay on Social Physics"). In it, he outlines the project of a social physics and describes his concept of the "average man" (*l'homme moyen*) who is characterized by the [mean values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean_value) of measured variables that follow a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462). He collected data about many such variables. Quetelet wrote about these values as "ideals" with deviations from them as being less than or more than ideal. He saw the average body as an ideal beauty and something to be desired and his work was influential on [Francis Galton](wikipedia://en/49072) who coined the term *eugenics*.

Quetelet's student [Pierre François Verhulst](wikipedia://en/491402) developed the [logistic function](wikipedia://en/84563) in the 1830s as a model of [population growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Population_growth); see Logistic function for details.[^n1]

When [Auguste Comte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auguste_Comte) discovered that Quetelet had appropriated the term 'social physics', which Comte had originally introduced, Comte found it necessary to invent the term 'sociologie' (sociology) because he disagreed with Quetelet's notion that a theory of society could be derived from a collection of statistics.

Adolphe Quetelet also had a significant influence on [Florence Nightingale](wikipedia://en/100127) who shared with him a religious view of statistics which saw understanding statistics as revealing the work of God in addition to statistics being a force of good administration. Nightingale met Quetelet in person at the 1860 International Statistical Congress in London, and they corresponded for years afterwards.[^o16]

### Criminology {#Criminology}

Quetelet was an influential figure in [criminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminology). Along with [Andre-Michel Guerry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andre-Michel_Guerry), he helped to establish the [cartographic school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crime_mapping) and positivist schools of criminology which made extensive use of statistical techniques. Through statistical analysis, Quetelet gained insight into the relationships between crime and other social factors. Among his findings were strong relationships between age and crime, as well as [gender and crime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender_and_crime). Other influential factors he found included [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate), poverty, education, and [alcohol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcoholic_beverage) consumption, with his research findings published in *Of the Development of the Propensity to Crime*.[^o18]

### Anthropometry {#Anthropometry}

In his 1835 text on social physics, he presented his theory of human [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) around the average, showing human traits were distributed according to a [normal curve](wikipedia://en/21462). The existence of such variation provided the basis for later writers, including Darwin, to argue that natural populations contained sufficient variability for [artificial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_selection) or [natural selection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_selection) to operate.[^o20]

In terms of influence over later [public health](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_health) agendas, one of Quetelet's lasting legacies was the establishment of a simple measure for classifying people's weight relative to an ideal for their height. His proposal, the [body mass index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_mass_index) (or Quetelet index), has endured with minor variations to the present day.[^o21] Anthropometric data is used in modern applications and referenced in the development of every consumer-based product.

## Awards and honours {#Awards_and_honours}

Quetelet was elected a [Foreign Member of the Royal Society (ForMemRS)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Fellows_of_the_Royal_Society_elected_in_1839) in 1839.[^o23]

The asteroid [1239 Queteleta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1239_Queteleta) is named after him. The title of [Quetelet professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quetelet_professor) at [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University) is awarded in his name.
