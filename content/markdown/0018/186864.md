# Primitive root modulo n

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Primitive root modulo n"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a number $g$ is a **primitive root modulo $n$** if every number $a$ [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to $n$ is [congruent](wikipedia://en/20087) to a power of $g$ [modulo](wikipedia://en/1352428) $n$. In symbols, $g$ is a primitive root modulo $n$ if for every integer $a$ coprime to $n$, there is some integer $k$ for which $g^{k} \equiv a\pmod{n}$.

:::

Primitive roots only exist for some integers $n$. Specifically, a primitive root exists modulo $n$ if and only if $n$ is 4, $p^{k}$ or $2p^{k}$ for some odd [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$ and some $k \ge 0$.[^o3][^o4] This was first proved by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125).[^o5] Gauss defined primitive roots in Article 57 of his *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)* (1801), where he credited [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) with coining the term. In Article 56 he stated that [Johann Heinrich Lambert](wikipedia://en/93085) and Euler knew of them, but he was the first to rigorously demonstrate that primitive roots exist for a prime number $n$. In fact, the *Disquisitiones* contains two proofs: The one in Article 54 is a nonconstructive [existence proof](wikipedia://en/251177), while the proof in Article 55 is [constructive](wikipedia://en/360113).

An equivalent characterization is that $g$ is a primitive root modulo $n$ if and only if $g$ is a [generator](wikipedia://en/99945) of the [multiplicative group of integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/1195234). Thus, primitive roots exist if and only if this group is a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327).

If $g$ is a primitive root modulo $n$ and $g^{k} \equiv a\pmod{n}$, then the value $k$ is called the *index* or *[discrete logarithm](wikipedia://en/181334)* of $a$ to the base $g$ modulo $n$.

## Elementary example {#Elementary_example}

The number 3 is a primitive root modulo 7[^o7] because

$$\begin{array}{rcrcrcrcrcr} 3^1 &=& 3^0 \times 3 &\equiv& 1 \times 3 &=& 3 &\equiv& 3 \pmod 7 \\ 3^2 &=& 3^1 \times 3 &\equiv& 3 \times 3 &=& 9 &\equiv& 2 \pmod 7 \\ 3^3 &=& 3^2 \times 3 &\equiv& 2 \times 3 &=& 6 &\equiv& 6 \pmod 7 \\ 3^4 &=& 3^3 \times 3 &\equiv& 6 \times 3 &=& 18 &\equiv& 4 \pmod 7 \\ 3^5 &=& 3^4 \times 3 &\equiv& 4 \times 3 &=& 12 &\equiv& 5 \pmod 7 \\ 3^6 &=& 3^5 \times 3 &\equiv& 5 \times 3 &=& 15 &\equiv& 1 \pmod 7 \end{array}$$

The remainders 3, 2, 6, 4, 5, 1 include each [congruence class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_class) relatively prime to 7. Higher powers repeat the same pattern [periodically](wikipedia://en/76848).

The number of congruence classes relatively prime to the modulus $n$ is given by [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) $\varphi$ applied to $n$. In this case, that is $\varphi(7) = 6$. For a prime modulus $n$, this period is always equal to $n - 1$, but this is not true for [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) $n$.

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If $n$ is a positive integer, the integers from 1 to $n$ − 1 that are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) to $n$ (or equivalently, the [congruence classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruence_class) coprime to $n$) form a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), with multiplication [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) $n$ as the operation; it is denoted by $\mathbb{Z}_n^\times$, and is called the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) modulo $n$, or the group of primitive classes modulo $n$. As explained in the article [multiplicative group of integers modulo $n$](wikipedia://en/1195234), this multiplicative group $\mathbb{Z}_n^\times$ is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) if and only if $n$ is equal to 2, 4, $p^{k}$, or $2p^{k}$ where $p^{k}$ is a power of an odd [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666).[^o3][^o11] When (and only when) this group $\mathbb{Z}_n^\times$ is cyclic, a [generator](wikipedia://en/99945) of this cyclic group is called a *primitive root modulo* $n$[^o12] (or in fuller language *primitive root of unity modulo $n$*, emphasizing its role as a fundamental solution of the **[roots of unity](wikipedia://en/30949769)** polynomial equations X${}^{m}$ − 1 in the ring $\mathbb{Z}_n$), or simply a **primitive element of** $\mathbb{Z}_n^\times$.

When $\mathbb{Z}_n^\times$ is non-cyclic, such primitive elements mod $n$ do not exist. Instead, each prime component of $n$ has its own sub-primitive roots (see $15$ in the examples below).

For any $n$ (whether or not $\mathbb{Z}_n^\times$ is cyclic), the order of $\mathbb{Z}_n^\times$ is given by [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452) $\varphi$($n$) . And then, [Euler's theorem](wikipedia://en/69566) says that $a$<sup>$\varphi$($n$)</sup> ≡ 1 (mod $n$) for every $a$ coprime to $n$; the lowest power of $a$ that is congruent to 1 modulo $n$ is called the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) of $a$ modulo $n$. In particular, for $a$ to be a primitive root modulo $n$, $\varphi$($n$) has to be the smallest power of $a$ satisfying $a$<sup>$x$</sup> ≡ 1 (mod $n$) .

:::

::: {#o14 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

For example, if $n$ = 14 then the elements of $\mathbb{Z}$${}^{\times}_{n}$ are the congruence classes {1, 3, 5, 9, 11, 13}; there are $\varphi$(14) = 6 of them. Here is a table of their powers modulo 14:

x x, x<sup>2</sup>, x<sup>3</sup>, ... (mod 14) 1 : 1 3 : 3, 9, 13, 11, 5, 1 5 : 5, 11, 13, 9, 3, 1 9 : 9, 11, 1 11 : 11, 9, 1 13 : 13, 1

The order of 1 is 1, the orders of 3 and 5 are 6, the orders of 9 and 11 are 3, and the order of 13 is 2. Thus, 3 and 5 are the primitive roots modulo 14.

For a second example let $n$ = 15 . The elements of $\mathbb{Z}$${}^{\times}_{15}$ are the congruence classes {1, 2, 4, 7, 8, 11, 13, 14}; there are $\varphi$(15) = 8 of them.

x x, x<sup>2</sup>, x<sup>3</sup>, ... (mod 15) 1 : 1 2 : 2, 4, 8, 1 4 : 4, 1 7 : 7, 4, 13, 1 8 : 8, 4, 2, 1 11 : 11, 1 13 : 13, 4, 7, 1 14 : 14, 1

Since there is no number whose order is 8, there are no primitive roots modulo 15. Indeed, $\lambda$(15) = 4, where $\lambda$ is the [Carmichael function](wikipedia://en/1181756).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

Gauss proved[^o20] that for any prime number $p$ (with the sole exception of $p$ = 3), the product of its primitive roots is congruent to 1 modulo $p$.

He also proved[^o21] that for any prime number $p$, the sum of its primitive roots is congruent to $\mu$($p$ − 1) modulo $p$, where $\mu$ is the [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961).

For example,

{|

\|- |$p$ = 3, || $\mu$(2) = −1. || The primitive root is 2. |- |$p$ = 5, || $\mu$(4) = 0. || The primitive roots are 2 and 3. |- |$p$ = 7, || $\mu$(6) = 1. || The primitive roots are 3 and 5. |- |$p$ = 31, ||$\mu$(30) = −1. || The primitive roots are 3, 11, 12, 13, 17, 21, 22 and 24. |}

E.g., the product of the latter primitive roots is $2^6\cdot 3^4\cdot 7\cdot 11^2\cdot 13\cdot 17 = 970377408 \equiv 1 \pmod{31}$, and their sum is $123 \equiv -1 \equiv \mu(31-1) \pmod{31}$.

If $a$ is a primitive root modulo the prime $p$, then $a^\frac{p-1}{2}\equiv -1 \pmod p$.

[Artin's conjecture on primitive roots](wikipedia://en/2085185) states that a given integer $a$ that is neither a [perfect square](wikipedia://en/213919) nor −1 is a primitive root modulo infinitely many [primes](wikipedia://en/23666).

:::

## Finding primitive roots {#Finding_primitive_roots}

No simple general formula to compute primitive roots modulo $n$ is known. There are however methods to locate a primitive root that are faster than simply trying out all candidates. If the [multiplicative order](wikipedia://en/344542) (its [exponent](wikipedia://en/3568695)) of a number $m$ modulo $n$ is equal to [$\varphi(n)$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_phi_function) (the order of $\mathbb{Z}$${}^{\times}_{n}$), then it is a primitive root. In fact the converse is true: If $m$ is a primitive root modulo $n$, then the multiplicative order of $m$ is $\varphi(n) = \lambda(n)~.$ We can use this to test a candidate $m$ to see if it is primitive.

For $n > 1$ first, compute $\varphi(n)~.$ Then determine the different [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) of $\varphi(n)$, say $p$<sub>1</sub>, ..., $p_{k}$. Finally, compute

$$g^{\varphi(n)/p_i}\bmod n \qquad\mbox{ for } i=1,\ldots,k$$

using a fast algorithm for [modular exponentiation](wikipedia://en/903032) such as [exponentiation by squaring](wikipedia://en/10237). A number $g$ for which these $k$ results are all different from 1 is a primitive root.

The number of primitive roots modulo $n$, if there are any, is equal to

$$\varphi\left(\varphi(n)\right)$$

since, in general, a cyclic group with $r$ elements has $\varphi(r)$ generators.

For prime $n$, this equals $\varphi(n-1)$, and since $n / \varphi(n-1) \in O(\log\log n)$ the generators are very common among {2, ..., $n$−1} and thus it is relatively easy to find one.[^o38]

If $g$ is a primitive root modulo $p$, then $g$ is also a primitive root modulo all powers $p^{k}$ unless $g$<sup>$p$−1</sup> ≡ 1 (mod $p$<sup>2</sup>); in that case, $g$ + $p$ is.[^o36]

If $g$ is a primitive root modulo $p^{k}$, then $g$ is also a primitive root modulo all smaller powers of $p$.

If $g$ is a primitive root modulo $p^{k}$, then either $g$ or $g$ + $p^{k}$ (whichever one is odd) is a primitive root modulo 2$p^{k}$.[^o36]

Finding primitive roots modulo $p$ is also equivalent to finding the roots of the ($p$ − 1)st [cyclotomic polynomial](wikipedia://en/171992) modulo $p$.

## Order of magnitude of primitive roots {#Order_of_magnitude_of_primitive_roots}

The least primitive root $g_{p}$ modulo $p$ (in the range 1, 2, ..., $p$ − 1) is generally small.

### Upper bounds {#Upper_bounds}

Burgess (1962) proved[^o39][^o26] that for every *ε* > 0 there is a $C$ such that $g_p \leq C\,p^{\frac{1}{4}+\varepsilon}.$

[Grosswald](wikipedia://en/21442284) (1981) proved[^o39][^o27] that if $p > e^{e^{24}} \approx 10^{11504079571}$, then $g_p < p^{0.499}.$

[Shoup](wikipedia://en/3978418) (1990, 1992) proved,[^o28] assuming the [generalized Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/152567), that $g_{p}$ = O(log<sup>6</sup> $p$).

### Lower bounds {#Lower_bounds}

Fridlander (1949) and Salié (1950) proved[^o39] that there is a positive constant $C$ such that for infinitely many primes $g_{p}$ > $C$ log $p$.

It can be proved[^o39] in an elementary manner that for any positive integer $M$ there are infinitely many primes such that $M$ \< $g_{p}$ \< $p$ − $M$.

::: {#o31 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

A primitive root modulo $n$ is often used in [pseudorandom number generators](wikipedia://en/45524)[^o32] and [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), including the [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903) scheme. [Sound diffusers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion_%28acoustics%29#Primitive-root_diffusors) have been based on number-theoretic concepts such as primitive roots and [quadratic residues](wikipedia://en/200091).[^o40][^o37]
