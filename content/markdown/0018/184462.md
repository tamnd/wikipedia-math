# David H. Bailey (mathematician)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="David H. Bailey"}

**David Harold Bailey** (born 14 August 1948) is a [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist). He received his B.S. in mathematics from [Brigham Young University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young_University) in 1972 and his Ph.D. in mathematics from [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University) in 1976. He worked for 14 years as a [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist) at [NASA Ames Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA_Ames_Research_Center), and then from 1998 to 2013 as a Senior Scientist at the [Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lawrence_Berkeley_National_Laboratory). He is now retired from the Berkeley Lab.

:::

Bailey is perhaps best known as a co-author (with [Peter Borwein](wikipedia://en/1339736) and [Simon Plouffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Plouffe)) of a 1997 paper that presented a new formula for [π](wikipedia://en/23601) (pi), which had been discovered by Plouffe in 1995. This [Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe formula](wikipedia://en/22684956) permits one to calculate binary or hexadecimal digits of pi beginning at an arbitrary position, by means of a simple algorithm. Subsequently, Bailey and [Richard Crandall](wikipedia://en/9210048) showed that the existence of this and similar formulas has implications for the long-standing question of "normality"[^o4]—whether and why the digits of certain mathematical constants (including pi) appear "random" in a particular sense.

Bailey was a long-time collaborator with [Jonathan Borwein](wikipedia://en/1145010) (Peter's brother). They co-authored five books and over 80 technical papers on [experimental mathematics](wikipedia://en/256363).

Bailey also does research in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) and [parallel computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_computing). He has published studies on the [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) (FFT), high-precision arithmetic, and the [PSLQ algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PSLQ_algorithm) (used for integer relation detection). He is a co-author of the [NAS Benchmarks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAS_Benchmarks), which are used to assess and analyze the performance of parallel scientific computers. A "4-step" method of calculating the FFT is widely known as [Bailey's FFT algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bailey's_FFT_algorithm) (Bailey himself credits it to W. M. Gentleman and G. Sande[^n1][^o6]).

He has also published articles in the area of [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680), including a 2014 paper "Pseudo-mathematics and financial charlatanism," which emphasizes the dangers of statistical overfitting and other abuses of mathematics in the financial field.

In 1993, Bailey received the Sidney Fernbach award from the [IEEE Computer Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Computer_Society), as well as the Chauvenet Prize[^o7] and the Hasse Prize from the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772). In 2008 he was a co-recipient of the [Gordon Bell Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gordon_Bell_Prize) from the [Association for Computing Machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Computing_Machinery). In 2017 he was a co-recipient of the [Levi L. Conant Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_L._Conant_Prize) from the [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822).

Bailey is a member of [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints). He has positioned himself as an advocate of the teaching of science and that accepting the conclusions of modern science is not incompatible with a religious view.[^o8]
