# Cubic equation

*Image: Polynomialdeg3.svg.* Graph of a cubic function with 3 [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) (where the curve crosses the horizontal axis at $y = 0$). The case shown has two [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424). Here the function is $\begin{aligned} f(x) &= \frac{1}{4}\left(x^{3} + 3x^{2} - 6x - 8\right) \\ &= \frac{1}{4}(x-2)(x+1)(x+4) \end{aligned}$ and therefore the three real roots are 2, −1 and −4.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cubic equation"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), a **cubic equation** in one variable is an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) of the form

$$ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0$$

in which $a$ is not zero.

:::

The solutions of this equation are called [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of the [cubic function](wikipedia://en/243709) defined by the left-hand side of the equation. If all of the [coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coefficients) $a$, $b$, $c$, and $d$ of the cubic equation are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), then it has at least one real root (this is true for all odd-degree [polynomial functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function)). All of the roots of the cubic equation can be found by the following means:

- [algebraically](wikipedia://en/18716923): more precisely, they can be expressed by a *cubic formula* involving the four coefficients, the four basic [arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operations), [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208), and [cube roots](wikipedia://en/292506). (This is also true of [quadratic](wikipedia://en/25175) (second-degree) and [quartic](wikipedia://en/251591) (fourth-degree) equations, but not for higher-degree equations, by the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518).)
- [geometrically](wikipedia://en/18973446): using [Omar Khayyam](wikipedia://en/92550)'s method.
- [trigonometrically](wikipedia://en/18717261)
- [numerical approximations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_approximation) of the roots can be found using [root-finding algorithms](wikipedia://en/153299) such as [Newton's method](wikipedia://en/22145).

The coefficients do not need to be real numbers. Much of what is covered below is valid for coefficients in any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) with [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) other than 2 and 3. The solutions of the cubic equation do not necessarily belong to the same field as the coefficients. For example, some cubic equations with rational coefficients have roots that are irrational (and even non-real) [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).

## History {#History}

Cubic equations were known to the ancient Babylonians, Greeks, Chinese, Indians, and Egyptians.[^o3][^o9][^o10] [Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia) (20th to 16th centuries BC) cuneiform tablets have been found with tables for calculating cubes and cube roots.[^o4][^o5] The Babylonians could have used the tables to solve cubic equations, but no evidence exists to confirm that they did.[^o6] The problem of [doubling the cube](wikipedia://en/91110) involves the simplest and oldest studied cubic equation, and one for which the ancient Egyptians did not believe a solution existed.[^o7] In the 5th century BC, [Hippocrates](wikipedia://en/3632522) reduced this problem to that of finding two mean proportionals between one line and another of twice its length, but could not solve this with a [compass and straightedge construction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_construction),[^o8] a task which is now known to be impossible. Methods for solving cubic equations appear in *[The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art](wikipedia://en/204207)*, a [Chinese mathematical](wikipedia://en/1524543) text compiled around the 2nd century BC and commented on by [Liu Hui](wikipedia://en/202719) in the 3rd century.[^o9]

In the 3rd century AD, the [Greek mathematician](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110) found integer or rational solutions for some bivariate cubic equations ([Diophantine equations](wikipedia://en/9109)).[^o10][^o11] Hippocrates, [Menaechmus](wikipedia://en/1944319) and [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) are believed to have come close to solving the problem of doubling the cube using intersecting [conic sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic_sections),[^o8] though historians such as Reviel Netz dispute whether the Greeks were thinking about cubic equations or just problems that can lead to cubic equations. Some others like [T. L. Heath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T._L._Heath), who translated all of Archimedes's works, disagree, putting forward evidence that Archimedes really solved cubic equations using intersections of two [conics](wikipedia://en/19008673), but also discussed the conditions where the roots are 0, 1 or 2.[^o12]

*Image: Graph of cubic polynomial.svg.* [Graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the cubic function *f*(*x*) = 2*x*<sup>3</sup> − 3*x*<sup>2</sup> − 3*x* + 2 = (*x* + 1) (2*x* − 1) (*x* − 2)

In the 7th century, the [Tang dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tang_dynasty) astronomer mathematician [Wang Xiaotong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wang_Xiaotong) in his mathematical treatise titled *[Jigu Suanjing](wikipedia://en/30257071)* systematically established and solved [numerically](wikipedia://en/21506) 25 cubic equations of the form $x^{3} + px^{2} + qx = N$, 23 of them with $p, q \ne 0$, and two of them with $q = 0$.[^o13]

In the 11th century, the Persian poet-mathematician, [Omar Khayyam](wikipedia://en/92550) (1048–1131), made significant progress in the theory of cubic equations. In an early paper, he discovered that a cubic equation can have more than one solution and stated that it cannot be solved using compass and straightedge constructions. He also found a geometric solution.[^o14] In his later work, the *[Treatise on Demonstration of Problems of Algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise_on_Demonstration_of_Problems_of_Algebra)*, he wrote a complete classification of cubic equations with general geometric solutions found by means of intersecting [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673).[^o15][^o16] Khayyam made an attempt to come up with an algebraic formula for extracting cubic roots. He wrote:

> “We have tried to express these roots by algebra but have failed. It may be, however, that men who come after us will succeed.”[^o17]

In the 12th century, the Indian mathematician Bhaskara II attempted the solution of cubic equations without general success. However, he gave one example of a cubic equation: $x^{3} + 12x = 6x^{2} + 35$.[^o18] In the 12th century, another [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_in_medieval_Islam) mathematician, [Sharaf al-Dīn al-Tūsī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sharaf_al-Dīn_al-Tūsī) (1135–1213), wrote the *Al-Muʿādalāt* (*Treatise on Equations*), which dealt with eight types of cubic equations with positive solutions and five types of cubic equations which may not have positive solutions. He used what would later be known as the *[Horner–Ruffini method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horner_method#Horner–Ruffini_method)* to [numerically approximate](wikipedia://en/21506) the [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of a cubic equation. He also used the concepts of [maxima and minima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_and_minima) of curves in order to solve cubic equations which may not have positive solutions. He understood the importance of the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of the cubic equation to find algebraic solutions to certain types of cubic equations.[^o20]

In his book *Flos*, Leonardo de Pisa, also known as [Fibonacci](wikipedia://en/17949) (1170–1250), was able to closely approximate the positive solution to the cubic equation $x^{3} + 2x^{2} + 10x = 20$. Writing in a [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) [numeral system](wikipedia://en/21170) he gave the result as 1,22,7,42,33,4,40 (equivalent to 1 + 22/60 + 7/60<sup>2</sup> + 42/60<sup>3</sup> + 33/60<sup>4</sup> + 4/60<sup>5</sup> + 40/60<sup>6</sup>), which has a [relative error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_error) of about 10<sup>−9</sup>.

In the early 16th century, the Italian mathematician [Scipione del Ferro](wikipedia://en/4560966) (1465–1526) found a method for solving a class of cubic equations, namely those of the form $x^{3} + mx = n$. In fact, all cubic equations can be reduced to this form if one allows $m$ and $n$ to be negative, but [negative numbers](wikipedia://en/154616) were not known to him at that time. Del Ferro kept his achievement secret until just before his death, when he told his student Antonio Fior about it.

*Image: Niccolò Tartaglia.jpg.* Niccolò Fontana Tartaglia

In 1535, [Niccolò Tartaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Tartaglia) (1500–1557) received two problems in cubic equations from [Zuanne da Coi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zuanne_da_Coi) and announced that he could solve them. He was soon challenged by Fior, which led to a famous contest between the two. Each contestant had to put up a certain amount of money and to propose a number of problems for his rival to solve. Whoever solved more problems within 30 days would get all the money. Tartaglia received questions in the form $x^{3} + mx = n$, for which he had worked out a general method. Fior received questions in the form $x^{3} + mx^{2} = n$, which proved to be too difficult for him to solve, and Tartaglia won the contest.

Later, Tartaglia was persuaded by [Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145) (1501–1576) to reveal his secret for solving cubic equations. In 1539, Tartaglia did so only on the condition that Cardano would never reveal it and that if he did write a book about cubics, he would give Tartaglia time to publish. Some years later, Cardano learned about del Ferro's prior work and published del Ferro's method in his book *[Ars Magna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Magna_%28Gerolamo_Cardano%29)* in 1545, meaning Cardano gave Tartaglia six years to publish his results (with credit given to Tartaglia for an independent solution).

Cardano's promise to Tartaglia said that he would not publish Tartaglia's work, and Cardano felt he was publishing del Ferro's, so as to get around the promise. Nevertheless, this led to a challenge to Cardano from Tartaglia, which Cardano denied. The challenge was eventually accepted by Cardano's student [Lodovico Ferrari](wikipedia://en/18720) (1522–1565). Ferrari did better than Tartaglia in the competition, and Tartaglia lost both his prestige and his income.[^o22]

Cardano noticed that Tartaglia's method sometimes required him to extract the square root of a negative number. He even included a calculation with these [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) in *Ars Magna*, but he did not really understand it. [Rafael Bombelli](wikipedia://en/1548091) studied this issue in detail[^o23] and is therefore often considered as the discoverer of complex numbers.

[François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238) (1540–1603) independently derived the trigonometric solution for the cubic with three real roots, and [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) (1596–1650) extended the work of Viète.[^o44]

## Factorization {#Factorization}

If the coefficients of a cubic equation are [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), one can obtain an equivalent equation with integer coefficients, by multiplying all coefficients by a [common multiple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_multiple) of their denominators. Such an equation

$$ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0,$$

with integer coefficients, is said to be [reducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducible_polynomial) if the polynomial on the left-hand side is the product of polynomials of lower degrees. By [Gauss's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_lemma_%28polynomial%29), if the equation is reducible, one can suppose that the [factors](wikipedia://en/82341) have integer coefficients.

Finding the roots of a reducible cubic equation is easier than solving the general case. In fact, if the equation is reducible, one of the factors must have degree one, and thus have the form

$$qx-p,$$

with $q$ and $p$ being [coprime integers](wikipedia://en/6556). The [rational root test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_root_test) allows finding $q$ and $p$ by examining a finite number of cases (because $q$ must be a divisor of $a$, and $p$ must be a divisor of $d$).

Thus, one root is $\textstyle x_1= \frac pq,$ and the other roots are the roots of the other factor, which can be found by [polynomial long division](wikipedia://en/314205). This other factor is

$$\frac aq\,x^2+ \frac{bq+ap}{q^2}\,x+\frac{cq^2+bpq+ap^2}{q^3}.$$

(The coefficients seem not to be integers, but must be integers if $p/q$ is a root.)

Then, the other roots are the roots of this [quadratic polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_polynomial) and can be found by using the [quadratic formula](wikipedia://en/59217).

## Depressed cubic {#Depressed_cubic}

Cubics of the form

$$t^3+pt+q$$

are said to be depressed. They are much simpler than general cubics, but are fundamental, because by a [Tschirnhaus transformation](wikipedia://en/595824), the study of any cubic may be reduced by a simple [change of variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Change_of_variable) to that of a depressed cubic:

Let

$$ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d = 0$$

be a cubic equation. The change of variable

$$x=t-\frac b{3a}$$

gives a cubic (in $t$) that has no term in $t^{2}$. In fact, $x=-\frac{b}{3a}$ is the [inflection point](wikipedia://en/379845) of the original cubic (where the curvature changes sign), so the transformation simply centers the cubic around the inflection point.

After dividing by $a$ one gets the *depressed cubic equation*

$$t^3+pt+q=0,$$

with

$$\begin{aligned} t={}&x+\frac b{3a}\\ p={}&\frac{3ac-b^2}{3a^2} \\ q={}&\frac{2b^3-9abc+27a^2d}{27a^3}. \end{aligned}$$

The [roots](wikipedia://en/264210) $x_1, x_2, x_3$ of the original equation are related to the roots $t_1,t_2,t_3$ of the depressed equation by the relations

$$x_i = t_i-\frac b{3a},$$

for $i = 1, 2, 3$.

:::

## Discriminant and nature of the roots {#Discriminant_and_nature_of_the_roots}

The nature (real or not, distinct or not) of the [roots](wikipedia://en/264210) of a cubic can be determined without computing them explicitly, by using the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607).

### Discriminant {#Discriminant}

The [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) is a function of its coefficients that is zero [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the polynomial has a [multiple root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_root), or, if it is divisible by the square of a non-constant polynomial. In other words, the discriminant is nonzero if and only if the polynomial is [square-free](wikipedia://en/3816655).

If $r_{1}, r_{2}, r_{3}$ are the three [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) (not necessarily distinct nor [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)) of the cubic $ax^3+bx^2+cx+d,$ then the discriminant is

$$a^4(r_1-r_2)^2(r_1-r_3)^2(r_2-r_3)^2.$$

The discriminant of the depressed cubic $t^3+pt+q$ is

$$-\left(4\,p^3+27\,q^2\right).$$

The discriminant of the general cubic $ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$ is

$$18\,abcd - 4\,b^3d + b^2c^2 - 4\,ac^3 - 27\,a^2d^2.$$

It is the product of $a^4$ and the discriminant of the corresponding depressed cubic. Using the formula relating the general cubic and the associated depressed cubic, this implies that the discriminant of the general cubic can be written as

$$\frac{4(b^2-3ac)^3-(2b^3-9abc +27 a^2d)^2}{27a^2}.$$

It follows that one of these two discriminants is zero if and only if the other is also zero, and, if the coefficients are [real](wikipedia://en/20646438), the two discriminants have the same sign. In summary, the same information can be deduced from either one of these two discriminants.

To prove the preceding formulas, one can use [Vieta's formulas](wikipedia://en/714050) to express everything as polynomials in $r_{1}, r_{2}, r_{3}$, and $a$. The proof then results in the verification of the equality of two polynomials.

### Nature of the roots {#Nature_of_the_roots}

If the coefficients of a polynomial are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), and its discriminant $\Delta$ is not zero, there are two cases:

- If $\Delta>0,$ the cubic has three distinct real [roots](wikipedia://en/264210)
- If $\Delta<0,$ the cubic has one real root and two non-real [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) roots.

This can be proved as follows. First, if $r$ is a root of a polynomial with real coefficients, then its [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) is also a root. So the non-real roots, if any, occur as pairs of complex conjugate roots. As a cubic polynomial has three roots (not necessarily distinct) by the [fundamental theorem of algebra](wikipedia://en/51414), at least one root must be real.

As stated above, if $r_{1}, r_{2}, r_{3}$ are the three roots of the cubic $ax^3+bx^2+cx+d$, then the discriminant is

$$\Delta=a^4(r_1-r_2)^2(r_1-r_3)^2(r_2-r_3)^2$$

If the three roots are real and distinct, the discriminant is a product of positive reals, that is $\Delta>0.$

If only one root, say $r_{1}$, is real, then $r_{2}$ and $r_{3}$ are complex conjugates, which implies that $r_{2} - r_{3}$ is a [purely imaginary number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purely_imaginary_number), and thus that $(r_{2} - r_{3})^{2}$ is real and negative. On the other hand, $r_{1} - r_{2}$ and $r_{1} - r_{3}$ are complex conjugates, and their product is real and positive.[^o31] Thus the discriminant is the product of a single negative number and several positive ones. That is $\Delta<0.$

### Multiple root {#Multiple_root}

If the discriminant of a cubic is zero, the cubic has a [multiple root](wikipedia://en/19211790#Multiplicity_of_a_root_of_a_polynomial). If furthermore its coefficients are real, then all of its roots are real.

The discriminant of the depressed cubic $t^3 + p t + q$ is zero if $4p^3 + 27q^2 = 0.$ If $p$ is also zero, then $p = q = 0$, and 0 is a triple root of the cubic. If $4p^3 + 27q^2 = 0,$ and $p \ne 0$, then the cubic has a simple root

$$t_1 = \frac{3q}{p}$$

and a double root

$$t_2 = t_3 = -\frac{3q}{2p}.$$

In other words,

$$t^3 + p t + q = \left(t - \frac{3q}{p}\right)\left(t + \frac{3q}{2p}\right)^2.$$

This result can be proved by expanding the latter product or retrieved by solving the rather simple [system of equations](wikipedia://en/249617) resulting from [Vieta's formulas](wikipedia://en/714050).

By using the reduction of a depressed cubic, these results can be extended to the general cubic. This gives: If the discriminant of the cubic $ax^3 + b x^2 + c x + d$ is zero, then

- either, if $b^2 = 3ac,$ the cubic has a triple root $$x_1 = x_2 = x_3 = -\frac{b}{3a},$$ and $$ax^3 + bx^2 + cx + d = a\left(x + \frac{b}{3a}\right)^3$$
- or, if $b^2 \ne 3ac,$ the cubic has a double root $$x_2 = x_3 = \frac{9ad - bc}{2(b^2 - 3ac)},$$ and a simple root, $$x_1 = \frac{4abc - 9a^2d - b^3}{a(b^2 - 3ac)}.$$ and thus $$a x^3 + b x^2 + c x + d = a(x - x_1)(x - x_2)^2.$$

### Characteristic 2 and 3 {#Characteristic_2_and_3}

The above results are valid when the coefficients belong to a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) other than 2 or 3, but must be modified for characteristic 2 or 3, because of the involved divisions by 2 and 3.

The reduction to a depressed cubic works for characteristic 2, but not for characteristic 3. However, in both cases, it is simpler to establish and state the results for the general cubic. The main tool for that is the fact that a multiple root is a common root of the polynomial and its [formal derivative](wikipedia://en/3832917). In these characteristics, if the derivative is not a constant, it is a linear polynomial in characteristic 3, and is the square of a linear polynomial in characteristic 2. Therefore, for either characteristic 2 or 3, the derivative has only one root. This allows computing the multiple root, and the third root can be deduced from the sum of the roots, which is provided by [Vieta's formulas](wikipedia://en/714050).

A difference with other characteristics is that, in characteristic 2, the formula for a double root involves a square root, and, in characteristic 3, the formula for a triple root involves a cube root.

## Cardano's formula {#Cardano's_formula}

[Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145) is credited with publishing the first formula for solving cubic equations, attributing it to [Scipione del Ferro](wikipedia://en/4560966) and [Niccolo Fontana Tartaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolo_Fontana_Tartaglia). The formula applies to depressed cubics, but, as shown in , it allows solving all cubic equations.

Cardano's result is that if

$$t^3+pt+q=0$$

is a cubic equation such that $p$ and $q$ are [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) such that $\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}$ is positive (this implies that the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of the equation is negative) then the equation has the real root $\sqrt[3]{u_1}+\sqrt[3]{u_2},$ where $u_1$ and $u_2$ are the two numbers $-\frac q2 + \sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}}$ and $-\frac q2 - \sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}}$.

See , below, for several methods for getting this result.

As shown in , the two other roots are non-real [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) numbers, in this case. It was later shown (Cardano did not know [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)) that the two other roots are obtained by multiplying one of the cube roots by the [primitive cube root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) $\varepsilon_1=\frac{-1+i\sqrt 3} 2,$ and the other cube root by the other primitive cube root of unity $\varepsilon_2=\varepsilon_1^2=\frac{-1-i\sqrt 3} 2.$ That is, the other roots of the equation are $\varepsilon_1\sqrt[3]{u_1}+\varepsilon_2 \sqrt[3]{u_2}$ and $\varepsilon_2\sqrt[3]{u_1}+\varepsilon_1 \sqrt[3]{u_2}.$[^o36]

If $4p^3+27q^2 < 0,$ there are three real roots, but [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) allows proving that, if there is no rational root, the roots cannot be expressed by an [algebraic expression](wikipedia://en/4283745) involving only real numbers. Therefore, the equation cannot be solved in this case with the knowledge of Cardano's time. This case has thus been called *[casus irreducibilis](wikipedia://en/11153189)*, meaning *irreducible case* in Latin.

In *casus irreducibilis*, Cardano's formula can still be used, but some care is needed in the use of cube roots. A first method is to define the symbols $\sqrt{{~}^{~}}$ and $\sqrt[3]{{~}^{~}}$ as representing the [principal values](wikipedia://en/408225) of the root function (that is the root that has the largest real part). With this convention Cardano's formula for the three roots remains valid, but is not purely algebraic, as the definition of a principal part is not purely algebraic, since it involves inequalities for comparing real parts. Also, the use of principal cube root may give a wrong result if the coefficients are non-real complex numbers. Moreover, if the coefficients belong to another [field](wikipedia://en/10603), the principal cube root is not defined in general.

The second way for making Cardano's formula always correct, is to remark that the product of the two cube roots must be $-p / 3$. It results that a root of the equation is

$$C-\frac p{3C}\quad\text{with}\quad C=\sqrt[3]{-\frac q2+\sqrt{\frac{q^2}4+\frac{p^3}{27}}}.$$

In this formula, the symbols $\sqrt{{~}^{~}}$ and $\sqrt[3]{{~}^{~}}$ denote any square root and any cube root. The other roots of the equation are obtained either by changing of cube root or, equivalently, by multiplying the cube root by a primitive cube root of unity, that is $\textstyle \frac{-1\pm \sqrt{-3}}2.$

This formula for the roots is always correct except when $p = q = 0$, with the proviso that if $p = 0$, the square root is chosen so that $C \ne 0$. However, Cardano's formula is useless if $p=0,$ as the roots are the cube roots of $-q.$ Similarly, the formula is also useless in the cases where no cube root is needed, that is when the cubic polynomial is not [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725); this includes the case $4p^3+27q^2=0.$

This formula is also correct when $p$ and $q$ belong to any [field](wikipedia://en/10603) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) other than 2 or 3.

## General cubic formula {#General_cubic_formula}

A *cubic formula* for the roots of the general cubic equation (with $a \ne 0$)

$$ax^3+bx^2+cx+d=0$$

can be deduced from every variant of Cardano's formula by reduction to a depressed cubic. The variant that is presented here is valid not only for complex coefficients, but also for coefficients $a, b, c, d$ belonging to any [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) other than 2 or 3. If the coefficients are real numbers, the formula covers all complex solutions, not just real ones.

The formula is rather complicated, so it is worth splitting it into smaller formulas.

Let

$$\begin{aligned} \Delta_0 &= b^2 - 3ac,\\ \Delta_1 &= 2b^3 - 9abc + 27a^2d. \end{aligned}$$

(Both $\Delta_0$ and $\Delta_1$ can be expressed as [resultants](wikipedia://en/2195020) of the cubic and its derivatives: $\Delta_1$ is $\tfrac{-1}{8a}$ times the resultant of the cubic and its second derivative, and $\Delta_0$ is $\tfrac{-1}{12a}$ times the resultant of the first and second derivatives of the cubic polynomial.)

Then let

$$C = \sqrt[3]{\frac{\Delta_1 \pm \sqrt{\Delta_1^2 - 4 \Delta_0^3}}2},$$

where the symbols $\sqrt{{~}^{~}}$ and $\sqrt[3]{{~}^{~}}$ are interpreted as *any* square root and *any* cube root, respectively (every nonzero complex number has two square roots and three cubic roots). The sign "$\pm$" before the square root is either "$+$" or "$-$"; the choice is almost arbitrary, and changing it amounts to choosing a different square root. However, if a choice yields $C = 0$ (this occurs if $\Delta_0=0$), then the other sign must be selected instead. If both choices yield $C = 0$, that is, if $\Delta_0=\Delta_1=0,$ a fraction $\tfrac{0}{0}$ occurs in following formulas; this fraction must be interpreted as equal to zero (see the end of this section). With these conventions, one of the roots is

$$x = - \frac{1}{3a}\left(b+C+\frac{\Delta_0}{C}\right).$$

The other two roots can be obtained by changing the choice of the cube root in the definition of $C$, or, equivalently by multiplying $C$ by a [primitive cube root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity), that is $\tfrac{-1 \pm \sqrt{-3}}{2}$. In other words, the three roots are

$$x_k = - \frac{1}{3a}\left(b+\xi^kC+\frac{\Delta_0}{\xi^kC}\right), \qquad k \in \{0,1,2\} \text{,}$$

where $\xi = \tfrac{-1 + \sqrt{-3}}{2}$.

As for the special case of a depressed cubic, this formula applies but is useless when the roots can be expressed without cube roots. In particular, if $\Delta_0=\Delta_1=0,$ the formula gives that the three roots equal $\frac {-b}{3a},$ which means that the cubic polynomial can be factored as $\textstyle a(x+\frac b{3a})^3.$ A straightforward computation allows verifying that the existence of this factorization is equivalent with $\Delta_0=\Delta_1=0.$

:::

## Trigonometric and hyperbolic solutions {#Trigonometric_and_hyperbolic_solutions}

### Trigonometric solution for three real roots {#Trigonometric_solution_for_three_real_roots}

When a cubic equation with real coefficients has three real roots, the formulas expressing these roots in terms of radicals involve complex numbers. [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) allows proving that when the three roots are real, and none is rational (*[casus irreducibilis](wikipedia://en/11153189)*), one cannot express the roots in terms of real radicals. Nevertheless, purely real expressions of the solutions may be obtained using [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/18717261), specifically in terms of [cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) and [arccosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arccosine).[^o42] More precisely, the roots of the depressed cubic

$$t^3 + pt + q = 0$$

are[^o43]

$$t_k = 2\,\sqrt{-\frac{p}{3}}\,\cos\left[\,\frac{1}{3} \arccos\left(\frac{3q}{2p}\sqrt{\frac{-3}{p}}\,\right) - \frac{2\pi k}{3}\,\right] \qquad \text{for } k=0,1,2.$$

This formula is due to [François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238).[^o44] It is purely real when the equation has three real roots (that is $4p^3 + 27q^2 < 0$). Otherwise, it is still correct but involves complex cosines and arccosines when there is only one real root, and it is nonsensical ([division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663)) when $p = 0$.

This formula can be straightforwardly transformed into a formula for the roots of a general cubic equation, using the back-substitution described in .

The formula can be proved as follows: Starting from the equation $t^{3} + pt + q = 0$, let us set $t = u \cos\,\theta$. The idea is to choose $u$ to make the equation coincide with the identity

$$4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta - \cos(3\theta) = 0.$$

For this, choose $u = 2\,\sqrt{-\frac{p}{3}}\,,$ and divide the equation by $\frac{u^3}{4}.$ This gives

$$4\cos^3\theta - 3\cos\theta - \frac{3q}{2p}\,\sqrt{ \frac{-3}{p} } = 0.$$

Combining with the above identity, one gets

$$\cos(3\theta) = \frac{3q}{2p}\sqrt{\frac{-3}{p}}\,,$$

and the roots are thus

$$t_k = 2\,\sqrt{-\frac{p}{3}}\,\cos\left[ \frac{1}{3} \arccos\left( \frac{3q}{2p}\sqrt{\frac{-3}{p}} \right) - \frac{2\pi k}{3} \right] \qquad \text{for } k=0,1,2.$$

### Hyperbolic solution for one real root {#Hyperbolic_solution_for_one_real_root}

When there is only one real root (and $p \ne 0$), this root can be similarly represented using [hyperbolic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_function), as[^o46][^o47]

$$\begin{aligned} t_0 &= -2\frac{|q|}{q}\sqrt{-\frac{p}{3}}\cosh\left[\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{arcosh}\left(\frac{-3|q|}{2p}\sqrt{\frac{-3}{p}}\right)\right] \qquad \text{if } ~ 4 p^3 + 27 q^2 > 0 ~\text{ and }~ p < 0,\\ t_0 & = -2\sqrt{\frac{p}{3}}\sinh\left[\frac{1}{3}\operatorname{arsinh}\left(\frac{3q}{2p}\sqrt{\frac{3}{p}}\right)\right] \qquad \text{if } ~ p > 0.\end{aligned}$$

If $p \ne 0$ and the inequalities on the right are not satisfied (the case of three real roots), the formulas remain valid but involve complex quantities.

When $p = \pm3$, the above values of $t_{0}$ are sometimes called the **Chebyshev cube root.**[^o48] More precisely, the values involving cosines and hyperbolic cosines define, when $p = -3$, the same [analytic function](wikipedia://en/61478) denoted $C_{1/3}(q)$, which is the proper Chebyshev cube root. The value involving hyperbolic sines is similarly denoted $S_{1/3}(q)$, when $p = 3$.

:::

## Geometric solutions {#Geometric_solutions}

### Omar Khayyám's solution {#Omar_Khayyám's_solution}

*Image: Omar Kayyám - Geometric solution to cubic equation.svg.* Omar Khayyám's geometric solution of a cubic equation, for the case $m = 2$, $n = 16$, giving the root $2$. The intersection of the vertical line on the $x$-axis at the center of the circle is happenstance of the example illustrated.

For solving the cubic equation $x^{3} + m^{2}x = n$ where $n > 0$, [Omar Khayyám](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Omar_Khayyám) constructed the parabola $y = x^{2}/m$, the circle that has as a diameter the [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) $[0, n/m^{2}]$ on the positive $x$-axis, and a vertical line through the point where the circle and the parabola intersect above the $x$-axis. The solution is given by the length of the horizontal line segment from the origin to the intersection of the vertical line and the $x$-axis (see the figure).

A simple modern proof is as follows. Multiplying the equation by $x/m^{2}$ and regrouping the terms gives

$$\frac{x^4}{m^2}= x\left(\frac{n}{m^2}-x\right).$$

The left-hand side is the value of $y^{2}$ on the parabola. The equation of the circle being $y^{2} + x(x - \tfrac{n}{m^{2}}) = 0$, the right hand side is the value of $y^{2}$ on the circle.

### Solution with angle trisector {#Solution_with_angle_trisector}

A cubic equation with real coefficients can be solved geometrically using [compass, straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass-and-straightedge_construction), and an [angle trisector](wikipedia://en/91111) if and only if it has three real roots.[^o52]

A cubic equation can be solved by compass-and-straightedge construction (without trisector) if and only if it has a [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) root. This implies that the old problems of [angle trisection](wikipedia://en/91111) and [doubling the cube](wikipedia://en/91110), set by [ancient Greek mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics), cannot be solved by compass-and-straightedge construction.

## Geometric interpretation of the roots {#Geometric_interpretation_of_the_roots}

### Three real roots {#Three_real_roots}

*Image: Trigonometric interpretation of a cubic equation with three real roots.svg.* For the cubic **((1))** with three real roots, the roots are the projection on the $x$-axis of the vertices $A$, $B$, and $C$ of an [equilateral triangle](wikipedia://en/173285). The center of the triangle has the same $x$-coordinate as the [inflection point](wikipedia://en/379845).

Viète's trigonometric expression of the roots in the three-real-roots case lends itself to a geometric interpretation in terms of a circle.[^o44][^o55] When the cubic is written in depressed form **((2))**, $t^{3} + pt + q = 0$, as shown above, the solution can be expressed as

$$t_k=2\sqrt{-\frac{p}{3}}\cos\left(\frac{1}{3}\arccos\left(\frac{3q}{2p}\sqrt{\frac{-3}{p}}\right)-k\frac{2\pi}{3}\right) \quad \text{for} \quad k=0,1,2 \,.$$

Here $\arccos\left(\frac{3q}{2p}\sqrt{\frac{-3}{p}}\right)$ is an angle in the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071); taking $\tfrac{1}{3}$ of that angle corresponds to taking a cube root of a complex number; adding $-k\tfrac{2\pi}{3}$ for $k = 1, 2$ finds the other cube roots; and multiplying the cosines of these resulting angles by $2\sqrt{-\frac{p}{3}}$ corrects for scale.

For the non-depressed case **((1))** (shown in the accompanying graph), the depressed case as indicated previously is obtained by defining $t$ such that $x = t - \tfrac{b}{3a}$ so $t = x + \tfrac{b}{3a}$. Graphically this corresponds to simply shifting the graph horizontally when changing between the variables $t$ and $x$, without changing the angle relationships. This shift moves the point of inflection and the centre of the circle onto the $y$-axis. Consequently, the roots of the equation in $t$ sum to zero.

### One real root {#One_real_root}

#### In the Cartesian plane {#In_the_Cartesian_plane}

*Image: Graphical interpretation of the complex roots of cubic equation.svg.* The slope of line RA is twice that of RH. Denoting the complex roots of the cubic as $g \pm hi$, $g = \overline{OM}$ (negative here) and $h$ = $\sqrt{\tan ORH}$ = $\sqrt{\operatorname{slope}\text{ of } \operatorname{line} RH}$ = $\overline{BE}$ = $\overline{DA}$.

When the graph of a [cubic function](wikipedia://en/243709) is plotted in the [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane), if there is only one real root, it is the [abscissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscissa) ($x$-coordinate) of the horizontal intercept of the curve (point R on the figure). Further,[^o58][^o59][^o60] if the complex conjugate roots are written as $g \pm hi$, then the [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) $g$ is the abscissa of the tangency point H of the [tangent line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_line) to cubic that passes through $x$-intercept R of the cubic (that is the signed length OM, negative on the figure). The [imaginary parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) $\pm h$ are the square roots of the tangent of the angle between this tangent line and the horizontal axis.

#### In the complex plane {#In_the_complex_plane}

With one real and two complex roots, the three roots can be represented as points in the complex plane, as can the two roots of the cubic's derivative. There is an interesting geometrical relationship among all these roots.

The points in the complex plane representing the three roots serve as the vertices of an [isosceles triangle](wikipedia://en/315428). (The triangle is isosceles because one root is on the horizontal (real) axis and the other two roots, being complex conjugates, appear symmetrically above and below the real axis.) [Marden's theorem](wikipedia://en/17185722) says that the points representing the roots of the derivative of the cubic are the [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607) of the [Steiner inellipse](wikipedia://en/17185864) of the triangle—the unique ellipse that is tangent to the triangle at the midpoints of its sides. If the angle at the vertex on the real axis is less than $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$ then the major axis of the ellipse lies on the real axis, as do its foci and hence the roots of the derivative. If that angle is greater than $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$, the major axis is vertical and its foci, the roots of the derivative, are complex conjugates. And if that angle is $\tfrac{\pi}{3}$, the triangle is equilateral, the Steiner inellipse is simply the triangle's incircle, its foci coincide with each other at the incenter, which lies on the real axis, and hence the derivative has duplicate real roots.

## Galois group {#Galois_group}

Given a cubic [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) over a field $K$ of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) different from 2 and 3, the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/61316#Permutation_group_approach_to_Galois_theory) over $K$ is the group of the [field automorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_automorphism) that fix $K$ of the smallest extension of $K$ ([splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440)). As these automorphisms must permute the roots of the polynomials, this group is either the group $S_{3}$ of all six permutations of the three roots, or the group $A_{3}$ of the three circular permutations.

The discriminant $\Delta$ of the cubic is the square of

$$\sqrt \Delta =a^2(r_1-r_2)(r_1-r_3)(r_2-r_3),$$

where $a$ is the leading coefficient of the cubic, and $r_{1}$, $r_{2}$ and $r_{3}$ are the three roots of the cubic. As $\sqrt \Delta$ changes sign if two roots are exchanged, $\sqrt \Delta$ is fixed by the Galois group only if the Galois group is $A_{3}$. In other words, the Galois group is $A_{3}$ if and only if the discriminant is the square of an element of $K$.

As most integers are not squares, when working over the field $\mathbf{Q}$ of the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), the Galois group of most irreducible cubic polynomials is the group $S_{3}$ with six elements. An example of a Galois group $A_{3}$ with three elements is given by $p(x) = x^{3} - 3x - 1$, whose discriminant is $81 = 9^{2}$.

## Derivation of the roots {#Derivation_of_the_roots}

This section regroups several methods for deriving Cardano's formula.

::: {#o65 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Cardano's formula"}

### Cardano's method {#Cardano's_method}

This method is due to [Scipione del Ferro](wikipedia://en/4560966) and [Tartaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Fontana_Tartaglia), but is named after [Gerolamo Cardano](wikipedia://en/13145) who first published it in his book [*Ars Magna*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ars_Magna_%28Gerolamo_Cardano%29) (1545).

This method applies to a depressed cubic $t^{3} + pt + q = 0$. The idea is to introduce two variables $u$ and $v$ such that $u+v=t$ and to substitute this in the depressed cubic, giving

$$u^3 + v^3 + (3uv + p)(u+v)+ q= 0.$$

At this point Cardano imposed the condition $3uv+p=0.$ This removes the third term in the previous equality, leading to the system of equations

$$\begin{aligned}u^3 + v^3&=-q \\ uv&=-\frac p3.\end{aligned}$$

This second equation is equivalent to $u^3 v^3 = -p^3/27$. Knowing the sum and the product of $u^{3}$ and $v^3,$ one deduces that they are the two solutions of the [quadratic equation](wikipedia://en/25175)

$$\begin{aligned} 0 &= (x - u^3)(x - v^3) \\ &= x^2 - (u^3 + v^3)x + u^3v^3 \\ &= x^2 - (u^3 + v^3)x + (uv)^3 \end{aligned}$$

so

$$x^2 + qx -\frac {p^3}{27}=0.$$

The discriminant of this equation is $\Delta = q^2 + \frac{4p^3}{27}$, and assuming it is positive, real solutions to this equation are (after folding division by 4 under the square root):

$$-\frac q2 \pm \sqrt {\frac {q^2}{4} +\frac {p^3}{27}}.$$

So ([without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) in choosing $u$ or $v$):

$$u = \sqrt[3]{-\frac q2 + \sqrt {\frac {q^2}{4} +\frac {p^3}{27}}}.$$

$$v = \sqrt[3]{-\frac q2 - \sqrt {\frac {q^2}{4} +\frac {p^3}{27}}}.$$

As $u+v=t,$ the sum of the cube roots of these solutions is a root of the equation. That is

$$t=\sqrt[3]{-{q\over 2}+\sqrt{{q^{2}\over 4}+{p^{3}\over 27}}} +\sqrt[3]{-{q\over 2}-\sqrt{{q^{2}\over 4}+{p^{3}\over 27}}}$$

is a root of the equation; this is Cardano's formula.

This works well when $4p^3+27q^2 > 0,$ but, if $4p^3+27q^2 < 0,$ the square root appearing in the formula is not real. As a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) has three cube roots, using Cardano's formula without care would provide nine roots, while a cubic equation cannot have more than three roots. This was clarified first by [Rafael Bombelli](wikipedia://en/1548091) in his book *L'Algebra* (1572). The solution is to use the fact that $uv=-\frac p3,$ that is, $v=\frac{-p}{3u}.$ This means that only one cube root needs to be computed, and leads to the second formula given in .

The other roots of the equation can be obtained by changing of cube root, or, equivalently, by multiplying the cube root by each of the two [primitive cube roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity), which are $\frac {-1\pm \sqrt{-3}}2.$

When only one root is real, $u$ and $v$ will be the complex conjugates of one another, implying that the one real root must be $t=2\mathrm{Re}(u)$.

:::

### Vieta's substitution {#Vieta's_substitution}

Vieta's substitution is a method introduced by [François Viète](wikipedia://en/310238) (Vieta is his Latin name) in a text published posthumously in 1615, which provides directly the second formula of , and avoids the problem of computing two different cube roots.[^o67]

Starting from the depressed cubic $t^{3} + pt + q = 0$, Vieta's substitution is $t = w - \tfrac{p}{3w}$.

The substitution $t = w - \tfrac{p}{3w}$ transforms the depressed cubic into

$$w^3+q-\frac{p^3}{27w^3}=0.$$

This is a quadratic equation in $w^3$, so there are six solutions for $w$. In the substitution, for each value of $t$ there are two possible values for $w$. Each root of the cubic equation is found twice.

Multiplying by $w^{3}$, one gets a quadratic equation in $w^{3}$:

$$(w^3)^2+q(w^3)-\frac{p^3}{27}=0.$$

Let

$$W=-\frac q 2\pm\sqrt{\frac{p^3}{27} + \frac {q^2} 4}$$

be any nonzero root of this quadratic equation. If $w_{1}$, $w_{2}$ and $w_{3}$ are the three [cube roots](wikipedia://en/292506) of $W$, then the roots of the original depressed cubic are $w_{1} - \tfrac{p}{3w_{1}}$, $w_{2} - \tfrac{p}{3w_{2}}$, and $w_{3} - \tfrac{p}{3w_{3}}$. The other root of the quadratic equation is $\textstyle -\frac {p^3}{27W}.$ This implies that changing the sign of the square root exchanges $w_{i}$ and $- \tfrac{p}{3w_{i}}$ for $i = 1, 2, 3$, and therefore does not change the roots. This method only fails when both roots of the quadratic equation are zero, that is when $p = q = 0$, in which case the only root of the depressed cubic is $0$.

### Lagrange's method {#Lagrange's_method}

In his paper *Réflexions sur la résolution algébrique des équations* ("Thoughts on the algebraic solving of equations"),[^o69] [Joseph Louis Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange) introduced a new method to solve equations of low degree in a uniform way, with the hope that he could generalize it for higher degrees. This method works well for cubic and [quartic equations](wikipedia://en/251591), but Lagrange did not succeed in applying it to a [quintic equation](wikipedia://en/303368), because it requires solving a resolvent polynomial of degree at least six.[^o70][^o71][^o72] Apart from the fact that nobody had previously succeeded, this was the first indication of the non-existence of an algebraic formula for degrees 5 and higher; as was later proved by the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518). Nevertheless, modern methods for solving solvable quintic equations are mainly based on Lagrange's method.[^o72]

In the case of cubic equations, Lagrange's method gives the same solution as Cardano's. Lagrange's method can be applied directly to the general cubic equation $ax^{3} + bx^{2} + cx + d = 0$, but the computation is simpler with the depressed cubic equation, $t^{3} + pt + q = 0$.

Lagrange's main idea was to work with the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) of the roots instead of with the roots themselves. More precisely, let $\xi$ be a [primitive third root of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity), that is a number such that $\xi^{3} = 1$ and $\xi^{2} + \xi + 1 = 0$ (when working in the space of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), one has $\textstyle \xi=\frac{-1\pm i\sqrt 3}2=e^{2i\pi/3},$ but this complex interpretation is not used here). Denoting $x_{0}$, $x_{1}$ and $x_{2}$ the three roots of the cubic equation to be solved, let

$$\begin{aligned} s_0 &= x_0 + x_1 + x_2,\\ s_1 &= x_0 + \xi x_1 + \xi^2 x_2,\\ s_2 &= x_0 + \xi^2 x_1 + \xi x_2, \end{aligned}$$

be the discrete Fourier transform of the roots. If $s_{0}$, $s_{1}$ and $s_{2}$ are known, the roots may be recovered from them with the inverse Fourier transform consisting of inverting this linear transformation; that is,

$$\begin{aligned} x_0 &= \tfrac13(s_0 + s_1 + s_2),\\ x_1 &= \tfrac13(s_0 + \xi^2 s_1 + \xi s_2),\\ x_2 &= \tfrac13(s_0 + \xi s_1 + \xi ^2 s_2). \end{aligned}$$

By [Vieta's formulas](wikipedia://en/714050), $s_{0}$ is known to be zero in the case of a depressed cubic, and $-\tfrac{b}{a}$ for the general cubic. So, only $s_{1}$ and $s_{2}$ need to be computed. They are not [symmetric functions](wikipedia://en/1440207) of the roots (exchanging $x_{1}$ and $x_{2}$ exchanges also $s_{1}$ and $s_{2}$), but some simple symmetric functions of $s_{1}$ and $s_{2}$ are also symmetric in the roots of the cubic equation to be solved. Thus these symmetric functions can be expressed in terms of the (known) coefficients of the original cubic, and this allows eventually expressing the $s_{i}$ as roots of a polynomial with known coefficients. This works well for every degree, but, in degrees higher than four, the resulting polynomial that has the $s_{i}$ as roots has a degree higher than that of the initial polynomial, and is therefore unhelpful for solving. This is the reason for which Lagrange's method fails in degrees five and higher.

In the case of a cubic equation, $P=s_1s_2,$ and $S=s_1^3+s_2^3$ are such symmetric polynomials (see below). It follows that $s_1^3$ and $s_2^3$ are the two roots of the quadratic equation $z^2-Sz+P^3=0.$ Thus the resolution of the equation may be finished exactly as with Cardano's method, with $s_1$ and $s_2$ in place of $u$ and $v.$

In the case of the depressed cubic, one has $x_0=\tfrac 13 (s_1+s_2)$ and $s_1s_2=-3p,$ while in Cardano's method we have set $x_0=u+v$ and $uv=-\tfrac 13 p.$ Thus, up to the exchange of $u$ and $v,$ we have $s_1=3u$ and $s_2=3v.$ In other words, in this case, Cardano's method and Lagrange's method compute exactly the same things, up to a factor of three in the auxiliary variables, the main difference being that Lagrange's method explains why these auxiliary variables appear in the problem.

::: {#o74 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Computation of and"}

#### Computation of $S$ and $P$ {#Computation_of_and}

A straightforward computation using the relations $\xi^{3} = 1$ and $\xi^{2} + \xi + 1 = 0$ gives

$$\begin{aligned} P&=s_1s_2=x_0^2+x_1^2+x_2^2-(x_0x_1+x_1x_2+x_2x_0),\\ S&=s_1^3+s_2^3=2(x_0^3+x_1^3+x_2^3)-3(x_0^2x_1+x_1^2x_2+x_2^2x_0+x_0x_1^2+x_1x_2^2+x_2x_0^2)+12x_0x_1x_2. \end{aligned}$$

This shows that $P$ and $S$ are symmetric functions of the roots. Using [Newton's identities](wikipedia://en/2468892), it is straightforward to express them in terms of the [elementary symmetric functions](wikipedia://en/1440695) of the roots, giving

$$\begin{aligned} P&=e_1^2-3e_2,\\ S&=2e_1^3-9e_1e_2+27e_3, \end{aligned}$$

with $e_{1} = 0$, $e_{2} = p$ and $e_{3} = -q$ in the case of a depressed cubic, and $e_{1} = -\tfrac{b}{a}$, $e_{2} = \tfrac{c}{a}$ and $e_{3} = -\tfrac{d}{a}$, in the general case.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

Cubic equations arise in various other contexts.

### In mathematics {#In_mathematics}

- [Angle trisection](wikipedia://en/91111) and [doubling the cube](wikipedia://en/91110) are two ancient problems of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) that have been proved to not be solvable by [straightedge and compass construction](wikipedia://en/61229), because they are equivalent to solving a cubic equation.
- [Marden's theorem](wikipedia://en/17185722) states that the [foci](wikipedia://en/1032607) of the [Steiner inellipse](wikipedia://en/17185864) of any triangle can be found by using the cubic function whose roots are the coordinates in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) of the triangle's three vertices. The roots of the [first derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_derivative) of this cubic are the complex coordinates of those foci.
- The [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of a regular [heptagon](wikipedia://en/365349) can be expressed in terms of the roots of a cubic. Further, the ratios of the long diagonal to the side, the side to the short diagonal, and the negative of the short diagonal to the long diagonal all satisfy a particular cubic equation. In addition, the ratio of the [inradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inradius) to the [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) of a [heptagonal triangle](wikipedia://en/50790424) is one of the solutions of a cubic equation. The values of trigonometric functions of angles related to $2\pi/7$ satisfy cubic equations.
- Given the cosine (or other trigonometric function) of an arbitrary angle, the cosine of [one-third of that angle](wikipedia://en/91111) is one of the roots of a cubic.
- The solution of the general [quartic equation](wikipedia://en/251591) relies on the solution of its [resolvent cubic](wikipedia://en/25975323).
- The [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of a 3×3 [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) are the roots of a cubic polynomial which is the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of the matrix.
- The [characteristic equation](wikipedia://en/31050469) of a third-order constant coefficients or [Cauchy–Euler](wikipedia://en/1038257) (equidimensional variable coefficients) [linear differential equation](wikipedia://en/379868) or [difference equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_equation) is a cubic equation.
- Intersection points of cubic [Bézier curve](wikipedia://en/4664) and straight line can be computed using direct cubic equation representing Bézier curve.
- [Critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) of a [quartic function](wikipedia://en/308152) are found by solving a cubic equation (the derivative set equal to zero).
- [Inflection points](wikipedia://en/379845) of a [quintic function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintic_function) are the solution of a cubic equation (the second derivative set equal to zero).

### In other sciences {#In_other_sciences}

- In [analytical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_chemistry), the [Charlot equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charlot_equation), which can be used to find the pH of [buffer solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buffer_solution), can be solved using a cubic equation.
- In [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952), [equations of state](wikipedia://en/9908#Cubic_equations_of_state) (which relate pressure, volume, and temperature of a substances), e.g. the [Van der Waals equation of state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubic_equations_of_state#Van_der_Waals_equation_of_state), are cubic in the volume.
- [Kinematic equations](wikipedia://en/65914) involving linear [rates of acceleration](wikipedia://en/16290) are cubic.
- The speed of seismic Rayleigh waves is a solution of the [Rayleigh wave](wikipedia://en/20756012#Rayleigh_wave_dispersion) cubic equation.
- The steady state speed of a vehicle moving on a slope with air friction for a given input power is solved by a depressed cubic equation.
- [Kepler's third law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler%27s_laws_of_planetary_motion#Third_law) of planetary motion is cubic in the semi-major axis.
