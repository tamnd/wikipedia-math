# Quotient ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quotient ring"}

In [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), a branch of [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **quotient ring**, also known as **factor ring**, **difference ring**[^o2] or **residue class ring**, is a construction quite similar to the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) and to the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422).[^o3][^o4] It is a specific example of a [quotient](wikipedia://en/310953), as viewed from the general setting of [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200). Starting with a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$ and a [two-sided ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-sided_ideal) $I$ in $R$, a new ring, the quotient ring $R\ /\ I$, is constructed, whose elements are the [cosets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosets) of $I$ in $R$ subject to $+$ and $\cdot$ operations compatible with $+$ and $\cdot$ in $R$. Quotient rings are denoted as a fraction, usually using the [fraction slash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_slash) "$/$" as the separator. The alternative of stacking the ring over the ideal using a horizontal line (i.e., the fraction bar) as a separator is sometimes used if the expressions for the ring or the ideal are long or cumbersome.

:::

Quotient rings are distinct from the "quotient field", or [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271), of an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) as well as from the more general "rings of quotients" obtained by [localization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring).

## Formal quotient ring construction {#Formal_quotient_ring_construction}

Given a ring $R$ and a two-sided ideal $I$ in $R$, we may define an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $\sim$ on $R$ as follows:

$a \sim b$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $a - b$ is in $I$.

Using the ideal properties, it is not difficult to check that $\sim$ is a [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167). In case $a \sim b$, we say that $a$ and $b$ are *congruent [modulo](wikipedia://en/25977)* $I.$ For example, in $\mathbb{Z},$ $16$ and $10$ are congruent modulo $(3)$, as their difference $6$ is an element of the ideal $(3)=3\mathbb{Z}.$[^o6]

There are several different notations for the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of the element $a$ in $R$:

$$\left[ a \right] = \overline{a} = a\ \operatorname{mod}\ I = a + I := \left\lbrace a + r : r \in I \right\rbrace$$

The notation employing the infix "mod" is a generalization of the notation for residue classes in [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087) and leads to the terminology "residue class of $a$ modulo $I$" for this equivalence class.

The set of all such equivalence classes is denoted by $R\ /\ I$; it becomes a ring, the **factor ring** or **quotient ring** of $R$ modulo $I$, if one defines

- $$(a + I) + (b + I) = (a + b) + I;$$
- $$(a + I)(b + I) = (ab) + I.$$

(Here one has to check that these definitions are [well-defined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-defined). Compare [coset](wikipedia://en/98759) and [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526).) The zero-element of $R\ /\ I$ is $\bar{0} = 0 + I = I$, and the multiplicative identity is $\bar{1} = 1 + I$.

The map $p$ from $R$ to $R\ /\ I$ defined by $p(a) = a + I$ is a [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411), sometimes called the ***natural quotient map***, ***natural projection map***, or the ***[canonical homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_homomorphism)***.

## Examples {#Examples}

- The quotient ring $R\ /\ \lbrace 0 \rbrace$ is [naturally isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturally_isomorphic) to $R$, and $R / R$ is the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) $\lbrace 0 \rbrace$, since, by our definition, for any $r \in R$, we have that $\left[ r \right] = r + R = \left\lbrace r + b : b \in R \right\rbrace$, which equals $R$ itself. This fits with the rule of thumb that the larger the ideal $I$, the smaller the quotient ring $R\ /\ I$. If $I$ is a proper ideal of $R$, i.e., $I \neq R$, then $R / I$ is not the zero ring.
- Consider the ring of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ and the ideal of even numbers, denoted by $2 \mathbb{Z}$. Then the quotient ring $\mathbb{Z} / 2 \mathbb{Z}$ has only two elements, the coset $0 + 2 \mathbb{Z}$ consisting of the even numbers and the coset $1 + 2 \mathbb{Z}$ consisting of the odd numbers; applying the definition, $\left[ z \right] = z + 2 \mathbb{Z} = \left\lbrace z + 2y : 2y \in 2\mathbb{Z} \right\rbrace$, where $2 \mathbb{Z}$ is the ideal of even numbers. It is naturally isomorphic to the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with two elements, $F_{2}$. Intuitively: if you think of all the even numbers as $0$, then every integer is either $0$ (if it is even) or $1$ (if it is odd and therefore differs from an even number by $1$). [Modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087) is essentially arithmetic in the quotient ring $\mathbb{Z} / n \mathbb{Z}$ (which has $n$ elements).
- Now consider the [ring of polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_polynomials) in the variable $X$ with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203), $\mathbb{R} [X]$, and the ideal $I = \left( X^2 + 1 \right)$ consisting of all multiples of the polynomial $X^2 + 1$. The quotient ring $\mathbb{R} [X]\ /\ ( X^2 + 1 )$ is naturally isomorphic to the field of complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$, with the class $[X]$ playing the role of the [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) $i$. The reason is that we "forced" $X^2 + 1 = 0$, i.e. $X^2 = -1$, which is the defining property of $i$. Since any integer exponent of $i$ must be either $\pm i$ or $\pm 1$, that means all possible polynomials essentially simplify to the form $a + bi$. (To clarify, the quotient ring $\mathbb{R} [X]\ /\ ( X^2 + 1 )$ is actually naturally isomorphic to the field of all linear polynomials $aX + b; a,b \in \mathbb{R}$, where the operations are performed modulo $X^2 + 1$. In return, we have $X^2 = -1$, and this is matching $X$ to the imaginary unit in the isomorphic field of complex numbers.)
- Generalizing the previous example, quotient rings are often used to construct [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634). Suppose $K$ is some [field](wikipedia://en/10603) and $f$ is an [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) in $K[X]$. Then $L = K[X]\ /\ (f)$ is a field whose [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) over $K$ is $f$, which contains $K$ as well as an element $x = X + (f)$.
- One important instance of the previous example is the construction of the finite fields. Consider for instance the field $F_3 = \mathbb{Z} / 3\mathbb{Z}$ with three elements. The polynomial $f(X) = X^2 +1$ is irreducible over $F_3$ (since it has no root), and we can construct the quotient ring $F_3 [X]\ /\ (f)$. This is a field with $3^2 = 9$ elements, denoted by $F_9$. The other finite fields can be constructed in a similar fashion.
- The [coordinate rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) are important examples of quotient rings in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). As a simple case, consider the real variety $V = \left\lbrace (x,y) | x^2 = y^3 \right\rbrace$ as a subset of the real plane $\mathbb{R}^2$. The ring of real-valued polynomial functions defined on $V$ can be identified with the quotient ring $\mathbb{R} [X,Y]\ /\ (X^2 - Y^3)$, and this is the coordinate ring of $V$. The variety $V$ is now investigated by studying its coordinate ring.
- Suppose $M$ is a $\mathbb{C}^{\infty}$-[manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), and $p$ is a point of $M$. Consider the ring $R = \mathbb{C}^{\infty}(M)$ of all $\mathbb{C}^{\infty}$-functions defined on $M$ and let $I$ be the ideal in $R$ consisting of those functions $f$ which are identically zero in some [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29) $U$ of $p$ (where $U$ may depend on $f$). Then the quotient ring $R\ /\ I$ is the ring of [germs](wikipedia://en/352714) of $\mathbb{C}^{\infty}$-functions on $M$ at $p$.
- Consider the ring $F$ of finite elements of a [hyperreal field](wikipedia://en/51429) $^* \mathbb{R}$. It consists of all hyperreal numbers differing from a standard real by an infinitesimal amount, or equivalently: of all hyperreal numbers $x$ for which a standard integer $n$ with $-n < x < n$ exists. The set $I$ of all infinitesimal numbers in $^* \mathbb{R}$, together with $0$, is an ideal in $F$, and the quotient ring $F\ /\ I$ is isomorphic to the real numbers $\mathbb{R}$. The isomorphism is induced by associating to every element $x$ of $F$ the [standard part](wikipedia://en/19145800) of $x$, i.e. the unique real number that differs from $x$ by an infinitesimal. In fact, one obtains the same result, namely $\mathbb{R}$, if one starts with the ring $F$ of finite hyperrationals (i.e. ratio of a pair of [hyperintegers](wikipedia://en/19327282)), see [construction of the real numbers](wikipedia://en/296666).

### Real quadratic algebras {#Real_quadratic_algebras}

The quotients $\mathbb{R} [X] / (X)$, $\mathbb{R} [X] / (X + 1)$, and $\mathbb{R} [X] / (X - 1)$ are all isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$ and gain little interest at first. But note that $\mathbb{R} [X] / (X^2)$ is called the [dual number](wikipedia://en/42169) plane in geometric algebra. It consists only of linear binomials as "remainders" after reducing an element of $\mathbb{R} [X]$ by $X^2$. This quadratic algebra arises as a [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239) whenever the algebra contains a [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) and a [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235).

Furthermore, the ring quotient $\mathbb{R} [X] / (X^2 - 1)$ does split into $\mathbb{R} [X] / (X + 1)$ and $\mathbb{R} [X] / (X - 1)$, so this algebra is often viewed as the [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_algebras) $\mathbb{R} \oplus \mathbb{R}$. Nevertheless, a quadratic algebra $z = x + yj$ is defined by $j$ as an element of the ideal of $(X^2 - 1)$, compared to $i$ as root of $X^2 + 1 = 0$. This plane of [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559) normalizes the direct sum $\mathbb{R} \oplus \mathbb{R}$ by providing a basis $\left\lbrace 1, j \right\rbrace$ for 2-space where the identity of the algebra is at unit distance from the zero. With this basis a [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) may be compared to the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) of the [ordinary complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). The unit hyperbola finds applications where the circle must be replaced with the hyperbola for purposes of analytic geometry.

### Quaternions and variations {#Quaternions_and_variations}

Suppose $X$ and $Y$ are two non-commuting [indeterminates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indeterminate_%28variable%29) and form the [free algebra](wikipedia://en/494669) $\mathbb{R} \langle X, Y \rangle$. Then Hamilton's [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) of 1843 can be cast as:

$$\mathbb{R} \langle X, Y \rangle / ( X^2 + 1,\, Y^2 + 1,\, XY + YX )$$

If $Y^2 - 1$ is substituted for $Y^2 + 1$, then one obtains the ring of [split-quaternions](wikipedia://en/1461265). The [anti-commutative property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-commutative_property) $YX = -XY$ implies that $XY$ has as its square:

$$(XY) (XY) = X (YX) Y = -X (XY) Y = -(XX) (YY) = -(-1)(+1) = +1$$

Substituting minus for plus in *both* the quadratic binomials also results in split-quaternions.

The three types of [biquaternions](wikipedia://en/1207070) can also be written as quotients by use of the [free algebra](wikipedia://en/494669) with three indeterminates $\mathbb{R} \langle X, Y, Z \rangle$ and constructing appropriate ideals.

## Properties {#Properties}

Clearly, if $R$ is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), then so is $R\ /\ I$; the converse, however, is not necessarily true.

The natural quotient map $p$ has $I$ as its [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240); since the kernel of every ring homomorphism is a two-sided ideal, we can state that two-sided ideals are precisely the kernels of ring homomorphisms.

The intimate relationship between ring homomorphisms, kernels and quotient rings can be summarized as follows: the ring homomorphisms defined on $R\ /\ I$ are essentially the same as the ring homomorphisms defined on $R$ that vanish (i.e. are zero) on $I$. More precisely, given a two-sided ideal $I$ in $R$ and a ring homomorphism $f : R \to S$ whose kernel contains $I$, there exists precisely one ring homomorphism $g : R\ /\ I \to S$ with $gp = f$ (where $p$ is the natural quotient map). The map $g$ here is given by the well-defined rule $g([a]) = f(a)$ for all $a$ in $1 R$. Indeed, this [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) can be used to *define* quotient rings and their natural quotient maps.

As a consequence of the above, one obtains the fundamental statement: every ring homomorphism $f : R \to S$ induces a [ring isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism) between the quotient ring $R\ /\ \ker (f)$ and the image $\mathrm{im} (f)$. (See also: *[Fundamental theorem on homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/11527)*.)

The ideals of $R$ and $R\ /\ I$ are closely related: the natural quotient map provides a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the two-sided ideals of $R$ that contain $I$ and the two-sided ideals of $R\ /\ I$ (the same is true for left and for right ideals). This relationship between two-sided ideal extends to a relationship between the corresponding quotient rings: if $M$ is a two-sided ideal in $R$ that contains $I$, and we write $M\ /\ I$ for the corresponding ideal in $R\ /\ I$ (i.e. $M\ /\ I = p(M)$), the quotient rings $R\ /\ M$ and $(R / I)\ /\ (M / I)$ are naturally isomorphic via the (well-defined) mapping $a + M \mapsto (a + I) + M / I$.

The following facts prove useful in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997): for $R \neq \lbrace 0 \rbrace$ commutative, $R\ /\ I$ is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) if and only if $I$ is a [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164), while $R / I$ is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) if and only if $I$ is a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928). A number of similar statements relate properties of the ideal $I$ to properties of the quotient ring $R\ /\ I$.

The [Chinese remainder theorem](wikipedia://en/7713) states that, if the ideal $I$ is the intersection (or equivalently, the product) of pairwise [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime#Generalizations) ideals $I_1, \ldots, I_k$, then the quotient ring $R\ /\ I$ is isomorphic to the [product](wikipedia://en/25063) of the quotient rings $R\ /\ I_n,\; n = 1, \ldots, k$.

## For algebras over a ring {#For_algebras_over_a_ring}

An [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) $A$ over a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $R$ is itself a ring. If $I$ is an ideal in $A$ (closed under $A$-multiplication: $AI \subseteq I$), then $A / I$ inherits the structure of an algebra over $R$ and is the **quotient algebra**.

:::
