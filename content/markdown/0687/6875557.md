# Complete manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complete manifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **complete manifold** (or **geodesically complete manifold**) $M$ is a ([pseudo](wikipedia://en/287555)-) [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) for which, starting at any point $p$ of $M$, there are straight paths extending infinitely in all directions.

:::

Formally, a manifold $M$ is (geodesically) complete if for any maximal [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) $\ell : I \to M$, it holds that $I=(-\infty,\infty)$.[^n1] A geodesic is **maximal** if its domain cannot be extended.

Equivalently, $M$ is (geodesically) complete if for all points $p \in M$, the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/276548) at $p$ is defined on $T_pM$, the entire [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at $p$.[^n1]

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Hopf–Rinow theorem"}

## Hopf–Rinow theorem {#Hopf–Rinow_theorem}

The [Hopf–Rinow theorem](wikipedia://en/681265) gives alternative characterizations of completeness. Let $(M,g)$ be a *connected* Riemannian manifold and let $d_g : M \times M \to [0,\infty)$ be its [Riemannian distance function](wikipedia://en/144652#Metric_space_structure).

The Hopf–Rinow theorem states that $(M,g)$ is (geodesically) complete if and only if it satisfies one of the following equivalent conditions:[^n2]

- The metric space $(M,d_g)$ is [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) (every $d_g$-[Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) converges),
- All closed and bounded subsets of $M$ are [compact](wikipedia://en/6042).

:::

## Examples and non-examples {#Examples_and_non-examples}

[Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$, the [sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) $\mathbb{S}^n$, and the [tori](wikipedia://en/74800) $\mathbb{T}^n$ (with their natural [Riemannian metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric)) are all complete manifolds.

All [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) Riemannian manifolds and all [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/363325) manifolds are geodesically complete. All [symmetric spaces](wikipedia://en/1642877) are geodesically complete.

::: {#o9 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-examples"}

### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

*Image: Punctured plane is not geodesically complete.svg.* The punctured plane $\mathbb R^2 \backslash \{(0,0)\}$ is not geodesically complete because the maximal geodesic with initial conditions $p = (1,1)$, $v = (1,1)$ does not have domain $\mathbb R$.

A simple example of a non-complete manifold is given by the punctured plane $\mathbb{R}^2 \smallsetminus \lbrace 0 \rbrace$ (with its induced metric). Geodesics going to the origin cannot be defined on the entire real line. By the Hopf–Rinow theorem, we can alternatively observe that it is not a complete metric space: any sequence in the plane converging to the origin is a non-converging Cauchy sequence in the punctured plane.

There exist non-geodesically complete compact pseudo-Riemannian (but not Riemannian) manifolds. An example of this is the [Clifton–Pohl torus](wikipedia://en/34242725).

In the theory of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), which describes gravity in terms of a pseudo-Riemannian geometry, many important examples of geodesically incomplete spaces arise, e.g. [non-rotating uncharged black-holes](wikipedia://en/264606) or cosmologies with a [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116). The fact that such incompleteness is fairly generic in general relativity is shown in the [Penrose–Hawking singularity theorems](wikipedia://en/294408).

:::

## Extendibility {#Extendibility}

If $M$ is geodesically complete, then it is not isometric to an open proper submanifold of any other Riemannian manifold. The converse does not hold.[^n3]
