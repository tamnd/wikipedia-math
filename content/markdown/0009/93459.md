# Karl Weierstrass

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Karl Weierstrass"}

**Karl Theodor Wilhelm Weierstrass** (;[^o3] Weierstraß ;[^o4] 31 October 1815 – 19 February 1897) was a German [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) often cited as the "[father of](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_people_considered_father_or_mother_of_a_field) modern [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396)".[^o5] Despite leaving university without a degree, he studied mathematics and trained as a school teacher, eventually teaching [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany) and [gymnastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnastics).[^o6] He later received an honorary doctorate and became professor of mathematics in Berlin.

:::

Among many other contributions, Weierstrass formalized the definition of the [continuity of a function](wikipedia://en/6122) and [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), proved the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884) and the [Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/31211), and used the latter to study the properties of continuous functions on closed bounded intervals.

## Biography {#Biography}

Weierstrass was born into a [Roman Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Catholic) family in Ostenfelde, a village near [Ennigerloh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ennigerloh), in the [Province of Westphalia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Province_of_Westphalia).[^o8]

Karl Weierstrass was the son of Wilhelm Weierstrass and Theodora Vonderforst, the former of whom was a government official and both of whom were Catholic [Rhinelanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhineland). His interest in mathematics began while he was a [gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28Germany%29) student at the [Theodorianum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_Theodorianum) in [Paderborn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paderborn). He was sent to the [University of Bonn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bonn) upon graduation, to prepare for a government position; to this end, his studies were to be in the fields of law, economics, and finance—a situation immediately in conflict with his own hopes to study mathematics. He resolved the conflict by paying little heed to his planned course of study but continuing to study mathematics in private, which ultimately resulted in his leaving the university without a degree.

Weierstrass continued to study mathematics at the [Münster Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Münster) (an institution even then famous for mathematics), and his father was able to obtain a place for him in a teacher-training school in [Münster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Münster); his efforts there did, eventually, lead to his certification as a teacher in that city. During this period of study, Weierstrass attended the lectures of [Christoph Gudermann](wikipedia://en/1503772) and became interested in [elliptic functions](wikipedia://en/69939).

In 1843 he taught in [Deutsch Krone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wałcz) in [West Prussia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Prussia), and from 1848 he taught at the [Lyceum Hosianum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collegium_Hosianum) in [Braunsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braunsberg).[^o9] Besides mathematics, he also taught physics, botany, and gymnastics.[^o8] At some point, Weierstrass may have had an illegitimate child with the widow of his friend [Carl Wilhelm Borchardt](wikipedia://en/482522).[^o10]

After 1850, Weierstrass suffered from a long period of illness, but was yet able to publish mathematical articles of sufficient quality and originality to bring him fame and distinction. The [University of Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Königsberg) conferred an [honorary doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honorary_doctorate) on him on 31 March 1854. In 1856 he took a chair at the *Gewerbeinstitut* in Berlin (an institute to educate technical workers, which would later merge with the *Bauakademie* to form the *[Technische Hochschule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Hochschule)* in Charlottenburg; now [Technische Universität Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technische_Universität_Berlin)). In 1864 he became professor at the Friedrich-Wilhelms-Universität Berlin, which later became the [Humboldt Universität zu Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humboldt_University_of_Berlin).

In 1870, at the age of fifty-five, Weierstrass met [Sofia Kovalevskaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sofia_Kovalevskaya) whom he tutored privately after failing to secure her admission to the university. They had a fruitful intellectual, and kindly personal relationship that "far transcended the usual teacher-student relationship". He mentored her for four years, and regarded her as his best student, helping to secure her a doctorate from Heidelberg University without the need for an oral thesis defense.

From 1870 until her death in 1891, Kovalevskaya corresponded with Weierstrass. Upon learning of her death, he burned her letters. About 150 of his letters to her have been preserved. Professor Reinhard Bölling discovered the draft of the letter she wrote to Weierstrass when she arrived in Stockholm in 1883 upon her appointment as *[Privatdocent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privatdocent)* at [Stockholm University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stockholm_University).[^o11]

Weierstrass was immobile for the last three years of his life, and died in Berlin from [pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia) on the 19th of February, 1897.[^o12]

## Mathematical contributions {#Mathematical_contributions}

### Soundness of calculus {#Soundness_of_calculus}

Weierstrass was interested in the [soundness](wikipedia://en/26819) of calculus, and at the time there were somewhat ambiguous definitions of the foundations of calculus so that important theorems could not be proven with sufficient rigour. Although [Bolzano](wikipedia://en/302185) had developed a reasonably rigorous definition of a [limit](wikipedia://en/285759) as early as 1817 (and possibly even earlier) his work remained unknown to most of the mathematical community until years later, and many mathematicians had only vague definitions of [limits](wikipedia://en/285759) and [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) of functions.

The basic idea behind [Delta-epsilon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%28ε,_δ%29-definition_of_limit) proofs is, arguably, first found in the works of [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) in the 1820s.[^o15][^o16] Cauchy did not clearly distinguish between continuity and uniform continuity on an interval. Notably, in his 1821 *Cours d'analyse,* Cauchy argued that the (pointwise) limit of (pointwise) continuous functions was itself (pointwise) continuous, a statement that is false in general. The correct statement is rather that the [*uniform* limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_limit) of continuous functions is continuous (also, the uniform limit of uniformly continuous functions is uniformly continuous). This required the concept of [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652), which was first observed by Weierstrass's advisor, [Christoph Gudermann](wikipedia://en/1503772), in an 1838 paper, where Gudermann noted the phenomenon but did not define it or elaborate on it. Weierstrass saw the importance of the concept, and both formalized it and applied it widely throughout the foundations of calculus.

The formal definition of continuity of a function, as formulated by Weierstrass, is as follows:

$\displaystyle f(x)$ is continuous at $\displaystyle x = x_0$ if $\displaystyle \forall \ \varepsilon > 0\ \exists\ \delta > 0$ such that for every $x$ in the domain of $f$, $\displaystyle \ |x-x_0| < \delta \Rightarrow |f(x) - f(x_0)| < \varepsilon.$ In simple English, $\displaystyle f(x)$ is continuous at a point $\displaystyle x = x_0$ if for each $x$ close enough to $x_0$, the function value $f(x)$ is very close to $f(x_0)$, where the "close enough" restriction typically depends on the desired closeness of $f(x_0)$ to $f(x).$ Using this definition, he proved the [Intermediate Value Theorem.](wikipedia://en/14884) He also proved the [Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/31211) and used it to study the properties of continuous functions on closed and bounded intervals.

### Calculus of variations {#Calculus_of_variations}

Weierstrass also made advances in the field of [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882). Using the apparatus of analysis that he helped to develop, Weierstrass was able to give a complete reformulation of the theory that paved the way for the modern study of the calculus of variations. Among several axioms, Weierstrass established a necessary condition for the existence of [strong extrema](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_extrema) of variational problems. He also helped devise the [Weierstrass–Erdmann condition](wikipedia://en/22091926), which gives sufficient conditions for an extremal to have a corner along a given extremum and allows one to find a minimizing curve for a given integral.

## Honours and awards {#Honours_and_awards}

The lunar [crater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater) [Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_%28crater%29) and the [asteroid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asteroid) [14100 Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/14100_Weierstrass) are named after him. Also, there is the [Weierstrass Institute for Applied Analysis and Stochastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_Institute_for_Applied_Analysis_and_Stochastics) in Berlin.
