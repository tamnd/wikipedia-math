[Quadratic residues](wikipedia://en/200091) are highlighted in yellow — note that no entry with a Jacobi symbol of −1 is a quadratic residue, and if *k* is a quadratic residue modulo a coprime *n*, then ($\tfrac{k}{n}$) = 1, but not all entries with a Jacobi symbol of 1 (see the *n* = 9 and *n* = 15 rows) are quadratic residues. Notice also that when either *n* or *k* is a square, all values are nonnegative.

The **Jacobi symbol** is a generalization of the [Legendre symbol](wikipedia://en/18567). Introduced by [Jacobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gustav_Jakob_Jacobi) in 1837,[^o2] it is of theoretical interest in [modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087) and other branches of [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), but its main use is in [computational number theory](wikipedia://en/511466), especially [primality testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primality_testing) and [integer factorization](wikipedia://en/15491); these in turn are important in [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432).

## Definition {#Definition}

For any integer *a* and any positive odd integer *n*, the Jacobi symbol ($\tfrac{a}{n}$) is defined as the product of the [Legendre symbols](wikipedia://en/18567) corresponding to the prime factors of *n*:

$$\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) := \left(\frac{a}{p_1}\right)^{\alpha_1}\left(\frac{a}{p_2}\right)^{\alpha_2}\cdots \left(\frac{a}{p_k}\right)^{\alpha_k},$$

where

$$n=p_1^{\alpha_1}p_2^{\alpha_2}\cdots p_k^{\alpha_k}$$

is the prime factorization of *n*.

The Legendre symbol ($\tfrac{a}{p}$) is defined for all integers *a* and all odd primes *p* by

$$\left(\frac{a}{p}\right) := \left\{ \begin{array}{rl} 0 & \text{if } a \equiv 0 \pmod{p},\\ 1 & \text{if } a \not\equiv 0\pmod{p} \text{ and for some integer } x\colon\;a\equiv x^2\pmod{p},\\ -1 & \text{if } a \not\equiv 0\pmod{p} \text{ and there is no such } x. \end{array} \right.$$

Following the normal convention for the [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289), ($\tfrac{a}{1}$) = 1.

When the lower argument is an odd prime, the Jacobi symbol is equal to the Legendre symbol.

## Properties {#Properties}

The following facts, even the reciprocity laws, are straightforward deductions from the definition of the Jacobi symbol and the corresponding properties of the Legendre symbol.[^o9]

The Jacobi symbol is defined only when the upper argument ("numerator") is an integer and the lower argument ("denominator") is a positive odd integer.

\1. If *n* is (an odd) prime, then the Jacobi symbol ($\tfrac{a}{n}$) is equal to (and written the same as) the corresponding Legendre symbol.

\2. If *a* ≡ *b* (mod *n*), then $\biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr) = \left(\frac{b}{n}\right) = \left(\frac{a \pm k \cdot n}{n}\right).$

\3. $\biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr) = \begin{cases} \, 0 & \text{if } \gcd(a,n) \ne 1,\\ \pm1 & \text{if } \gcd(a,n) = 1. \end{cases}$

If either the top or bottom argument is fixed, the Jacobi symbol is a [completely multiplicative function](wikipedia://en/1055940) in the remaining argument:

\4. $\;\left(\frac{ab}{n}\right)\; = \biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr)\left(\frac{b}{n}\right),\quad\text{so } \left(\frac{a^2}{n}\right) = \biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr)^2 = +1 \text{ or } 0.$

\5. $\biggl(\frac{a}{mn}\biggr)=\biggl(\frac{a}{m}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr),\quad\text{so } \left(\frac{a}{n^2}\right) = \biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr)^2 = +1 \text{ or } 0.$

The [law of quadratic reciprocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_quadratic_reciprocity): if *m* and *n* are odd positive [coprime integers](wikipedia://en/6556), then

\6. $\biggl(\frac{m}{n}\biggr)\biggl(\frac{n}{m}\biggr) = (-1)^{\tfrac{m-1}{2}\cdot\tfrac{n-1}{2}} = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if } n \equiv 1 \text{ or } m \equiv 1 \pmod 4,\\ -1 & \text{if } n \equiv m \equiv 3 \pmod 4. \end{cases}$

and its supplements

\7. $\;\left(\frac{1}{n}\right)\; = \biggl(\frac{n}{1}\biggr) = +1.$

\8. $\left(\frac{-1}{n}\right) = (-1)^\tfrac{n-1}{2} \,= \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if }n \equiv 1 \pmod 4,\\ -1 & \text{if }n \equiv 3 \pmod 4. \end{cases}$

\9. $\;\left(\frac{2}{n}\right)\; = (-1)^\tfrac{n^2-1}{8} = \begin{cases} +1 & \text{if }n \equiv 1,7 \pmod 8,\\ -1 & \text{if }n \equiv 3,5 \pmod 8. \end{cases}$

Combining properties 4 and 9 gives:

\10. $\left(\frac{2a}{n}\right) = \left(\frac{2}{n}\right) \biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr) = \begin{cases} \phantom{-}\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) & \text{if }n \equiv 1,7 \pmod 8,\\ - \left(\frac{a}{n}\right) & \text{if }n \equiv 3, 5\pmod 8. \end{cases}$

Combining properties 2, 6 and 10 gives:

\11. If *n* ≡ *m* (mod 4*a*), then $\biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr) = \biggl(\frac{a}{m}\biggr) = \biggl(\frac{a}{n + 4k\left|a\right|}\biggr).$[^o10]

\12. $\biggl(\frac{a}{n+2\left|a\right|}\biggr) = \biggl(\frac{a}{n}\biggr)(-1)^\tfrac{a(a-1)}{2} = \begin{cases} \phantom{-}\left(\frac{a}{n}\right) & \text{if }a \equiv 0,1 \pmod 4,\\ - \left(\frac{a}{n}\right) & \text{if }a \equiv 2,3 \pmod 4. \end{cases}$

Like the Legendre symbol:

- If ($\tfrac{a}{n}$) = −1 then *a* is a quadratic nonresidue modulo *n*.
- If *a* is a [quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091) modulo *n* and [gcd](wikipedia://en/12354)(*a*,*n*) = 1, then ($\tfrac{a}{n}$) = +1.

But, unlike the Legendre symbol:

- If ($\tfrac{a}{n}$) = +1 then *a* may or may not be a quadratic residue modulo *n*.
- If *a* is a quadratic nonresidue modulo *n*, then ($\tfrac{a}{n}$) may be −1 or +1.

This is because for *a* to be a quadratic residue modulo *n*, it has to be a quadratic residue modulo *every* [prime factor](wikipedia://en/23666) of *n*. However, the Jacobi symbol equals +1 if, for example, *a* is a non-residue modulo exactly two of the prime factors of *n*.

Although the Jacobi symbol cannot be uniformly interpreted in terms of squares and non-squares, it can be uniformly interpreted as the sign of a permutation by [Zolotarev's lemma](wikipedia://en/2995566).

The Jacobi symbol ($\tfrac{a}{n}$) is a [Dirichlet character](wikipedia://en/174515) to the modulus *n*.

:::

## Calculating the Jacobi symbol {#Calculating_the_Jacobi_symbol}

The above formulas lead to an efficient [*O*](wikipedia://en/44578)(log *a* log *b*)[^o12] [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for calculating the Jacobi symbol, analogous to the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) for finding the gcd of two numbers. (This should not be surprising in light of rule 2.)

1. Reduce the "numerator" modulo the "denominator" using rule 2.
1. Extract any even "numerator" using rule 10.
1. If the "numerator" is 1, rules 3 and 4 give a result of 1. If the "numerator" and "denominator" are not coprime, rule 3 gives a result of 0.
1. Otherwise, the "numerator" and "denominator" are now odd positive coprime integers, so we can flip the symbol using rule 6, then return to step 1.

In addition to the codes below, Riesel[^o13] has it in [Pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_%28programming_language%29). The below implementations can be straightforwardly translated to any language with integer operations.

### Implementation in [Lua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lua_%28programming_language%29) {#Implementation_in_Lua}

```lua
function jacobi(a, n)
  assert(n > 0 and n % 2 == 1)
  a = a % n               -- step 1
  t = 1                   -- sign of return value
  while a ~= 0 do
    while a % 2 == 0 do   -- step 2, rule 10
      a = a / 2
      r = n % 8
      if r == 3 or r == 5 then
        t = -t
      end
    end
    if a % 4 == 3 and n % 4 == 3 then  -- step 4, rule 6: flip sign
      t = -t
    end
    a, n = n, a  -- ... swap. Rearranging step 6 as (a/n) = k * (n/a), where k is +1 or -1.
    a = a % n    -- and step 1 again
  end
  if n == 1 then           -- step 3. at this point, n = gcd(a, n) (as initially passed)
    return t
  else
    return 0
  end
end
```

(Lua uses a 64-bit signed integer type.)

### Implementation in [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29)/[C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++) {#Implementation_in_C/C++}

```c
// a/n is represented as (a,n)
int jacobi(int a, int n) {
    assert(n > 0 && n % 2 == 1);
    // Step 1
    a %= n;
    if (a < 0) a += n; // C returns negative (a%b) for negative a. Flip it to the mathematical modulo direction.
    int t = 0;         // sign of return value. XOR of bits 1 and 2 determines sign of return value
    while (a) {
        // Step 2
        while (a % 4 == 0) a /= 4; // shortcut
        if (a % 2 == 0) {
            a /= 2;
            t ^= n;  // Could be "^= n & 6"; we only care about bits 1 and 2
                     // This is an encoding of rule 10. Sign is flipped here if 2 <= n <= 5 (mod 8).
                     // Although n = 2,4 (mod 8) are also affected here, we know as a precondition n%2 == 1.
        }
        // Step 4
        t ^= a & n & 2;  // Flip sign by flipping bit 1 if a = n = 3 (mod 4). 
                         // (Technically checks whether both a and n \in [3,4] (mod 4), but again, the precondition.)
        int r = n % a;   // swap & step 1
        n = a;
        a = r;
    }
    // Step 3
    if (n != 1)
        return 0;
    else if ((t + 2) & 4)
        return -1;
    else
        return 1;
}
```

(The signed `int` type is only necessary on the return value; all other values may be instead unsigned integers.)

## Primality testing {#Primality_testing}

There is another way the Jacobi and Legendre symbols differ. If the [Euler's criterion](wikipedia://en/105622) formula is used modulo a [composite number](wikipedia://en/82289), the result may or may not be the value of the Jacobi symbol, and in fact may not even be −1 or 1. For example,

$$\begin{aligned} \left(\frac{19}{45}\right) &= 1 &&\text{ and } & 19^\frac{45-1}{2} &\equiv 1\pmod{45}. \\ \left(\frac{ 8}{21}\right) &= -1 &&\text{ but } & 8^\frac{21-1}{2} &\equiv 1\pmod{21}. \\ \left(\frac{ 5}{21}\right) &= 1 &&\text{ but } & 5^\frac{21-1}{2} &\equiv 16\pmod{21}. \end{aligned}$$

So if it is unknown whether a number *n* is prime or composite, we can pick a random number *a*, calculate the Jacobi symbol ($\tfrac{a}{n}$) and compare it with Euler's formula; if they differ modulo *n*, then *n* is composite; if they have the same residue modulo *n* for many different values of *a*, then *n* is "[probably prime](wikipedia://en/160560)".

This is the basis for the probabilistic [Solovay–Strassen primality test](wikipedia://en/1013089) and refinements such as the [Baillie–PSW primality test](wikipedia://en/3609890) and the [Miller–Rabin primality test](wikipedia://en/188999).

As an indirect use, it is possible to use it as an error detection routine during the execution of the [Lucas–Lehmer primality test](wikipedia://en/325739) which, even on modern [computer hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_hardware), can take weeks to complete when processing [Mersenne numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_number) over $\begin{aligned}2^{136,279,841} - 1\end{aligned}$ (the largest known Mersenne prime as of October 2024). In nominal cases, the Jacobi symbol:

$$\begin{aligned}\left(\frac{s_i - 2}{M_p}\right) &= -1 & i \ne 0\end{aligned}$$

This also holds for the final residue $\begin{aligned}s_{p-2}\end{aligned}$ and hence can be used as a verification of probable validity. However, if an error occurs in the hardware, there is a 50% chance that the result will become 0 or 1 instead, and won't change with subsequent terms of $\begin{aligned}s\end{aligned}$ (unless another error occurs and changes it back to −1).
