# János Bolyai

Bolyai János

*Image: Janos Bolyai memorial plaque.jpg.* Memorial plaque of János Bolyai in [Olomouc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olomouc), [Czech Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Czech_Republic)

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="János Bolyai"}

**János Bolyai** (;[^o4] ; 15 December 1802 – 27 January 1860) or **Johann Bolyai**,[^o5] was a [Hungarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hungarian_people) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who developed [absolute geometry](wikipedia://en/699294)—a geometry that includes both [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) and [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291). The discovery of a consistent alternative geometry that might correspond to the structure of the universe helped to free mathematicians to study abstract concepts irrespective of any possible connection with the physical world.[^o6]

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

*Image: Bolyai Janos szulohaza.jpg.* The house in [Cluj-Napoca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluj-Napoca) where János Bolyai was born

Bolyai was born in the town of Kolozsvár, [Grand Principality of Transylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_Principality_of_Transylvania), [Kingdom of Hungary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kingdom_of_Hungary) (now [Cluj-Napoca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluj-Napoca) in [Romania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romania)), the son of Zsuzsanna Benkő and the well-known mathematician [Farkas Bolyai](wikipedia://en/1159131).

By the age of 13, he had mastered [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and other forms of [analytical mechanics](wikipedia://en/454450), receiving instruction from his father. He studied at the [Imperial and Royal Military Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theresian_Military_Academy) (TherMilAk) in [Vienna](wikipedia://en/55866) from 1818 to 1822, and Bolyai received his commission as sub-lieutenant. At the age of 21, he was already a lieutenant, at the age of 22, a first lieutenant and at the age of 24, a captain.[^o8]

## Career {#Career}

Bolyai became so obsessed with [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) that his father, who had pursued the same subject for many years, wrote to him in 1820: "You must not attempt this approach to parallels. I know this way to the very end. I have traversed this bottomless night, which extinguished all light and joy in my life. I entreat you, leave the science of parallels alone...Learn from my example."[^o10]

János, however, persisted in his quest and eventually came to the conclusion that the postulate is independent of the other axioms of geometry and that different consistent geometries can be constructed on its negation. In 1823, he wrote to his father: "I have discovered such wonderful things that I was amazed...out of nothing I have created a strange new universe."[^o10][^o27] Between 1820 and 1823 he had prepared a treatise on [parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines) that he called [absolute geometry](wikipedia://en/699294). Bolyai's work was published in 1832 as an appendix to a mathematics textbook by his father.

[Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125), on reading the Appendix, wrote in a letter, "I regard this young [geometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometer) Bolyai as a genius of the first order." To his old friend Farkas Bolyai, however, Gauss wrote: "To praise it would amount to praising myself. For the entire content of the work...coincides almost exactly with my own meditations which have occupied my mind for the past thirty or thirty-five years."[^o12][^o10][^o11][^o27] Indeed, Gauss had disclosed his discovery of a consistent non-Euclidean geometry in a letter in 1827, and in 1829 wrote that he feared backlash if he published about it.[^o13] János suspected that Gauss had been secretly informed about his discoveries by his father, causing a rift between him and his father.[^o14] He later bitterly complained about Gauss's attitude.[^o15]

In 1848 Bolyai learned that [Nikolai Ivanovich Lobachevsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Ivanovich_Lobachevsky) had published a piece of work similar to his appendix in 1829. Though Lobachevsky published his work a few years earlier than Bolyai, it contained only [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291). Working independently, Bolyai and Lobachevsky pioneered the investigation of [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610).

In addition to his work in geometry, Bolyai developed a rigorous geometric concept of [complex numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) as ordered pairs of [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers). Although he never published more than the 24 pages of the Appendix, he left more than 20,000 pages of mathematical manuscripts when he died. These can now be found in the [Teleki-Bolyai Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleki_Library) in [Târgu Mureș](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Târgu_Mureș), where Bolyai died. His grave lies in the Lutheran Cemetery in Târgu Mureș.

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

He was an accomplished linguist, speaking several foreign [languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language) beside his native Hungarian, these being [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language), [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin), [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language), [Italian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italian_language) and [Romanian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Romanian_language). He learned the violin and performed in Vienna.[^o18]

It is related of him that he was challenged by thirteen officers of his garrison, a thing not unlikely to happen considering how differently he thought from everyone else. He fought them all in succession—making it his only condition that he should be allowed to play on his violin for an interval between meeting each opponent. He disarmed or wounded all his antagonists. It can be easily imagined that a temperament such as his was not one congenial to his military superiors. He was retired in 1833.[^o19]

No original portrait of Bolyai survives. An unauthentic picture appears in some encyclopedias and on a Hungarian postage stamp.[^o2]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

The [Babeș-Bolyai University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babeș-Bolyai_University) in [Cluj-Napoca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cluj-Napoca), that was established in 1959, bears his name, as does the professional society of Hungarian mathematicians, the [János Bolyai Mathematical Institute](wikipedia://en/30865370), at the [University of Szeged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Szeged). There is also a mathematical award given out every five years, named the [Bolyai Prize](wikipedia://en/18706230). Bolyai is a minor character in the 1969 science-fiction/fantasy story ["Operation Changeling"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Chaos_%28novel%29), where his unique abilities allow the protagonists to navigate the non-Euclidean geometry of [Hell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hell).

The crater [Bolyai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bolyai_%28crater%29) on the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon)[^o21] and [1441 Bolyai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1441_Bolyai), a [minor planet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minor_planet) discovered in 1937, are also named after him.

Several primary and secondary schools in the [Carpathian Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pannonian_Basin) also bear Bolyai's name; for instance, Bolyai János Műszaki Szakközépiskola in [Budapest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Budapest), Bolyai János Gyakorló Általános Iskola és Gimnázium in [Szombathely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szombathely), and the Bolyai János Általános Iskola in [Debrecen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debrecen). Streets in Budapest, Cluj-Napoca,[^o22] and [Timișoara](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timișoara)[^o23] are named after Bolyai.
