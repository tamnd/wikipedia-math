# Andrey Kolmogorov

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Andrey Kolmogorov"}

Nikolaevich

**Andrey Nikolaevich Kolmogorov** (Андре́й Никола́евич Колмого́ров, 25 April 1903 – 20 October 1987)[^o6][^o7] was a Soviet mathematician who played a central role in the creation of modern [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). He also gave fundamental contributions to the mathematics of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664), [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), [algorithmic information theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_information_theory) and [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/2230).[^o8][^o5]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

### Early life {#Early_life}

Andrey Kolmogorov was born in [Tambov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tambov), about 500 kilometers southeast of [Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow), in 1903. His unmarried mother, Maria Yakovlevna Kolmogorova, died giving birth to him.[^o12] Andrey was raised by two of his aunts in [Tunoshna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunoshna) (near [Yaroslavl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yaroslavl)) at the estate of his grandfather, a well-to-do [nobleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_nobility).

Little is known about Andrey's father. He was supposedly named Nikolai Matveyevich Katayev and had been an [agronomist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agronomy). Katayev had been exiled from [Saint Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg) to the Yaroslavl province after his participation in the revolutionary movement against the [tsars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsar). He disappeared in 1919 and was presumed to have been killed in the [Russian Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Civil_War).

Andrey Kolmogorov was educated in his aunt Vera's village school, and his earliest literary efforts and mathematical papers were printed in the school journal "The Swallow of Spring". Andrey (at the age of five) was the "editor" of the mathematical section of this journal. Kolmogorov's interest in mathematics was spurred when he noticed, at the age of six, the regularity in the sum of the series of odd numbers: $1 = 1^2; 1 + 3 = 2^2; 1 + 3 + 5 = 3^2,$ etc.[^o13]

In 1910, his aunt adopted him, and they moved to Moscow, where he graduated from [high school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gymnasium_%28school%29) in 1920. Later that same year, Kolmogorov began to study at [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University) and at the same time [Mendeleev Moscow Institute of Chemistry and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D._Mendeleev_University_of_Chemical_Technology_of_Russia).[^o14] Kolmogorov writes about this time: "I arrived at Moscow University with a fair knowledge of mathematics. I knew in particular the beginning of [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553). I studied many questions in articles in the *[Encyclopedia of Brockhaus and Efron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brockhaus_and_Efron_Encyclopedic_Dictionary)*, filling out for myself what was presented too concisely in these articles."[^o15]

Kolmogorov gained a reputation for his wide-ranging erudition. While an undergraduate student in college, he attended the seminars of the Russian historian [S. V. Bakhrushin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergey_Bakhrushin), and he published his first research paper on the fifteenth and sixteenth centuries' [landholding](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Land_tenure) practices in the [Novgorod Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novgorod_Republic).[^o16] During the same period (1921–22), Kolmogorov worked out and proved several results in [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) and in the theory of [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038).

### Adulthood {#Adulthood}

In 1922, Kolmogorov gained international recognition for constructing a [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) that [diverges](wikipedia://en/927236) [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688).[^o18][^o19] Around this time, he decided to devote his life to [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831).

In 1925, Kolmogorov graduated from [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University) and began to study under the supervision of [Nikolai Luzin](wikipedia://en/576228). He formed a lifelong close friendship with [Pavel Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/576245), a fellow student of Luzin; indeed, several researchers have concluded that the two friends were sexually involved,[^o21][^o22][^o23] although neither acknowledged this openly. Kolmogorov (together with [Aleksandr Khinchin](wikipedia://en/1029748)) became interested in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542). Also in 1925, he published his work in [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262), "On the principle of the excluded middle," in which he proved that under a certain interpretation, all statements of classical formal logic can be formulated as those of intuitionistic logic. In 1929, Kolmogorov earned his Doctor of Philosophy degree from Moscow State University. In 1929, Kolmogorov and Alexandrov during a long travel stayed about a month in an island in lake [Sevan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Sevan) in Armenia.[^o24]

In 1930, Kolmogorov went on his first long trip abroad, traveling to [Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Göttingen,_Germany) and [Munich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Munich) and then to [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989). He had various scientific contacts in Göttingen, first with [Richard Courant](wikipedia://en/715238) and his students working on limit theorems, where [diffusion processes](wikipedia://en/1667059) proved to be the limits of discrete random processes, then with [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544) in intuitionistic logic, and lastly with [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230) in function theory. His pioneering work *About the Analytical Methods of Probability Theory* was published (in German) in 1931. Also in 1931, he became a professor at [Moscow State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow_State_University).

In 1933, Kolmogorov published his book *Foundations of the Theory of Probability*, laying the modern axiomatic [foundations of probability theory](wikipedia://en/23539) and establishing his reputation as the world's leading expert in this field. In 1935, Kolmogorov became the first chairman of the department of probability theory at Moscow State University. Around the same years (1936) Kolmogorov contributed to the field of ecology and generalized the [Lotka–Volterra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lotka–Volterra) model of [predator–prey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predator–prey) systems.

During the [Great Purge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Purge) in 1936, Kolmogorov's doctoral advisor [Nikolai Luzin](wikipedia://en/576228) became a high-profile target of Stalin's regime in what is now called the "Luzin Affair". Kolmogorov and several other students of Luzin testified against Luzin, accusing him of plagiarism, nepotism, and other forms of misconduct; the hearings eventually concluded that he was a servant to "fascistoid science" and thus an enemy of the Soviet people. Luzin lost his academic positions, but curiously, he was neither arrested nor expelled from the [Academy of Sciences of the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Academy_of_Sciences_of_the_Soviet_Union).[^o25] The question of whether Kolmogorov and others were coerced into testifying against their teacher remains a topic of considerable speculation among historians; all parties involved refused to publicly discuss the case for the rest of their lives. Soviet-Russian mathematician [Semën Samsonovich Kutateladze](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semën_Samsonovich_Kutateladze) concluded in 2013, after reviewing archival documents made available during the 1990s and other surviving testimonies, that the students of Luzin had initiated the accusations against Luzin out of personal acrimony; there was no definitive evidence that the students were coerced by the state, nor was there any definitive evidence to support their allegations of academic misconduct.[^o27] Soviet historian of mathematics [A.P. Yushkevich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A.P._Yushkevich) surmised that, unlike many of the other high-profile persecutions of the era, Stalin did not personally initiate the persecution of Luzin and instead eventually concluded that he was not a threat to the regime, which would explain the unusually mild punishment relative to other contemporaries.[^o28]

In a 1938 paper, Kolmogorov "established the basic theorems for smoothing and predicting stationary [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes)"—a paper that had major military applications during the [Cold War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cold_War).[^o29] In 1939, he was elected a full member (academician) of the [USSR Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Russian_Academy_of_Sciences).

During [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II) Kolmogorov contributed to the Soviet war effort by applying statistical theory to artillery fire, developing a scheme of stochastic distribution of [barrage balloons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barrage_balloon) intended to help protect Moscow from German bombers during the [Battle of Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_Moscow).[^o30]

In his study of [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes), especially [Markov processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Markov_process), Kolmogorov and the British mathematician [Sydney Chapman](wikipedia://en/768762) independently developed a pivotal set of equations in the field that have been given the name of the [Chapman–Kolmogorov equations](wikipedia://en/35623859).

Later, Kolmogorov focused his research on [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664), beginning his publications in 1941. In [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), he is best known for the [Kolmogorov–Arnold–Moser theorem](wikipedia://en/16972), first presented in 1954 at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842).[^o8] In 1957, working jointly with his student [Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490), he solved a particular interpretation of [Hilbert's thirteenth problem](wikipedia://en/2336190). Around this time, he also began to develop and has since been considered a founder of [algorithmic complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_complexity_theory) – often referred to as [Kolmogorov complexity theory](wikipedia://en/1635).

Kolmogorov married Anna Dmitrievna Egorova in 1942. He pursued a vigorous teaching routine throughout his life, both at the university level and also with younger children, as he was actively involved in developing a [pedagogy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pedagogy) for gifted children in literature, music, and mathematics. At Moscow State University, Kolmogorov occupied different positions, including the heads of several departments: [probability](wikipedia://en/22934), [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), and [random processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_process); [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636). He also served as the Dean of the Moscow State University Department of Mechanics and Mathematics.

In 1971, Kolmogorov joined an [oceanographic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanographic) expedition aboard the research vessel *[Dmitri Mendeleev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Mendeleev).* He wrote a number of articles for the *[Great Soviet Encyclopedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Soviet_Encyclopedia).* In his later years, he devoted much of his effort to the mathematical and philosophical relationship between [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) in abstract and applied areas.[^o31]

Kolmogorov died in Moscow in 1987, and his remains were buried in the [Novodevichy cemetery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Novodevichy_cemetery).

A quotation attributed to Kolmogorov is \[translated into English\]: "Every mathematician believes that he is ahead of the others. The reason none state this belief in public is because they are intelligent people."

[Vladimir Arnold](wikipedia://en/32490) once said: "Kolmogorov – [Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) – [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) – [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) – [Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), are only five lives separating us from the source of our science."[^o32]
