# Fermat number

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Fermat number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Fermat number**, named after [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966) (1601–1665), the first known to have studied them, is a [positive integer](wikipedia://en/21474) of the form: $F_{n} = 2^{2^n} + 1,$ where *n* is a [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative) integer. The first few Fermat numbers are: [3](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3_%28number%29), [5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_%28number%29), [17](wikipedia://en/208288), [257](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/257_%28number%29), [65537](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/65537_%28number%29), 4294967297, 18446744073709551617, 340282366920938463463374607431768211457, ... .

:::

If 2<sup>*k*</sup> + 1 is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) and *k* > 0, then *k* itself must be a power of 2,[^o3] so 2<sup>*k*</sup> + 1 is a Fermat number; such primes are called **Fermat primes**. , the only known Fermat primes are *F*<sub>0</sub> = 3, *F*<sub>1</sub> = 5, *F*<sub>2</sub> = 17, *F*<sub>3</sub> = 257, and *F*<sub>4</sub> = 65537 .

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

The Fermat numbers satisfy the following [recurrence relations](wikipedia://en/146806):

$$F_{n} = (F_{n-1}-1)^{2}+1$$

$$F_{n} = F_{0} F_{1} \cdots F_{n-1} + 2$$

for *n* ≥ 1,

$$F_{n} = F_{n-1} + 2^{2^{n-1}}F_{0} F_{1} \cdots F_{n-2}$$

$$F_{n} = (F_{n-1} - 2F_{n-2} + 1)^2 + (2(F_{n-2} - 2)(F_{n-3} - 1))^2 + (2F_{n-2} - 3)^2$$

$$F_{n} = F_{n-1}^2 - 2(F_{n-2}-1)^2$$

for *n* ≥ 2. Each of these relations can be proved by [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881). From the second equation, we can deduce **Goldbach's theorem** (named after [Christian Goldbach](wikipedia://en/6290)): no two Fermat numbers [share a common integer factor greater than 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime). To see this, suppose that 0 ≤ *i* \< *j* and *F*<sub>*i*</sub> and *F*<sub>*j*</sub> have a common factor *a* > 1. Then *a* divides both

$$F_{0} F_{1} \cdots F_{j-1}$$

and *F*<sub>*j*</sub>; hence *a* divides their difference, 2. Since *a* > 1, this forces *a* = 2. This is a [contradiction](wikipedia://en/75807), because each Fermat number is clearly odd. As a [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865), we obtain another proof of the [infinitude of the prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitude_of_the_prime_numbers): for each *F*<sub>*n*</sub>, choose a prime factor *p*<sub>*n*</sub>; then the sequence $\{p_{n}\}$ is an infinite sequence of distinct primes.

### Further properties {#Further_properties}

- No Fermat prime can be expressed as the difference of two *p*th powers, where *p* is an odd prime.
- With the exception of *F*<sub>0</sub> and *F*<sub>1</sub>, the last decimal digit of a Fermat number is 7.
- The [sum of the reciprocals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sums_of_reciprocals) of all the Fermat numbers is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689). ([Solomon W. Golomb](wikipedia://en/1860707), 1963)

## Primality {#Primality}

Fermat numbers and Fermat primes were first studied by Pierre de Fermat, who [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that all Fermat numbers are prime. Indeed, the first five Fermat numbers *F*<sub>0</sub>, ..., *F*<sub>4</sub> are easily shown to be prime. Fermat's conjecture was refuted by [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) in 1732 when he showed, by dividing by 641 that

$$F_{5} = 2^{2^5} + 1 = 2^{32} + 1 = 4294967297 = 641 \times 6700417.$$

Euler proved that every factor of *F*<sub>*n*</sub> must have the form *k*hair2<sup>*n*+1</sup> + 1 (later improved to *k*hair2<sup>*n*+2</sup> + 1 by [Lucas](wikipedia://en/150102)) for *n* ≥ 2.

That 641 is a factor of *F*<sub>5</sub> can be deduced, in hindsight, as follows: From the equalities 641 = 2<sup>7</sup> × 5 + 1 and 641 = 2<sup>4</sup> + 5<sup>4</sup>. It follows from the first equality that 2<sup>7</sup> × 5 ≡ −1 (mod 641) and therefore (raising to the fourth power) that 2<sup>28</sup> × 5<sup>4</sup> ≡ 1 (mod 641). On the other hand, the second equality implies that 5<sup>4</sup> ≡ −2<sup>4</sup> (mod 641). These [congruences](wikipedia://en/20087) imply that 2<sup>32</sup> ≡ −1 (mod 641).

Fermat was probably aware of the form of the factors later proved by Euler, so it seems curious that he failed to follow through on the straightforward calculation to find the factor.[^o9] One common explanation is that Fermat made a computational mistake.

There are no other known Fermat primes *F*<sub>*n*</sub> with *n* > 4, but little is known about Fermat numbers for large *n*.[^o10] In fact, each of the following is an open problem:

- Is *F*<sub>*n*</sub> [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) [for all](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/For_all) *n* > 4?
- Are there infinitely many Fermat primes? ([Eisenstein](wikipedia://en/1452482) 1844[^o11])
- Are there infinitely many composite Fermat numbers?
- Does a Fermat number exist that is not [square-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-free_number)?

, it is known that *F*<sub>*n*</sub> is composite for 5 ≤ *n* ≤ 32, although of these, complete factorizations of *F*<sub>*n*</sub> are known only for 0 ≤ *n* ≤ 11, and there are no known prime factors for *n* = 20 and *n* = 24.[^o20] The largest Fermat number known to be composite is *F*<sub>18233954</sub>, and its prime factor 7 × 2<sup>18233956</sup> + 1 was discovered in October 2020.

### Heuristic arguments {#Heuristic_arguments}

Heuristics suggest that *F*<sub>4</sub> is the last Fermat prime.

The [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692) implies that a random integer in a suitable interval around *N* is prime with probability 1hair/hairln *N*. If one uses the heuristic that a Fermat number is prime with the same probability as a random integer of its size, and that *F*<sub>5</sub>, ..., *F*<sub>32</sub> are composite, then the expected number of Fermat primes beyond *F*<sub>4</sub> (or equivalently, beyond *F*<sub>32</sub>) should be

$$\sum_{n \ge 33} \frac{1}{\ln F_{n}} < \frac{1}{\ln 2} \sum_{n \ge 33} \frac{1}{\log_2(2^{2^n})} = \frac{1}{\ln 2} 2^{-32} < 3.36 \times 10^{-10}.$$

One may interpret this number as an upper bound for the probability that a Fermat prime beyond *F*<sub>4</sub> exists.

This argument is not a rigorous proof. For one thing, it assumes that Fermat numbers behave "randomly", but the factors of Fermat numbers have special properties. Boklan and [Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_H._Conway) published a more precise analysis suggesting that the probability that there is another Fermat prime is less than one in a billion.[^o13]

Anders Bjorn and [Hans Riesel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Riesel) estimated the number of square factors of Fermat numbers from *F*<sub>5</sub> onward as

$$\sum_{n \ge 5} \sum_{k \ge 1} \frac{1}{k (k 2^n + 1) \ln(k 2^n)} < \frac{\pi^2}{6 \ln 2} \sum_{n \ge 5} \frac{1}{n 2^n} \approx 0.02576;$$

in other words, there are unlikely to be any non-squarefree Fermat numbers, and in general square factors of $a^{2^n} + b^{2^n}$ are very rare for large *n*.[^o14]

### Equivalent conditions {#Equivalent_conditions}

Let $F_n=2^{2^n}+1$ be the *n*th Fermat number. Pépin's test states that for *n* > 0,

$F_n$ is prime if and only if $3^{(F_n-1)/2}\equiv-1\pmod{F_n}.$

The expression $3^{(F_n-1)/2}$ can be evaluated modulo $F_n$ by [repeated squaring](wikipedia://en/10237). This makes the test a fast [polynomial-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial-time) algorithm. But Fermat numbers grow so rapidly that only a handful of them can be tested in a reasonable amount of time and space.

There are some tests for numbers of the form *k*hair2<sup>*m*</sup> + 1, such as factors of Fermat numbers, for primality.

**[Proth's theorem](wikipedia://en/3225985)** (1878). Let *N* = *k*hair2<sup>*m*</sup> + 1 with odd *k* \< 2<sup>*m*</sup>. If there is an integer *a* such that

$$a^{(N-1)/2} \equiv -1\pmod{N}$$

then $N$ is prime. Conversely, if the above congruence does not hold, and in addition

$\left(\frac{a}{N}\right)=-1$ (See [Jacobi symbol](wikipedia://en/97313))

then $N$ is composite.

If *N* = *F*<sub>*n*</sub> > 3, then the above Jacobi symbol is always equal to −1 for *a* = 3, and this special case of Proth's theorem is known as [Pépin's test](wikipedia://en/3266846). Although Pépin's test and Proth's theorem have been implemented on computers to prove the compositeness of some Fermat numbers, neither test gives a specific nontrivial factor. In fact, no specific prime factors are known for *n* = 20 and 24.

:::

## Factorization {#Factorization}

Because of Fermat numbers' size, it is difficult to factorize or even to check primality. [Pépin's test](wikipedia://en/3266846) gives a necessary and sufficient condition for primality of Fermat numbers, and can be implemented by modern computers. The [elliptic curve method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_curve_method) is a fast method for finding small prime divisors of numbers. Distributed computing project *Fermatsearch* has found some factors of Fermat numbers. Yves Gallot's proth.exe has been used to find factors of large Fermat numbers. [Édouard Lucas](wikipedia://en/150102), improving Euler's above-mentioned result, proved in 1878 that every factor of the Fermat number $F_n$, with *n* at least 2, is of the form $k\times2^{n+2}+1$ (see [Proth number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proth_number)), where *k* is a positive integer. By itself, this makes it easy to prove the primality of the known Fermat primes.

## Pseudoprimes and Fermat numbers {#Pseudoprimes_and_Fermat_numbers}

Like [composite numbers](wikipedia://en/82289) of the form 2<sup>*p*</sup> − 1, every composite Fermat number is a [strong pseudoprime](wikipedia://en/1100422) to base 2. This is because all strong pseudoprimes to base 2 are also [Fermat pseudoprimes](wikipedia://en/45891) – i.e.,

$$2^{F_n-1} \equiv 1 \pmod{F_n}$$

for all Fermat numbers.[^o23]

In 1904, Cipolla showed that the product of at least two distinct prime or composite Fermat numbers $F_{a} F_{b} \dots F_{s},$ $a > b > \dots > s > 1$ will be a Fermat pseudoprime to base 2 if and only if $2^s > a$.[^o24]

## Other theorems about Fermat numbers {#Other_theorems_about_Fermat_numbers}

A Fermat number cannot be a perfect number or part of a pair of [amicable numbers](wikipedia://en/3259). (Luca 2000)

The series of reciprocals of all prime divisors of Fermat numbers is [convergent](wikipedia://en/1743431). (Křížek Luca Somer 2002)

If *n*<sup>*n*</sup> + 1 is prime and $n \ge 2$, there exists an integer *m* such that *n* = 2<sup>2<sup>*m*</sup></sup>. The equation *n*<sup>*n*</sup> + 1 = *F*<sub>(2<sup>*m*</sup>+*m*)</sub> holds in that case.[^o29]

Let the largest prime factor of the Fermat number *F*<sub>*n*</sub> be *P*(*F*<sub>*n*</sub>). Then,

$P(F_n) \ge 2^{n+2}(4n+9) + 1.$ (Grytczuk Luca Wójtowicz 2001)

:::

## Relationship to constructible polygons {#Relationship_to_constructible_polygons}

*Image: Constructible polygon set.svg.* Number of sides of known constructible polygons having up to 1000 sides (bold) or odd side count (red)

[Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) developed the theory of [Gaussian periods](wikipedia://en/456085) in his *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)* and formulated a [sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficient_condition) for the constructibility of regular polygons. Gauss stated that this condition was also [necessary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_condition),[^o33] but never published a proof. [Pierre Wantzel](wikipedia://en/2610953) gave a full proof of necessity in 1837. The result is known as the **Gauss–Wantzel theorem**:

An *n*-sided regular polygon can be constructed with [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge) if and only if *n* is the product of a power of 2 and distinct Fermat primes: in other words, if and only if *n* is of the form *n* = 2<sup>*k*</sup>*p*<sub>1</sub>*p*<sub>2</sub>...*p*<sub>*s*</sub>, where *k*, *s* are nonnegative integers and the *p*<sub>*i*</sub> are distinct Fermat primes.

A positive integer *n* is of the above form if and only if its [totient](wikipedia://en/53452) *φ*(*n*) is a power of 2.

## Applications of Fermat numbers {#Applications_of_Fermat_numbers}

### Pseudorandom number generation {#Pseudorandom_number_generation}

Fermat primes are particularly useful in generating pseudo-random sequences of numbers in the range 1, ..., *N*, where *N* is a power of 2. The most common method used is to take any seed value between 1 and *P* − 1, where *P* is a Fermat prime. Now multiply this by a number *A*, which is greater than the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of *P* and is a [primitive root](wikipedia://en/186864) modulo *P* (i.e., it is not a [quadratic residue](wikipedia://en/200091)). Then take the result modulo *P*. The result is the new value for the RNG.

$V_{j+1} = (A \times V_j) \bmod P$ (see [linear congruential generator](wikipedia://en/45527))

This is useful in computer science, since most data structures have members with 2<sup>*X*</sup> possible values. For example, a byte has 256 (2<sup>8</sup>) possible values (0–255). Therefore, to fill a byte or bytes with random values, a random number generator that produces values 1–256 can be used, the byte taking the output value −1. Very large Fermat primes are of particular interest in data encryption for this reason. This method produces only [pseudorandom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom) values, as after *P* − 1 repetitions, the sequence repeats. A poorly chosen multiplier can result in the sequence repeating sooner than *P* − 1.

## Generalized Fermat numbers {#Generalized_Fermat_numbers}

Numbers of the form $\frac{a^{2^n}+b^{2^n}}{gcd(a+b,2)}$ with *a*, *b* any [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) integers, *a* > *b* > 0, are called **generalized Fermat numbers**. An odd prime *p* is a generalized Fermat number if and only if *p* is congruent to [1 (mod 4)](wikipedia://en/2669448). (Here we consider only the case *n* > 0, so 3 = $2^{2^{0}} \!+ 1$ is not a counterexample.)

An example of a [probable prime](wikipedia://en/160560) of this form is 200<sup>262144</sup> + 119<sup>262144</sup> (found by Kellen Shenton).[^o38]

By analogy with the ordinary Fermat numbers, it is common to write generalized Fermat numbers of the form $a^{2^{ \overset{n} {}}} \!\!+ 1$ as *F*<sub>*n*</sub>(*a*). In this notation, for instance, the number 100,000,001 would be written as *F*<sub>3</sub>(10). In the following we shall restrict ourselves to primes of this form, $a^{2^{ \overset{n} {}}} \!\!+ 1$, such primes are called "Fermat primes base *a*". Of course, these primes exist only if *a* is [even](wikipedia://en/143135).

### Generalized Fermat primes of the form F<sub>*n*</sub>(*a*) {#Generalized_Fermat_primes_of_the_form_Fn(a)}

Because of the ease of proving their primality, generalized Fermat primes have become in recent years a topic for research within the field of number theory. Many of the largest known primes today are generalized Fermat primes.

Generalized Fermat numbers can be prime only for [even](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) $a$, because if $a$ is [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) then every generalized Fermat number will be divisible by 2. The smallest prime number $F_n(a)$ with $n>4$ is $F_5(30)$, or 30<sup>32</sup> + 1. Besides, we can define "half generalized Fermat numbers" for an odd base, a half generalized Fermat number to base *a* (for odd *a*) is $\frac{a^{2^n} \!+ 1}{2}$, and it is also to be expected that there will be only finitely many half generalized Fermat primes for each odd base.

In this list, the generalized Fermat numbers ($F_n(a)$) to an even $a$ are $a^{2^n} \!+ 1$, for odd $a$, they are $\frac{a^{2^n} \!\!+ 1}{2}$. If $a$ is a [perfect power](wikipedia://en/4023591) with an odd exponent , then all generalized Fermat numbers can be factored algebraically, so they cannot be prime.

A more elaborate theory can be used to predict the number of bases for which $F_n(a)$ will be prime for fixed $n$. The number of generalized Fermat primes can be roughly expected to halve as $n$ is increased by 1.

### Generalized Fermat primes of the form F<sub>n</sub>(*a*, *b*) {#Generalized_Fermat_primes_of_the_form_Fn(a,_b)}
