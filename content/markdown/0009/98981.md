# Algebraic integer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebraic integer"}

In [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), an **algebraic integer** is a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) that is [integral](wikipedia://en/9478630) over the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties). That is, an algebraic integer is a complex [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of some [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) which is [monic](wikipedia://en/314730) (its [leading coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_coefficient) is 1) and has coefficients that are all integers. The set of all algebraic integers $A$ is closed under addition, subtraction and multiplication and therefore is a [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of the complex numbers.

:::

The [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) $K$, denoted by $O_{K}$, is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of $K$ and $A$: it can also be characterized as the maximal [order](wikipedia://en/1114171) of the [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$. Each algebraic integer belongs to the ring of integers of some number field. A number $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the ring $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/58527) as an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), which is to say, as a $\mathbb{Z}$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

The following are equivalent definitions of an algebraic integer. Let $K$ be a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) (i.e., a [finite extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_extension) of $\mathbb{Q}$, the field of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024)), in other words, $K = \mathbb{Q}(\theta)$ for some [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) $\theta \in \mathbb{C}$ by the [primitive element theorem](wikipedia://en/448933).

- $\alpha \in K$ is an algebraic integer if there exists a monic polynomial $f(x) \in \mathbb{Z}[x]$ such that $f(\alpha) = 0$.
- $\alpha \in K$ is an algebraic integer if the [minimal](wikipedia://en/9667106) monic polynomial of $\alpha$ over $\mathbb{Q}$ is in $\mathbb{Z}[x]$.
- $\alpha \in K$ is an algebraic integer if $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha]$ is a finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-module.
- $\alpha \in K$ is an algebraic integer if there exists a non-zero finitely generated $\mathbb{Z}$-[submodule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submodule) $M \subset \mathbb{C}$ such that $\alpha M \subseteq M$.

Algebraic integers are a special case of [integral elements](wikipedia://en/9478630) of a ring extension. In particular, an algebraic integer is an integral element of a finite extension $K / \mathbb{Q}$.

Note that if $P(x)$ is a [primitive polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_polynomial_%28ring_theory%29) that has integer coefficients but is not monic, and $P$ is [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) over $\mathbb{Q}$, then none of the roots of $P$ are algebraic integers (but *are* [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158)). Here *primitive* is used in the sense that the [highest common factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_common_factor) of the coefficients of $P$ is 1, which is weaker than requiring the coefficients to be pairwise relatively prime.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The only algebraic integers that are found in the set of rational numbers are the integers. In other words, the intersection of $\mathbb{Q}$ and $A$ is exactly $\mathbb{Z}$. The rational number $\tfrac{a}{b}$ is not an algebraic integer unless $b$ [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) $a$. The leading coefficient of the polynomial $bx - a$ is the integer $b$.
- The [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) $\sqrt{n}$ of a nonnegative integer $n$ is an algebraic integer, but is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689) unless $n$ is a [perfect square](wikipedia://en/213919).
- If $d$ is a [square-free integer](wikipedia://en/29525) then the [extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $K = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{d}\,)$ is a [quadratic field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_field_extension) of rational numbers. The ring of algebraic integers $O_{K}$ contains $\sqrt{d}$ since this is a root of the monic polynomial $x^{2} - d$. Moreover, if $d \equiv 1 \bmod 4$, then the element $\frac{1}{2}(1 + \sqrt{d}\,)$ is also an algebraic integer. It satisfies the polynomial $x^{2} - x + \tfrac{1}{4}(1 - d)$ where the [constant term](wikipedia://en/79861) $\tfrac{1}{4}(1 - d)$ is an integer. The full ring of integers is generated by $\sqrt{d}$ or $\frac{1}{2}(1 + \sqrt{d}\,)$ respectively. See [Quadratic integer](wikipedia://en/21834785) for more.
- The ring of integers of the field $F = \mathbb{Q}[\alpha]$, $\alpha = \sqrt[3]{m}$ (where $m$ is [cube-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube-free)), has the following [integral basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_basis), writing $m = hk^{2}$ for two [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525) [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) integers $h$ and $k$:[^o6] $$\begin{cases} 1, \alpha, \dfrac{\alpha^2 \pm k^2 \alpha + k^2}{3k} & m \equiv \pm 1 \bmod 9 \\ 1, \alpha, \dfrac{\alpha^2}k & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$
- If $\zeta_{n}$ is a [primitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primitive_root_of_unity) $n$th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950), then the ring of integers of the [cyclotomic field](wikipedia://en/30872462) $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_n)$ is precisely $\mathbb{Z}[\zeta_n]$.
- If $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer then $\beta = \sqrt[n]{\alpha}$ is another algebraic integer. A polynomial for $\beta$ is obtained by substituting $x^{n}$ in the polynomial for $\alpha$.

:::

## Finite generation of ring extension {#Finite_generation_of_ring_extension}

For any $\alpha$, the [ring extension](wikipedia://en/24873145#Ring_extensions) (in the sense that is equivalent to [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634)) of the integers by $\alpha$, denoted by $\mathbb{Z}[\alpha] \equiv \left\{\sum_{i=0}^n \alpha^i z_i | z_i\in \mathbb{Z}, n\in \mathbb{Z}\right\}$, is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/58527) if and only if $\alpha$ is an algebraic integer.

The proof is analogous to that of the [corresponding fact](wikipedia://en/1158#Degree_of_simple_extensions_of_the_rationals_as_a_criterion_to_algebraicity) regarding [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158), with $\mathbb{Q}$ there replaced by $\mathbb{Z}$ here, and the notion of [field extension degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) replaced by finite generation (using the fact that $\mathbb{Z}$ is finitely generated itself); the only required change is that only non-negative powers of $\alpha$ are involved in the proof.

The analogy is possible because both algebraic integers and algebraic numbers are defined as roots of monic polynomials over either $\mathbb{Z}$ or $\mathbb{Q}$, respectively.

## Ring {#Ring}

The sum, difference and product of two algebraic integers is an algebraic integer. In general their quotient is not. Thus the algebraic integers form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404).

This can be shown analogously to [the corresponding proof](wikipedia://en/1158#Field) for [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158), using the integers $\mathbb{Z}$ instead of the rationals $\mathbb{Q}$.

One may also construct explicitly the monic polynomial involved, which is generally of higher [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) than those of the original algebraic integers, by taking [resultants](wikipedia://en/2195020) and factoring. For example, if $x^{2} - x - 1 = 0$, $y^{3} - y - 1 = 0$ and $z = xy$, then eliminating $x$ and $y$ from $z - xy = 0$ and the polynomials satisfied by $x$ and $y$ using the resultant gives $z^{6} - 3z^{4} - 4z^{3} + z^{2} + z - 1 = 0$, which is irreducible, and is the monic equation satisfied by the product. (To see that the $xy$ is a root of the $x$-resultant of $z - xy$ and $x^{2} - x - 1$, one might use the fact that the resultant is contained in the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) generated by its two input polynomials.)

### Integral closure {#Integral_closure}

Every root of a monic polynomial whose coefficients are algebraic integers is itself an algebraic integer. In other words, the algebraic integers form a ring that is [integrally closed](wikipedia://en/21420209) in any of its extensions.

Again, the proof is analogous to [the corresponding proof](wikipedia://en/1158#Algebraic_closure) for [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158) being [algebraically closed](wikipedia://en/1018).

## Additional facts {#Additional_facts}

- Any number constructible out of the integers with roots, addition, and multiplication is an algebraic integer; but not all algebraic integers are so constructible: in a naïve sense, most roots of irreducible [quintics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quintic) are not. This is the [Abel–Ruffini theorem](wikipedia://en/152518).
- The ring of algebraic integers is a [Bézout domain](wikipedia://en/3131407), as a consequence of the [principal ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/3136164).
- If the monic polynomial associated with an algebraic integer has constant term 1 or −1, then the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of that algebraic integer is also an algebraic integer, and each is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666), an element of the [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) of the ring of algebraic integers.
- If $x$ is an algebraic number then $a_{n}x$ is an algebraic integer, where $x$ satisfies a polynomial $p(x)$ with integer coefficients and where $a_{n}x^{n}$ is the highest-degree term of $p(x)$. The value $y = a_{n}x$ is an algebraic integer because it is a root of $q(y) = a^{n - 1}_{n} p(y/a_{n})$, where $q(y)$ is a monic polynomial with integer coefficients.
- If $x$ is an algebraic number then it can be written as the ratio of an algebraic integer to a non-zero algebraic integer. In fact, the denominator can always be chosen to be a positive integer. The ratio is $\lvert a_{n}\rvert x / \lvert a_{n}\rvert$, where $x$ satisfies a polynomial $p(x)$ with integer coefficients and where $a_{n}x^{n}$ is the highest-degree term of $p(x)$.
- The only rational algebraic integers are the integers. That is, if $x$ is an algebraic integer and $x\in\mathbb{Q}$ then $x\in\mathbb{Z}$. This is a direct result of the [rational root theorem](wikipedia://en/26426) for the case of a monic polynomial.
