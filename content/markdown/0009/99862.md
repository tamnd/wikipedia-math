# Hyperplane

*Image: Intersecting\_planes.svg.* Two intersecting planes: Two-dimensional planes are the hyperplanes in three-dimensional space.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hyperplane"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **hyperplane** is a generalization of a [two-dimensional plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) to [mathematical spaces](wikipedia://en/5308894) of arbitrary [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398). Like a [plane in space](wikipedia://en/84029), a hyperplane is a [flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flat_space) [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101), a [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28mathematics%29) whose [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) is one less than that of the [ambient space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ambient_space). Two lower-dimensional examples of hyperplanes are [one-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-dimensional) [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) in a plane and [zero-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-dimensional) [points](wikipedia://en/593693) on a line.

:::

Most commonly, the ambient space is $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), in which case the hyperplanes are the $(n\,-\,1)$-dimensional ["flats"](wikipedia://en/13005617), each of which separates the space into two [half spaces](wikipedia://en/647297).[^o2] A [reflection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflection_%28geometry%29) across a hyperplane is a kind of [motion](wikipedia://en/36132944) ([geometric transformation](wikipedia://en/43194879) preserving [distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distance_%28mathematics%29) between points), and the [group](wikipedia://en/19447) of all motions is [generated](wikipedia://en/99945) by the reflections. A [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885) is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of half-spaces.

In [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610), the ambient space might be the [$n$-dimensional sphere](wikipedia://en/39782) or [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742), or more generally a [pseudo-Riemannian](wikipedia://en/287555) [space form](wikipedia://en/1087009), and the hyperplanes are the hypersurfaces consisting of all [geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096) through a point which are [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to a specific [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) geodesic.

In other kinds of ambient spaces, some properties from Euclidean space are no longer relevant. For example, in [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), there is no concept of distance, so there are no reflections or motions. In a [non-orientable](wikipedia://en/187446) space such as [elliptic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_space) or [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135), there is no concept of half-planes. In greatest generality, the notion of hyperplane is meaningful in any mathematical space in which the concept of the dimension of a subspace is defined (see also Matroid#Hyperplanes (coatoms).

The difference in dimension between a subspace and its ambient space is known as its *[codimension](wikipedia://en/525149)*. A hyperplane has codimension $1$.

## Technical description {#Technical_description}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **hyperplane** of an [*n*-dimensional space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-dimensional_space) *V* is a subspace of dimension *n* − 1, or equivalently, of [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) 1 in *V*. The space *V* may be a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) or more generally an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), or a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) or a [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135), and the notion of hyperplane varies correspondingly since the definition of subspace differs in these settings; in all cases however, any hyperplane can be given in [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate) as the solution of a single (due to the "codimension 1" constraint) [algebraic equation](wikipedia://en/404001) of degree 1.

If *V* is a vector space, one distinguishes "vector hyperplanes" (which are [linear subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357), and therefore must pass through the origin) and "affine hyperplanes" (which need not pass through the origin; they can be obtained by [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) of a vector hyperplane). A hyperplane in a Euclidean space separates that space into two [half spaces](wikipedia://en/647297), and defines a [reflection](wikipedia://en/295829) that fixes the hyperplane and interchanges those two half spaces.

## Special types of hyperplanes {#Special_types_of_hyperplanes}

Several specific types of hyperplanes are defined with properties that are well suited for particular purposes. Some of these specializations are described here.

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Affine hyperplane"}

### Affine hyperplanes {#Affine_hyperplanes}

An **affine hyperplane** is an [affine subspace](wikipedia://en/298834) of [codimension](wikipedia://en/525149) 1 in an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834). In [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates), such a hyperplane can be described with a single [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570) of the following form (where at least one of the $a_i$s is non-zero and $b$ is an arbitrary constant):

$$a_1x_1 + a_2x_2 + \cdots + a_nx_n = b.\$$

In the case of a real affine space, in other words when the coordinates are real numbers, this affine space separates the space into two half-spaces, which are the [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) of the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of the hyperplane, and are given by the [inequalities](wikipedia://en/89489)

$$a_1x_1 + a_2x_2 + \cdots + a_nx_n < b\$$

and

$$a_1x_1 + a_2x_2 + \cdots + a_nx_n > b.\$$

As an example, a point is a hyperplane in 1-dimensional space, a line is a hyperplane in 2-dimensional space, and a plane is a hyperplane in 3-dimensional space. A line in 3-dimensional space is not a hyperplane, and does not separate the space into two parts (the complement of such a line is connected).

Any hyperplane of a Euclidean space has exactly two unit normal vectors: $\pm\hat{n}$. In particular, if we consider $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ equipped with the conventional inner product ([dot product](wikipedia://en/157093)), then one can define the affine subspace with normal vector $\hat{n}$ and origin translation $\tilde{b} \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ as the set of all $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n+1}$ such that $\hat{n} \cdot (x-\tilde{b})=0$.

Affine hyperplanes are used to define decision boundaries in many [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) algorithms such as linear-combination (oblique) [decision trees](wikipedia://en/577003), and [perceptrons](wikipedia://en/172777).

:::

### Vector hyperplanes {#Vector_hyperplanes}

In a vector space, a vector hyperplane is a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of codimension 1, only possibly shifted from the origin by a vector, in which case it is referred to as a [flat](wikipedia://en/13005617). Such a hyperplane is the solution of a single [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570).

::: {#o9 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Projective hyperplane"}

### Projective hyperplanes {#Projective_hyperplanes}

**Projective hyperplanes** are used in [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849). A [projective subspace](wikipedia://en/243849#Projective_subspace) is a set of points with the property that for any two points of the set, all the points on the line determined by the two points are contained in the set.[^o10] Projective geometry can be viewed as [affine geometry](wikipedia://en/243890) with [vanishing points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanishing_point) (points at infinity) added. An affine hyperplane together with the associated points at infinity forms a projective hyperplane. One special case of a projective hyperplane is the **infinite** or **ideal hyperplane**, which is defined with the set of all points at infinity.

In projective space, a hyperplane does not divide the space into two parts; rather, it takes two hyperplanes to separate points and divide up the space. The reason for this is that the space essentially "wraps around" so that both sides of a lone hyperplane are connected to each other.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

In [convex geometry](wikipedia://en/4165882), two [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) [convex sets](wikipedia://en/6292) in n-dimensional Euclidean space are separated by a hyperplane, a result called the [hyperplane separation theorem](wikipedia://en/4739827).

In [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), hyperplanes are a key tool to create [support vector machines](wikipedia://en/65309) for such tasks as [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision) and [natural language processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_language_processing).

In multiple [linear regression](wikipedia://en/48758386) with more than two regressors, the datapoint and its predicted value via a linear model is a hyperplane.

In [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), hyperplanes can be used to calculate shortest distance between star systems, galaxies and celestial bodies with regard of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) and curvature of [space-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-time) as optimized [geodesic](wikipedia://en/91096) or paths influenced by gravitational fields.

:::

## Dihedral angles {#Dihedral_angles}

The [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046) between two non-parallel hyperplanes of a Euclidean space is the angle between the corresponding [normal vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_vector). The product of the transformations in the two hyperplanes is a [rotation](wikipedia://en/410009) whose axis is the [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_subspace) of codimension 2 obtained by intersecting the hyperplanes, and whose angle is twice the angle between the hyperplanes.

### Support hyperplanes {#Support_hyperplanes}

A hyperplane H is called a "support" hyperplane of the polyhedron P if P is contained in one of the two closed half-spaces bounded by H and $H\cap P \neq \varnothing$.[^o15] The intersection of P and H is defined to be a "face" of the polyhedron. The theory of polyhedra and the dimension of the faces are analyzed by looking at these intersections involving hyperplanes.
