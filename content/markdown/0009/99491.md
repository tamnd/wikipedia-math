# Exponentiation

*Image: Exponential Functions.svg.* Graphs of $y = b^{x}$ for various bases $b$. Each curve passes through the point $(0, 1)$ because any nonzero number raised to the power of $0$ is $1$. At $x = 1$, the value of $y$ equals the base because any number raised to the power of $1$ is the number itself.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Exponentiation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **exponentiation**, denoted $b^{n}$, is an [operation](wikipedia://en/4140245) involving two numbers: the *base*, $b$, and the *exponent*, $n$. When $n$ is a positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563), exponentiation corresponds to repeated [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) of the base: that is, $b^{n}$ is the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of multiplying $b$ by itself $n$ times:

$$b^n = \underbrace{b \times b \times \dots \times b \times b}_{n \text{ times}}.$$

:::

The definition of exponentiation can be extended in a natural way to define $b^x$ for any positive [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) base $b$ and any real number exponent $x$. A more involved definition supports arbitrary [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) as base and exponent. Other generalizations allow [algebraic objects](wikipedia://en/106364), such as [square matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_matrices), to be the base.

Exponentiation is a fundamental operation used throughout mathematics, science, and engineering.

::: {#o3 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

This article uses the three common notations for [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845), $x\times y$, $xy$, and $x\cdot y$, interchangeably.

The exponent is usually shown as a [superscript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superscript) to the right of the base as $b^{n}$. Sometimes an [up arrow](wikipedia://en/258733) or [caret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caret) is used as the operator, e.g. $b \uparrow n$ or `b^n`.

In abstract algebra, when there are several operations that may be repeated, it is common to indicate the repeated operation by placing its symbol in the superscript, before the exponent. For example, if $f$ is a function whose valued can be multiplied, $f^{\circ n}$ denotes exponentiation with respect to function composition, whereas $f^n$ may denote exponentiation with respect to multiplication. That is,

$$(f^n)(x)=(f(x))^n=f(x) \,f(x) \cdots f(x),$$

and

$$(f^{\circ n})(x)=f(f(\cdots f(f(x))\cdots)).$$

Commonly, $(f^n)(x)$ is denoted $f(x)^n,$ while $(f^{\circ n})(x)$ is denoted $f^n(x).$

:::

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

The exponent is also known as the *power*,[^o6][^o12] or, in British English, the *index*.[^o7] The result of the exponentiation may also be referred to as a *power*.[^o8][^o9][^o10][^o11]

The expression $b^{n}$ is often read as "$b$ to the power (of) $n$"; it may also be referred to as "$b$ (raised) to the $n$th (power)", "the $n$th power of $b$", or, most briefly, "$b$ to the $n$".

The expression $b^{2} = b \cdot b$ is called "the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) of $b$" or "$b$ squared", because the area of a square with side-length $b$ is $b^{2}$. Similarly, the expression $b^{3} = b \cdot b \cdot b$ is called "the [cube](wikipedia://en/451445) of $b$" or "$b$ cubed", because the volume of a cube with side-length $b$ is $b^{3}$.

:::

## Integer exponents {#Integer_exponents}

The exponentiation operation with integer exponents may be defined directly from elementary [arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operation).

### Positive exponents {#Positive_exponents}

The definition of the exponentiation as an [iterated](wikipedia://en/1448702) multiplication can be [formalized](wikipedia://en/1197184) by using [induction](wikipedia://en/18881),[^o15] and this definition can be used as soon as one has an [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) multiplication:

The base case is

$$b^1 = b$$

and the [recurrence](wikipedia://en/146806) is

$$b^{n+1} = b^n \cdot b.$$

The associativity of multiplication implies that for any positive integers $m$ and $n$, when multiplying exponentiation results with the same base $b$ and exponents $m$ and $n$, the product is the same base $b$ to the power of the sum $m + n$ of the exponents:

$$b^{m+n} = b^m \cdot b^n,$$

and, when taking the result of exponentiation to another power, the base remains the same, and the exponents multiply:

$$(b^m)^n=b^{mn}.$$

These rules can be derived intuitively as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} b^n \times b^m & = \underbrace{b \times \dots \times b}_{n \text{ times}} \times \underbrace{b \times \dots \times b}_{m \text{ times}} \\[1ex] & = \underbrace{b \times \dots \times b}_{n+m \text{ times}} = b^{n+m} \end{aligned}$$

$$\begin{aligned} (b^m)^n &= \underbrace{\underbrace{b \times \dots \times b}_{m \text{ times}} \times \dots \times \underbrace{b \times \dots \times b}_{m \text{ times}}}_{n \text{ times}} \\[1ex] &= \underbrace{b \times \dots \times b}_{m \times n \text{ times}} = b^{mn} \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="To the power of zero"}

### Zero exponent {#Zero_exponent}

A number raised to the $0$ power is generally defined to be $1$:[^o18]

$$b^0=1.$$

This ensures that the multiplication rule mentioned above,

$$b^{m+n}=b^m\cdot b^n$$

, also holds for $m=0$:

$$b^n = 1 \times b^n = b^0 \times b^n = b^{0+n} = b^n.$$

This value is also obtained by the [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289) convention, which may be used in every [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) with a multiplication that has an [identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity).

Depending on the context, there may be an exception for [zero to the power of zero](wikipedia://en/32130416).

:::

::: {#o20 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Negative Exponents"}

### Negative exponents {#Negative_exponents}

Exponentiation with negative exponents is defined by the following identity, which holds for any integer $n$ and nonzero $b$:

$$b^{-n} = \frac{1}{b^n}$$

Raising 0 to a negative exponent, which is related to [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663), is typically undefined but in some circumstances it may be interpreted as [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824).[^o21]

This definition of exponentiation with negative exponents is the only one that allows extending the identity $b^{m+n}=b^m\cdot b^n$ to negative exponents (consider the case $m=-n$).

The same definition applies to [invertible elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_element) in a multiplicative [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652), that is, an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364), with an associative multiplication and a [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) denoted $1$ (for example, the [square matrices](wikipedia://en/166022) of a given dimension). In particular, in such a structure, the inverse of an [invertible element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_element) $x$ is standardly denoted $x^{-1}.$

:::

::: {#o23 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Law of indices"}

### Identities and properties {#Identities_and_properties}

The following [identities](wikipedia://en/298428), often called **exponent rules**, hold for all integer exponents, provided that the base is not zero:

$$\begin{aligned} b^m \cdot b^n &= b^{m + n} \\ \left(b^m\right)^n &= b^{m \cdot n} \\ b^n \cdot c^n &= (b \cdot c)^n \end{aligned}$$

Unlike addition and multiplication, exponentiation is not [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative): for example, $2^3 = 8$, but reversing the operands gives the different value $3^2=9$. Also unlike addition and multiplication, exponentiation is not [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative): for example, $(2^{3})^{2} = 8^{2} = 64$, whereas $2^{(3^{2})} = 2^{9} = 512$. Without parentheses, the conventional [order of operations](wikipedia://en/212980) for [serial exponentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serial_exponentiation) in superscript notation is top-down (or *right*-associative), not bottom-up (or *left*-associative).[^o121][^o122][^o123] That is,

$$b^{p^q} = b^{\left(p^q\right)},$$

which, in general, is different from

$$\left(b^p\right)^q = b^{p q} .$$

:::

::: {#o25 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Commutative exponentiation"}

### Powers of a sum {#Powers_of_a_sum}

The powers of a sum can normally be computed from the powers of the summands by the [binomial formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binomial_formula)

$$(a+b)^n=\sum_{i=0}^n \binom{n}{i}a^ib^{n-i}=\sum_{i=0}^n \frac{n!}{i!(n-i)!}a^ib^{n-i}.$$

However, this formula is true only if the summands commute (i.e. that $ab = ba$), which is implied if they belong to a [structure](wikipedia://en/106364) that is [commutative](wikipedia://en/294390). Otherwise, if $a$ and $b$ are, say, [square matrices](wikipedia://en/166022) of the same size, this formula cannot be used. It follows that in [computer algebra](wikipedia://en/23659805), many [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) involving integer exponents must be changed when the exponentiation bases do not commute. Some general purpose [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427) use a different notation (sometimes $\hat{}\hat{}$ instead of $\hat{}$) for exponentiation with non-commuting bases, which is then called **non-commutative exponentiation**.

:::

### Combinatorial interpretation {#Combinatorial_interpretation}

For nonnegative integers $n$ and $m$, the value of $n^{m}$ is the number of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) from a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of $m$ elements to a set of $n$ elements (see [cardinal exponentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_exponentiation)). Such functions can be represented as $m$-[tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) from an $n$-element set (or as $m$-letter words from an $n$-letter [alphabet](wikipedia://en/4292269)). Some examples for particular values of $m$ and $n$ are given in the following table:

### Particular bases {#Particular_bases}

#### Powers of ten {#Powers_of_ten}

In the base ten ([decimal](wikipedia://en/8214)) number system, integer powers of $10$ are written as the digit $1$ followed or preceded by a number of zeroes determined by the sign and magnitude of the exponent. For example, $e=3 = 1000$ and $e=-4 = 0.0001$.

Exponentiation with base $10$ is used in [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715) to denote large or small numbers. For instance, 299792458 m/s (the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) in vacuum, in [metres per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metres_per_second)) can be written as \2.99792458 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s and then [approximated](wikipedia://en/336271) as \2.998 × 10<sup>8</sup> m/s.

[Metric prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix) based on powers of $10$ are also used to describe small or large quantities. For example, the prefix [kilo-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilo-) means $e=3 = 1000$, so a kilometre is 1000 metres.

#### Powers of two {#Powers_of_two}

The first negative powers of $2$ have special names: $2^{-1}$is a *[half](wikipedia://en/464963)*; $2^{-2}$ is a *quarter.*

Powers of $2$ appear in [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), since a set with $n$ members has a [power set](wikipedia://en/23799), the set of all of its [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631), which has $2^{n}$ members.

Integer powers of $2$ are important in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science). The positive integer powers $2^{n}$ give the number of possible values for an $n$-[bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit) integer [binary number](wikipedia://en/238686); for example, a [byte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byte) may take $2^{8} = 256$ different values. The [binary number system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_number_system) expresses any number as a sum of powers of $2$, and denotes it as a sequence of $0$ and $1$, separated by a [binary point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_point), where $1$ indicates a power of $2$ that appears in the sum; the exponent is determined by the place of this $1$: the nonnegative exponents are the rank of the $1$ on the left of the point (starting from $0$), and the negative exponents are determined by the rank on the right of the point.

#### Powers of one {#Powers_of_one}

Every power of one equals: $1^{n} = 1$.

#### Powers of zero {#Powers_of_zero}

For a positive exponent $n > 0$, the $n$th power of zero is zero: $0^{n} = 0$. For a negative exponent, $0^{-n}=1/0^n=1/0$ is undefined.

In some contexts (e.g., [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170)), the expression [$0^{0}$](wikipedia://en/32130416) is defined to be equal to $1$; in others (e.g., [analysis](wikipedia://en/48396)), it is often undefined.

#### Powers of negative one {#Powers_of_negative_one}

Since a negative number times another negative is positive, we have:

$$(-1)^n = \left\{\begin{array}{rl} 1 & \text{for even } n, \\ -1 & \text{for odd } n. \\ \end{array}\right.$$

Because of this, powers of $-1$ are useful for expressing alternating [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838). For a similar discussion of powers of the complex number $i$, see .

### Large exponents {#Large_exponents}

The [limit of a sequence](wikipedia://en/285773) of powers of a number greater than one diverges; in other words, the sequence grows without bound:

$$b^n \rightarrow \infty \text{ as } n \rightarrow \infty \text{ when } b > 1$$

This can be read as "*b* to the power of *n* tends to [\+∞](wikipedia://en/51698) as *n* tends to infinity when *b* is greater than one".

Powers of a number with [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) less than one tend to zero:

$$b^n \rightarrow 0 \text{ as } n \rightarrow \infty \text{ when } \left|b\right| < 1$$

Any power of one is always one:

$$b^n = 1 \text{ for all } n \text{ for } b = 1$$

Powers of a negative number $b \leq -1$ alternate between positive and negative as $n$ alternates between even and odd, and thus do not tend to any limit as $n$ grows.

If the exponentiated number varies while tending to $1$ as the exponent tends to infinity, then the limit is not necessarily one of those above. A particularly important case is

$$(1 + \frac{1}{n})^n \rightarrow e \text{ as } n \rightarrow \infty$$

See below.

Other limits, in particular those of expressions that take on an [indeterminate form](wikipedia://en/366723), are described in below.

### Power functions {#Power_functions}

*Image: Potenssi 1 3 5.svg.* Power functions for $n = 1, 3, 5$

*Image: Potenssi 2 4 6.svg.* Power functions for $n = 2, 4, 6$

Real functions of the form $f(x) = cx^n$, where $c \ne 0$, are sometimes called power functions.[^o37] When $n$ is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) and $n \ge 1$, two primary families exist: for $n$ even, and for $n$ odd. In general for $c > 0$, when $n$ is even $f(x) = cx^n$ will tend towards positive [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824) with increasing $x$, and also towards positive infinity with decreasing $x$. All graphs from the family of even power functions have the general shape of $y=cx^2$, flattening more in the middle as $n$ increases.[^o38] Functions with this kind of [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) ($f(-x)= f(x)$) are called [even functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_functions).

When $n$ is odd, $f(x)$'s [asymptotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic) behavior reverses from positive $x$ to negative $x$. For $c > 0$, $f(x) = cx^n$ will also tend towards positive [infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity_%28mathematics%29) with increasing $x$, but towards negative infinity with decreasing $x$. All graphs from the family of odd power functions have the general shape of $y=cx^3$, flattening more in the middle as $n$ increases and losing all flatness there in the straight line for $n=1$. Functions with this kind of symmetry ($f(-x)= -f(x)$) are called [odd functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_function).

For $c < 0$, the same behavior occurs, but with positive and negative infinity exchanged.[^o38]

## Rational exponents {#Rational_exponents}

*Image: Mplwp roots 01.svg.* From top to bottom: $x^{1/8}$, $x^{1/4}$, $x^{1/2}$, $x^{1}$, $x^{2}$, $x^{4}$, $x^{8}$.

If $x$ is a nonnegative [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438), and $n$ is a positive integer, $x^{1/n}$ or $\sqrt[n]x$ denotes the unique nonnegative real [$n$th root](wikipedia://en/235029) of $x$, that is, the unique nonnegative real number $y$ such that $y^n=x.$

If $x$ is a positive real number, and $\frac pq$ is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024), with $p$ and $q > 0$ integers, then $x^{p/q}$ is defined as

$$x^\frac{p}{q} = \left(x^p\right)^\frac{1}{q} = \sqrt[q]{x^p} = (x^\frac{1}{q})^p = \left(\sqrt[q]{x}\right)^p.$$

The equality on the right may be derived by setting $y=x^\frac 1q,$ and writing $(x^\frac 1q)^p=y^p=\left((y^p)^q\right)^\frac 1q=\left((y^q)^p\right)^\frac 1q=(x^p)^\frac 1q.$

If $r$ is a positive rational number, $0^{r} = 0$, by definition.

All these definitions are required for extending the identity $(x^r)^s = x^{rs}$ to rational exponents.

On the other hand, there are problems with the extension of these definitions to bases that are not positive real numbers. For example, a negative real number has a real $n$th root, which is negative, if $n$ is [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number), and no real root if $n$ is even. In the latter case, whichever complex $n$th root one chooses for $x^\frac 1n$, the identity $(x^a)^b=x^{ab}$ cannot be satisfied. For example,

$$\left((-1)^2\right)^\frac 12 = 1^\frac 12= 1\neq (-1)^{2\cdot\frac 12} =(-1)^1=-1.$$

See and for details on the way these problems may be handled.

## Real exponents {#Real_exponents}

For positive real numbers, exponentiation to real powers can be defined in two equivalent ways, either by extending the rational powers to reals by continuity (, below), or in terms of the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of the base and the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) (, below). The result is always a positive real number, and the identities and properties shown above for integer exponents remain true with these definitions for real exponents. The second definition is more commonly used, since it generalizes straightforwardly to [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) exponents.

On the other hand, exponentiation to a real power of a negative real number is much more difficult to define consistently, as it may be non-real and have several values. One may choose one of these values, called the [principal value](wikipedia://en/408225), but there is no choice of the principal value for which the identity

$$\left(b^r\right)^s = b^{r s}$$

is true; see . Therefore, exponentiation with a basis that is not a positive real number is generally viewed as a [multivalued function](wikipedia://en/222367).

### Limits of rational exponents {#Limits_of_rational_exponents}

*Image: Continuity of the Exponential at 0.svg.* The limit of $e^{1/n}$ is $e^{0} = 1$ when $n$ tends to the infinity.

Since any [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) can be expressed as the [limit of a sequence](wikipedia://en/285773) of rational numbers, exponentiation of a positive real number $b$ with an arbitrary real exponent $x$ can be defined by [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) with the rule[^o44]

$$b^x = \lim_{r (\in \mathbb{Q}) \to x} b^r \quad (b \in \mathbb{R}^+,\, x \in \mathbb{R}),$$

where the limit is taken over rational values of $r$ only. This limit exists for every positive $b$ and every real $x$.

For example, if $x = \pi$, the non-terminating [decimal representation](wikipedia://en/3293594) $\pi = 3.14159...$ and the [monotonicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_function) of the rational powers can be used to obtain intervals bounded by rational powers that are as small as desired, and must contain $b^\pi:$

$$\left[b^3, b^4\right], \left[b^{3.1}, b^{3.2}\right], \left[b^{3.14}, b^{3.15}\right], \left[b^{3.141}, b^{3.142}\right], \left[b^{3.1415}, b^{3.1416}\right], \left[b^{3.14159}, b^{3.14160}\right], \ldots$$

So, the upper bounds and the lower bounds of the intervals form two [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) that have the same limit, denoted $b^\pi.$

This defines $b^x$ for every positive $b$ and real $x$ as a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) of $b$ and $x$.[^o45]

### Exponential function {#Exponential_function}

The *exponential function* may be defined as $x\mapsto e^x,$ where $e\approx 2.718$ is [Euler's number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_number), but to avoid [circular reasoning](wikipedia://en/313944), this definition cannot be used here. Rather, we give an independent definition of the exponential function $\exp(x),$ and of $e=\exp(1)$, relying only on positive integer powers (repeated multiplication). Then we sketch the proof that this agrees with the previous definition: $\exp(x)=e^x.$

There are [many equivalent ways to define the exponential function](wikipedia://en/853175), one of them being

$$\exp(x) = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n.$$

One has $\exp(0)=1,$ and the *exponential identity* (or multiplication rule) $\exp(x)\exp(y)=\exp(x+y)$ holds as well, since

$$\exp(x)\exp(y) = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \left(1 + \frac{x}{n}\right)^n\left(1 + \frac{y}{n}\right)^n = \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} \left(1 + \frac{x+y}{n} + \frac{xy}{n^2}\right)^n,$$

and the second-order term $\frac{xy}{n^2}$ does not affect the limit, yielding $\exp(x)\exp(y) = \exp(x+y)$.

Euler's number can be defined as $e=\exp(1)$. It follows from the preceding equations that $\exp(x)=e^x$ when $x$ is an integer (this results from the repeated-multiplication definition of the exponentiation). If $x$ is real, $\exp(x)=e^x$ results from the definitions given in preceding sections, by using the exponential identity if $x$ is rational, and the continuity of the exponential function otherwise.

The limit that defines the exponential function converges for every [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) value of $x$, and therefore it can be used to extend the definition of $\exp(z)$, and thus $e^z,$ from the real numbers to any complex argument $z$. This extended exponential function still satisfies the exponential identity, and is commonly used for defining exponentiation for complex base and exponent.

### Powers via logarithms {#Powers_via_logarithms}

The definition of $e^{x}$ as the exponential function allows defining $b^{x}$ for every positive real numbers $b$, in terms of exponential and [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) function. Specifically, the fact that the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) $\ln(x)$ is the [inverse](wikipedia://en/14907) of the exponential function $e^{x}$ means that one has

$$b = \exp(\ln b)=e^{\ln b}$$

for every $b > 0$. For preserving the identity $(e^x)^y=e^{xy},$ one must have

$$b^x=\left(e^{\ln b} \right)^x = e^{x \ln b}$$

So, $e^{x \ln b}$ can be used as an alternative definition of $b^{x}$ for any positive real $b$. This agrees with the definition given above using rational exponents and continuity, with the advantage to extend straightforwardly to any complex exponent.

::: {#o49 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Imaginary exponent"}

## Complex exponents with a positive real base {#Complex_exponents_with_a_positive_real_base}

If $b$ is a positive real number, exponentiation with base $b$ and [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) exponent $z$ is defined by means of the exponential function with complex argument (see the end of , above) as

$$b^z = e^{(z\ln b)},$$

where $\ln b$ denotes the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) of $b$.

This satisfies the identity

$$b^{z+t} = b^z b^t,$$

In general, $\left(b^z\right)^t$ is not defined, since $b^{z}$ is not a real number. If a meaning is given to the exponentiation of a complex number (see , below), one has, in general,

$$\left(b^z\right)^t \ne b^{zt},$$

unless $z$ is real or $t$ is an integer.

[Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613),

$$e^{iy} = \cos y + i \sin y,$$

allows expressing the [polar form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_form) of $b^z$ in terms of the [real and imaginary parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_and_imaginary_parts) of $z$, namely

$$b^{x+iy}= b^x(\cos(y\ln b)+i\sin(y\ln b)),$$

where the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the [trigonometric](wikipedia://en/18717261) factor is one. This results from

$$b^{x+iy}=b^x b^{iy}=b^x e^{iy\ln b} =b^x(\cos(y\ln b)+i\sin(y\ln b)).$$

:::

## Non-integer exponents with a complex base {#Non-integer_exponents_with_a_complex_base}

In the preceding sections, exponentiation with non-integer exponents has been defined for positive real bases only. For other bases, difficulties appear already with the apparently simple case of $n$th roots, that is, of exponents $1/n,$ where $n$ is a positive integer. Although the general theory of exponentiation with non-integer exponents applies to $n$th roots, this case can be treated independently, as it does not depend on the use of [complex logarithms](wikipedia://en/4870290).

### $n$th roots of a complex number {#th_roots_of_a_complex_number}

Every nonzero complex number $z$ may be written in [polar form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_form) as

$$z=\rho e^{i\theta}=\rho(\cos \theta +i \sin \theta),$$

where $\rho$ is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of $z$, and $\theta$ is its [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765). The argument is defined [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) an integer multiple of $2\pi$; this means that, if $\theta$ is the argument of a complex number, then $\theta +2k\pi$ is also an argument of the same complex number for every integer $k$.

The polar form of the product of two complex numbers is obtained by multiplying the absolute values and adding the arguments. It follows that the polar form of an $n$th root of a complex number can be obtained by taking the $n$th root of the absolute value and dividing its argument by $n$:

$$\left(\rho e^{i\theta}\right)^\frac 1n=\sqrt[n]\rho \,e^\frac{i\theta}n.$$

If $2\pi$ is added to $\theta$, the complex number is not changed, but this adds $2i\pi/n$ to the argument of the $n$th root, and provides a new $n$th root. This can be done $n$ times ($k=0,1,...,n-1$), and provides the $n$ $n$th roots of the complex number:

$$\left(\rho e^{i(\theta+2k\pi)}\right)^\frac 1n=\sqrt[n]\rho \,e^\frac{i(\theta+2k\pi)}n.$$

It is usual to choose one of the $n$ $n$th roots as the [principal root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_root). The common choice is to choose the $n$th root for which $-\pi<\theta\le \pi,$ that is, the $n$th root that has the largest real part, and, if there are two, the one with positive imaginary part. This makes the principal $n$th root a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) in the whole complex plane, except for negative real values of the [radicand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radicand). This function equals the usual $n$th root for positive real radicands. For negative real radicands, and odd exponents, the principal $n$th root is not real, although the usual $n$th root is real. [Analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) shows that the principal $n$th root is the unique [complex differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_differentiable) function that extends the usual $n$th root to the complex plane without the nonpositive real numbers.

If the complex number is moved around zero by increasing its argument, after an increment of $2\pi,$ the complex number comes back to its initial position, and its $n$th roots are [permuted circularly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circular_permutation) (they are multiplied by $e^{2i\pi/n}$). This shows that it is not possible to define a $n$th root function that is continuous in the whole complex plane.

#### Roots of unity {#Roots_of_unity}

*Image: One3Root.svg.* The three third roots of $1$

The $n$th roots of unity are the $n$ complex numbers such that $\omega^n = 1$, where $n$ is a positive integer. They arise in various areas of mathematics, such as in [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) or algebraic solutions of algebraic equations ([Lagrange resolvent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrange_resolvent)).

The $n$th roots of unity are the $n$ first powers of $\omega =e^{2\pi i/n}$, that is $1=\omega^0=\omega^n$, $\omega=\omega^1$, $\omega^2$, ..., $\omega^{n-1}$. The $n$th roots of unity that have this generating property are called *primitive $n$th roots of unity*; they have the form $\omega^k=e^{2k\pi i/n}$ with $k$ [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556) with $n$. The unique primitive [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of unity is $-1;$ the primitive fourth roots of unity are $i$ and $-i$.

The $n$th roots of unity allow expressing all $n$th roots of a complex number $z$ as the $n$ products of a given $n$th roots of $z$ with a $n$th root of unity.

Geometrically, the $n$th roots of unity lie on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) at the vertices of a [regular $n$-gon](wikipedia://en/333306) with one vertex on the real number 1.

As the number $e^{2k\pi i/n}$ is the primitive $n$th root of unity with the smallest positive [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765), it is called the *principal primitive $n$th root of unity*, sometimes shortened as *principal $n$th root of unity*, although this terminology can be confused with the [principal value](wikipedia://en/408225) of $1^{1/n}$, which is 1.[^o53][^o54]

### Complex exponentiation {#Complex_exponentiation}

Defining exponentiation with complex bases leads to difficulties that are similar to those described in the preceding section, except that there are, in general, infinitely many possible values for $z^w$. So, either a [principal value](wikipedia://en/408225) is defined, which is not continuous for the values of $z$ that are real and nonpositive, or $z^w$ is defined as a [multivalued function](wikipedia://en/222367).

In all cases, the [complex logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290) is used to define complex exponentiation as

$$z^w=e^{w\log z},$$

where $\log z$ is the variant of the complex logarithm that is used, which is a function or a multivalued function such that

$$e^{\log z}=z$$

for every $z$ in its [domain of definition](wikipedia://en/50263).

#### Principal value {#Principal_value}

The [principal value](wikipedia://en/408225) of the [complex logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290) is the unique continuous function, commonly denoted $\log,$ such that, for every nonzero complex number $z$,

$$e^{\log z}=z,$$

and the [argument](wikipedia://en/14699765) of $z$ satisfies

$$-\pi <\operatorname{Arg}z \le \pi.$$

The principal value of the complex logarithm is not defined for $z=0,$ it is [discontinuous](wikipedia://en/6122) at negative real values of $z$, and it is [holomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic) (that is, complex differentiable) elsewhere. If $z$ is real and positive, the principal value of the complex logarithm is the natural logarithm: $\log z=\ln z.$

The principal value of $z^w$ is defined as $z^w=e^{w\log z},$ where $\log z$ is the principal value of the logarithm.

The function $(z,w)\to z^w$ is holomorphic except in the [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of the points where $z$ is real and nonpositive.

If $z$ is real and positive, the principal value of $z^w$ equals its usual value defined above. If $w=1/n,$ where $n$ is an integer, this principal value is the same as the one defined above.

#### Multivalued function {#Multivalued_function}

In some contexts, there is a problem with the discontinuity of the principal values of $\log z$ and $z^w$ at the negative real values of $z$. In this case, it is useful to consider these functions as [multivalued functions](wikipedia://en/222367).

If $\log z$ denotes one of the values of the multivalued logarithm (typically its principal value), the other values are $2ik\pi +\log z,$ where $k$ is any integer. Similarly, if $z^w$ is one value of the exponentiation, then the other values are given by

$$e^{w(2ik\pi +\log z)} = z^we^{2ik\pi w},$$

where $k$ is any integer.

Different values of $k$ give different values of $z^w$ unless $w$ is a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024), that is, there is an integer $d$ such that $dw$ is an integer. This results from the [periodicity](wikipedia://en/76848) of the exponential function, more specifically, that $e^a=e^b$ if and only if $a-b$ is an integer multiple of $2\pi i.$

If $w=\frac mn$ is a rational number with $m$ and $n$ [coprime integers](wikipedia://en/6556) with $n>0,$ then $z^w$ has exactly $n$ values. In the case $m=1,$ these values are the same as those described in § $n$th roots of a complex number. If $w$ is an integer, there is only one value that agrees with that of .

The multivalued exponentiation is holomorphic for $z\ne 0,$ in the sense that its [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) consists of several sheets that define each a holomorphic function in the neighborhood of every point. If $z$ varies continuously along a circle around $0$, then, after a turn, the value of $z^w$ has changed of sheet.

::: {#o60 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Computation"}

#### Computation {#Computation}

The *canonical form* $x+iy$ of $z^w$ can be computed from the canonical form of $z$ and $w$. Although this can be described by a single formula, it is clearer to split the computation in several steps.

- *[Polar form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_form) of $z$*. If $z=a+ib$ is the canonical form of $z$ ($a$ and $b$ being real), then its polar form is $$z=\rho e^{i\theta}= \rho (\cos\theta + i \sin\theta),$$ with $\rho=\sqrt{a^2+b^2}$ and $\theta=\operatorname{atan2}(b,a)$, where $\operatorname{atan2}$ is the [two-argument arctangent](wikipedia://en/3856703) function.
- *[Logarithm](wikipedia://en/4870290) of $z$*. The [principal value](wikipedia://en/408225) of this logarithm is $\log z=\ln \rho+i\theta,$ where $\ln$ denotes the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476). The other values of the logarithm are obtained by adding $2ik\pi$ for any integer $k$.
- *Canonical form of $w\log z.$* If $w=c+di$ with $c$ and $d$ real, the values of $w\log z$ are $$w\log z = (c\ln \rho - d\theta-2dk\pi) +i (d\ln \rho + c\theta+2ck\pi),$$ the principal value corresponding to $k=0.$
- *Final result*. Using the identities $e^{x+y}=e^xe^y$ and $e^{y\ln x} = x^y,$ one gets $$z^w=\rho^c e^{-d(\theta+2k\pi)} \left(\cos (d\ln \rho + c\theta+2ck\pi) +i\sin(d\ln \rho + c\theta+2ck\pi)\right),$$ with $k=0$ for the principal value.

::: {#o62 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

##### Examples {#Examples}

- $i^i$ The polar form of $i$ is $i=e^{i\pi/2},$ and the values of $\log i$ are thus $$\log i=i\left(\frac \pi 2 +2k\pi\right).$$ It follows that $$i^i=e^{i\log i}=e^{-\frac \pi 2} e^{-2k\pi}.$$ So, all values of $i^i$ are real, the principal one being $$e^{-\frac \pi 2} \approx 0.2079.$$
- $(-2)^{3+4i}$ Similarly, the polar form of $-2$ is $-2 = 2e^{i \pi}.$ So, the above described method gives the values $$\begin{aligned} (-2)^{3 + 4i} &= 2^3 e^{-4(\pi+2k\pi)} (\cos(4\ln 2 + 3(\pi +2k\pi)) +i\sin(4\ln 2 + 3(\pi+2k\pi)))\\ &=-2^3 e^{-4(\pi+2k\pi)}(\cos(4\ln 2) +i\sin(4\ln 2)). \end{aligned}$$ In this case, all the values have the same argument $4\ln 2,$ and different absolute values.

In both examples, all values of $z^w$ have the same argument. More generally, this is true if and only if the [real part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) of $w$ is an integer.

#### Failure of power and logarithm identities {#Failure_of_power_and_logarithm_identities}

Some identities for powers and logarithms for positive real numbers will fail for complex numbers, no matter how complex powers and complex logarithms are defined *as single-valued functions*. For example:

The identity $\log(b^{x}) = x \cdot \log\,b$ holds whenever $b$ is a positive real number and $x$ is a real number. But for the [principal branch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_branch) of the complex logarithm one has $\log((-i)^2) = \log(-1) = i\pi \neq 2\log(-i) = 2\log(e^{-i\pi/2})=2\,\frac{-i\pi}{2} = -i\pi$ Regardless of which branch of the logarithm is used, a similar failure of the identity will exist. The best that can be said (if only using this result) is that: $\log w^z \equiv z \log w \pmod{2 \pi i}$ This identity does not hold even when considering log as a multivalued function. The possible values of $\log(w^{z})$ contain those of $z \cdot \log\,w$ as a [proper subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subset). Using $\operatorname{Log}(w)$ for the principal value of $\log(w)$ and $m$, $n$ as any integers the possible values of both sides are: $\begin{aligned} \left\{\log w^z \right\} &= \left\{ z \cdot \operatorname{Log} w + z \cdot 2 \pi i n + 2 \pi i m \mid m,n\in\mathbb{Z}\right\} \\ \left\{z \log w \right\} &= \left\{ z \operatorname{Log} w + z \cdot 2 \pi i n \mid n\in \mathbb{Z}\right\} \end{aligned}$; The identities $(bc)^{x} = b^{x}c^{x}$ and $(b/c)^{x} = b^{x}/c^{x}$ are valid when $b$ and $c$ are positive real numbers and $x$ is a real number. But, for the principal values, one has $(-1 \cdot -1)^\frac{1}{2} =1 \neq (-1)^\frac{1}{2} (-1)^\frac{1}{2} =i \cdot i=i^2 =-1$ and $\left(\frac{1}{-1}\right)^\frac{1}{2} = (-1)^\frac{1}{2} = i \neq \frac{1^\frac{1}{2}}{(-1)^\frac{1}{2}} = \frac{1}{i} = -i$ On the other hand, when $x$ is an integer, the identities are valid for all nonzero complex numbers. If exponentiation is considered as a multivalued function then the possible values of $(-1 \cdot -1)^{1/2}$ are $\{1, -1\}$. The identity holds, but saying $\{1\} = \{(-1 \cdot -1)^{1/2}\}$ is incorrect.; The identity $(e^{x})^{y} = e^{xy}$ holds for real numbers $x$ and $y$, but assuming its truth for complex numbers leads to the following [paradox](wikipedia://en/254364), discovered in 1827 by [Clausen](wikipedia://en/16768984):[^o64] For any integer $n$, we have: $e^{1 + 2 \pi i n} = e^1 e^{2 \pi i n} = e \cdot 1 = e$ $\left(e^{1 + 2\pi i n}\right)^{1 + 2 \pi i n} = e\qquad$ (taking the $(1 + 2 \pi i n)$-th power of both sides) $e^{1 + 4 \pi i n - 4 \pi^2 n^2} = e\qquad$ (using $\left(e^x\right)^y = e^{xy}$ and expanding the exponent) $e^1 e^{4 \pi i n} e^{-4 \pi^2 n^2} = e\qquad$ (using $e^{x+y} = e^x e^y$) $e^{-4 \pi^2 n^2} = 1\qquad$ (dividing by $e$) but this is false when the integer $n$ is nonzero. The error is the following: by definition, $e^y$ is a notation for $\exp(y),$ a true function, and $x^y$ is a notation for $\exp(y\log x),$ which is a multi-valued function. Thus the notation is ambiguous when $x = e$. Here, before expanding the exponent, the second line should be $\exp\left((1 + 2\pi i n)\log \exp(1 + 2\pi i n)\right) = \exp(1 + 2\pi i n).$ Therefore, when expanding the exponent, one has implicitly supposed that $\log \exp z =z$ for complex values of $z$, which is wrong, as the complex logarithm is multivalued. In other words, the wrong identity $(e^{x})^{y} = e^{xy}$ must be replaced by the identity $\left(e^x\right)^y = e^{y\log e^x},$ which is a true identity between multivalued functions.

## Irrationality and transcendence {#Irrationality_and_transcendence}

If $b$ is a positive real [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158), and $x$ is a rational number, then $b^{x}$ is an algebraic number. This results from the theory of [algebraic extensions](wikipedia://en/2125). This remains true if $b$ is any algebraic number, in which case, all values of $b^{x}$ (as a multivalued function) are algebraic. If $x$ is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689) (that is, *not rational*), and both $b$ and $x$ are algebraic, the Gelfond–Schneider theorem asserts that all values of $b^{x}$ are [transcendental](wikipedia://en/30325) (that is, not algebraic), except if $b$ equals $0$ or $1$.

In other words, if $x$ is irrational and $b\not\in \{0,1\},$ then at least one of $b$, $x$ and $b^{x}$ is transcendental.

## Integer powers in algebra {#Integer_powers_in_algebra}

The definition of exponentiation with positive integer exponents as repeated multiplication may apply to any [associative operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_operation) denoted as a multiplication.[^o67] The definition of $x^{0}$ requires further the existence of a [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity).[^o68]

An [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) consisting of a set together with an associative operation denoted multiplicatively, and a multiplicative identity denoted by $1$ is a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652). In such a monoid, exponentiation of an element $x$ is defined inductively by

- $$x^0 = 1,$$
- $x^{n+1} = x x^n$ for every nonnegative integer $n$.

If $n$ is a negative integer, $x^n$ is defined only if $x$ has a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940).[^o69] In this case, the inverse of $x$ is denoted $x^{-1}$, and $x^{n}$ is defined as $\left(x^{-1}\right)^{-n}.$

Exponentiation with integer exponents obeys the following laws, for $x$ and $y$ in the algebraic structure, and $m$ and $n$ integers:

$$\begin{aligned} x^0&=1\\ x^{m+n}&=x^m x^n\\ (x^m)^n&=x^{mn}\\ (xy)^n&=x^n y^n \quad \text{if } xy=yx, \text{and, in particular, if the multiplication is commutative.} \end{aligned}$$

These definitions are widely used in many areas of mathematics, notably for [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), [square matrices](wikipedia://en/166022) (which form a ring). They apply also to [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) from a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) to itself, which form a monoid under [function composition](wikipedia://en/195947). This includes, as specific instances, [geometric transformations](wikipedia://en/43194879), and [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) of any [mathematical structure](wikipedia://en/1809181).

### In a group {#In_a_group}

A [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) is a set with as [associative operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_operation) denoted as multiplication, that has an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962), and such that every element has an inverse.

So, if $G$ is a group, $x^n$ is defined for every $x\in G$ and every integer $n$.

The set of all powers of an element of a group form a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011). A group (or subgroup) that consists of all powers of a specific element $x$ is the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) generated by $x$. If all the powers of $x$ are distinct, the group is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the [additive group](wikipedia://en/332229) $\mathbb{Z}$ of the integers. Otherwise, the cyclic group is [finite](wikipedia://en/323707) (it has a finite number of elements), and its number of elements is the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of $x$. If the order of $x$ is $n$, then $x^n=x^0=1,$ and the cyclic group generated by $x$ consists of the $n$ first powers of $x$ (starting indifferently from the exponent $0$ or $1$).

Order of elements play a fundamental role in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890). For example, the order of an element in a finite group is always a divisor of the number of elements of the group (the *order* of the group). The possible orders of group elements are important in the study of the structure of a group (see [Sylow theorems](wikipedia://en/53993)), and in the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445).

Superscript notation is also used for [conjugation](wikipedia://en/49176); that is, $g^{h} = h^{-1}gh$, where $g$ and $h$ are elements of a group. This notation cannot be confused with exponentiation, since the superscript is not an integer. The motivation of this notation is that conjugation obeys some of the laws of exponentiation, namely $(g^h)^k=g^{hk}$ and $(gh)^k=g^kh^k.$

### In a ring {#In_a_ring}

In a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), it may occur that some nonzero elements satisfy $x^n=0$ for some integer $n$. Such an element is said to be [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235). In a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), the nilpotent elements form an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977), called the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636) of the ring.

If the nilradical is reduced to the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) (that is, if $x\neq 0$ implies $x^n\neq 0$ for every positive integer $n$), the commutative ring is said to be [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749). Reduced rings are important in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), since the [coordinate ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_ring) of an [affine algebraic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_algebraic_set) is always a reduced ring.

More generally, given an ideal $I$ in a commutative ring $R$, the set of the elements of $R$ that have a power in $I$ is an ideal, called the [radical](wikipedia://en/252009) of $I$. The nilradical is the radical of the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal). A [radical ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_ideal) is an ideal that equals its own radical. In a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $k[x_1, \ldots, x_n]$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $k$, an ideal is radical if and only if it is the set of all polynomials that are zero on an affine algebraic set (this is a consequence of [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215)).

### Matrices and linear operators {#Matrices_and_linear_operators}

If $A$ is a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022), then the product of $A$ with itself $n$ times is called the [matrix power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_power). Also $A^0$ is defined to be the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718),[^o73] and if $A$ is [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122), then $A^{-n} = \left(A^{-1}\right)^n$.

Matrix powers appear often in the context of [discrete dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_dynamical_system), where the matrix $A$ expresses a transition from a state vector $x$ of some system to the next state $Ax$ of the system.[^o74] This is the standard interpretation of a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876), for example. Then $A^2x$ is the state of the system after two time steps, and so forth: $A^nx$ is the state of the system after $n$ time steps. The matrix power $A^n$ is the transition matrix between the state now and the state at a time $n$ steps in the future. So computing matrix powers is equivalent to solving the evolution of the dynamical system. In many cases, matrix powers can be expediently computed by using [eigenvalues and eigenvectors](wikipedia://en/2161429).

Apart from matrices, more general [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) can also be exponentiated. An example is the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) operator of calculus, $d/dx$, which is a linear operator acting on functions $f(x)$ to give a new function $(d/dx)f(x) = f'(x)$. The $n$th power of the differentiation operator is the $n$th derivative:

$$\left(\frac{d}{dx}\right)^nf(x) = \frac{d^n}{dx^n}f(x) = f^{(n)}(x).$$

These examples are for discrete exponents of linear operators, but in many circumstances it is also desirable to define powers of such operators with continuous exponents. This is the starting point of the mathematical theory of [semigroups](wikipedia://en/1644938).[^o75] Just as computing matrix powers with discrete exponents solves discrete dynamical systems, so does computing matrix powers with continuous exponents solve systems with continuous dynamics. Examples include approaches to solving the [heat equation](wikipedia://en/166415), [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874), [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691), and other partial differential equations including a time evolution. The special case of exponentiating the derivative operator to a non-integer power is called the [fractional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_derivative) which, together with the [fractional integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_integral), is one of the basic operations of the [fractional calculus](wikipedia://en/229939).

### Finite fields {#Finite_fields}

A [field](wikipedia://en/10603) is an algebraic structure in which multiplication, addition, subtraction, and division are defined and satisfy the properties that multiplication is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) and every nonzero element has a [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940). This implies that exponentiation with integer exponents is well-defined, except for nonpositive powers of $0$. Common examples are the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) and the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), considered earlier in this article, which are all [infinite](wikipedia://en/142679).

A *finite field* is a field with a [finite number](wikipedia://en/11742) of elements. This number of elements is either a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) or a [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483); that is, it has the form $q=p^k,$ where $p$ is a prime number, and $k$ is a positive integer. For every such $q$, there are fields with $q$ elements. The fields with $q$ elements are all [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic), which allows, in general, working as if there were only one field with $q$ elements, denoted $\mathbb F_q.$

One has

$$x^q=x$$

for every $x\in \mathbb F_q.$

A [primitive element](wikipedia://en/19127149) in $\mathbb F_q$ is an element $g$ such that the set of the $q - 1$ first powers of $g$ (that is, $\{g^1=g, g^2, \ldots, g^{p-1}=g^0=1\}$) equals the set of the nonzero elements of $\mathbb F_q.$ There are $\varphi (p-1)$ primitive elements in $\mathbb F_q,$ where $\varphi$ is [Euler's totient function](wikipedia://en/53452).

In $\mathbb F_q,$ the [freshman's dream](wikipedia://en/17373539) identity

$$(x+y)^p = x^p+y^p$$

is true for the exponent $p$. As $x^p=x$ in $\mathbb F_q,$ It follows that the map

$$\begin{aligned} F\colon{} & \mathbb F_q \to \mathbb F_q\\ & x\mapsto x^p \end{aligned}$$

is [linear](wikipedia://en/18102) over $\mathbb F_q,$ and is a [field automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_automorphism), called the [Frobenius automorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius_automorphism). If $q=p^k,$ the field $\mathbb F_q$ has $k$ automorphisms, which are the $k$ first powers (under [composition](wikipedia://en/195947)) of $F$. In other words, the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) of $\mathbb F_q$ is [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) of order $k$, generated by the Frobenius automorphism.

The [Diffie–Hellman key exchange](wikipedia://en/7903) is an application of exponentiation in finite fields that is widely used for [secure communications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secure_communication). It uses the fact that exponentiation is computationally inexpensive, whereas the inverse operation, the [discrete logarithm](wikipedia://en/181334), is computationally expensive. More precisely, if $g$ is a primitive element in $\mathbb F_q,$ then $g^e$ can be efficiently computed with [exponentiation by squaring](wikipedia://en/10237) for any $e$, even if $q$ is large, while there is no known computationally practical algorithm that allows retrieving $e$ from $g^e$ if $q$ is sufficiently large.

## Powers of sets {#Powers_of_sets}

The [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ and $T$ is the set of the [ordered pairs](wikipedia://en/22362) $(x,y)$ such that $x\in S$ and $y\in T.$ This operation is not properly [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) nor [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), but has these properties [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) [canonical](wikipedia://en/23335649) [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828), that allow identifying, for example, $(x,(y,z)),$ $((x,y),z),$ and $(x,y,z).$

This allows defining the $n$th power $S^n$ of a set $S$ as the set of all $n$-[tuples](wikipedia://en/132729) $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ of elements of $S$.

When $S$ is endowed with some structure, it is frequent that $S^n$ is naturally endowed with a similar structure. In this case, the term "[direct product](wikipedia://en/8887)" is generally used instead of "Cartesian product", and exponentiation denotes product structure. For example $\mathbb{R}^n$ (where $\mathbb{R}$ denotes the real numbers) denotes the Cartesian product of $n$ copies of $\mathbb{R},$ as well as their direct product as [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), etc.

::: {#o79 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Set exponentiation"}

### Sets as exponents {#Sets_as_exponents}

A $n$-tuple $(x_1, \ldots, x_n)$ of elements of $S$ can be considered as a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) from $\{1,\ldots, n\}.$ This generalizes to the following notation.

Given two sets $S$ and $T$, the set of all functions from $T$ to $S$ is denoted $S^T$. This exponential notation is justified by the following canonical isomorphisms (for the first one, see [Currying](wikipedia://en/6600)):

$$(S^T)^U\cong S^{T\times U},$$

$$S^{T\sqcup U}\cong S^T\times S^U,$$

where $\times$ denotes the Cartesian product, and $\sqcup$ the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/188946).

One can use sets as exponents for other operations on sets, typically for [direct sums](wikipedia://en/2084687) of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), or [modules](wikipedia://en/276410). For distinguishing direct sums from direct products, the exponent of a direct sum is placed between parentheses. For example, $\mathbb{R}^\mathbb{N}$ denotes the vector space of the [infinite sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_sequence) of real numbers, and $\mathbb{R}^{(\mathbb{N})}$ the vector space of those sequences that have a finite number of nonzero elements. The latter has a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) consisting of the sequences with exactly one nonzero element that equals $1$, while the [Hamel bases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamel_basis) of the former cannot be explicitly described (because their existence involves [Zorn's lemma](wikipedia://en/51442)).

In this context, $2$ can represents the set $\{0,1\}.$ So, $2^S$ denotes the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of $S$, that is the set of the functions from $S$ to $\{0,1\},$ which can be identified with the set of the [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of $S$, by mapping each function to the [inverse image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_image) of $1$.

This fits in with the [exponentiation of cardinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_exponentiation), in the sense that $\lvert S^{T}\rvert = \lvert S\rvert^{\lvert T\rvert}$, where $\lvert X\rvert$ is the cardinality of $X$.

:::

### In category theory {#In_category_theory}

In the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829), the [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) between sets $X$ and $Y$ are the functions from $X$ to $Y$. It results that the set of the functions from $X$ to $Y$ that is denoted $Y^X$ in the preceding section can also be denoted $\hom(X,Y).$ The isomorphism $(S^T)^U\cong S^{T\times U}$ can be rewritten

$$\hom(U,S^T)\cong \hom(T\times U,S).$$

This means the functor "exponentiation to the power $T\$" is a [right adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_adjoint) to the functor "direct product with $T\$".

This generalizes to the definition of [exponentiation in a category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponential_%28category_theory%29) in which finite [direct products](wikipedia://en/8887) exist: in such a category, the functor $X\to X^T$ is, if it exists, a right adjoint to the functor $Y\to T\times Y.$ A category is called a *Cartesian closed category*, if direct products exist, and the functor $Y\to X\times Y$ has a right adjoint for every $T$.

## Repeated exponentiation {#Repeated_exponentiation}

Just as exponentiation of natural numbers is motivated by repeated multiplication, it is possible to define an operation based on repeated exponentiation; this operation is sometimes called hyper-4 or tetration. Iterating tetration leads to another operation, and so on, a concept named hyperoperation. This sequence of operations is expressed by the [Ackermann function](wikipedia://en/2925) and [Knuth's up-arrow notation](wikipedia://en/258733). Just as exponentiation grows faster than multiplication, which is faster-growing than addition, tetration is faster-growing than exponentiation. Evaluated at $(3, 3)$, the functions addition, multiplication, exponentiation, and tetration yield 6, 9, 27, and 7625597484987 ($=3^{27} = 3^{3^{3}} =^{3}3$) respectively.

## Limits of powers {#Limits_of_powers}

[Zero to the power of zero](wikipedia://en/32130416) gives a number of examples of limits that are of the [indeterminate form](wikipedia://en/366723) 0<sup>0</sup>. The limits in these examples exist, but have different values, showing that the two-variable function $x^{y}$ has no limit at the point $(0, 0)$. One may consider at what points this function does have a limit.

More precisely, consider the function $f(x,y) = x^y$ defined on $D = \{(x, y) \in \mathbf{R}^2 : x > 0 \}$. Then $D$ can be viewed as a subset of $\overline{\mathbf{R}}^{2}$ (that is, the set of all pairs $(x, y)$ with $x$, $y$ belonging to the [extended real number line](wikipedia://en/51698) $\overline{\mathbf{R}} = [-\infty, +\infty]$, endowed with the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910)), which will contain the points at which the function $f$ has a limit.

In fact, $f$ has a limit at all [accumulation points](wikipedia://en/226505) of $D$, except for $(0, 0)$, $(+\infty, 0)$, $(1, +\infty)$ and $(1, -\infty)$.[^o83] Accordingly, this allows one to define the powers $x^{y}$ by continuity whenever $0 \le x \le +\infty$, $-\infty \le y \le +\infty$, except for $0^{0}$, $(+\infty)^{0}$, $1^{+\infty}$ and $1^{-\infty}$, which remain indeterminate forms.

Under this definition by continuity, we obtain:

- $x^{+\infty} = +\infty$ and $x^{-\infty} = 0$, when $1 < x \le +\infty$.
- $x^{+\infty} = 0$ and $x^{-\infty} = +\infty$, when $0 < x < 1$.
- $0^{y} = 0$ and $(+\infty)^{y} = +\infty$, when $0 < y \le +\infty$.
- $0^{y} = +\infty$ and $(+\infty)^{y} = 0$, when $-\infty \le y < 0$.

These powers are obtained by taking limits of $x^{y}$ for *positive* values of $x$. This method does not permit a definition of $x^{y}$ when $x < 0$, since pairs $(x, y)$ with $x < 0$ are not accumulation points of $D$.

On the other hand, when $n$ is an integer, the power $x^{n}$ is already meaningful for all values of $x$, including negative ones. This may make the definition $0^{n} = +\infty$ obtained above for negative $n$ problematic when $n$ is odd, since in this case $x^{n} \to +\infty$ as $x$ tends to $0$ through positive values, but not negative ones.

## Efficient computation with integer exponents {#Efficient_computation_with_integer_exponents}

Computing $b^{n}$ using iterated multiplication requires $n - 1$ multiplication operations, but it can be computed more efficiently than that, as illustrated by the following example. To compute $2^{100}$, apply [Horner's rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horner's_rule) to the exponent 100 written in binary:

$$100 = 2^2 +2^5 + 2^6 = 2^2(1+2^3(1+2)).$$

Then compute the following terms in order, reading Horner's rule from right to left.

::: {#o85 .table cue=table}

| 2<sup>2</sup> = 4 |
|---|
| 2 (2<sup>2</sup>) = 2<sup>3</sup> = 8 |
| (2<sup>3</sup>)<sup>2</sup> = 2<sup>6</sup> = 64 |
| (2<sup>6</sup>)<sup>2</sup> = 2<sup>12</sup> = 4,096 |
| (2<sup>12</sup>)<sup>2</sup> = 2<sup>24</sup> = 16,777,216 |
| 2 (2<sup>24</sup>) = 2<sup>25</sup> = 33,554,432 |
| (2<sup>25</sup>)<sup>2</sup> = 2<sup>50</sup> = 1,125,899,906,842,624 |
| (2<sup>50</sup>)<sup>2</sup> = 2<sup>100</sup> = 1,267,650,600,228,229,401,496,703,205,376 |

:::

This series of steps only requires 8 multiplications instead of 99.

In general, the number of multiplication operations required to compute $b^{n}$ can be reduced to $\sharp n +\lfloor \log_{2} n\rfloor -1,$ by using [exponentiation by squaring](wikipedia://en/10237), where $\sharp n$ denotes the number of $1$s in the [binary representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_representation) of $n$. For some exponents (100 is not among them), the number of multiplications can be further reduced by computing and using the minimal [addition-chain exponentiation](wikipedia://en/856356). Finding the *minimal* sequence of multiplications (the minimal-length addition chain for the exponent) for $b^{n}$ is a difficult problem, for which no efficient algorithms are currently known (see [Subset sum problem](wikipedia://en/36811)), but many reasonably efficient heuristic algorithms are available.[^o86] However, in practical computations, exponentiation by squaring is efficient enough, and much easier to implement.

## Iterated functions {#Iterated_functions}

[Function composition](wikipedia://en/195947) is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) that is defined on [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) such that the [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) of the function written on the right is included in the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of the function written on the left. It is denoted $g\circ f,$ and defined as

$$(g\circ f)(x)=g(f(x))$$

for every $x$ in the domain of $f$.

If the domain of a function $f$ equals its codomain, one may compose the function with itself an arbitrary number of time, and this defines the $n$th power of the function under composition, commonly called the *$n$th iterate* of the function. Thus $f^n$ denotes generally the $n$th iterate of $f$; for example, $f^3(x)$ means $f(f(f(x))).$[^o125]

When a multiplication is defined on the codomain of the function, this defines a multiplication on functions, the [pointwise multiplication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointwise_multiplication), which induces another exponentiation. When using [functional notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_notation), the two kinds of exponentiation are generally distinguished by placing the exponent of the functional iteration *before* the parentheses enclosing the arguments of the function, and placing the exponent of pointwise multiplication *after* the parentheses. Thus $f^2(x)= f(f(x)),$ and $f(x)^2= f(x)\cdot f(x).$ When functional notation is not used, disambiguation is often done by placing the composition symbol before the exponent; for example $f^{\circ 3}=f\circ f \circ f,$ and $f^3=f\cdot f\cdot f.$ For historical reasons, the exponent of a repeated multiplication is placed before the argument for some specific functions, typically the [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367). So, $\sin^2 x$ and $\sin^2(x)$ both mean $\sin(x)\cdot\sin(x)$ and not $\sin(\sin(x))$. Historically, several variants of these notations were used by different authors.[^o126][^o127][^o124]

In this context, the exponent $-1$ denotes always the [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907), if it exists. So $\sin^{-1}x=\sin^{-1}(x) = \arcsin x.$ For the [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) fractions are generally used as in $1/\sin(x)=\frac 1{\sin x}.$

## In programming languages {#In_programming_languages}

[Programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015) generally express exponentiation either as an infix [operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_%28computer_programming%29) or as a function application, as they do not support superscripts. The most common operator symbol for exponentiation is the [caret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caret) (`^`). The [original version of ASCII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ASCII#1963) included an uparrow symbol (`↑`), intended for exponentiation, but this was [replaced by the caret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caret#History) in 1967, so the caret became usual in programming languages.[^o90] The notations include:

- `x ^ y`: [AWK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AWK), [BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BASIC), [J](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_%28programming_language%29), [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29), [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412), [Wolfram Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfram_Language) ([Mathematica](wikipedia://en/49024)), [R](wikipedia://en/376707), [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel), [Analytica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytica_%28software%29), [TeX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TeX) (and its derivatives), [TI-BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-BASIC), [bc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bc_%28programming_language%29) (for integer exponents), [Haskell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haskell) (for nonnegative integer exponents), [Lua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lua), and most [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427).
- `x ** y`. The [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran) character set did not include lowercase characters or punctuation symbols other than `+-*/()&=.,'` and so used `**` for exponentiation[^o120][^o91] (the initial version used `a xx b` instead.[^o119]). Many other languages followed suit: [Ada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_%28programming_language%29), [Z shell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_shell), [KornShell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KornShell), [Bash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bash_%28Unix_shell%29), [COBOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COBOL), [CoffeeScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoffeeScript), [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran), [FoxPro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FoxPro), [Gnuplot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gnuplot), [Groovy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apache_Groovy), [JavaScript](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JavaScript), [OCaml](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OCaml), [Object REXX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_REXX) (ooRexx), [F#](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F_Sharp_%28programming_language%29), [Perl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perl), [PHP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PHP), [PL/I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PL/I), [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29), [Rexx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rexx), [Ruby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruby_%28programming_language%29), [SAS](wikipedia://en/19060492), [Tcl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tcl_%28programming_language%29), [ABAP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ABAP), [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28programming_language%29), Haskell (for floating-point exponents), [Turing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_%28programming_language%29), and [VHDL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VHDL).
- `x ↑ y`: [ALGOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL) (reference language standard), [Commodore BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commodore_BASIC), [TRS-80 Level II/III BASIC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TRS-80_Level_II_BASIC).[^o92][^o93]
- `x ^^ y`: Haskell (for fractional base, integer exponents), [D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D_%28programming_language%29).
- `x⋆y`: [APL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APL_%28programming_language%29).

In most programming languages with an infix exponentiation operator, it is [right-associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operator_associativity), that is, `a^b^c` is interpreted as `a^(b^c)`.[^o94] This is because `(a^b)^c` is equal to `a^(b*c)` and thus not as useful. In some languages, it is left-associative, notably in [ALGOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALGOL), [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412), and the [Microsoft Excel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Excel) formula language.

Other programming languages use [functional programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming) notation:

- `(expt x y)`: [Common Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Lisp).
- `pown x y`: [F#](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F_Sharp_%28programming_language%29) (for integer base, integer exponent).

Still others only provide exponentiation as part of standard [libraries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_%28computing%29):

- `pow(x, y)`: [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29), [C++](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++) (in `math` library).
- `Math.Pow(x, y)`: [C#](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_Sharp_%28programming_language%29).
- `math:pow(X, Y)`: [Erlang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erlang_%28programming_language%29).
- `Math.pow(x, y)`: [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java_%28programming_language%29).
- `[Math]::Pow(x, y)`: [PowerShell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PowerShell).

In some [statically typed](wikipedia://en/199701) languages that prioritize [type safety](wikipedia://en/602650) such as [Rust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rust_%28programming_language%29), exponentiation is performed via a multitude of methods:

- `x.pow(y)` for `x` and `y` as integers
- `x.powf(y)` for `x` and `y` as floating-point numbers
- `x.powi(y)` for `x` as a float and `y` as an integer

## History {#History}

The term *exponent* originates from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) *exponentem*, the [present participle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Present_participle) of *exponere*, meaning "to put forth".[^o96] The term *power* (potentia, potestas, dignitas) is a mistranslation[^o97][^o98] of the [ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) δύναμις (*dúnamis*, here: "amplification"[^o97]) used by the [Greek](wikipedia://en/1503369) mathematician [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) for the square of a line, following [Hippocrates of Chios](wikipedia://en/3632522).[^o100]

In *[The Sand Reckoner](wikipedia://en/276396)*, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) proved the law of exponents, $10^{a} \cdot 10^{b} = 10^{a+b}$, necessary to manipulate powers of $10$.[^o101] He then used powers of $10$ to estimate the number of grains of sand that can be contained in the universe.

In the 9th century, the Persian mathematician [Al-Khwarizmi](wikipedia://en/649861) used the terms مَال (*māl*, "possessions", "property") for a [square](wikipedia://en/659942)—the Muslims, "like most mathematicians of those and earlier times, thought of a squared number as a depiction of an area, especially of land, hence property"[^o107]—and كَعْبَة (*[Kaʿbah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kaaba)*, "cube") for a [cube](wikipedia://en/451445), which later [Islamic mathematicians](wikipedia://en/3304216) represented in [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) as the letters *[mīm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mīm)* (m) and *[kāf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kāf)* (k), respectively, by the 15th century, as seen in the work of [Abu'l-Hasan ibn Ali al-Qalasadi](wikipedia://en/8064048).

[Nicolas Chuquet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolas_Chuquet) used a form of exponential notation in the 15th century, for example $12^{2}$ to represent $12x^{2}$.[^o103] This was later used by [Henricus Grammateus](wikipedia://en/5787009) and Michael Stifel in the 16th century. In the late 16th century, [Jost Bürgi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jost_Bürgi) would use [Roman numerals](wikipedia://en/25657) for exponents in a way similar to that of Chuquet, for example iii for $4x^{3}$.[^o104]

The word *exponent* was [coined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neologism) in 1544 by [Michael Stifel](wikipedia://en/26485293).[^o105][^o106] In the 16th century, [Robert Recorde](wikipedia://en/310247) used the terms "square", "cube", "zenzizenzic" ([fourth power](wikipedia://en/665027)), "sursolid" ([fifth](wikipedia://en/40150949)), "zenzicube" ([sixth](wikipedia://en/54558894)), "second sursolid" ([seventh](wikipedia://en/54558969)), and "[zenzizenzizenzic](wikipedia://en/499002)" ([eighth](wikipedia://en/60976257)).[^o107] "Biquadrate" has been used to refer to the fourth power as well.

In 1636, [James Hume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Hume_%28mathematician%29) used in essence modern notation, when in *L'algèbre de Viète* he wrote $A^{\operatorname{iii}}$ for $A^{3}$.[^o108] Early in the 17th century, the first form of our modern exponential notation was introduced by [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525) in his text titled *[La Géométrie](wikipedia://en/239981)*; there, the notation is introduced in Book I:[^o109]

Some mathematicians (such as Descartes) used exponents only for powers greater than two, preferring to represent squares as repeated multiplication. Thus they would write [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), for example, as $ax + bxx + cx^{3} + d$.

[Samuel Jeake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Jeake) introduced the term *indices* in 1696.[^o99] The term *involution* was used synonymously with the term *indices*, but had declined in usage[^o110] and should not be confused with [its more common meaning](wikipedia://en/293450).

In 1748, [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) introduced [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109) exponents, and, implicitly, non-integer exponents by writing: Consider exponentials or powers in which the exponent itself is a variable. It is clear that quantities of this kind are not [algebraic functions](wikipedia://en/974169), since in those the exponents must be constant.[^o128] As [calculation was mechanized](wikipedia://en/432897), notation was adapted to numerical capacity by conventions in exponential notation. The theoretical concept of [floating-point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating-point) representation was originally introduced by Spanish engineer [Leonardo Torres Quevedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_Torres_Quevedo) in his 1914 *Essays on Automatics*.[^o111][^o112] Later, , German engineer [Konrad Zuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse) achieved its first physical implementation in his [Z1 computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z1_%28computer%29).[^o113] In Zuse's design, one register contained the representation of leading digits, and a second contained the representation of the exponent. The more flexible [decimal floating-point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_floating_point) representation was introduced in 1946 with a [Bell Laboratories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Laboratories) computer. Eventually educators and engineers adopted [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715) of numbers, consistent with common reference to [order of magnitude](wikipedia://en/22657) in a [ratio scale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio_scale).[^o113]

For instance, in 1961 the [School Mathematics Study Group](wikipedia://en/1700591) developed the notation in connection with units used in the [metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_system).[^o114][^o115]
