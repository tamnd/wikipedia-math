# Theophrastus

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Theophrastus"}

**Theophrastus** (; Θεόφραστος; c. 371 – c. 287 BC) was an ancient Greek [philosopher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophy) and [naturalist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history). A native of [Eresos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eresos) in [Lesbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesbos), he was [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)'s close colleague and successor as head of the [Lyceum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyceum_%28classical%29), the [Peripatetic school of philosophy](wikipedia://en/529396) in [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens). Theophrastus wrote numerous treatises across all areas of philosophy, working to support, improve, expand, and develop [the Aristotelian system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_system). He made significant contributions to various fields, including [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics), [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics), [botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany), and [natural history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history). Often considered the "father of botany" for his groundbreaking works "[Enquiry into Plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Plantarum_%28Theophrastus%29)" (Περὶ φυτῶν ἱστορία) and "On the Causes of Plants", (Περὶ αἰτιῶν φυτικῶν) Theophrastus established the foundations of [botanical science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany). His given name was Tyrtamos ([Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek): Τύρταμος); the nickname Theophrastus ("divine speaker") was reputedly given to him by Aristotle in recognition of his eloquent style.

:::

He came to [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens) at a young age and initially studied in [Plato's school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_Academy). After Plato's death, he attached himself to Aristotle who took to Theophrastus in his writings. When Aristotle fled Athens, Theophrastus took over as head of the [Lyceum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyceum_%28classical%29).[^o5] Theophrastus presided over the Peripatetic school for thirty-six years, during which time the school flourished greatly. He is often considered the father of [botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany) for his works on plants.[^o6] After his death, the Athenians honoured him with a public funeral. His successor as head of the school was [Strato of Lampsacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strato_of_Lampsacus).

The interests of Theophrastus were wide ranging, including [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics) and [metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics). His two surviving botanical works, *[Enquiry into Plants (Historia Plantarum)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Plantarum_%28Theophrastus_book%29)* and *On the Causes of Plants*, were an important influence on [Renaissance science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_science). There are also surviving works *On Moral Characters*, *On Sense Perception*, and *On Stones*, as well as fragments on *Physics* and *Metaphysics*. In philosophy, he studied [grammar](wikipedia://en/12569) and [language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Language) and continued Aristotle's work on [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065). He also viewed space as merely the arrangement and position of bodies, time as a byproduct of motion, and motion as an inevitable result of all activity. In [ethics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethics), he regarded [happiness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Happiness) as depending on external influences as well as on [virtue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtue).

## Life {#Life}

Most of the biographical information about Theophrastus was provided by [Diogenes Laërtius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes_Laërtius)' *[Lives and Opinions of Eminent Philosophers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lives_and_Opinions_of_Eminent_Philosophers)*, written more than four hundred years after Theophrastus's time.[^o8] He was a native of [Eresos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eresos) in [Lesbos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lesbos_Island).[^o9] His given name was **Tyrtamus** (Τύρταμος), but he later became known by the nickname "Theophrastus", given to him, it is said, by [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) to indicate the grace of his conversation (from Ancient Greek Θεός 'god' and φράζειν 'to phrase', i.e. divine expression).[^o10]

*Image: Aristotle Theophrastus Strato Lebiedzki Rahl.jpg.* [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), Theophrastus, and [Strato of Lampsacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strato_of_Lampsacus). Part of a fresco in the portico of the [University of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_and_Kapodistrian_University_of_Athens) painted by [Carl Rahl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Rahl), c. 1888.

After receiving instruction in philosophy on Lesbos from one Alcippus, he moved to [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens), where he may have studied under [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954). He became friends with Aristotle, and when Plato died (348/7 BC) Theophrastus may have joined Aristotle in his self-imposed exile from Athens. When Aristotle moved to [Mytilene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mytilene) on Lesbos in 345/4, it is very likely that he did so at the urging of Theophrastus.[^n1] It seems that it was on Lesbos that Aristotle and Theophrastus began their research into [natural science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_science), with Aristotle studying animals and Theophrastus studying plants.[^n2] Theophrastus probably accompanied Aristotle to [Macedonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macedon) when Aristotle was appointed tutor to [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great) in 343/2.[^n1] Around 335 BC, Theophrastus moved with Aristotle to Athens, where Aristotle began teaching in the [Lyceum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyceum_%28classical%29). When, after the death of Alexander, anti-Macedonian feeling forced Aristotle to leave Athens, Theophrastus remained behind as head (*[scholarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholarch)*) of the [Peripatetic school](wikipedia://en/529396),[^n1] a position he continued to hold after Aristotle's death in 322/1.

Aristotle in his will made him guardian of his children, including [Nicomachus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus_%28son_of_Aristotle%29), with whom he was close. Aristotle likewise bequeathed to him his library and the originals of his works, and designated him as his successor at the Lyceum.[^o15] [Eudemus of Rhodes](wikipedia://en/3454204) also had some claims to this position, and [Aristoxenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristoxenus) is said to have resented Aristotle's choice.[^n3]

Theophrastus presided over the Peripatetic school for 35 years,[^n4] and died at age 85, according to Diogenes.[^n5] He is said to have remarked, "We die just when we are beginning to live".[^o19]

Under his guidance, the school flourished greatly—there were at one period more than 2,000 students, Diogenes affirms[^n6]—and at his death, according to the terms of his will preserved by Diogenes, he bequeathed to it his garden with house and colonnades as a permanent seat of instruction. The comic poet [Menander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Menander) was among his pupils.[^n6] His popularity was shown in the regard paid to him by [Philip](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_II_of_Macedon), [Cassander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cassander), and [Ptolemy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ptolemy_I_of_Egypt), and by the complete failure of a charge of impiety brought against him.[^o22][^n7] He was honored with a public funeral, and "the whole population of Athens, honouring him greatly, followed him to the grave."[^n3][^n8] He was succeeded as head of the Lyceum by [Strato of Lampsacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strato_of_Lampsacus).

## Writings {#Writings}

*Image: Theophrastus - Historia plantarum, 1549 - 3034262.tif.* *[Historia plantarum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Plantarum_%28Theophrastus%29)*, 1549

From the lists of Diogenes, giving 227 titles, it appears that the activity of Theophrastus extended over the whole field of contemporary knowledge. His writing probably differed little from Aristotle's treatment of the same themes, though supplementary in details. Like Aristotle, most of his writings are [lost works](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_literary_work).[^n3] Thus Theophrastus, like Aristotle, had composed a first and second *Analytic* (Ἀναλυτικῶν προτέρων and Ἀναλυτικῶν ὑστέρων).[^n9] He had also written books on *Topics* (Ἀνηγμένων τόπων, Τοπικῶν and Τὰ πρὸ τῶν τόπων);[^n10] on the *Analysis of Syllogisms* (Περὶ ἀναλύσεως συλλογισμῶν and Περὶ συλλογισμῶν λύσεως), on *Sophisms* (Σοφισμάτων) and on *Affirmation and Denial* (Περὶ καταφάσεως καὶ ἀποφάσεως)[^n11] as well as on the *Natural Philosophy* (Περὶ φύσεως, Περὶ φυσικῶν, Φυσικῶν and others), on *Heaven* (Περὶ οὐρανοῦ), and on *Meteorological Phenomena* (Τῆς μεταρσιολεσχίας and Μεταρσιολογικῶν).[^n12]

*Image: 161Theophrastus 161 frontespizio.jpg.* Frontispiece to the illustrated 1644 edition of the *Enquiry into Plants* (*[Historia Plantarum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Plantarum_%28Theophrastus%29)*)

In addition, Theophrastus wrote on the *Warm and the Cold* (Περὶ θερμοῦ καὶ ψυχροῦ),[^n13] on *Water* (Περὶ ὕδατος), *Fire* (Περὶ πυρóς),[^n14] the *Sea* (Περὶ θαλάττης),[^n14] on *Coagulation and Melting* (Περὶ πήξεων καὶ τήξεων), on various phenomena of organic and spiritual life,[^n14] and on the *Soul* (Περὶ ψυχῆς), on *Experience* (Περὶ ἐμπειρίας) and *On Sense Perception* (also known as *On the Senses*; Περὶ αἰσθήσεων).[^n15] Likewise, we find mention of monographs of Theophrastus on the early Greek philosophers [Anaximenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaximenes_of_Miletus), [Anaxagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anaxagoras), [Empedocles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empedocles), [Archelaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archelaus_%28philosopher%29),[^n16] [Diogenes of Apollonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diogenes_of_Apollonia), [Democritus](wikipedia://en/8211),[^n17] which were made use of by [Simplicius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicius_of_Cilicia); and also on [Xenocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenocrates),[^n18] against the [Academics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism),[^n19] and a sketch of the political doctrine of [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954).[^n17]

He studied general history, as we know from [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch)'s lives of [Lycurgus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lycurgus_of_Sparta), [Solon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solon), [Aristides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristides), [Pericles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pericles), [Nicias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicias), [Alcibiades](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alcibiades), [Lysander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lysander), [Agesilaus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Agesilaus), and [Demosthenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demosthenes), which were probably borrowed from the work on *Lives* (Περὶ βίων).[^n9] But his main efforts were to continue the labours of Aristotle in [natural history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_history). This is testified to not only by a number of treatises on individual subjects of [zoology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zoology), of which, besides the titles, only fragments remain, but also by his books *On Stones*, his *Enquiry into Plants*, and *On the Causes of Plants* (see below), which have come down to us entire. In politics, also, he seems to have trodden in the footsteps of Aristotle. Besides his books on the *State* (Πολιτικῶν and Πολιτικοῦ), we find quoted various treatises on *Education* (Περὶ παιδείας βασιλέως and Περὶ παιδείας),[^n20] on *Royalty* (Περὶ βασιλείας, Περὶ παιδείας βασιλέως and Πρὸς Κάσανδρον περὶ βασιλείας),[^n21] on the *Best State* (Περὶ τῆς ἀρίστης πολιτείας), on *Political Morals* (Πολιτικῶν ἐθῶν), and particularly his works on the *Laws* (Νόμων κατὰ στοιχεῖον, Νόμων ἐπιτομῆς and Περὶ νόμων), one of which, containing a recapitulation of the laws of various [barbarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbarian) as well as [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greece) states, was intended to be a companion to Aristotle's outline of *Politics*, and must have been similar to it.[^o45] He also wrote on [oratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_speaking) and [poetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry).[^o46] Theophrastus, without doubt, departed further from Aristotle in his [ethical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethical) writings,[^n22] as also in his [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) investigations of [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_%28physics%29), the [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_%28spirit%29), and [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God).[^n23]

Besides these writings, Theophrastus wrote several collections of problems, out of which some things at least have passed into the *[Problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Problems_%28Aristotle%29)* that have come down to us under the name of Aristotle,[^o49] and commentaries,[^o50] partly [dialogue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogues),[^o51] to which probably belonged the *Erotikos* (Ἐρωτικός),[^o52] *Megacles* (Μεγακλῆς),[^n18] *Callisthenes* (Καλλισθένης),[^o54] and *Megarikos* (Μεγαρικός),[^n13] and letters,[^n24] partly books on [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) sciences and their history.[^n25]

Many of his surviving works exist only in fragmentary form. "The style of these works, as of the botanical books, suggests that, as in the case of Aristotle, what we possess consists of notes for lectures or notes taken of lectures," his translator Arthur F. Hort remarks.[^o8] "There is no literary charm; the sentences are mostly compressed and highly elliptical, to the point sometimes of obscurity".[^o8] The text of these fragments and extracts is often so corrupt that there is a certain plausibility to the well-known story that the works of Aristotle and Theophrastus were allowed to languish in the cellar of [Neleus of Scepsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neleus_of_Scepsis) and his descendants.[^o58]

### On plants {#On_plants}

The most important of his books are two large botanical treatises, *[Enquiry into Plants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Historia_Plantarum_%28Theophrastus%29)* (Περὶ φυτῶν ἱστορία, generally known as Historia Plantarum), and *On the Causes of Plants* ([Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_language): Περὶ αἰτιῶν φυτικῶν, [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin): De causis plantarum), which constitute the most important contribution to botanical science during antiquity and the Middle Ages,[^n3] the first systemization of the botanical world; on the strength of these works some, following [Linnaeus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Linnaeus), call him the "father of [botany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Botany)".[^n2]

The *Enquiry into Plants* was originally ten books, of which nine survive. The work is arranged into a system whereby plants are classified according to their modes of generation, their localities, their sizes, and according to their practical uses such as foods, juices, herbs, etc.[^o63] The first book deals with the parts of plants; the second book with the reproduction of plants and the times and manner of sowing; the third, fourth, and fifth books are devoted to trees, their types, their locations, and their practical applications; the sixth book deals with [shrubs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrubs) and spiny plants; the seventh book deals with herbs; the eighth book deals with plants that produce edible seeds; and the ninth book deals with plants that produce useful juices, [gums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_gum), [resins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resins), etc.[^o63]

*On the Causes of Plants* was originally eight books, of which six survive. It concerns the growth of plants; the influences on their fecundity; the proper times they should be sown and reaped; the methods of preparing the soil, manuring it, and the use of tools; and of the smells, tastes, and properties of many types of plants.[^o63] The work deals mainly with the economical uses of plants rather than their medicinal uses, although the latter is sometimes mentioned.[^o63] A book on wines and a book on plant smells may have once been part of the complete work.[^o64]

Although these works contain many absurd and fabulous statements, they include valuable observations concerning the functions and properties of plants.[^o63] Theophrastus observed the process of [germination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germination) and recognized the significance of climate to plants. Much of the information on the Greek plants may have come from his own observations, as he is known to have travelled throughout Greece, and to have had a botanical garden of his own; but the works also profit from the reports on plants of Asia brought back from those who followed [Alexander the Great](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_the_Great): to the reports of Alexander's followers he owed his accounts of such plants as the [cotton-plant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cotton), [banyan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banyan), [pepper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black_pepper), [cinnamon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cinnamon), [myrrh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myrrh), and [frankincense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankincense).[^o8]

Theophrastus's *Enquiry into Plants* was first published in a [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) translation by [Theodore Gaza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theodore_Gaza), at Treviso, 1483; in its original [Greek it first appeared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Editio_princeps) from the press of [Aldus Manutius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldus_Manutius) at Venice, 1495–98, from a third-rate manuscript, which, like the majority of the manuscripts that were sent to printers' workshops in the fifteenth and sixteenth century, has disappeared. [Christian Wimmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Friedrich_Heinrich_Wimmer) identified two manuscripts of first quality, the *Codex Urbinas* in the [Vatican Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vatican_Library), which was not made known to [J. G. Schneider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Gottlob_Schneider), who made the first modern critical edition, 1818–21, and the excerpts in the *Codex Parisiensis* in the [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibliothèque_nationale_de_France).

### On moral characters {#On_moral_characters}

His book *Characters* (originally titled Χαρακτήρες, literally *Traits*, also known in antiquity as Ἠθικοὶ χαρακτῆρες, literally *Character Traits*, and best known in the tradition simply as *Characters*[^o66]) contains thirty brief outlines of moral types. They are the first recorded attempt at systematic [character writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Character_sketch). The book has been regarded by some as an independent work; others incline to the view that the sketches were written from time to time by Theophrastus, and collected and edited after his death; others, again, regard the *Characters* as part of a larger systematic work, but the style of the book is against this. Theophrastus has found many imitators in this kind of writing, notably [Joseph Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Hall_%28bishop%29) (1608), [Sir Thomas Overbury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Overbury) (1614–16), [Bishop Earle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Earle_%28bishop%29) (1628), 17-century poet [Samuel Butler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Butler_%28poet%29) (1613), and [Jean de La Bruyère](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_de_La_Bruyère) (1688), who also translated the *Characters*.[^n3] [George Eliot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Eliot) also took inspiration from Theophrastus's *Characters*, most notably in her book of caricatures, *[Impressions of Theophrastus Such](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impressions_of_Theophrastus_Such)*. Writing the "character sketch" as a scholastic exercise also originated in Theophrastus's typology.

### On sensation {#On_sensation}

A treatise *On Sense Perception* (Περὶ αἰσθήσεων) and its objects is important for a knowledge of the doctrines of the more ancient Greek philosophers regarding the subject. A paraphrase and commentary on this work was written by [Priscian of Lydia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Priscian_of_Lydia) in the sixth century.[^o63] With this type of work we may connect the fragments on *Smells*, on *Fatigue*, on *Dizziness*, on *Sweat*, on *Swooning*, on *Palsy*, and on *Honey*.[^o58]

### Physics {#Physics}

Fragments of a *History of Physics* (Περὶ φυσικῶν ἱστοριῶν) are extant. To this class of work belong the still extant sections on *Fire*, on the *Winds*, and on the signs of *Waters*, *Winds*, and *Storms*.[^o70]

Various smaller scientific fragments have been collected in the editions of [Johann Gottlob Schneider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Gottlob_Schneider) (1818–21) and [Friedrich Wimmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christian_Friedrich_Heinrich_Wimmer) (1842–62) and in [Hermann Usener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Usener)'s *Analecta Theophrastea*.[^n3]

### Metaphysics {#Metaphysics}

The *Metaphysics* (anachronistic Greek title: Θεοφράστου τῶν μετὰ τὰ φυσικά),[^o73] in nine chapters (also known as *On First Principles*), was considered a fragment of a larger work by Usener in his edition (Theophrastos, *Metaphysica*, Bonn, 1890), but according to Ross and Fobes in their edition (Theophrastus, *Metaphysica*, Oxford, 1929), the treatise is complete (p. X) and this opinion is now widely accepted. There is no reason for assigning this work to some other author because it is not noticed in [Hermippus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermippus_of_Smyrna) and [Andronicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andronicus_of_Rhodes), especially as [Nicolaus of Damascus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicolaus_of_Damascus) had already mentioned it.[^o58]

### On stones {#On_stones}

In his treatise *On Stones* (Περὶ λίθων), which would become a source for other [lapidaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapidary_%28text%29) until at least the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance),[^n26] Theophrastus classified rocks and gems based on their behavior when heated, further grouping minerals by common properties, such as [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber) and [magnetite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetite), which both have the power of attraction.[^o77][^o78][^o79]

Theophrastus describes different [marbles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marble); mentions [coal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coal), which he says is used for heating by metal-workers; describes the various [metal ores](wikipedia://en/22595); and knew that [pumice stones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pumice) had a [volcanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Volcanic) origin. He also deals with precious stones, [emeralds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emeralds), [amethysts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amethysts), [onyx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Onyx), [jasper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jasper), etc., and describes a variety of "sapphire" that was blue with veins of gold, and thus was presumably [lapis lazuli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lapis_lazuli).[^o77]

He knew that [pearls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearls) came from [shellfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shellfish), that [coral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral) came from India, and speaks of the [fossilized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fossilized) remains of organic life.[^o77] He also considers the practical uses of various stones, such as the minerals necessary for the manufacture of glass; for the production of various pigments of paint such as [ochre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ochre); and for the manufacture of [plaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plaster).[^o77]

Many of the rarer minerals were found in mines, and Theophrastus mentions the famous [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper) mines of [Cyprus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyprus) and the even more famous [silver mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver_mine), presumably of [Laurium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurium) near [Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Athens) – the basis of the wealth of the city – as well as referring to [gold mines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold_mines). The Laurium silver mines, which were the property of the state, were usually leased for a fixed sum and a percentage on the working. Towards the end of the fifth century BCE the output fell, partly owing to the [Spartan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sparta) occupation of [Decelea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decelea) from c. 413 BCE. But the mines continued to be worked, though [Strabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strabo) (c. 64 BCE to c. 24 CE) records that in his time the tailings were being worked over, and [Pausanias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pausanias_%28geographer%29) (c. 110 to c. 180) speaks of the mines as a thing of the past. The ancient workings, consisting of shafts and galleries for excavating the ore, and washing tables for extracting the metal, may still be seen. Theophrastus wrote a separate work *On Mining*,[^n13] which – like most of his writings – is a [lost work](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lost_literary_work).

[Pliny the Elder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pliny_the_Elder) makes clear references to his use of *On Stones* in his *[Naturalis Historia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalis_Historia)* of 77 AD, while updating and making much new information available on [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minerals) himself. Although Pliny's treatment of the subject is more extensive, Theophrastus is more systematic and his work is comparatively free from fable and magic,[^n27] although he did describe [lyngurium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyngurium), a gemstone supposedly formed of the solidified urine of the [lynx](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynx) (the best ones coming from wild males), which featured in many lapidaries until it gradually disappeared from view in the 17th century.[^n28] It is mistakenly attributed to Theophrastus the first record of pyroelectricity. The misconception arose soon after the discovery of the pyroelectric properties of [tourmaline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tourmaline), which made mineralogists of the time associate the *[lyngurium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyngurium)* with it.[^o83] [Lyngurium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lyngurium) is described in the work of Theophrastus as being similar to [amber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amber), capable of attracting "straws and bits of wood", but without specifying any pyroelectric properties.[^o84]

## Philosophy {#Philosophy}

*Image: Theophrastus Nuremberg Chronicle.jpg.* Theophrastus, depicted as a medieval scholar in the *[Nuremberg Chronicle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuremberg_Chronicle)*

The extent to which Theophrastus followed Aristotle's doctrines, or defined them more accurately, or conceived them in a different form, and what additional structures of thought he placed upon them, can only be partially determined because of the loss of so many of his writings.[^o58] Many of his opinions have to be reconstructed from the works of later writers such as [Alexander of Aphrodisias](wikipedia://en/1599) and [Simplicius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicius_of_Cilicia).[^o94]

### Logic {#Logic}

Theophrastus seems to have carried out still further the [grammatical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical) foundation of [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) and [rhetoric](wikipedia://en/25447), since in his book on the elements of [speech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speech), he distinguished the main parts of speech from the subordinate parts, and also direct expressions (κυρία λέξις grc) from metaphorical expressions, and dealt with the emotions (πάθη grc) of speech.[^o96] He further distinguished a twofold reference of speech (σχίσις grc) to things (πράγματα grc) and to the hearers, and referred poetry and rhetoric to the latter.[^o97]

He wrote at length on the unity of [judgment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judgment),[^o98] on the different kinds of negation,[^o99] and on the difference between unconditional and conditional necessity.[^o100] In his doctrine of [syllogisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllogisms) he brought forward the proof for the conversion of universal affirmative judgments, differed from Aristotle here and there in the laying down and arranging the *modi* of the syllogisms,[^o101] partly in the proof of them,[^o102] partly in the doctrine of mixture, i.e. of the influence of the modality of the premises upon the modality of the conclusion.[^o103] Then, in two separate works, he dealt with the reduction of arguments to the syllogistic form and on the resolution of them;[^o104] and further, with hypothetical conclusions.[^o105] For the doctrine of [proof](wikipedia://en/27952971), [Galen](wikipedia://en/12326) quotes the second *Analytic* of Theophrastus, in conjunction with that of Aristotle, as the best treatises on that doctrine.[^o106] In different [monographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph) he seems to have tried to expand it into a general theory of [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science). To this, too, may have belonged the proposition quoted from his *Topics*, that the *principles of opposites* are themselves opposed, and cannot be deduced from one and the same higher genus.[^o107] For the rest, some minor deviations from the Aristotelian definitions are quoted from the *Topica* of Theophrastus.[^o108] Closely connected with this treatise was that upon ambiguous words or ideas,[^o109] which, without doubt, corresponded to book Ε of Aristotle's *Metaphysics*.[^o58]

### Physics and metaphysics {#Physics_and_metaphysics}

Theophrastus introduced his Physics with the proof that all natural existence, being corporeal and composite, requires *principles*,[^o111] and first and foremost, [motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_%28physics%29), as the basis of all change.[^o112] Denying the substance of [space](wikipedia://en/27667), he seems to have regarded it, in opposition to Aristotle, as the mere arrangement and position (grc and grc) of bodies.[^o113] [Time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) he called an accident of motion, without, it seems, viewing it, with Aristotle, as the numerical determinant of motion.[^o114] He attacked the doctrine of the four [classical elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_elements) and challenged whether [fire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fire_%28classical_element%29) could be called a primary element when it appears to be compound, requiring, as it does, another material for its own nutriment.[^o115]

*Image: Aristoteles Louvre.jpg.* [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308)

He departed more widely from Aristotle in his doctrine of motion, since on the one hand he extended it over all categories, and did not limit it to those laid down by Aristotle.[^o116] He viewed motion, with Aristotle, as an activity, not carrying its own goal in itself (grc), of that which only [potentially](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potentiality_and_actuality) exists,[^o117] but he opposed Aristotle's view that motion required a special explanation, and he regarded it as something proper both to nature in general and the celestial system in particular:

Surely, then, if the life in animals does not need explanation or is to be explained only in this way, may it not be the case that in the heavens too, and in the heavenly bodies, movement does not need explanation or is to be explained in a special way?

He recognised no activity without motion,[^o119] and so referred all activities of the [soul](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soul_%28spirit%29) to motion: the desires and emotions to corporeal motion, judgment (grc) and contemplation to spiritual motion.[^o120] The idea of a spirit entirely independent of organic activity, must therefore have appeared to him very doubtful; yet he appears to have contented himself with developing his doubts and difficulties on the point, without positively rejecting it.[^o121] Other Peripatetics, like [Dicaearchus](wikipedia://en/47724), [Aristoxenus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristoxenus), and especially [Strato](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strato_of_Lampsacus), developed further this [naturalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naturalism_%28philosophy%29) in Aristotelian doctrine.

Theophrastus seems, generally speaking, where the investigation overstepped the limits of experience, to have preferred to develop the difficulties rather than solve them, as is especially apparent in his *Metaphysics*.[^o58] He was doubtful of Aristotle's [teleology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teleology) and recommended that such ideas be used with caution:

With regard to the view that all things are for the sake of an end and nothing is in vain, the assignation of ends is in general not easy, as it is usually stated to be ... we must set certain limits to purposiveness and to the effort after the best, and not assert it to exist in all cases without qualification.

He did not follow the incessant attempts by Aristotle to refer phenomena to their ultimate foundations, or his attempts to unfold the internal connections between the latter, and between them and phenomena.[^o58] In antiquity, it was a subject of complaint that Theophrastus had not expressed himself with precision and consistency respecting [God](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/God), and had understood it at one time as [Heaven](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaven), at another an (enlivening) breath (*[pneuma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneuma)*).[^o123]

### Ethics {#Ethics}

*Image: Theophrastus.jpg.* The bust inscribed "Θεόφραστος Μελάντα Ἐρέσιος (grc)"

Theophrastus did not allow a happiness resting merely upon virtue,[^o125] or, consequently, to hold fast by the unconditional value of [morality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morality). He subordinated moral requirements to the advantage at least of a friend,[^o126] and had allowed in prosperity the existence of an influence injurious to them. In later times, fault was found with his expression in the *Callisthenes*, "life is ruled by fortune, not wisdom" (*vitam regit fortuna non sapientia*).[^o127] That in the definition of pleasure, likewise, he did not coincide with Aristotle, seems to be indicated by the titles of two of his writings, one of which dealt with pleasure generally, the other with pleasure as Aristotle had defined it.[^n13] Although, like his teacher, he preferred contemplative (theoretical), to active (practical) life,[^o129] he preferred to set the latter free from the restraints of family life, etc. in a manner of which Aristotle would not have approved.[^o130]

Theophrastus was opposed to eating meat on the grounds that it robbed animals of life and was therefore unjust. Non-human animals, he said, can reason, sense, and feel just as human beings do.[^o131]

## The "portrait" of Theophrastus {#The_"portrait"_of_Theophrastus}

The marble [herm figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herma) with the bearded head of philosopher type, bearing the explicit inscription, must be taken as purely conventional. Unidentified portrait heads did not find a ready market in post-Renaissance Rome. This bust was formerly in the collection of marchese Pietro Massimi at Palazzo Massimi and belonged to marchese L. Massimi at the time the engraving was made. It is now in the [Villa Albani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Albani), Rome (inv. 1034). The inscribed bust has often been illustrated in engravings[^o133] and photographs: a photograph of it forms the frontispiece to the [Loeb Classical Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loeb_Classical_Library) *Theophrastus: Enquiry into Plants* vol. I, 1916. [André Thevet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/André_Thevet) illustrated[^o134] in his iconographic compendium, Les vraies Pourtrats et vies des Hommes Illustres (Paris, 1584), an alleged portrait plagiarized from the bust, supporting his fraud with the invented tale that he had obtained it from the library of a Greek in Cyprus and that he had seen a confirming bust in the ruins of Antioch.[^o135]
