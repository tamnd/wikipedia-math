# Louis François Antoine Arbogast

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Louis François Antoine Arbogast"}

**Louis François Antoine Arbogast** (4 October 1759 – 8[^o2] April 1803) was a French [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). He was born at [Mutzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mutzig) in Alsace and died at [Strasbourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strasbourg), where he was professor. He wrote on [series](wikipedia://en/15287) and the [derivatives](wikipedia://en/5176) known by his name: he was the first writer to separate the symbols of operation from those of quantity, introducing systematically the operator notation *DF* for the derivative of the function *F*.[^o5] In 1800, he published a [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) [treatise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise)[^o6] where the first known[^o7] statement of what is currently known as [Faà di Bruno's formula](wikipedia://en/425943) appears, 55 years before the first published paper[^o8] of [Francesco Faà di Bruno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Faà_di_Bruno) on that topic.

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

*Image: Antoine Arbogast.png.* Frontpage of Arbogast's book *Du calcul des derivations* (1800)

He was professor of mathematics at the Collège de [Colmar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colmar) and entered a mathematical competition run by the [Saint Petersburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saint_Petersburg) Academy. His entry was to bring him fame and an important place in the history of the development of the calculus. Arbogast submitted an essay to the Saint Petersburg Academy in which he came down firmly on the side of [Euler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler). In fact he went much further than Euler in the type of arbitrary functions introduced by integrating [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564),[^o10] claiming that the functions could be discontinuous not only in the limited sense claimed by Euler, but discontinuous in a more general sense that he defined that allowed [piecewise functions](wikipedia://en/404130) consisting of portions of different curves. Arbogast won the prize with his essay, and his notion of [discontinuous function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discontinuous_function) became important in [Cauchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy)'s more rigorous approach to analysis.

In 1789 he submitted in Strasbourg a major report on the differential and integral calculus to the [Académie des Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Académie_des_Sciences) in Paris which was never published. In the Preface of a later work he described the ideas that prompted him to write the major report of 1789. Essentially he realised that there were no rigorous methods to show [convergence of series](wikipedia://en/1743431). In addition to his mathematics post, he was appointed as professor of physics at the Collège Royal in Strasbourg and from April 1791 he served as its rector until October 1791 when he was appointed rector of the University of Strasbourg; in 1794 he was appointed Professor of Calculus at the École centrale des travaux publics et militarisée (soon to become École Polytechnique) but he taught at the École préparatoire.

His contributions to mathematics show him as a philosophical thinker. As well as introducing discontinuous functions, he described [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) with operational symbols. The formal algebraic manipulation of series investigated by [Lagrange](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_Lagrange) and [Laplace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace) in the 1770s was put in the form of [operational calculus](wikipedia://en/8950551) by Arbogast in 1800. He coined the term *[factorial](wikipedia://en/10606)* for a product of a finite number of terms in [arithmetic progression](wikipedia://en/168389).
