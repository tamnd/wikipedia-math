# Equality (mathematics)

*Image: Equals sign typewriter.svg.* The [equals sign](wikipedia://en/501828), used to represent equality symbolically in an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Equality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **equality** is a relationship between two [quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantities) or [expressions](wikipedia://en/609125), stating that they have the same value, or represent the same [mathematical object](wikipedia://en/19453961).[^o2][^o3] Equality between $A$ and $B$ is denoted with an [equals sign](wikipedia://en/501828) as $A = B$, and read "$A$ equals $B$". A written expression of equality is called an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) or [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) depending on the context. Two objects that are [not equal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Not_equal) are said to be **distinct**.[^o4]

:::

Equality is often considered a [primitive notion](wikipedia://en/23706), meaning it is not formally defined, but rather informally said to be "a relation each thing bears to itself and nothing else". This characterization is notably circular ("nothing *else*"), reflecting a general conceptual difficulty in fully characterizing the concept. Basic properties about equality like [reflexivity](wikipedia://en/200458), [symmetry](wikipedia://en/200459), and [transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463) have been understood intuitively since at least the [ancient Greeks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greeks), but were not symbolically stated as general properties of relations until the late 19th century by [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043). Other properties like [substitution](wikipedia://en/5438948#Mathematics) and [function application](wikipedia://en/20576545) weren't formally stated until the development of [symbolic logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_logic).

There are generally two ways that equality is formalized in mathematics: through [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065) or through [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553). In logic, equality is a primitive [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematical_logic%29) (a [statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29) that may have [free variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variables)) with the reflexive property (called the [law of identity](wikipedia://en/1403190)), and the substitution property. From those, one can derive the rest of the properties usually needed for equality. After the [foundational crisis in mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_crisis_in_mathematics) at the turn of the 20th century, set theory (specifically [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214)) became the most common [foundation of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_of_mathematics). In set theory, any two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) are defined to be equal if they have all the same [members](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29). This is called the [axiom of extensionality](wikipedia://en/52376).

## Etymology {#Etymology}

*Image: First Equation Ever.png.* The first use of an [equals sign](wikipedia://en/501828), in an equation expressed as $14x+15=71$ using modern notation, from *[The Whetstone of Witte](wikipedia://en/19934039)* (1557) by [Robert Recorde](wikipedia://en/310247)

*Image: Recorde - The Whetstone of Witte - equals.jpg.* Recorde's introduction of \=. "And to avoid the tedious repetition of these words: 'is equal to' I will set as I do often in work use, a pair of parallels, or twin lines of one \[the same\] length, thus: ==, because no 2 things can be more equal."[^o6]

In English, the word *equal* is derived from the [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) aequālis ('like', 'comparable', 'similar'), which itself stems from aequus ('level', 'just').[^o7] The word entered [Middle English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_English) around the 14th century, borrowed from [Old French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_French) equalité (modern égalité).[^o8] More generally, the interlingual synonyms of *equal* have been used more broadly throughout history (see ).

Before the 16th century, there was no common symbol for equality, and equality was usually expressed with a word, such as *aequales, aequantur, esgale, faciunt, ghelijck,* or *gleich,* and sometimes by the abbreviated form *aeq*, or simply $\langle \text{æ}\rangle$ and $\langle \text{œ}\rangle$.[^n1] [Diophantus](wikipedia://en/9110)'s use of $\langle \text{ἴ}\sigma\rangle$, short for ἴσος (grc 'equals'), in *[Arithmetica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetica)* (c. 250 AD) is considered one of the first uses of an [equals sign](wikipedia://en/501828).[^o10]

The sign =, now universally accepted in mathematics for equality, was first recorded by Welsh mathematician [Robert Recorde](wikipedia://en/310247) in *[The Whetstone of Witte](wikipedia://en/19934039)* (1557), just one year before his death. The original form of the symbol was much wider than the present form. In his book, Recorde explains his symbol as "Gemowe lines", from the Latin gemellus ('twin'), using two [parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines) to represent equality because he believed that "no two things could be more equal."[^o6][^n1]

Recorde's symbol was not immediately popular. After its introduction, it wasn't used again in print until 1618 (61 years later), in an anonymous Appendix in [Edward Wright's](wikipedia://en/17214829) English translation of *[Descriptio](wikipedia://en/26291704)*, by [John Napier](wikipedia://en/15993). It wasn't until 1631 that it received more than general recognition in England, being adopted as the symbol for equality in a few influential works. Later used by several influential mathematicians, most notably, both [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) and [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz), and due to the prevalence of [calculus at the time](wikipedia://en/746117#Newton_and_Leibniz), it quickly spread throughout the rest of Europe.[^n1]

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

**[Reflexivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflexive_property)**

For every $a$, one has $a = a$.[^o15][^o16]

**[Symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_property)**

For every $a$ and $b$, if $a = b$, then $b = a$.[^o15][^o16]

**[Transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463)**

For every $a$, $b$, and $c$, if $a = b$ and $b = c$, then $a = c$.[^o15][^o16]

**[Substitution](wikipedia://en/5438948#Mathematics)**

Informally, this just means that if $a = b$, then $a$ can replace $b$ in any mathematical [expression](wikipedia://en/609125) or [formula](wikipedia://en/164040) without changing its meaning.[^o15][^o17][^o19] (For a formal explanation, see ) For example: Given [real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) $a$ and $b$, if $a = b$, then $a > 0$ implies $b > 0.$

**[Function application](wikipedia://en/20576545)**

For every $a$ and $b$, with some [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f(x),$ if $a = b$, then $f(a) = f(b).$[^o18][^o19] For example: Given [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $a$ and $b$, if $a = b$, then $3a+1 = 3b+1.$ (Here, $f(x) = 3x+1$).

The first three properties are generally attributed to [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) for being the first to explicitly state these as fundamental properties of equality in his *[Arithmetices principia](wikipedia://en/51988929)* (1889).[^o20][^n2] However, the basic notions have always existed; for example, in [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107) (c. 300 BC), [he includes 'common notions'](wikipedia://en/9417#common_notions): "Things that are equal to the same thing are also equal to one another" (transitivity), "Things that coincide with one another are equal to one another" (reflexivity), along with some function-application properties for addition and subtraction.[^o22] The function-application property was also stated in Peano's *Arithmetices principia*,[^o20] however, it had been common practice in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) since at least Diophantus (c. 250 AD).[^o23] The substitution property is generally attributed to [Gottfried Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281) (c. 1686), and often called *Leibniz's Law*.[^o17][^o24]

## Equations {#Equations}

*Image: Balance scale.svg.* [Balance scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_scales) are used to help students of algebra visualize how equations can be transformed to determine unknown values.

An [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) is a [symbolic](wikipedia://en/73634) equality of two [mathematical expressions](wikipedia://en/609125) connected with an [equals sign](wikipedia://en/501828) (=).[^o26] [Algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) is the branch of mathematics concerned with [equation solving](wikipedia://en/537048): the problem of finding values of some [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109), called unknown, for which the specified equality is true. Each value of the unknown for which the equation holds is called a solution of the given equation; also stated as satisfying the equation. For example, the equation $x^2 - 6x + 5=0$ has the values $x=1$ and $x=5$ as its only solutions. The terminology is used similarly for equations with several unknowns.[^o27] The set of solutions to an equation or [system of equations](wikipedia://en/249617) is called its [solution set](wikipedia://en/567840).[^o28]

In [mathematics education](wikipedia://en/326471), students are taught to rely on concrete models and visualizations of equations, including geometric analogies, manipulatives including sticks or cups, and "function machines" representing equations as [flow diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_diagram). One method uses [balance scales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balance_scales) as a pictorial approach to help students grasp basic problems of algebra. The mass of some objects on the scale is unknown and represents variables. Solving an equation corresponds to adding and removing objects on both sides in such a way that the sides stay in balance until the only object remaining on one side is the object of unknown mass.[^o29]

Often, equations are considered to be a statement, or [relation](wikipedia://en/22517558), which can be [true or false](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/True_or_false). For example, $1+1=2$ is true, and $1+1=3$ is false. Equations with unknowns are considered [conditionally true](wikipedia://en/45132); for example, $x^2 - 6x + 5=0$ is true when $x=1$ or $x=5,$ and false otherwise.[^o30] There are several different terminologies for this. In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), an equation is a binary [predicate](wikipedia://en/285109) (i.e. a [logical statement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statement_%28logic%29), that can have [free variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variables)) which satisfies [certain properties](wikipedia://en/90446#Axioms).[^o63] In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), an equation is defined as a [boolean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_data_type)-valued [expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expression_%28computer_science%29), or [relational operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_operator), which returns 1 and 0 for true and false respectively.[^o31]

### Identities {#Identities}

An [identity](wikipedia://en/298428) is an equality that is true for all values of its variables in a given domain.[^o33][^o34] An "equation" may sometimes mean an identity, but more often than not, it specifies a subset of the variable space to be the subset where the equation is true. An example is $\left(x + 1\right)\left(x + 1\right) = x^2 + 2 x + 1,$ which is true for each [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x.$ There is no standard notation that distinguishes an equation from an identity, or other use of the equality relation: one has to guess an appropriate interpretation from the semantics of expressions and the context.[^o35] Sometimes, but not always, an identity is written with a [triple bar](wikipedia://en/1693123): $\left(x + 1\right)\left(x + 1\right) \equiv x^2 + 2 x + 1.$[^o36] This notation was introduced by [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) in his 1857 *Elliptische Funktionen* lectures (published in 1899).[^n3][^o38][^o39]

Alternatively, identities may be viewed as an equality of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), where instead of writing $f(a) = g(a) \text{ for all } a,$ one may simply write $f = g.$[^o40][^n4] This is called the [extensionality](wikipedia://en/294056) of functions.[^o42][^n5] In this sense, the function-application property refers to [operators](wikipedia://en/22213), operations on a [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) (functions mapping between functions) like [composition](wikipedia://en/195947)[^o44] or the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921), commonly used in [operational calculus](wikipedia://en/8950551).[^n6] An identity can contain functions as "unknowns", which can be solved for similarly to a regular equation, called a [functional equation](wikipedia://en/364377).[^o46] A functional equation involving derivatives is called a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309).[^o47]

::: {#o49 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

Equations are often used to introduce new terms or symbols for constants, [assert](wikipedia://en/15014170) equalities, and introduce shorthand for complex expressions, which is called "equal by definition", and often denoted with ($:=$).[^o50] It is similar to the concept of [assignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assignment_%28computer_science%29) of a [variable in computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variable_%28computer_science%29). For example, $\mathbb{e} := \sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{1}{n!}$ defines [Euler's number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_number),[^o51] and $i^2 = -1$ is the defining property of the [imaginary number](wikipedia://en/14554) $i.$[^o52]

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), this is called an [extension by definition](wikipedia://en/12219849) (by equality) which is a [conservative extension](wikipedia://en/1840214) to a [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102).[^n7] This is done by taking the equation defining the new constant symbol as a new [axiom](wikipedia://en/928) of the [theory](wikipedia://en/3512103). The first recorded symbolic use of "Equal by definition" appeared in *Logica Matematica* (1894) by [Cesare Burali-Forti](wikipedia://en/2452025), an Italian mathematician. Burali-Forti, in his book, used the notation ($=_\text{Def}$).[^o54][^o55]

:::

## In logic {#In_logic}

Identity (philosophy)

### History {#History}

*Image: Aristotle Altemps Inv8575.jpg.* In his *[Categories](wikipedia://en/1692652)* (c. 350 BC), [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) defined *quantity* in terms of a [primitive notion](wikipedia://en/23706) of equality, with non-quantities unable be considered equal or unequal with other things.

Equality is often considered a [primitive notion](wikipedia://en/23706), informally said to be "a relation each thing bears to itself and to no other thing".[^o58] This tradition can be traced at least as far back as [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308), who in his *[Categories](wikipedia://en/1692652)* (c. 350 BC) defines the notion of *quantity* in terms of a more primitive *equality* (distinct from [identity](wikipedia://en/89532) or [similarity](wikipedia://en/66431011)), stating:[^o59]

> The most distinctive mark of quantity is that equality and inequality are predicated of it. Each of the aforesaid quantities is said to be equal or unequal. For instance, one solid is said to be equal or unequal to another; number, too, and time can have these terms applied to them, indeed can all those kinds of quantity that have been mentioned.That which is not a quantity can by no means, it would seem, be termed equal or unequal to anything else. One particular disposition or one particular quality, such as whiteness, is by no means compared with another in terms of equality and inequality but rather in terms of similarity. Thus it is the distinctive mark of quantity that it can be called equal and unequal. ― (translated by [E. M. Edghill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._M._Edghill))

Aristotle had separate categories for [quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantities) (number, length, volume) and [qualities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quality_%28philosophy%29) (temperature, density, pressure), now called [intensive and extensive properties](wikipedia://en/204464). The [Scholastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholastics), particularly [Richard Swineshead](wikipedia://en/4556117) and other [Oxford Calculators](wikipedia://en/4555383) in the 14th century, began seriously thinking about [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914) and quantitative treatment of qualities. For example, two flames have the same heat-intensity if they produce the same effect on water (e.g., warming vs [boiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boiling)). Since two intensities could be shown to be equal, and equality was considered the defining feature of quantities, it meant those intensities were quantifiable.[^o60][^o61]

The precursor to the substitution property of equality was first formulated by [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) in his *[Discourse on Metaphysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_on_Metaphysics)* (1686), stating, roughly, that "No two distinct things can have all properties in common." This has since broken into two principles, the substitution property (if $x=y,$ then any property of $x$ is a property of $y$), and its [converse](wikipedia://en/155941), the [identity of indiscernibles](wikipedia://en/630398) (if $x$ and $y$ have all properties in common, then $x=y$).[^o62]

Around the turn of the 20th century, it would become necessary to have a more concrete description of equality. In 1879 [Gottlob Frege](wikipedia://en/48416) would publish his pioneering text *[Begriffsschrift](wikipedia://en/900125)*, which would shift the focus of logic from [Aristotelian logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_logic), focused on classes of objects, to being property-based, with what would grow to become modern [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic). This was followed by a movement for describing mathematics in logical foundations, called [logicism](wikipedia://en/350672). This trend lead to the axiomatization of equality through the [law of identity](wikipedia://en/1403190) and the [substitution property](wikipedia://en/5438948#Mathematics) especially in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636)[^o17][^o63] and [analytic philosophy](wikipedia://en/159211).[^o64]

Later, Frege's *[Foundations of Arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundations_of_Arithmetic)* (1884) and *[Basic Laws of Arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basic_Laws_of_Arithmetic)* (1893, 1903) would attempt to derive the foundations of mathematics from the logical system developed in his *Begriffsschrift.* This would eventually be shown to be flawed by allowing [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095), and would contribute to the [foundational crisis of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_crisis_of_mathematics). The work of Frege would eventually be resolved by a three volume work by [Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) and [Alfred Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) known as *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* (19101913). Russell and Whitehead's work would also introduce and formalize the Leibniz' Law to symbolic logic, wherein they claim it follows from their [axiom of reducibility](wikipedia://en/10591072), but credit Leibniz for the idea.[^o65]

### Axioms {#Axioms}

*Image: Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz, Bernhard Christoph Francke.jpg.* The substitution property of equality is also known as "Leibniz's law", after [Gottfried Leibniz](wikipedia://en/12281), a major contributor to [17th-century mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/17th-century_mathematics) and [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439).

- **[Law of identity](wikipedia://en/1403190):** Stating that each thing is identical with itself, without restriction. That is, [for every](wikipedia://en/76174) $a,$ $a = a.$ It is the first of the traditional [three laws of thought](wikipedia://en/1252308#Three_traditional_laws).[^o68] The above can be stated symbolically as: $\forall a(a = a).$
- **[Substitution property](wikipedia://en/5438948#Mathematics):** Generally stating that if two things are equal, then any property of one must be a property of the other. It is sometimes referred to as "[Leibniz's](wikipedia://en/12281) law".[^o69] It can be stated formally as: for every $a$ and $b$, and any [formula](wikipedia://en/404582) $\phi(x),$ with a [free variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variable) $x$, if $a=b,$ then $\phi(a)$ [implies](wikipedia://en/658808#Definitions) $\phi(b).$ The above can be stated symbolically as: $(a=b) \implies \bigl[ \phi(a) \Rightarrow \phi(b) \bigr].$

Function application is also sometimes included in the axioms of equality,[^o18] but isn't necessary as it can be deduced from the other two axioms, and similarly for symmetry and transitivity (see ). In [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983), these are [axiom schemas](wikipedia://en/764613) (usually, see below), each of which specify an infinite set of axioms.[^o70] If a theory has a predicate that satisfies the law of identity and substitution property, it is common to say that it "has equality", or is "a theory with equality".[^n7]

The use of "equality" here somewhat of a [misnomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Misnomer) in that any system with equality can be modeled by a theory without standard identity, and with [indiscernibles](wikipedia://en/4075543).[^o72][^o70] Those two axioms are strong enough, however, to be isomorphic to a model with identity; that is, if a system has a predicate satisfying those axioms without standard equality, there is a model of that system with standard equality.[^o70] This can be done by defining a new [domain](wikipedia://en/229180) whose objects are the [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of the original "equality".[^n8] If a model is interpreted to have equality then those properties are enough, since if $x$ has all the same properties as $y,$ and $x$ has the property of being equal to $x,$ then $y$ has the property of being equal to $x.$[^o65][^o74]

As axioms, one can [deduce](wikipedia://en/37531624) from the first using [universal instantiation](wikipedia://en/1564194), and the from second, given $a = b$ and $\phi (a),$ by using [modus ponens](wikipedia://en/18900) twice. Alternatively, each of these may be included in logic as [rules of inference](wikipedia://en/252311).[^o70] The first called "equality introduction", and the second "equality elimination"[^o75] (also called [paramodulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paramodulation)), used by some [theoretical computer scientists](wikipedia://en/323392) like [John Alan Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Alan_Robinson) in their work on [resolution](wikipedia://en/2724082) and [automated theorem proving](wikipedia://en/2546).[^o76]

The substitution property can produce false statements when applied naively. For example, if $n$ denotes "the number of planets in the solar system," then the statement "[Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) did not know that $n > 6$" is true, since [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus) and [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune) were discovered after his death. However, since $n = 8$, applying the substitution property gives the statement "Johannes Kepler did not know that $8 > 6$" which is false.[^n9] The difference here is that while the expressions "the number of planets" and "8" refer to the same object (their [extension](wikipedia://en/39095)), they have different meanings (their [intension](wikipedia://en/14570)). Thus, the substitution property can only be guaranteed in [extensional contexts](wikipedia://en/2625672), which is guaranteed in modern mathematics by the [axiom of extensionality](wikipedia://en/90446#In_set_theory).[^o78]

:::

### Derivations of basic properties {#Derivations_of_basic_properties}

- **Reflexivity:** Given any expression $a,$ by the law of identity, $a = a.$[^n10]
- **Symmetry:** Given $a = b,$ take the formula $\phi(x) : x = a.$ Accordingly, $(a=b) \implies ((a=a) \Rightarrow (b=a)).$ Since $a=b$ by assumption, and $a=a$ by reflexivity, it follows that $b=a.$[^n10]
- **Transitivity:** Given $a = b$ and $b = c,$ take the formula $\phi(x): x=c.$ Accordingly, $(b=a) \implies ((b=c) \Rightarrow (a=c)).$ Since $b=a$ by symmetry, and $b=c$ by assumption, it follows that $a=c.$[^n10]
- **Function application:** Given some [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f(x)$ and expressions $a$ and $b$, such that $a = b$, then take the formula $\phi(x): f(a) = f(x).$[^n10] Accordingly, $(a=b) \implies [(f(a) = f(a)) \Rightarrow (f(a) = f(b))].$ Since $a=b$ by assumption, and $f(a) = f(a)$ by reflexivity, it follows that $f(a) = f(b).$

## In set theory {#In_set_theory}

[Set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) is the branch of mathematics that studies [sets](wikipedia://en/26691), which can be informally described as "collections of objects".[^o85] Although objects of any kind can be collected into a set, set theory—as a branch of mathematics—is mostly concerned with those that are relevant to mathematics as a whole. Sets are uniquely characterized by their [elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Element_%28mathematics%29); this means that two sets that have precisely the same elements are equal (they are the same set).[^n11] In a [formalized set theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_set_theory), this is usually defined by an [axiom](wikipedia://en/928) called the [Axiom of extensionality](wikipedia://en/52376).[^o87]

For example, using [set builder notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_builder_notation), the following states that "The set of all [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $(\mathbb{Z})$ greater than 0 but not more than 3 is equal to the set containing only 1, 2, and 3", despite the differences in formulation.

$$\{x\in \mathbb{Z} \mid 0<x\le 3\} = \{1,2,3\},$$

The term *extensionality*, as used in *'Axiom of Extensionality**has its roots in logic and grammar (*****cf. [Extension (semantics)](wikipedia://en/39095)*). In grammar, an [intensional definition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensional_definition) describes the [necessary and sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient) conditions for a term to apply to an object. For example: "A [Platonic solid](wikipedia://en/23905) is a [convex](wikipedia://en/1817885), [regular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/333299) in [three-dimensional Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/3054853)." An extensional definition instead lists all objects where the term applies. For example: "A Platonic solid is one of the following: [Tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606), [Cube](wikipedia://en/6285), [Octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), [Dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407), or [Icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084)." In logic, the [extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_%28logic%29) of a [predicate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_%28mathematical_logic%29) is the set of all objects for which the predicate is true.[^o88] Further, the logical principle of [extensionality](wikipedia://en/294056) judges two objects to be equal if they satisfy the same external properties. Since, by the axiom, two sets are defined to be equal if they satisfy [membership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Membership_%28set_theory%29), sets are extentional.[^o89]***

[José Ferreirós](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/José_Ferreirós) credits [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) for being the first to explicitly state the principle, although he does not assert it as a definition:[^n12] It very frequently happens that different things a, b, c... considered for any reason under a common point of view, are collected together in the mind, and one then says that they form a system S; one calls the things a, b, c... the elements of the system S, they are contained in S; conversely, S consists of these elements. Such a system S (or a collection, a manifold, a totality), as an object of our thought, is likewise a thing; it is completely determined when, for every thing, it is determined whether it is an element of S or not.

### Background {#Background}

*Image: Ernst Zermelo 1900s.jpg.* [Ernst Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655) was the first to explicitly formalize set equality as part of his [Zermelo set theory](wikipedia://en/322623), of which a description was first published in 1908.[^o92]

Around the turn of the 20th century, mathematics faced several [paradoxes](wikipedia://en/24390) and counter-intuitive results. For example, [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095) showed a contradiction of [naive set theory](wikipedia://en/4944), it was shown that the [parallel postulate](wikipedia://en/33731493) cannot be proved, the existence of [mathematical objects](wikipedia://en/19453961) that cannot be computed or explicitly described, and the existence of theorems of arithmetic that cannot be proved with [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic). The result was a [foundational crisis of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundational_crisis_of_mathematics).[^n13]

The resolution of this crisis involved the rise of a new mathematical discipline called [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), which studies [formal logic](wikipedia://en/46426065#Formal_logic) within mathematics. Discoveries made during the 20th century stabilized the foundations of mathematics, and produced a coherent framework valid for all branches of the discipline. This framework is based on a systematic use of [axiomatic method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiomatic_method) and on set theory, specifically [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214), developed by [Ernst Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655) and [Abraham Fraenkel](wikipedia://en/528451). This set theory (and set theory in general) is now considered the most common [foundation of mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foundation_of_mathematics).[^n14]

### Set equality based on first-order logic with equality {#Set_equality_based_on_first-order_logic_with_equality}

In first-order logic with equality (see ), the axiom of extensionality states that two sets that contain the same elements are the same set.[^o96]

- Logic axiom: $x = y \implies \forall z, (z \in x \iff z \in y)$
- Logic axiom: $x = y \implies \forall z, (x \in z \iff y \in z)$
- Set theory axiom: $(\forall z, (z \in x \iff z \in y)) \implies x = y$

The first two are given by the substitution property of equality from first-order logic; the last is a new axiom of the theory. Incorporating half of the work into the first-order logic may be regarded as a mere matter of convenience, as noted by [Azriel Lévy](wikipedia://en/18329294): The reason why we take up first-order predicate calculus with equality is a matter of convenience; by this, we save the labor of defining equality and proving all its properties; this burden is now assumed by the logic.[^o97]

### Set equality based on first-order logic without equality {#Set_equality_based_on_first-order_logic_without_equality}

In first-order logic without equality, two sets are *defined* to be equal if they contain the same elements. Then the axiom of extensionality states that two equal sets *are contained in* the same sets.[^o99]

- Set theory definition: $(x = y) \ := \ \forall z, (z \in x \iff z \in y)$
- Set theory axiom: $x = y \implies \forall z, (x \in z \iff y \in z)$

Or, equivalently, one may choose to define equality in a way that mimics, the substitution property explicitly, as the [conjunction](wikipedia://en/18152) of all [atomic formulas](wikipedia://en/4472066):[^o100]

- Set theory definition: $(x = y) \, := \, \forall z ( z \in x \implies z \in y) \, \land \, \forall w (x \in w \implies y \in w)$
- Set theory axiom: $(\forall z, (z \in x \iff z \in y)) \implies x = y$

In either case, the axiom of extensionality based on first-order logic without equality states that sets which contain the same elements are always contained in the same sets:

$$\forall z ( z \in x \iff z \in y) \implies \forall w (x \in w \iff y \in w).$$

::: {#o102 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of basic properties"}

### Proof of basic properties {#Proof_of_basic_properties}

- **Reflexivity:** Given a set $X,$ assume $z \in X,$ it follows trivially that $z \in X,$ and the same follows in reverse, thus $\forall z, (z \in X \iff z \in X),$ therefore $X = X.$[^o103]
- **Symmetry:** Given sets $X, Y,$ such that $X=Y,$ then $\forall z, (z \in X \iff z \in Y),$ which implies $\forall z, (z \in Y \iff z \in X),$ therefore $Y=X.$[^o103]
- **Transitivity:** Given sets $X, Y, Z,$ such that:
  1. $X = Y,$ and
  1. $$Y = Z,$$

assume $z \in X.$ Then, $z \in Y$ by (1), which implies $z \in Z$ by (2), and similarly for the reverse. Thus $\forall z, (z \in X \iff z \in Z),$ therefore $X=Z.$[^o103]

- **Substitution:** See Substitution (logic).
- **Function application:** Given $a = b$ and $f(a) = c,$ then $(a,c) \in f.$ Since $a = b$ and $c = c,$ then $(a,c) = (b,c).$ This is the defining property of an [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362).[^n15] Since $(a,c) = (b,c),$ by the axiom of extensionality, they must belong to the same sets. Thus, since $(a,c) \in f,$ it follows that $(b,c) \in f,$ or $f(b) = c.$ Therefore, $f(a) = f(b).$

:::

## Similar relations {#Similar_relations}

### Approximate equality {#Approximate_equality}

*Image: Archimedes pi.svg.* The sequence given by the [perimeters](wikipedia://en/23636) of regular *n*-sided [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621) that [circumscribe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumscribe) the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) approximates $2\pi$.

[Numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) is the study of [constructive](wikipedia://en/360113) methods and [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms) to find numerical [approximations](wikipedia://en/336271) (as opposed to [symbolic manipulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symbolic_computation)) of solutions to problems in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396). Especially those which cannot be [solved analytically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytic_solution).[^o107]

Calculations are likely to involve [rounding errors](wikipedia://en/432450) and other [approximation errors](wikipedia://en/640422). [Log tables](wikipedia://en/17860), slide rules, and calculators produce approximate answers to all but the simplest calculations. The results of computer calculations are normally an approximation, expressed in a limited number of significant digits, although they can be programmed to produce more precise results.[^o108]

If approximate equality is viewed as a [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) (denoted by the symbol $\approx$) between [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) or other things, any rigorous definition of it will not be an equivalence relation, due to its not being transitive. This is the case even when it is modeled as a [fuzzy relation](wikipedia://en/63233310).[^o109]

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), equality is expressed using [relational operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_operator). On computers, physical constraints fundamentally limit the level of precision with which numbers can be represented. Thus, the real numbers are often approximated by [floating-point numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating-point_numbers). Each floating-point number is represented as a [significand](wikipedia://en/419250)—comprising some fixed-length sequence of digits in a given base—which is scaled by some integer [exponent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent) of said base, in effect enabling the [radix point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radix_point) to "float" between each possible location in the significand. This allows numbers spanning many orders of magnitude to be represented, but only as fuzzy ranges of values that become less precise as they increase in magnitude.[^o110] In order to avoid losing precision, it is common to represent real numbers on computers in the form of an [expression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expression_%28computer_science%29) that denotes the real number. However, the equality of two real numbers given by an expression is known to be [undecidable](wikipedia://en/15631055) (specifically, real numbers defined by expressions involving the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), the basic [arithmetic operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_operation), the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) and the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678)). In other words, there cannot exist any [algorithm](wikipedia://en/775) for deciding such an equality (see [Richardson's theorem](wikipedia://en/13463844)).[^o111]

### Equivalence relation {#Equivalence_relation}

*Image: Equivalentie.svg.* Graph of an example equivalence with 7 classes

An [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) is a [mathematical relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_relation) that generalizes the idea of similarity or sameness. It is defined on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $X$ as a [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) $\sim$ that satisfies the three properties: [reflexivity](wikipedia://en/200458), [symmetry](wikipedia://en/200459), and [transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463). Reflexivity means that every element in $X$ is equivalent to itself ($a \sim a$ for all $a \in X$). Symmetry requires that if one element is equivalent to another, the reverse also holds ($a \sim b \implies b \sim a$). Transitivity ensures that if one element is equivalent to a second, and the second to a third, then the first is equivalent to the third ($a \sim b$ and $b \sim c \implies a \sim c$).[^n16] These properties are enough to [partition a set](wikipedia://en/340240) into disjoint [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260). Conversely, every partition defines an equivalence class.[^n17]

The equivalence relation of equality is a special case, as, if restricted to a given set $S,$ it is the strictest possible equivalence relation on $S$; specifically, equality partitions a set into equivalence classes consisting of all [singleton sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set).[^n17] Other equivalence relations, since they're less restrictive, generalize equality by identifying elements based on shared properties or transformations, such as [congruence in modular arithmetic](wikipedia://en/20087#Congruence) or [similarity in geometry](wikipedia://en/39377).[^o116][^o117]

#### Congruence relation {#Congruence_relation}

In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167) extends the idea of an equivalence relation to include the [function-application property](wikipedia://en/90446#Basic_properties). That is, given a set $X,$ and a set of operations on $X,$ then a congruence relation $\sim$ has the property that $a \sim b \implies f(a) \sim f(b)$ for all operations $f$ (here, written as unary to avoid cumbersome notation, but $f$ may be of any [arity](wikipedia://en/42301)). A congruence relation on an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) such as a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), or [module](wikipedia://en/276410) is an equivalence relation that respects the operations defined on that structure.[^o119]

### Isomorphism {#Isomorphism}

In mathematics, especially in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) and [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), it is common to deal with objects that already have some internal [structure](wikipedia://en/1809181). An [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) describes a kind of structure-preserving correspondence between two objects, establishing them as essentially identical in their structure or properties.[^o122][^o121]

More formally, an isomorphism is a bijective [mapping](wikipedia://en/516931) (or [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891)) $f$ between two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) or structures $A$ and $B$ such that $f$ and its inverse $f^{-1}$ preserve the [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245), [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558), or [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) defined on those structures.[^o122] This means that any operation or relation valid in $A$ corresponds precisely to the operation or relation in $B$ under the mapping. For example, in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a [group isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397) $f: G \mapsto H$ satisfies $f(a * b) = f(a) * f(b)$ for all elements $a, b,$ where $*$ denotes the group operation.[^o123]

When two objects or systems are isomorphic, they are considered indistinguishable in terms of their internal structure, even though their elements or representations may differ. For instance, all [cyclic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_groups) of order $\infty$ are isomorphic to the integers, $\mathbb{Z},$ with addition.[^o124] Similarly, in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), two [vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_spaces) are isomorphic if they have the same [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267), as there exists a [linear bijection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_isomorphism) between their elements.[^o125]

When objects such as the ones discussed above are defined in terms of their mapping properties (i.e., [universal properties](wikipedia://en/32245)), any two such objects are isomorphic to each other in a unique manner. Whether they should be considered to be the same (unique) object and be regarded as equal, even if their concrete constructions are different, is a matter of some discussion. This is sometimes done tacitly in the mathematical literature when such objects are canonically identified and connected using the equal sign '=', in some cases hiding technical issues that may need (potentially tedious) verification. However, this practice has led to difficulties when proofs asserting such an 'equality' are formally checked using [proof assistants](wikipedia://en/1258607).[^o126]

The concept of isomorphism extends to numerous branches of mathematics, including [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) ([graph isomorphism](wikipedia://en/247577)), [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) ([homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660)), and algebra (group and [ring isomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism)), among others. Isomorphisms facilitate the classification of mathematical entities and enable the transfer of results and techniques between similar systems. Bridging the gap between isomorphism and equality was one motivation for the development of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), as well as for [homotopy type theory](wikipedia://en/33974223) and [univalent foundations](wikipedia://en/44554214).[^o127][^o128][^o129]

### Geometry {#Geometry}

*Image: Congruent non-congruent triangles.svg.* The two leftmost triangles are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) with one another, and are both [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) to the third triangle. The rightmost triangle is neither congruent nor similar to any of the others.

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), formally, two figures are equal if they contain exactly the same [points](wikipedia://en/593693). However, historically, geometric-equality has always been taken to be much broader. [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331) and [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) used "equal" (ἴσος grc) often referring to figures with the same area or those that could be cut and rearranged to form one another. For example, Euclid stated the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) as "the square on the hypotenuse is equal to the squares on the sides, taken together", and Archimedes said that "a circle is equal to the rectangle whose sides are the radius and half the circumference."[^o131] (See Area of a circle.)

This notion persisted until [Adrien-Marie Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) introduced the term "equivalent" in 1867 to describe figures of equal area, and reserved "equal" to mean "[congruent](wikipedia://en/39330)"the same [shape](wikipedia://en/169191) and [size](wikipedia://en/43537463), or if one has the same shape and size as the [mirror image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mirror_image) of the other.[^o132][^o133] Euclid's terminology continued in the work of [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) in his *[Grundlagen der Geometrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grundlagen_der_Geometrie)*, who further refined Euclid's ideas by introducing the notions of polygons being "divisibly equal" (zerlegungsgleich) if they can be cut into finitely many triangles which are congruent, and "equal in content" (inhaltsgleichheit) if one can add finitely many divisibly equal polygons to each such that the resulting polygons are divisibly equal.[^o134]

After the rise of set theory, around the 1960s, there was a push for a reform in [mathematics education](wikipedia://en/326471) called "[New Math](wikipedia://en/404988)", following [Andrey Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/91161), who, in an effort to restructure Russian geometry courses, proposed presenting geometry through the lens of [transformations](wikipedia://en/473774) and set theory. Since a figure was seen as a set of points, it could only be equal to itself, as a result of Kolmogorov, the term "congruent" became standard in schools for figures that were previously called "equal", which popularized the term.[^o135]

While Euclid addressed [proportionality](wikipedia://en/81863) and figures of the same shape, it was not until the 17th century that the concept of [similarity](wikipedia://en/39377) was formalized in the modern sense. Similar figures are those that have the same shape but can differ in size; they can be transformed into one another by [scaling](wikipedia://en/651752) and congruence.[^o136] Later a concept of equality of [directed line segments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directed_line_segment), [equipollence](wikipedia://en/24022514), was advanced by [Giusto Bellavitis](wikipedia://en/23435437) in 1835.[^o137]
