# Parametrization (geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Parametrization"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more specifically in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **parametrization** (or **parameterization**; also **parameterisation**, **parametrisation**) is the process of finding [parametric equations](wikipedia://en/576108) of a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246), a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970), or, more generally, a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) or a [variety](wikipedia://en/248808), defined by an [implicit equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicit_equation). The inverse process is called [implicitization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Implicitization).[^o2] "To parameterize" by itself means "to express in terms of [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065)".[^o3]

:::

Parametrization is a [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) process consisting of expressing the state of a [system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/System), [process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Process_%28science%29) or model as a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of some independent quantities called [parameters](wikipedia://en/25065). The state of the system is generally determined by a finite set of [coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate), and the parametrization thus consists of one [function of several real variables](wikipedia://en/39783039) for each coordinate. The number of parameters is the number of [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622) of the system.

For example, the position of a [point](wikipedia://en/593693) that moves on a [curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curve_%28geometry%29) in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) is determined by the time needed to reach the point when starting from a fixed origin. If $x, y, z$ are the coordinates of the point, the movement is thus described by a parametric equation [^o2]

$$\begin{aligned} x&=f(t)\\y&=g(t)\\z&=h(t), \end{aligned}$$

where $t$ is the parameter and denotes the time. Such a parametric equation completely determines the curve, without the need of any interpretation of $t$ as time, and is thus called a *parametric equation* of the curve (this is sometimes abbreviated by saying that one has a *parametric curve*). One similarly gets the parametric equation of a surface by considering functions of two parameters $t$ and $u$.

## Non-uniqueness {#Non-uniqueness}

Parametrizations are not generally [unique](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_%28mathematics%29). The ordinary [three-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional) object can be parametrized (or "coordinatized") equally efficiently with [Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinate) (*x*, *y*, *z*), [cylindrical polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylindrical_polar_coordinates) ([ρ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_distance_%28geometry%29), [φ](wikipedia://en/47487), [*z*](wikipedia://en/5660713)), [spherical coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_coordinate) ([*r*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radial_distance_%28geometry%29), φ, θ) or other [coordinate systems](wikipedia://en/81931).

Similarly, the color space of human [trichromatic color vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichromacy) can be parametrized in terms of the three colors red, green and blue, [RGB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RGB), or with cyan, magenta, yellow and black, [CMYK](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CMYK).

## Dimensionality {#Dimensionality}

Generally, the minimum number of parameters required to describe a model or geometric object is equal to its [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398), and the scope of the parameters—within their allowed ranges—is the [parameter space](wikipedia://en/2882813). Though a good set of parameters permits identification of every point in the object space, it may be that, for a given parametrization, different parameter values can refer to the same point. Such mappings are [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) but not [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective). An example is the pair of cylindrical polar coordinates (ρ, φ, *z*) and (ρ, φ + 2π, *z*).

## Invariance {#Invariance}

As indicated above, there is arbitrariness in the choice of parameters of a given model, geometric object, etc. Often, one wishes to determine intrinsic properties of an object that do not depend on this arbitrariness, which are therefore independent of any particular choice of parameters. This is particularly the case in physics, wherein parametrization [invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invariant_%28physics%29) (or 'reparametrization invariance') is a guiding principle in the search for [physically acceptable theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_theory) (particularly in [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024)).

For example, whilst the location of a fixed point on some curved line may be given by a set of numbers whose values depend on how the curve is parametrized, the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) (appropriately defined) of the curve between *two* such fixed points will be independent of the particular choice of parametrization (in this case: the method by which an arbitrary point on the line is uniquely indexed). The length of the curve is therefore a parametrization-invariant quantity. In such cases parametrization is a mathematical tool employed to extract a result whose value does not depend on, or make reference to, the details of the parametrization. More generally, parametrization invariance of a physical theory implies that either the [dimensionality](wikipedia://en/8398) or the volume of the parameter space is larger than is necessary to describe the physics (the quantities of physical significance) in question.

Though the theory of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024) can be expressed without reference to a coordinate system, calculations of physical (i.e. observable) quantities such as the curvature of [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) invariably involve the introduction of a particular coordinate system in order to refer to spacetime points involved in the calculation. In the context of general relativity then, the choice of coordinate system may be regarded as a method of 'parametrizing' the spacetime, and the insensitivity of the result of a calculation of a physically-significant quantity to that choice can be regarded as an example of parametrization invariance.

As another example, physical theories whose observable quantities depend only on the *relative* distances (the ratio of distances) between pairs of objects are said to be [scale invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariant). In such theories any reference in the course of a calculation to an absolute distance would imply the introduction of a parameter to which the theory is invariant.
