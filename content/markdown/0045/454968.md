# Extreme point

*Image: Extreme points.svg.* A convex set in light blue, and its extreme points in red

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Extreme point"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **extreme point** of a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) $S$ in a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) or [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834) is a point in $S$ that does not lie in any open [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) joining two points of $S.$ The extreme points of a line segment are called its *[endpoints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endpoint_%28geometry%29)*. In [linear programming](wikipedia://en/43730) problems, an extreme point is also called *[vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417)* or *corner point* of $S.$

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Throughout, it is assumed that $X$ is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) or [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834).

For any $p, x, y \in X,$ say that $p$ **lies between**[^n1] $x$ and $y$ if $x \neq y$ and there exists a $0 < t < 1$ such that $p = t x + (1-t) y.$

If $K$ is a subset of $X$ and $p \in K,$ then $p$ is called an **extreme point**[^n1] of $K$ if it does not lie between any two distinct points of $K.$ That is, if there does not exist $x, y \in K$ and $0 < t < 1$ such that $x \neq y$ and $p = t x + (1-t) y.$ The set of all extreme points of $K$ is denoted by $\operatorname{extreme}(K).$

**Generalizations**

If $S$ is a subset of a vector space then a linear sub-variety (that is, an [affine subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_subspace)) $A$ of the vector space is called a support variety if $A$ meets $S$ (that is, $A \cap S$ is not empty) and every open segment $I \subseteq S$ whose interior meets $A$ is necessarily a subset of $A.$[^n2] A 0-dimensional support variety is called an extreme point of $S.$[^n2]

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterizations"}

### Characterizations {#Characterizations}

The **midpoint**[^n1] of two elements $x$ and $y$ in a vector space is the vector $\tfrac{1}{2}(x+y).$

For any elements $x$ and $y$ in a vector space, the set $[x, y] = \{t x + (1-t) y : 0 \leq t \leq 1\}$ is called the **closed line segment** or **closed interval** between $x$ and $y.$ The **open line segment** or **open interval** between $x$ and $y$ is $(x, x) = \varnothing$ when $x = y$ while it is $(x, y) = \{t x + (1-t) y : 0 < t < 1\}$ when $x \neq y.$[^n1] The points $x$ and $y$ are called the **endpoints** of these interval. An interval is said to be a **non−degenerate interval** or a **proper interval** if its endpoints are distinct. The **midpoint of an interval** is the midpoint of its endpoints.

The closed interval $[x, y]$ is equal to the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of $(x, y)$ if (and only if) $x \neq y.$ So if $K$ is convex and $x, y \in K,$ then $[x, y] \subseteq K.$

If $K$ is a nonempty subset of $X$ and $F$ is a nonempty subset of $K,$ then $F$ is called a **face**[^n1] of $K$ if whenever a point $p \in F$ lies between two points of $K,$ then those two points necessarily belong to $F.$

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem[^n1].**

Let $K$ be a non-empty convex subset of a vector space $X$ and let $p \in K.$ Then the following statements are equivalent:

$p$ is an extreme point of $K.$ $K \setminus \{p\}$ is convex. $p$ is not the midpoint of a non-degenerate line segment contained in $K.$ for any $x, y \in K,$ if $p \in [x, y]$ then $x = p \text{ or } y = p.$ if $x \in X$ is such that both $p + x$ and $p - x$ belong to $K,$ then $x = 0.$ $\{p\}$ is a face of $K.$

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

If $a < b$ are two real numbers then $a$ and $b$ are extreme points of the interval $[a, b].$ However, the open interval $(a, b)$ has no extreme points.[^n1] Any [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) in $\mathbb{R}$ has no extreme points while any non-degenerate [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) not equal to $\mathbb{R}$ does have extreme points (that is, the closed interval's endpoint(s)). More generally, any [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) of finite-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ has no extreme points.

The extreme points of the [closed unit disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_unit_disk) in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071).

The perimeter of any convex polygon in the plane is a face of that polygon.[^n1] The vertices of any convex polygon in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ are the extreme points of that polygon.

An injective linear map $F : X \to Y$ sends the extreme points of a convex set $C \subseteq X$ to the extreme points of the convex set $F(X).$[^n1] This is also true for injective affine maps.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

The extreme points of a compact convex set form a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550) (with the subspace topology) but this set may fail to be closed in $X.$[^n1]

## Theorems {#Theorems}

::: {#o26 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Krein–Milman theorem"}

### Krein–Milman theorem {#Krein–Milman_theorem}

The [Krein–Milman theorem](wikipedia://en/3206654) is arguably one of the most well-known theorems about extreme points.

::: {#o27 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

If $S$ is convex and [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) in a [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844), then $S$ is the closed [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of its extreme points: In particular, such a set has extreme points.

:::

:::

### For Banach spaces {#For_Banach_spaces}

These theorems are for [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) with the [Radon–Nikodym property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_property).

A theorem of [Joram Lindenstrauss](wikipedia://en/20653945) states that, in a Banach space with the Radon–Nikodym property, a nonempty [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) and [bounded set](wikipedia://en/48258) has an extreme point. (In infinite-dimensional spaces, the property of [compactness](wikipedia://en/6042) is stronger than the joint properties of being closed and being bounded.[^o29])

::: {#o30 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem (Gerald Edgar)"}

**Theorem ([Gerald Edgar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gerald_Edgar)).**

Let $E$ be a Banach space with the Radon–Nikodym property, let $C$ be a separable, closed, bounded, convex subset of $E,$ and let $a$ be a point in $C.$ Then there is a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) $p$ on the universally measurable sets in $C$ such that $a$ is the [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter) of $p,$ and the set of extreme points of $C$ has $p$-measure 1.[^o31]

:::

Edgar’s theorem implies Lindenstrauss’s theorem.

:::

## Related notions {#Related_notions}

A closed convex subset of a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) is called [strictly convex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strictly_convex_set) if every one of its [(topological) boundary points](wikipedia://en/72824) is an extreme point.[^n3] The [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball) of any [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) is a strictly convex set.[^n3]

### *k*-extreme points {#k-extreme_points}

More generally, a point in a convex set $S$ is **$k$-extreme** if it lies in the interior of a $k$-dimensional convex set within $S,$ but not a $k + 1$-dimensional convex set within $S.$ Thus, an extreme point is also a $0$-extreme point. If $S$ is a polytope, then the $k$-extreme points are exactly the interior points of the $k$-dimensional faces of $S.$ More generally, for any convex set $S,$ the $k$-extreme points are partitioned into $k$-dimensional open faces.

The finite-dimensional Krein–Milman theorem, which is due to Minkowski, can be quickly proved using the concept of $k$-extreme points. If $S$ is closed, bounded, and $n$-dimensional, and if $p$ is a point in $S,$ then $p$ is $k$-extreme for some $k \leq n.$ The theorem asserts that $p$ is a convex combination of extreme points. If $k = 0$ then it is immediate. Otherwise $p$ lies on a line segment in $S$ which can be maximally extended (because $S$ is closed and bounded). If the endpoints of the segment are $q$ and $r,$ then their extreme rank must be less than that of $p,$ and the theorem follows by induction.
