# Solid mechanics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Solid mechanics"}

**Solid mechanics** (also known as **mechanics of solids**) is the branch of [continuum mechanics](wikipedia://en/5918) that studies the behavior of [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid) materials, especially their motion and [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29) under the action of [forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Force), [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050) changes, [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28chemistry%29) changes, and other external or internal agents.

:::

Solid mechanics is fundamental for [civil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_engineering), [aerospace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_engineering), [nuclear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_engineering), [biomedical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomedical_engineering) and [mechanical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanical_engineering), for [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), and for many branches of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry) such as [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science).[^o10] It has specific applications in many other areas, such as understanding the [anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomy) of living beings, and the design of [dental prostheses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dental_prosthesis) and [surgical implants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surgical_implant). One of the most common practical applications of solid mechanics is the [Euler–Bernoulli beam equation](wikipedia://en/2064935). Solid mechanics extensively uses [tensors](wikipedia://en/29965) to describe stresses, strains, and the relationship between them.

Solid mechanics is a vast subject because of the wide range of solid materials available, such as steel, wood, concrete, biological materials, textiles, geological materials, and plastics.

## Fundamental aspects {#Fundamental_aspects}

A *solid* is a material that can support a substantial amount of [shearing force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_force) over a given time scale during a natural or industrial process or action. This is what distinguishes solids from [fluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid), because fluids also support *normal forces* which are those forces that are directed perpendicular to the material plane across from which they act and *normal stress* is the [normal force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_force) per unit area of that material plane. *Shearing forces* in contrast with *normal forces*, act parallel rather than perpendicular to the material plane and the shearing force per unit area is called *shear stress*.

Therefore, solid mechanics examines the shear stress, deformation and the failure of solid materials and structures.

The most common topics covered in solid mechanics include:

1. **[stability of structures](wikipedia://en/92290#Stability)** - examining whether structures can return to a given equilibrium after disturbance or partial/complete failure, *see [Structural mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structural_mechanics)*
1. **[dynamical systems](wikipedia://en/990632) and [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295)** - dealing with mechanical systems highly sensitive to their given initial position
1. **thermomechanics** - analyzing materials with models derived from principles of [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952)
1. **[biomechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biomechanics)** - solid mechanics applied to [biological materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biotic_material) e.g. muscles, bones, heart tissue
1. **[geomechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geomechanics)** - solid mechanics applied to geological materials e.g. ice, soil, rock
1. **[vibrations](wikipedia://en/20646772) of solids and structures** - examining vibration and wave propagation from oscillating particles and structures i.e. vital in mechanical, civil, mining, aeronautical, maritime/marine, aerospace engineering
1. **[fracture](wikipedia://en/843211) and [damage](wikipedia://en/12098816) mechanics** - dealing with crack-growth mechanics in solid materials
1. **[composite materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composite_material)** - solid mechanics applied to materials made up of more than one compound e.g. [reinforced plastics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibre-reinforced_plastic), [reinforced concrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reinforced_concrete), [fiber glass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_glass)
1. **variational formulations and [computational mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_mechanics)** - numerical solutions to mathematical equations arising from various branches of solid mechanics e.g. [finite element method (FEM)](wikipedia://en/18233581)
1. **experimental mechanics** - design and analysis of experimental methods to examine the behavior of solid materials and structures

## Response models {#Response_models}

A material has a rest shape and its shape departs away from the rest shape due to stress. The amount of departure from rest shape is called [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29), the proportion of deformation to original size is called strain. If the applied stress is sufficiently low (or the imposed strain is small enough), almost all solid materials behave in such a way that the strain is directly proportional to the stress; the coefficient of the proportion is called the [modulus of elasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulus_of_elasticity). This region of deformation is known as the linearly elastic region.

It is most common for analysts in solid mechanics to use [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear) material models, due to ease of computation. However, real materials often exhibit [non-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear) behavior. As new materials are used and old ones are pushed to their limits, non-linear material models are becoming more common.

These are basic models that describe how a solid responds to an applied stress:

1. [Elasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elasticity_%28physics%29) – When an applied stress is removed, the material returns to its undeformed state. Linearly elastic materials, those that deform proportionally to the applied load, can be described by the [linear elasticity](wikipedia://en/301429) equations such as [Hooke's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law).
1. [Viscoelasticity](wikipedia://en/711288) – These are materials that behave elastically, but also have [damping](wikipedia://en/11062): when the stress is applied and removed, work has to be done against the damping effects and is converted in heat within the material resulting in a [hysteresis loop](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hysteresis_loop) in the stress–strain curve. This implies that the material response has time-dependence.
1. [Plasticity](wikipedia://en/240123) – Materials that behave elastically generally do so when the applied stress is less than a yield value. When the stress is greater than the yield stress, the material behaves plastically and does not return to its previous state. That is, deformation that occurs after yield is permanent.
1. [Viscoplasticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscoplasticity) - Combines theories of viscoelasticity and plasticity and applies to materials like [gels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gel) and [mud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mud).
1. Thermoelasticity - There is coupling of mechanical with thermal responses. In general, thermoelasticity is concerned with elastic solids under conditions that are neither isothermal nor adiabatic. The simplest theory involves the [Fourier's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier's_law) of heat conduction, as opposed to advanced theories with physically more realistic models.

- 1452–1519 [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) made many contributions[^o6]
- 1638: [Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374) published the book "[Two New Sciences](wikipedia://en/268475)" in which he examined the failure of simple structures

- 1660: [Hooke's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hooke's_law) by [Robert Hooke](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Hooke)
- 1687: [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) published "[Philosophiae Naturalis Principia Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophiae_Naturalis_Principia_Mathematica)" which contains [Newton's laws of motion](wikipedia://en/55212)

- 1750: [Euler–Bernoulli beam equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Bernoulli_beam_equation)
- 1700–1782: [Daniel Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/46784) introduced the principle of [virtual work](wikipedia://en/1211986)
- 1707–1783: [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) developed the theory of [buckling](wikipedia://en/815969) of columns

*Image: Leonhard Euler 2.jpg.* [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) developed the theory of [buckling](wikipedia://en/815969) of columns

- 1826: [Claude-Louis Navier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Claude-Louis_Navier) published a treatise on the elastic behaviors of structures
- 1873: [Carlo Alberto Castigliano](wikipedia://en/3289085) presented his dissertation "Intorno ai sistemi elastici", which contains [his theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Castigliano's_method) for computing displacement as partial derivative of the strain energy. This theorem includes the method of *least work* as a special case
- 1874: [Otto Mohr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Mohr) formalized the idea of a statically indeterminate structure.
- 1922: [Timoshenko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_Timoshenko) corrects the [Euler–Bernoulli beam equation](wikipedia://en/2064935)
- 1936: [Hardy Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy_Cross)' publication of the moment distribution method, an important innovation in the design of continuous frames.
- 1941: [Alexander Hrennikoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Hrennikoff) solved the discretization of plane elasticity problems using a lattice framework
- 1942: [R. Courant](wikipedia://en/715238) divided a domain into finite subregions
- 1956: J. Turner, R. W. Clough, H. C. Martin, and L. J. Topp's paper on the "Stiffness and Deflection of Complex Structures" introduces the name "finite-element method" and is widely recognized as the first comprehensive treatment of the method as it is known today
