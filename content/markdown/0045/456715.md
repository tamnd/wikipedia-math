# Kerr metric

*Image: Ergosphere and event horizon of a rotating black hole (no animation).gif.* Model of the spacetime surrounding a near-[extremal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremal_black_hole) **Kerr black hole**

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kerr metric"}

The **Kerr metric** or **Kerr geometry** describes the geometry of empty [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) around a rotating uncharged [axially symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axially_symmetric) [black hole](wikipedia://en/4650) with a quasispherical [event horizon](wikipedia://en/29320146). The Kerr [metric](wikipedia://en/195795) is an [exact solution](wikipedia://en/1686520) of the [Einstein field equations](wikipedia://en/424540) of [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024); these equations are highly [non-linear](wikipedia://en/146103), which makes exact solutions very difficult to find.

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

The Kerr metric is a generalization to a rotating body of the Schwarzschild metric, discovered by [Karl Schwarzschild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Schwarzschild) in 1915, which described the geometry of spacetime around an uncharged, spherically symmetric, and non-rotating body. The corresponding solution for a *charged*, spherical, non-rotating body, the [Reissner–Nordström metric](wikipedia://en/711862), was discovered soon afterwards (1916–1918). However, the exact solution for an uncharged, *rotating* black hole, the Kerr metric, remained unsolved until 1963, when it was discovered by [Roy Kerr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Kerr).[^o3][^o4] The natural extension to a charged, rotating black hole, the [Kerr–Newman metric](wikipedia://en/1447904), was discovered shortly thereafter in 1965. These four related solutions may be summarized by the following table, where *Q* represents the body's [electric charge](wikipedia://en/9804) and *J* represents its spin [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839):

{| class="wikitable"

\|-

! ! Non-rotating (*J* = 0) ! Rotating (any *J*)

\|-

! Uncharged (*Q* = 0)

\| [Schwarzschild](wikipedia://en/264606) (1915) | Kerr (1963) |-

! Charged (any *Q*)

\| [Reissner–Nordström](wikipedia://en/711862) (1916–1918) | [Kerr–Newman](wikipedia://en/1447904) (1965) |- |}

According to the Kerr metric, a rotating body should exhibit [frame-dragging](wikipedia://en/27086745) (also known as [Lense–Thirring precession](wikipedia://en/11898194)), a distinctive prediction of general relativity. The first measurement of this frame dragging effect was done in 2011 by the [Gravity Probe B](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_Probe_B) experiment. Roughly speaking, this effect predicts that objects coming close to a rotating mass will be entrained to participate in its rotation, not because of any applied force or torque that can be felt, but rather because of the swirling curvature of spacetime itself associated with rotating bodies. In the case of a rotating black hole, at close enough distances, all objects – even light – *must* rotate with the black hole; the region where this holds is called the [ergosphere](wikipedia://en/1508445).

The light from distant sources can travel around the event horizon several times (if close enough); [creating multiple images of the same object](wikipedia://en/18117469). To a distant viewer, the apparent perpendicular distance between images decreases at a factor of [$e$](wikipedia://en/9633)<sup>2[$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601)</sup> (about 535). However, fast spinning black holes have less distance between multiplicity images.[^o5][^o6]

Rotating black holes have surfaces where the metric seems to have apparent [singularities](wikipedia://en/25269252); the size and shape of these surfaces depends on the black hole's [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) and angular momentum. The outer surface encloses the ergosphere and has a shape similar to a flattened sphere. The inner surface marks the event horizon; objects passing into the interior of this horizon can never again communicate with the world outside that horizon. However, neither surface is a true singularity, since their apparent singularity can be eliminated in a different [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931). A similar situation obtains when considering the Schwarzschild metric which also appears to result in a singularity at $r = r_\text{s}$ dividing the space above and below $r_\text{s}$ into two disconnected patches; using a different coordinate transformation one can then relate the extended external patch to the inner patch (see *Schwarzschild metric#Singularities and black holes*) – such a coordinate transformation eliminates the apparent singularity where the inner and outer surfaces meet. Objects between these two surfaces must co-rotate with the rotating black hole, as noted above; this feature can in principle be used to extract energy from a rotating black hole, up to its [invariant mass](wikipedia://en/162321) energy, $Mc^2$.

The LIGO experiment that first detected gravitational waves, announced in 2016, also provided the [first direct observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_observation_of_gravitational_waves) of a pair of **Kerr black holes**.[^o7]

## Metric {#Metric}

The Kerr metric is commonly expressed in one of two forms, the Boyer–Lindquist form and the Kerr–Schild form. It can be readily derived from the Schwarzschild metric, using the [Newman–Janis algorithm](wikipedia://en/63151790)[^o9] by [Newman–Penrose formalism](wikipedia://en/6176811) (also known as the spin–coefficient formalism),[^o10] [Ernst equation](wikipedia://en/17254254),[^o11] or Ellipsoid coordinate transformation.[^o12]

### Boyer–Lindquist coordinates {#Boyer–Lindquist_coordinates}

The Kerr metric describes the geometry of spacetime in the vicinity of a mass $M$ rotating with angular momentum $J$.[^o14] The metric (or equivalently its [line element](wikipedia://en/855138) for [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418)) in [Boyer–Lindquist coordinates](wikipedia://en/2597983) is[^o15][^o16]

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\begin{aligned} ds^{2} &= -c^{2} d\tau^{2} \\ &= -\left( 1 - \frac{r_\text{s} r}{\Sigma} \right) c^{2} dt^{2} + \frac{\Sigma}{\Delta} dr^{2} + \Sigma d\theta^{2} + \left(r^2+ a^2 + \frac{r_\text{s} r a^{2}}{\Sigma} \sin^{2}\theta \right) \sin^{2}\theta \ d\phi^{2} - \frac{2r_\text{s} ra \sin^{2} \theta}{\Sigma} c \, dt \, d\phi \end{aligned} \tag{1}$$

:::

where the coordinates $r, \theta, \phi$ are standard [oblate spheroidal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5007562), which are equivalent to the cartesian coordinates[^o51][^o18]

::: {#o19 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$x = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2} \sin\theta\cos\phi \tag{2}$$

:::

::: {#o20 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$y = \sqrt{r^2 + a^2} \sin\theta\sin\phi \tag{3}$$

:::

::: {#o21 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$z = r \cos\theta, \tag{4}$$

:::

where $r_\text{s}$ is the [Schwarzschild radius](wikipedia://en/264606)

::: {#o22 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$r_\text{s} = \frac{2GM}{c^2}, \tag{5}$$

:::

and where for brevity, the length scales $a, \Sigma$ and $\Delta$ have been introduced as

::: {#o23 .equation cue=numblk name="6"}

$$a = \frac{J}{Mc}, \tag{6}$$

:::

::: {#o24 .equation cue=numblk name="7"}

$$\Sigma = r^{2} + a^{2} \cos^{2}\theta, \tag{7}$$

:::

::: {#o25 .equation cue=numblk name="8"}

$$\Delta = r^{2} - r_\text{s} r + a^{2}. \tag{8}$$

:::

A key feature to note in the above metric is the cross-term $dt \, d\phi$. This implies that there is coupling between time and motion in the plane of rotation that disappears when the black hole's angular momentum goes to zero.

In the non-relativistic limit where $M$ (or, equivalently, $r_\text{s}$) goes to zero, the Kerr metric becomes the orthogonal metric for the [oblate spheroidal coordinates](wikipedia://en/5007562)

::: {#o26 .equation cue=numblk name="9"}

$$g \mathop\longrightarrow_{M \to 0} - c^{2} dt^{2} + \frac{\Sigma}{r^{2} + a^{2}} dr^{2} + \Sigma d\theta^{2} + \left(r^{2} + a^{2} \right) \sin^{2}\theta d\phi^{2} \tag{9}$$

:::

### Kerr–Schild coordinates {#Kerr–Schild_coordinates}

The Kerr metric can be expressed in ["Kerr–Schild" form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerr–Schild_perturbations), using a particular set of [Cartesian coordinates](wikipedia://en/7706) as follows.[^o28][^o29][^o30] These solutions were proposed by [Kerr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roy_Patrick_Kerr) and [Schild](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Schild) in 1965.

::: {#o31 .equation cue=numblk name="10"}

$$g_{\mu \nu} = \eta_{\mu \nu} + fk_{\mu}k_{\nu} \! \tag{10}$$

:::

::: {#o32 .equation cue=numblk name="11"}

$$f = \frac{2GMr^3}{r^4 + a^2z^2} \tag{11}$$

:::

::: {#o33 .equation cue=numblk name="12"}

$$\mathbf{k} = ( k_{x} ,k_{y} ,k_{z} ) = \left( \frac{rx+ay}{r^2 + a^2} , \frac{ry-ax}{r^2 + a^2}, \frac{z}{r} \right) \tag{12}$$

:::

::: {#o34 .equation cue=numblk name="13"}

$$k_{0} = 1. \! \tag{13}$$

:::

Notice that **k** is a [unit 3-vector](wikipedia://en/167053), making the 4-vector a [null vector](wikipedia://en/1453977), with respect to both *g* and *η*.[^o35] Here *M* is the constant mass of the spinning object, *η* is the [Minkowski tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_space#Standard_basis), and *a* is a constant rotational parameter of the spinning object. It is understood that the vector $\vec{a}$ is directed along the positive z-axis. The quantity *r* is not the radius, but rather is implicitly defined by

::: {#o36 .equation cue=numblk name="14"}

$$\frac{x^2+y^2}{r^2 + a^2} + \frac{z^2}{r^2} = 1 \tag{14}$$

:::

Notice that the quantity *r* becomes the usual radius *R*

$$r \to R = \sqrt{x^2 + y^2 + z^2}$$

when the rotational parameter $a$ approaches zero. In this form of solution, units are selected so that the speed of light is unity ($c = 1$). At large distances from the source (*R* ≫ *a*), these equations reduce to the [Eddington–Finkelstein form](wikipedia://en/293670) of the Schwarzschild metric.

In the Kerr–Schild form of the Kerr metric, the determinant of the metric tensor is everywhere equal to negative one, even near the source.[^o37]

### Soliton coordinates {#Soliton_coordinates}

As the Kerr metric (along with the [Kerr–NUT metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerr–NUT_metric)) is axially symmetric, it can be cast into a form to which the [Belinski–Zakharov transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belinski–Zakharov_transform) can be applied. This implies that the Kerr black hole has the form of a [gravitational soliton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_soliton).[^o39]

## Mass of rotational energy {#Mass_of_rotational_energy}

If the complete rotational energy $E_{\rm rot} = c^2\left(M -M_{\rm irr}\right)$ of a black hole is extracted, for example with the [Penrose process](wikipedia://en/2975616),[^o57][^o41] the remaining mass cannot shrink below the irreducible mass. Therefore, if a black hole rotates with the spin $a=M$, its total mass-equivalent $M$ is higher by a factor of $\sqrt{2}$ in comparison with a corresponding Schwarzschild black hole where $M$ is equal to $M_\text{irr}$. The reason for this is that in order to get a static body to spin, energy needs to be applied to the system. Because of the [mass–energy equivalence](wikipedia://en/422481) this energy also has a mass-equivalent, which adds to the total mass–energy of the system, $M$.

The total mass equivalent $M$ (the gravitating mass) of the body (including its [rotational energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotational_energy)) and its irreducible mass $M_\text{irr}$ are related by[^o42][^o43]

$$2 M_{\rm irr}^2 = M^2 + \sqrt{M^4 - J^2 c^2 / G^2} \Longrightarrow M^2 = M_{\rm irr}^2 + \frac{J^2 c^2}{4 M_{\rm irr}^2 G^2}.$$

## Wave operator {#Wave_operator}

Since even a direct check on the Kerr metric involves cumbersome calculations, the [contravariant](wikipedia://en/202886) components $g^{ik}$ of the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795) in Boyer–Lindquist coordinates are shown below in the expression for the square of the [four-gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-gradient) [operator](wikipedia://en/263886):[^o57]

::: {#o45 .equation cue=numblk name="15"}

$$\begin{aligned} g^{\mu\nu} \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\mu} \frac{\partial}{\partial x^\nu} &=-\frac{1}{c^{2}\Delta} \left(r^{2} + a^{2} + \frac{r_\text{s}ra^{2}}{\Sigma}\sin^{2}\theta\right) \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial t}\right)^{2} \\ &- \frac{2r_\text{s}ra}{c\Sigma\Delta} \frac{\partial}{\partial\phi} \frac{\partial}{\partial{t}} +\frac{1}{\Delta\sin^{2}\theta} \left(1 - \frac{r_\text{s}r}{\Sigma}\right) \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\phi}\right)^{2} \\ &+ \frac{\Delta}{\Sigma} \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial r}\right)^{2} + \frac{1}{\Sigma} \left(\frac{\partial}{\partial\theta}\right)^{2} \end{aligned} \tag{15}$$

:::

## Frame dragging {#Frame_dragging}

The Kerr metric may be rewritten ((1)) in the following form:

::: {#o47 .equation cue=numblk name="16"}

$$c^{2} d\tau^{2} = \left( g_{tt} - \frac{g_{t\phi}^{2}}{g_{\phi\phi}} \right) dt^{2} + g_\mathrm{rr} dr^{2} + g_{\theta\theta} d\theta^{2} + g_{\phi\phi} \left( d\phi + \frac{g_{t\phi}}{g_{\phi\phi}} dt \right)^{2}. \tag{16}$$

:::

This metric is equivalent to a co-rotating reference frame that is rotating with angular speed Ω that depends on both the radius *r* and the [colatitude](wikipedia://en/1828681) *θ*, where Ω is called the [Killing horizon](wikipedia://en/1720938).

::: {#o48 .equation cue=numblk name="17"}

$$\Omega = -\frac{g_{t\phi}}{g_{\phi\phi}} = \frac{r_\text{s} r a c}{\Sigma \left( r^{2} + a^{2} \right) + r_\text{s} r a^{2} \sin^{2}\theta}. \tag{17}$$

:::

Thus, an inertial reference frame is entrained by the rotating central mass to participate in the latter's rotation; this is called frame-dragging, and has been tested experimentally.[^o49] Qualitatively, frame-dragging can be viewed as the gravitational analog of electromagnetic induction. An "ice skater", in orbit over the equator and rotationally at rest with respect to the stars, extends her arms. The arm extended toward the black hole will be torqued spinward. The arm extended away from the black hole will be torqued anti-spinward. She will therefore be rotationally sped up, in a counter-rotating sense to the black hole. This is the opposite of what happens in everyday experience. If she is already rotating at a certain speed when she extends her arms, inertial effects and frame-dragging effects will balance and her spin will not change. Due to the [equivalence principle](wikipedia://en/857235), gravitational effects are locally indistinguishable from inertial effects, so this rotation rate, at which when she extends her arms nothing happens, is her local reference for non-rotation. This frame is rotating with respect to the fixed stars and counter-rotating with respect to the black hole. A useful metaphor is a [planetary gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetary_gear) system with the black hole being the sun gear, the ice skater being a planetary gear and the outside universe being the ring gear. This can also be interpreted through [Mach's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mach's_principle).

## Important surfaces {#Important_surfaces}

*Image: Kerr surfaces.svg.* Location of the horizons, ergospheres and the ring singularity of the Kerr spacetime in Cartesian Kerr–Schild coordinates[^o51]

*Image: Kerr.black.hole.shadow.and.horizons.thumb.gif.* Comparison of the shadow (black) and the important surfaces (white) of a black hole. The spin parameter $a$ is animated from $0$ to $M$, while the left side of the black hole is rotating towards the observer.[^o52]

There are several important surfaces in the Kerr metric ((1)). The inner surface corresponds to an event horizon similar to that observed in the Schwarzschild metric; this occurs where the purely radial component *g*${}_{\operatorname{rr}}$ of the metric goes to infinity. Solving the quadratic equation $\tfrac{1}{g_{\operatorname{rr}}}$ = 0 yields the solution:

$$r_{\rm H}^{\pm} = \frac{r_\text{s} \pm \sqrt{r_\text{s}^{2} - 4a^2}}{2}$$

which in natural units (that give $G = M = c = 1$) simplifies to:

$$r_{\rm H}^{\pm} = 1 \pm \sqrt{1-a^2}$$

While in the Schwarzschild metric the event horizon is also the place where the purely temporal component *g*${}_{\operatorname{tt}}$ of the metric changes sign from positive to negative, in Kerr metric that happens at a different distance. Again solving a quadratic equation *g*${}_{\operatorname{tt}}$ = 0 yields the solution:

$$r_{\rm E}^{\pm} = \frac{r_\text{s} \pm \sqrt{r_\text{s}^{2} - 4a^{2} \cos^{2}\theta}}{2}$$

or in natural units:

$$r_{\rm E}^{\pm} = 1 \pm \sqrt{1-a^2 \cos ^2\theta}$$

Due to the cos${}^{2}$*θ* term in the square root, this outer surface resembles a flattened sphere that touches the inner surface at the poles of the rotation axis, where the colatitude *θ* equals 0 or *π*; the space between these two surfaces is called the ergosphere. Within this volume, the purely temporal component *g*${}_{\operatorname{tt}}$ is negative, i.e., acts like a purely spatial metric component. Consequently, particles within this ergosphere must co-rotate with the inner mass, if they are to retain their time-like character. A moving particle experiences a positive [proper time](wikipedia://en/494418) along its [worldline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worldline), its path through spacetime. However, this is impossible within the ergosphere, where *g*${}_{\operatorname{tt}}$ is negative, unless the particle is co-rotating around the interior mass $M$ with an angular speed at least of $\Omega$. Thus, no particle can move in the direction opposite to central mass's rotation within the ergosphere.

As with the event horizon in the Schwarzschild metric, the apparent singularity at *r*${}_{H}$ is due to the choice of coordinates (i.e., it is a [coordinate singularity](wikipedia://en/25269252)). In fact, the spacetime can be smoothly continued through it by an appropriate choice of coordinates. In turn, the outer boundary of the ergosphere at *r*${}_{E}$ is not singular by itself even in Kerr coordinates due to non-zero $dt\ d\phi$ term.

## Ergosphere and the Penrose process {#Ergosphere_and_the_Penrose_process}

A black hole in general is surrounded by a surface, called the event horizon and situated at the [Schwarzschild radius](wikipedia://en/206115) for a nonrotating black hole, where the escape velocity is equal to the velocity of light. Within this surface, no observer/particle can maintain itself at a constant radius. It is forced to fall inwards, and so this is sometimes called the *static limit*.

A rotating black hole has the same static limit at its event horizon but there is an additional surface outside the event horizon named the "ergosurface" given by

$$(r-M)^{2} = M^{2}-J^{2}\cos^{2}\theta$$

in [Boyer–Lindquist coordinates](wikipedia://en/2597983), which can be intuitively characterized as the sphere where "the rotational velocity of the surrounding space" is dragged along with the velocity of light. Within this sphere the dragging is greater than the speed of light, and any observer/particle is forced to co-rotate.

The region outside the event horizon but inside the surface where the rotational velocity is the speed of light, is called the *ergosphere* (from Greek *ergon* meaning *work*). Particles falling within the ergosphere are forced to rotate faster and thereby gain energy. Because they are still outside the event horizon, they may escape the black hole. The net process is that the rotating black hole emits energetic particles at the cost of its own total energy. The possibility of extracting spin energy from a rotating black hole was first proposed by the mathematician [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193) in 1969 and is thus called the Penrose process. Rotating black holes in astrophysics are a potential source of large amounts of energy and are used to explain energetic phenomena, such as [gamma-ray bursts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gamma-ray_burst).

## Features {#Features}

The Kerr geometry exhibits many noteworthy features: the maximal analytic extension includes a sequence of [asymptotically flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotically_flat) exterior regions, each associated with an [ergosphere](wikipedia://en/1508445), stationary limit surfaces, [event horizons](wikipedia://en/29320146), [Cauchy horizons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_horizon), [closed timelike curves](wikipedia://en/287091), and a ring-shaped [curvature singularity](wikipedia://en/54244). The [geodesic equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geodesic_equation) can be solved exactly in closed form. In addition to two [Killing vector fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killing_vector_fields) (corresponding to *[time translation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_translation)* and *axisymmetry*), the Kerr geometry admits a remarkable [Killing tensor](wikipedia://en/7913943). There is a pair of principal null congruences (one *ingoing* and one *outgoing*). The [Weyl tensor](wikipedia://en/1026848) is [algebraically special](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_special), in fact it has [Petrov type](wikipedia://en/2045417) **D**.

Note that the inner Kerr geometry is unstable with regard to perturbations in the interior region. This instability means that although the Kerr metric is axis-symmetric, a black hole created through gravitational collapse may not be so.[^o51] This instability also implies that many of the features of the Kerr geometry described above may not be present inside such a black hole.[^o72][^o73]

A surface on which light can orbit a black hole is called a photon sphere. The Kerr solution has infinitely many [photon spheres](wikipedia://en/3072290), lying between an inner one and an outer one. In the nonrotating, Schwarzschild solution, with $a = 0$, the inner and outer photon spheres degenerate, so that there is only one photon sphere at a single radius. The greater the spin of a black hole, the farther from each other the inner and outer photon spheres move. A beam of light traveling in a direction opposite to the spin of the black hole will circularly orbit the hole at the outer photon sphere. A beam of light traveling in the same direction as the black hole's spin will circularly orbit at the inner photon sphere. Orbiting geodesics with some angular momentum perpendicular to the axis of rotation of the black hole will orbit on photon spheres between these two extremes. Because the spacetime is rotating, such orbits exhibit a precession, since there is a shift in the $\phi$ variable after completing one period in the $\theta$ variable.

### Trajectory equations {#Trajectory_equations}

*Image: Wiki-en-kerr-mini.gif.* Animation of a test-particle's orbit around a spinning black hole. Left: top view, right: side view.

*Image: Generic geodesic orbit around a Kerr black hole.png.* Another trajectory of a test mass around a spinning (Kerr) black hole. Unlike orbits around a Schwarzschild black hole, the orbit is not confined to a single plane, but will [ergodically](wikipedia://en/5456824) fill a [toruslike](wikipedia://en/74800) region around the equator.

The [equations of motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations_of_motion) for [test particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Test_particle) in the Kerr spacetime are governed by four [constants of motion](wikipedia://en/5391037).[^o56] The first is the invariant mass $\mu$ of the test particle, defined by the relation

$$-\mu^2 = p^\alpha g_{\alpha\beta}p^\beta,$$

where $p^\alpha$ is the [four-momentum](wikipedia://en/204680) of the particle. Furthermore, there are two constants of motion given by the time translation and rotation symmetries of Kerr spacetime, the energy $E$, and the component of the orbital angular momentum parallel to the spin of the black hole $L_z$.[^o57][^o58]

$$E = -p_t,$$

and

$$L_z = p_\phi$$

Using [Hamilton–Jacobi theory](wikipedia://en/897539), [Brandon Carter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brandon_Carter) showed that there exists a fourth constant of motion, $Q$,[^o56] now referred to as the [Carter constant](wikipedia://en/21761927). It is related to the total angular momentum of the particle and is given by

$$Q = p_{\theta}^{2} + \cos^{2}\theta \left(a^{2}\left(\mu^{2} - E^{2}\right) + \left(\frac{L_z}{\sin\theta} \right)^{2} \right).$$

Since there are four (independent) constants of motion for degrees of freedom, the equations of motion for a test particle in Kerr spacetime are [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable).

Using these constants of motion, the trajectory equations for a test particle can be written (using natural units of $G = M = c = 1$),[^o56]

$$\begin{aligned} \Sigma\frac{dr}{d\lambda} &= \pm\sqrt{R(r)} \\ \Sigma\frac{d\theta}{d\lambda} &= \pm\sqrt{\Theta(\theta)} \\ \Sigma\frac{d\phi}{d\lambda} &= -\left(aE - \frac{L_z}{\sin^2\theta}\right) + \frac{a}{\Delta}P(r) \\ \Sigma\frac{dt}{d\lambda} &= -a\left(aE\sin^2\theta - L_z\right) + \frac{r^2 + a^2}{\Delta}P(r) \end{aligned}$$

with

- $$\Theta(\theta) = Q - \cos^2\theta\left(a^2\left(\mu^2 - E^2\right) + \frac{L_z^2}{\sin^2\theta}\right)$$
- $$P(r) = E\left(r^2 + a^2\right) - aL_z$$
- $$R(r) = P(r)^2 - \Delta\left(\mu^2 r^2 + (L_z - aE)^2 + Q\right)$$

where $\lambda$ is an [affine parameter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_parameter) such that $\frac{dx^\alpha}{d\lambda} = p^\alpha$. In particular, when $\mu>0$ the affine parameter $\lambda$, is related to the proper time $\tau$ through $\lambda = \tau/\mu$.

Because of the [frame-dragging](wikipedia://en/27086745)-effect, a zero-angular-momentum observer (ZAMO) is corotating with the angular velocity $\Omega$ which is defined with respect to the bookkeeper's coordinate time $t$.[^o59] The local velocity $v$ of the test-particle is measured relative to a probe corotating with $\Omega$. The gravitational time-dilation between a ZAMO at fixed $r$ and a stationary observer far away from the mass is

$$\frac{t}{\tau} = \sqrt{{\frac{\left(a^2 + r^2\right)^2 - a^2 \Delta \sin^2\theta}{\Delta\ \Sigma}}}.$$

In Cartesian Kerr–Schild coordinates, the equations for a photon are[^o60]

$$\ddot{x}+i\ddot{y}=4iMa\frac{r}{\Sigma^2}W\left [\dot{x}+i\dot{y}-\frac{x+iy}{r}\dot{r}\right ] - M(x+iy) \left ( \frac{4r^2}{\Sigma}-1\right )\frac{C-a^2 W^2}{r\Sigma^2}$$

$$\ddot{z}=-Mz\left(\frac{4r^2}{\Sigma}-1\right)\frac{C}{r\Sigma^2}$$

where $C$ is analogous to Carter's constant and $W$ is a useful quantity

$$C=p_{\theta}^2+\left(aE\sin{\theta}-\frac{L_z}{\sin{\theta}}\right)^2$$

$$W=\dot{t}-a \sin^2{\theta}\dot{\phi}$$

If we set $a=0$, the [Schwarzschild geodesics](wikipedia://en/1410576) are restored.

::: {#o62 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Symmetries"}

## Symmetries {#Symmetries}

The group of isometries of the Kerr metric is the subgroup of the ten-dimensional [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) which takes the two-dimensional locus of the singularity to itself. It retains the [time translations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_translation) (one dimension) and rotations around its axis of rotation (one dimension). Thus it has two dimensions. Like the Poincaré group, it has four connected components: the component of the identity; the component which reverses time and longitude; the component which reflects through the equatorial plane; and the component that does both. These isometries are encoded in the timelike and azimuthal Killing vectors.

In physics, symmetries are typically associated with conserved constants of motion, in accordance with [Noether's theorem](wikipedia://en/150159). As shown above, the geodesic equations have four conserved quantities: one of which comes from the definition of a geodesic, and two of which arise from the time translation and rotation symmetry of the Kerr geometry. The fourth conserved quantity does not arise from a symmetry in the standard sense and is commonly referred to as a hidden symmetry. This symmetry is encoded in a Killing tensor, whose conserved quantities are quadratic in the momenta.

:::

## Overextreme Kerr solutions {#Overextreme_Kerr_solutions}

The location of the event horizon is determined by the larger root of $\Delta=0$. When $r_\text{s} /2 < a$ (i.e. $G M^2 < J c$), there are no (real valued) solutions to this equation, and there is no event horizon. With no event horizons to hide it from the rest of the universe, the black hole ceases to be a black hole and will instead be a [naked singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naked_singularity).[^o64]

## Kerr black holes as wormholes {#Kerr_black_holes_as_wormholes}

Although the Kerr solution appears to be singular at the roots of $\Delta = 0$, these are actually coordinate singularities, and, with an appropriate choice of new coordinates, the Kerr solution can be smoothly extended through the values of $r$ corresponding to these roots. The larger of these roots determines the location of the event horizon, and the smaller determines the location of a [Cauchy horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy_horizon). A (future-directed, time-like) curve can start in the exterior and pass through the event horizon. Once having passed through the event horizon, the $r$ coordinate now behaves like a time coordinate, so it must decrease until the curve passes through the Cauchy horizon.[^o66][^o67][^o68]

## Anti-universe region {#Anti-universe_region}

The Kerr metric, which describes the spacetime geometry around a rotating black hole, can be extended beyond the inner event horizon. In the Boyer-Lindquist coordinate system $(t, r, \theta, \phi)$, this inner horizon is located at

$$r_- = M - \sqrt{M^2 - a^2}.$$

As one crosses this inner horizon, the radial coordinate $r$ continues to decrease, even becoming negative.

### Ring singularity and beyond {#Ring_singularity_and_beyond}

At $r=0$, a peculiar feature arises: a ring singularity. Unlike the point singularity in the Schwarzschild metric (a non-rotating black hole), the Kerr singularity is not a single point but a ring lying in the equatorial plane ($\theta = \pi/2$). This ring singularity acts as a portal to a new region of spacetime.

If we avoid the equatorial plane ($\theta \neq \pi/2$), we can smoothly continue the coordinate $r$ to negative values. This region with $r < 0$ is interpreted as an entirely new, asymptotically flat universe, often called the "anti-universe" or "negative-$r$ universe sheet". This anti-universe has some surprising properties:

**Negative ADM mass**: when $a$ is different from $0$, the Kerr metric remains valid for a negative value of the radial coordinate $r$, which is used in the coefficients of the Kerr metric through the terms $r^2$ and $mr$ with $m=GM/c^2$. From an algebraic point of view, replacing $r$ with $-r$ is equivalent to replacing $m$ with $-m$ in these terms. Thus, far out in a negative-$r$ universe sheet, the gravitational field is asymptotically equivalent to that of a Kerr solution with negative [Arnowitt-Deser-Misner (ADM) mass](wikipedia://en/3839357) $=-M$, which can be thought of as the total mass-energy of the spacetime as measured at infinity. A negative mass is a highly unusual concept in general relativity, and its physical interpretation is still debated.

Geodesics — trajectories subject only to the gravitational field of the Kerr black hole — therefore show repulsive behavior, unlike the attractive behavior observed in a positive-$r$ universe sheet (our universe).

**No horizon**: there is no horizon in a negative-$r$ universe sheet because a negative value of $r$ cannot cancel out $\Delta=r^2-mr+a^2$, which means that the singularity is naked and that there is only one negative-$r$ region, which extends the positive-$r$ Kerr spacetime described above.

### Closed timelike curves {#Closed_timelike_curves}

Within the anti-universe, an even stranger phenomenon occurs. The metric component $g_{\phi\phi}$, which is related to the azimuthal direction around the ring singularity, can change sign. Specifically, $g_{\phi\phi}$ is given by:

$$g_{\phi\phi} = \frac{-(r^2+a^2)^2 + \Delta a^2 \sin^2\theta}{\Sigma} \sin^2\theta.$$

When $g_{\phi\phi}$ becomes negative, the coordinate $\phi$ becomes timelike, and a linear combination of the coordinates $t$ and $\phi$ becomes spacelike. This leads to the existence of closed timelike curves (CTCs). A CTC is a path through spacetime where an object could travel back to its own past, violating causality.

The boundary where $g_{\phi\phi}$ changes sign and CTCs first appear is defined by the condition $g_{\phi\phi} = 0$, which gives

$$(r^2 + a^2)^2 = a^2 \Delta \sin^2\theta .$$

Beyond this boundary, the familiar notions of cause and effect break down. The presence of CTCs raises fundamental questions about the predictability and consistency of the laws of physics in these extreme regions of spacetime.

The anti-universe region of the extended Kerr metric is a mathematical construct with negative mass, reversed time orientation, and the possibility of time travel through closed timelike curves.[^o72][^o73] While the physical reality of the anti-universe remains uncertain, its study provides insight into the nature of spacetime, gravity, and the limits of our current understanding of the universe.

While it is expected that the exterior region of the Kerr solution is stable, and that all rotating black holes will eventually approach a Kerr metric, the interior region of the solution appears to be unstable, much like a pencil balanced on its point.[^o74][^o51] This is related to the idea of [cosmic censorship](wikipedia://en/7609).

## Relation to other exact solutions {#Relation_to_other_exact_solutions}

The Kerr geometry is a particular example of a [stationary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stationary_spacetime) [axially symmetric](wikipedia://en/2217599#Three_dimensions) [vacuum solution](wikipedia://en/1441464) to the [Einstein field equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_field_equation). The family of all stationary axially symmetric vacuum solutions to the Einstein field equation are the [Ernst vacuums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_vacuum).

The Kerr solution is also related to various non-vacuum solutions which model black holes. For example, the [Kerr–Newman electrovacuum](wikipedia://en/1447904) models a (rotating) black hole endowed with an electric charge, while the [Kerr–Vaidya null dust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerr–Vaidya_null_dust) models a (rotating) hole with infalling electromagnetic radiation.

The special case $a = 0$ of the Kerr metric yields the Schwarzschild metric, which models a *nonrotating* black hole which is [static](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_spacetime) and [spherically symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherically_symmetric), in the [Schwarzschild coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarzschild_coordinates). (In this case, every Geroch moment but the mass vanishes.)

The *interior* of the Kerr geometry, or rather a portion of it, is locally [isometric](wikipedia://en/254777) to the [Chandrasekhar–Ferrari CPW vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandrasekhar–Ferrari_CPW_vacuum), an example of a [colliding plane wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colliding_plane_wave) model. This is particularly interesting, because the [global structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_spacetime_structure) of this CPW solution is quite different from that of the Kerr geometry, and in principle, an experimenter could hope to study the geometry of (the outer portion of) the Kerr interior by arranging the collision of two suitable [gravitational plane waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravitational_plane_waves).

## Multipole moments {#Multipole_moments}

Each [asymptotically flat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotically_flat) Ernst vacuum can be characterized by giving the infinite sequence of relativistic [multipole moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipole_moment), the first two of which can be interpreted as the [mass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass) and [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/2839) of the source of the field. There are alternative formulations of relativistic multipole moments due to Hansen, Thorne, and Geroch, which turn out to agree with each other. The relativistic multipole moments of the Kerr geometry were computed by Hansen; they turn out to be

$$M_n = M [i a]^n$$

Thus, the special case of the [Schwarzschild vacuum](wikipedia://en/264606) ($a = 0$) gives the "monopole [point source](wikipedia://en/7622892)" of general relativity.[^o77]

*Weyl multipole moments* arise from treating a certain metric function (formally corresponding to Newtonian gravitational potential) which appears the Weyl–Papapetrou chart for the Ernst family of all stationary axisymmetric vacuum solutions using the standard euclidean scalar [multipole moments](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multipole_moment). They are distinct from the moments computed by Hansen, above. In a sense, the Weyl moments only (indirectly) characterize the "mass distribution" of an isolated source, and they turn out to depend only on the *even order* relativistic moments. In the case of solutions symmetric across the equatorial plane the *odd order* Weyl moments vanish. For the Kerr vacuum solutions, the first few Weyl moments are given by

$$a_0 = M, \qquad a_1 = 0, \qquad a_2 = M \left( \frac{M^2}{3} - a^2 \right) .$$

In particular, we see that the Schwarzschild vacuum has nonzero second order Weyl moment, corresponding to the fact that the "Weyl monopole" is the [Chazy–Curzon vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chazy–Curzon_vacuum) solution, not the Schwarzschild vacuum solution, which arises from the Newtonian potential of a certain finite length uniform density thin *rod*.

In weak field general relativity, it is convenient to treat isolated sources using another type of multipole, which generalize the Weyl moments to *mass multipole moments* and *momentum multipole moments*, characterizing respectively the distribution of mass and of [momentum](wikipedia://en/20431) of the source. These are multi-indexed quantities whose suitably symmetrized and anti-symmetrized parts can be related to the real and imaginary parts of the relativistic moments for the full nonlinear theory in a rather complicated manner.

Perez and Moreschi have given an alternative notion of "monopole solutions" by expanding the standard NP tetrad of the Ernst vacuums in powers of $r$ (the radial coordinate in the Weyl–Papapetrou chart). According to this formulation:

- the isolated mass monopole source with *zero* angular momentum is the *Schwarzschild vacuum* family (one parameter),
- the isolated mass monopole source with *radial* angular momentum is the *[Taub–NUT vacuum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taub–NUT_vacuum)* family (two parameters; not quite asymptotically flat),
- the isolated mass monopole source with *axial* angular momentum is the *Kerr vacuum* family (two parameters).

In this sense, the Kerr vacuums are the simplest stationary axisymmetric asymptotically flat vacuum solutions in general relativity.

## Open problems {#Open_problems}

The Kerr geometry is often used as a model of a [rotating black hole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotating_black_hole) but if the solution is held to be valid only outside some compact region (subject to certain restrictions), in principle, it should be able to be used as an [exterior solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_solution) to model the gravitational field around a rotating massive object other than a black hole such as a [neutron star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neutron_star), or the Earth. This works out very nicely for the non-rotating case, where the Schwarzschild vacuum exterior can be matched to a [Schwarzschild fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarzschild_fluid) interior, and indeed to more general [static spherically symmetric perfect fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Static_spherically_symmetric_perfect_fluid) solutions. However, the problem of finding a rotating perfect-fluid interior which can be matched to a Kerr exterior, or indeed to any asymptotically flat vacuum exterior solution, has proven very difficult. In particular, the [Wahlquist fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wahlquist_fluid), which was once thought to be a candidate for matching to a Kerr exterior, is now known not to admit any such matching. At present, it seems that only approximate solutions modeling slowly rotating fluid balls are known (These are the relativistic analog of oblate spheroidal balls with nonzero mass and angular momentum but vanishing higher multipole moments). However, the exterior of the [Neugebauer–Meinel disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neugebauer–Meinel_disk), an exact [dust solution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dust_solution) which models a rotating thin disk, approaches in a limiting case the $GM^2 = cJ$ Kerr geometry. Physical thin-disk solutions obtained by identifying parts of the Kerr spacetime are also known.[^o80]
