# Positive operator

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Positive operator"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) (specifically [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), [operator theory](wikipedia://en/712675), and [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180)) as well as [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $A$ acting on an [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856) is called **positive-semidefinite** (or *non-negative*) if, for every $x \in \operatorname{Dom}(A)$, $\langle Ax, x\rangle \in \mathbb{R}$ and $\langle Ax, x\rangle \geq 0$, where $\operatorname{Dom}(A)$ is the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of $A$. Positive-semidefinite operators are denoted as $A\ge 0$. The operator is said to be **positive-definite**, and written $A>0$, if $\langle Ax,x\rangle>0$ for all $x\in\mathop{\mathrm{Dom}}(A) \setminus \{0\}$.[^o2]

:::

Many authors define a **positive operator** $A$ to be a [self-adjoint](wikipedia://en/187408) (or at least symmetric) non-negative operator. We show below that for a complex Hilbert space the self adjointness follows automatically from non-negativity. For a real Hilbert space non-negativity does not imply self adjointness.

In physics (specifically [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202)), such operators represent [quantum states](wikipedia://en/30876419), via the [density matrix](wikipedia://en/62844) formalism.

::: {#o4 .statement cue=named-heading name="Cauchy–Schwarz inequality"}

## Cauchy–Schwarz inequality {#Cauchy–Schwarz_inequality}

Take the inner product $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ to be [anti-linear](wikipedia://en/293432) on the *first* argument and linear on the second and suppose that $A$ is positive and symmetric, the latter meaning that $\langle Ax,y \rangle= \langle x,Ay \rangle$. Then the non negativity of

$$\begin{aligned} \langle A(\lambda x+\mu y),\lambda x+\mu y \rangle =|\lambda|^2 \langle Ax,x \rangle + \lambda^* \mu \langle Ax,y \rangle+ \lambda \mu^* \langle Ay,x \rangle + |\mu|^2 \langle Ay,y \rangle \\[1mm] = |\lambda|^2 \langle Ax,x \rangle + \lambda^* \mu \langle Ax,y \rangle+ \lambda \mu^* (\langle Ax,y \rangle)^* + |\mu|^2 \langle Ay,y \rangle \end{aligned}$$

for all complex $\lambda$ and $\mu$ shows that

$$\left|\langle Ax,y\rangle \right|^2 \leq \langle Ax,x\rangle \langle Ay,y\rangle.$$

It follows that $\mathop{\text{Im}}A \perp \mathop{\text{Ker}}A.$ If $A$ is defined everywhere, and $\langle Ax,x\rangle = 0,$ then $Ax = 0.$

:::

## On a complex Hilbert space, if an operator is non-negative then it is symmetric {#On_a_complex_Hilbert_space,_if_an_operator_is_non-negative_then_it_is_symmetric}

For $x,y \in \operatorname{Dom}A,$ the [polarization identity](wikipedia://en/2047965)

$$\begin{aligned} \langle Ax,y\rangle = \frac{1}{4}({} & \langle A(x+y),x+y\rangle - \langle A(x-y),x-y\rangle \\[1mm] & {} - i\langle A(x+iy),x+iy\rangle + i\langle A(x-iy),x-iy\rangle) \end{aligned}$$

and the fact that $\langle Ax,x\rangle = \langle x,Ax\rangle,$ for positive operators, show that $\langle Ax,y\rangle = \langle x,Ay\rangle,$ so $A$ is symmetric.

In contrast with the complex case, a positive-semidefinite operator on a real Hilbert space $H_\mathbb{R}$ may not be symmetric. As a counterexample, define $A : \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}^2$ to be an operator of rotation by an [acute angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_angle) $\varphi \in ( -\pi/2,\pi/2).$ Then $\langle Ax,x \rangle = \|Ax\|\|x\|\cos\varphi > 0,$ but $A^* = A^{-1} \neq A,$ so $A$ is not symmetric.

## If an operator is non-negative and defined on the whole complex Hilbert space, then it is self-adjoint and [bounded](wikipedia://en/455961) {#If_an_operator_is_non-negative_and_defined_on_the_whole_complex_Hilbert_space,_then_it_is_self-adjoint_and_bounded}

The symmetry of $A$ implies that $\operatorname{Dom}A \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}A^*$ and $A = A^*|_{\operatorname{Dom}(A)}.$ For $A$ to be self-adjoint, it is necessary that $\operatorname{Dom}A = \operatorname{Dom}A^*.$ In our case, the equality of [domains](wikipedia://en/50263) holds because $H_\mathbb{C} = \operatorname{Dom}A \subseteq \operatorname{Dom}A^*,$ so $A$ is indeed self-adjoint. The fact that $A$ is bounded now follows from the [Hellinger–Toeplitz theorem](wikipedia://en/592897).

This property does not hold on $H_\mathbb{R}.$

## Partial order of self-adjoint operators {#Partial_order_of_self-adjoint_operators}

A natural [partial ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_ordering) of self-adjoint operators arises from the definition of positive operators. Define $B \geq A$ if the following hold:

1. $A$ and $B$ are self-adjoint
1. $$B - A \geq 0$$

It can be seen that a similar result as the [Monotone convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/146263) holds for [monotone increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotone_increasing), bounded, self-adjoint operators on Hilbert spaces.[^o8]

## Application to physics: quantum states {#Application_to_physics:_quantum_states}

The definition of a [quantum system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_system) includes a complex [separable Hilbert space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_Hilbert_space) $H_\mathbb{C}$ and a set $\cal S$ of positive [trace-class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trace-class) [operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_operator) $\rho$ on $H_\mathbb{C}$ for which $\mathop{\text{Trace}}\rho = 1.$ The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $\cal S$ is *the set of states*. Every $\rho \in {\cal S}$ is called a *state* or a *density operator*. For $\psi \in H_\mathbb{C},$ where $\|\psi\| = 1,$ the operator $P_\psi$ of projection onto the [span](wikipedia://en/56353) of $\psi$ is called a *[pure state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pure_state)*. (Since each pure state is identifiable with a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) $\psi \in H_\mathbb{C},$ some sources define pure states to be unit elements from $H_\mathbb{C}).$ States that are not pure are called *[mixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_state_%28physics%29)*.
