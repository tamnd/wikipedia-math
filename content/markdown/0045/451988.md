# J-invariant

*Image: KleinInvariantJ.jpg.* Klein's $j$-invariant in the complex plane

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="J-invariant"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **$j$-invariant** or **$j$ function** is a [modular function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_function) of weight zero for the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $\operatorname{SL}(2,\mathbb{Z})$ defined on the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). It is the unique such function that is [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) away from a simple pole at the [cusp](wikipedia://en/6851758) such that

:::

$$j\big(e^{2\pi i/3}\big) = 0, \quad j(i) = 1728 = 12^3.$$

[Rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) of $j$ are modular, and in fact give all modular functions of weight 0. Classically, the $j$-invariant was studied as a parameterization of [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225) over $\mathbb{C}$, but it also has surprising connections to the symmetries of the [Monster group](wikipedia://en/47422) (this connection is referred to as [monstrous moonshine](wikipedia://en/728168)).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: J-inv-real.jpeg.* Real part of the $j$-invariant as a function of the square of the [nome](wikipedia://en/1471192) on the unit disk

*Image: J-inv-phase.jpeg.* Phase of the $j$-invariant as a function of the square of the nome on the unit disk

The $j$-invariant can be defined as a function on the [upper half-plane](wikipedia://en/287826) $\mathcal{H}=\{\tau\in\mathbb{C} \mid \operatorname{Im}(\tau)>0\}$, by

$$j(\tau) = 1728 \frac{g_2(\tau)^3}{\Delta(\tau)} = 1728 \frac{g_2(\tau)^3}{g_2(\tau)^3 - 27g_3(\tau)^2} = 1728 \frac{g_2(\tau)^3}{(2\pi)^{12}\,\eta(\tau)^{24}}$$

with the third definition implying $j(\tau)$ can be expressed as a [cube](wikipedia://en/451445), also since [1728](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1728_%28number%29)${} = 12^3$. The function cannot be continued analytically beyond the upper half-plane due to the [natural boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_boundary) at the real line.

The given functions are the [modular discriminant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_discriminant) $\Delta(\tau) = g_2(\tau)^3 - 27g_3(\tau)^2 = (2\pi)^{12}\,\eta(\tau)^{24}$, [Dedekind eta function](wikipedia://en/447020) $\eta(\tau)$, and modular invariants,

$$g_2(\tau) = 60G_4(\tau) = 60\sum_{(m,n) \neq (0,0)} \left(m + n\tau\right)^{-4}$$

$$g_3(\tau) = 140G_6(\tau) = 140\sum_{(m,n) \neq (0,0)} \left(m + n\tau\right)^{-6}$$

where $G_4(\tau)$, $G_6(\tau)$ are [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/1270458#Fourier_series),

$$\begin{aligned} G_4(\tau)&=\frac{\pi^4}{45}\, E_4(\tau) \\[4pt] G_6(\tau)&=\frac{2\pi^6}{945}\, E_6(\tau) \end{aligned}$$

and $E_4(\tau)$, $E_6(\tau)$ are [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458#Identities_involving_Eisenstein_series),

$$\begin{aligned} E_4(\tau)&= 1+ 240\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{n^3 q^n}{1-q^n} \\[4pt] E_6(\tau)&= 1- 504\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{n^5 q^n}{1-q^n} \end{aligned}$$

and $q=e^{2\pi i \tau}$ (the square of the [nome](wikipedia://en/1471192)). The $j$-invariant can then be directly expressed in terms of the Eisenstein series as,

$$j(\tau) = 1728 \frac{E_4(\tau)^3}{E_4(\tau)^3 - E_6(\tau)^2}$$

with no numerical factor other than 1728. This implies a third way to define the modular discriminant,[^o4]

$$\Delta(\tau) = (2\pi)^{12}\,\frac{E_4(\tau)^3 - E_6(\tau)^2}{1728}$$

For example, using the definitions above and $\tau = 2i$, then the Dedekind eta function $\eta(2i)$ has the [exact value](wikipedia://en/447020#Special_values),

$$\eta(2i) = \frac{\Gamma \left(\frac14\right)}{2^{11/8} \pi^{3/4}}$$

implying the [transcendental numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendental_numbers),

$$g_2(2i) = \frac{11\,\Gamma \left(\frac14\right)^8}{2^{8} \pi^2},\qquad g_3(2i) = \frac{7\,\Gamma \left(\frac14\right)^{12}}{2^{12} \pi^3}$$

but yielding the [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) (in fact, an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563)),

$$j(2i) = 1728 \frac{g_2(2i)^3}{g_2(2i)^3 - 27g_3(2i)^2} = 66^3.$$

In general, this can be motivated by viewing each $\tau$ as representing an isomorphism class of elliptic curves. Every elliptic curve $E$ over $\mathbf{C}$ is a [complex torus](wikipedia://en/3055042), and thus can be identified with a rank 2 lattice; that is, a two-dimensional lattice of $\mathbf{C}$. This lattice can be rotated and scaled (operations that preserve the isomorphism class), so that it is generated by $1$ and $\tau$$\in \mathbf{H}$. This lattice corresponds to the elliptic curve $y^2=4x^3-g_2(\tau)x-g_3(\tau)$ (see [Weierstrass elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass's_elliptic_functions#General_theory)).

Note that $j$ is defined everywhere in $\mathbf{H}$ as the modular discriminant is non-zero. This is due to the corresponding cubic polynomial having distinct roots.

:::

## The fundamental region {#The_fundamental_region}

*Image: ModularGroup-FundamentalDomain.svg.* The usual choice of a fundamental domain (gray) for the modular group acting on the upper half plane

It can be shown that $\Delta$ is a [modular form](wikipedia://en/286000) of weight twelve, and $g_{2}$ one of weight four, so that its third power is also of weight twelve. Thus their quotient, and therefore $j$, is a modular function of weight zero, in particular a holomorphic function $\mathbf{H} \to \mathbf{C}$ invariant under the action of $\operatorname{SL}(2, \mathbf{Z})$. Quotienting out by its centre $\{ \pm I \}$ yields the [modular group](wikipedia://en/325019), which we may identify with the [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) $\operatorname{PSL}(2, \mathbf{Z})$.

By a suitable choice of transformation belonging to this group,

$$\tau \mapsto \frac{a\tau + b}{c\tau +d}, \qquad ad-bc =1,$$

we may reduce $\tau$ to a value giving the same value for $j$, and lying in the [fundamental region](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_region) for $j$, which consists of values for $\tau$ satisfying the conditions

$$\begin{aligned} |\tau| &\ge 1 \\[5pt] -\tfrac{1}{2} &< \mathfrak{R}(\tau) \le \tfrac{1}{2} \\[5pt] -\tfrac{1}{2} &< \mathfrak{R}(\tau) < 0 \Rightarrow |\tau| > 1 \end{aligned}$$

The function $j(\tau)$ when restricted to this region still takes on every value in the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbf{C}$ exactly once. In other words, for every $c$ in $\mathbf{C}$, there is a unique τ in the fundamental region such that $c = j(\tau)$. Thus, $j$ has the property of mapping the fundamental region to the entire complex plane.

Additionally two values $\tau,\tau' \in\mathbf{H}$ produce the same elliptic curve iff $\tau = T(\tau')$ for some $T \in \operatorname{PSL}(2, \mathbf{Z})$. This means $j$ provides a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) from the set of elliptic curves over $\mathbf{C}$ to the complex plane.[^o6]

As a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181), the fundamental region has genus $0$, and every ([level one](wikipedia://en/286000#Definition)) modular function is a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) in $j$; and, conversely, every rational function in $j$ is a modular function. In other words, the field of modular functions is $\mathbf{C}(j)$.

## Class field theory and $j$ {#Class_field_theory_and}

The $j$-invariant has many remarkable properties:

- If $\tau$ is any point of the upper half plane whose corresponding elliptic curve has [complex multiplication](wikipedia://en/537183) (that is, if $\tau$ is any element of an imaginary [quadratic field](wikipedia://en/454781) with positive imaginary part, so that $j$ is defined), then $j(\tau)$ is an [algebraic integer](wikipedia://en/98981).[^o8] These special values are called [singular moduli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_moduli).
- The [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $\mathbf{Q}[j(\tau), \tau]/\mathbf{Q}(\tau)$ is abelian, that is, it has an abelian [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673).
- Let $\Lambda$ be the lattice in $\mathbf{C}$ generated by $\{1, \tau\}.$ It is easy to see that all of the elements of $\mathbf{Q}(\tau)$ which fix $\Lambda$ under multiplication form a ring with units, called an [order](wikipedia://en/1114171). The other lattices with generators $\{1, \tau\},$ associated in like manner to the same order define the [algebraic conjugates](wikipedia://en/458467) $j(\tau)$ of $j(\tau)$ over $\mathbf{Q}(\tau)$. Ordered by inclusion, the unique maximal order in $\mathbf{Q}(\tau)$ is the ring of algebraic integers of $\mathbf{Q}(\tau)$, and values of $\tau$ having it as its associated order lead to [unramified extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unramified_extension) of $\mathbf{Q}(\tau)$.

These classical results are the starting point for the theory of complex multiplication.

## Transcendence properties {#Transcendence_properties}

In 1937 [Theodor Schneider](wikipedia://en/10312621) proved the aforementioned result that if $\tau$ is a quadratic [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) in the upper half plane then $j(\tau)$ is an algebraic integer. In addition he proved that if $\tau$ is an [algebraic number](wikipedia://en/1158) but not imaginary quadratic then $j(\tau)$ is transcendental.

The $j$ function has numerous other transcendental properties. [Kurt Mahler](wikipedia://en/4421496) conjectured a particular transcendence result that is often referred to as Mahler's conjecture, though it was proved as a corollary of results by Yu. V. Nesterenko and Patrice Phillipon in the 1990s. Mahler's conjecture (now proven) is that, if $\tau$ is in the upper half plane, then $e^{2\pi i\tau}$ and $j(\tau)$ are never both simultaneously algebraic. Stronger results are now known, for example if $e^{2\pi i\tau}$ is algebraic then the following three numbers are algebraically independent, and thus at least two of them transcendental:

$$j(\tau), \frac{j^\prime(\tau)}{\pi}, \frac{j^{\prime\prime}(\tau)}{\pi^2}$$

## The $q$-expansion and moonshine {#The_-expansion_and_moonshine}

Several remarkable properties of $j$ have to do with its [$q$-expansion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Q-expansion) ([Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) expansion), written as a [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213) in terms of $q = e^{2\pi i\tau}$, which begins:

$$j(\tau) = q^{-1} + 744 + 196884 q + 21493760 q^2 + 864299970 q^3 + 20245856256 q^4 + \cdots$$

Note that $j$ has a [simple pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_pole) at the cusp, so its $q$-expansion has no terms below $q^{-1}$.

All the Fourier coefficients are integers, which results in several [almost integers](wikipedia://en/14041933), notably [Ramanujan's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan's_constant):

$$e^{\pi \sqrt{163}} \approx 640320^3 + 744.$$

The [asymptotic formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_formula) for the coefficient of $q^{n}$ is given by

$$\frac{e^{4\pi\sqrt{n}}}{\sqrt{2}\,n^{3/4}},$$

as can be proved by the [Hardy–Littlewood circle method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hardy–Littlewood_circle_method).[^o11][^o12]

### Moonshine {#Moonshine}

More remarkably, the Fourier coefficients for the positive exponents of $q$ are the dimensions of the graded part of an infinite-dimensional [graded algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graded_algebra) representation of the [monster group](wikipedia://en/47422) called the *[moonshine module](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonshine_module)* – specifically, the coefficient of $q^{n}$ is the dimension of grade-$n$ part of the moonshine module, the first example being the [Griess algebra](wikipedia://en/728596), which has dimension 196,884, corresponding to the term $196884q$. This startling observation, first made by [John McKay](wikipedia://en/4583624), was the starting point for [moonshine theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moonshine_theory).

The study of the Moonshine conjecture led [John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807) and [Simon P. Norton](wikipedia://en/1641166) to look at the genus-zero modular functions. If they are normalized to have the form

$$q^{-1} + {O}(q)$$

then [John G. Thompson](wikipedia://en/862189) showed that there are only a finite number of such functions (of some finite level), and Chris J. Cummins later showed that there are exactly 6486 of them, 616 of which have integral coefficients.[^o14]

## Alternate expressions {#Alternate_expressions}

We have

$$j(\tau) = \frac{256\left(1-x\right)^3}{x^2}$$

where $x = \lambda(1 - \lambda)$ and $\lambda$ is the [modular lambda function](wikipedia://en/27903678)

$$\lambda(\tau) = \frac{\theta_2(e^{\pi i\tau})^4}{\theta_3(e^{\pi i\tau})^4} = k(\tau)^2$$

a ratio of [Jacobi theta functions](wikipedia://en/449745) $\theta_{m}$, and is the square of the elliptic modulus $k(\tau)$.[^o16] The value of $j$ is unchanged when $\lambda$ is replaced by any of the six values of the [cross-ratio](wikipedia://en/401767):[^o17]

$$\left\lbrace { \lambda, \frac{1}{1-\lambda}, \frac{\lambda-1}{\lambda}, \frac{1}{\lambda}, \frac{\lambda}{\lambda-1}, 1-\lambda } \right\rbrace$$

The branch points of $j$ are at $\{0, 1, \infty\}$, so that $j$ is a [Belyi function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belyi_function).[^o18]

## Expressions in terms of theta functions {#Expressions_in_terms_of_theta_functions}

Define the [nome](wikipedia://en/1471192) $q = e^{\pi i\tau}$ and the [Jacobi theta function](wikipedia://en/449745),

$$\vartheta(0; \tau) = \vartheta_{00}(0; \tau) = 1 + 2 \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(e^{\pi i\tau}\right)^{n^2} = \sum_{n=-\infty}^\infty q^{n^2}$$

from which one can derive the auxiliary theta functions, defined [here](wikipedia://en/449745#Auxiliary_functions). Let,

$$\begin{aligned} a &= \theta_{2}(q) = \vartheta_{10}(0; \tau) \\ b &= \theta_{3}(q) = \vartheta_{00}(0; \tau) \\ c &= \theta_{4}(q) = \vartheta_{01}(0; \tau) \end{aligned}$$

where $\vartheta_{ij}$ and $\theta_{n}$ are alternative notations, and $a^{4} - b^{4} + c^{4} = 0$. Then we have the for [modular invariants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass's_elliptic_functions#The_constants_e1.2C_e2_and_e3) $g_{2}$, $g_{3}$,

$$\begin{aligned} g_2(\tau) &= \tfrac{2}{3}\pi^4 \left(a^8 + b^8 + c^8\right) \\ g_3(\tau) &= \tfrac{4}{27}\pi^6 \sqrt{\frac{\left(a^8+b^8+c^8\right)^3-54\left(abc\right)^8}{2}} \\ \end{aligned}$$

and modular discriminant,

$$\Delta = g_2^3-27g_3^2 = (2\pi)^{12} \left(\tfrac{1}{2}a b c\right)^8 = (2\pi)^{12}\eta(\tau)^{24}$$

with [Dedekind eta function](wikipedia://en/447020) $\eta(\tau)$. The $j(\tau)$ can then be rapidly computed,

$$j(\tau) = 1728\frac{g_2^3}{g_2^3-27g_3^2} = 32 \frac{\left(a^8 + b^8 + c^8\right)^3 }{ \left(a b c\right)^8}$$

## Algebraic definition {#Algebraic_definition}

So far we have been considering $j$ as a function of a complex variable. However, as an invariant for isomorphism classes of elliptic curves, it can be defined purely algebraically.[^o21] Let

$$y^2 + a_1 xy + a_3 y = x^3 + a_2 x^2 + a_4 x + a_6$$

be a plane elliptic curve *over any field*. Then we may perform successive transformations to get the above equation into the standard form $y^{2} = 4x^{3} - g_{2}x - g_{3}$ (note that this transformation can only be made when the characteristic of the field is not equal to 2 or 3). The resulting coefficients are

$$\begin{aligned} b_2 &= a_1^2 + 4a_2,\quad &b_4 &= a_1a_3 + 2a_4,\\ b_6 &= a_3^2 + 4a_6,\quad &b_8 &= a_1^2a_6 - a_1a_3a_4 + a_2a_3^2 + 4a_2a_6 - a_4^2,\\ c_4 &= b_2^2 - 24b_4,\quad &c_6 &= -b_2^3 + 36b_2b_4 - 216b_6, \end{aligned}$$

where $g_{2} = c_{4}$ and $g_{3} = c_{6}$. We also have the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607#low_degrees)

$$\Delta = -b_2^2b_8 + 9b_2b_4b_6 - 8b_4^3 - 27b_6^2.$$

The $j$-invariant for the elliptic curve may now be defined as

$$j = \frac{c_4^3}{\Delta}.$$

In the case that the field over which the curve is defined has characteristic different from 2 or 3, this is equal to

$$j = 1728\frac{c_4^3}{c_4^3-c_6^2}.$$

In case of a short Weierstraß elliptic curve $y^2 = x^3+ax+b$, this formula simplifies to

$$j = 1728\frac{4a^3}{4a^3+27b^2}.$$

In case of a Montgomery elliptic curve $By^2 = x^3 + Ax^2 + x$, it simplifies to

$$j = 256\frac{(A^2-3)^3}{(A+2)(A-2)}.$$

## Inverse function {#Inverse_function}

The [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) of the $j$-invariant can be expressed in terms of the [hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/2055223) ${}_{2}F_{1}$ (see also the article [Picard–Fuchs equation](wikipedia://en/2071385)). Explicitly, given a number $N$, to solve the equation $j(\tau) = N$ for $\tau$ can be done in at least four ways.

**Method 1**: Solving the [sextic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sextic) in $\lambda$,

$$j(\tau) = \frac{256\bigl(1-\lambda(1-\lambda)\bigr)^3}{\bigl(\lambda(1-\lambda)\bigr)^2} = \frac{256\left(1-x\right)^3}{x^2}$$

where $x = \lambda(1 - \lambda)$, and $\lambda$ is the [modular lambda function](wikipedia://en/27903678) so the sextic can be solved as a cubic in $x$. Then,

$$\tau = i \ \frac{{}_2F_1 \left (\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},1;1 - \lambda \right )}{{}_2F_1 \left (\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{2},1;\lambda \right)}=i\frac{\operatorname{M}(1,\sqrt{1-\lambda})}{\operatorname{M}(1,\sqrt{\lambda})}$$

for any of the six values of $\lambda$, where $M$ is the [arithmetic–geometric mean](wikipedia://en/3090).[^o23]

**Method 2**: Solving the [quartic](wikipedia://en/308152) in $\gamma$,

$$j(\tau) = \frac{27\left(1 + 8\gamma\right)^3}{\gamma\left(1 - \gamma\right)^3}$$

then for any of the four [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function),

$$\tau = \frac{i}{\sqrt{3}} \frac{{}_2F_1 \left (\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{2}{3},1;1-\gamma \right)}{{}_2F_1 \left(\tfrac{1}{3},\tfrac{2}{3},1;\gamma \right )}$$

**Method 3**: Solving the [cubic](wikipedia://en/243709) in $\beta$,

$$j(\tau) = \frac{64\left(1+3\beta\right)^3}{\beta\left(1-\beta\right)^2}$$

then for any of the three roots,

$$\tau = \frac{i}{\sqrt{2}} \frac{{}_2F_1 \left (\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1;1-\beta \right)}{{}_2F_1 \left(\tfrac{1}{4},\tfrac{3}{4},1;\beta \right )}$$

**Method 4**: Solving the [quadratic](wikipedia://en/25175) in $\alpha$,

$$j(\tau)=\frac{1728}{4\alpha(1-\alpha)}$$

then,

$$\tau = i \ \frac{{}_2F_1 \left (\tfrac{1}{6},\tfrac{5}{6},1;1-\alpha \right)}{{}_2F_1 \left(\tfrac{1}{6},\tfrac{5}{6},1;\alpha \right )}$$

One root gives $\tau$, and the other gives $-\tfrac{1}{\tau}$, but since $j(\tau) = j(-\tfrac{1}{\tau})$, it makes no difference which $\alpha$ is chosen. The latter three methods can be found in [Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan)'s theory of [elliptic functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptic_functions) to alternative bases.

The inversion is applied in high-precision calculations of elliptic function periods even as their ratios become unbounded. A related result is the expressibility via quadratic radicals of the values of $j$ at the points of the imaginary axis whose magnitudes are powers of 2 (thus permitting [compass and straightedge constructions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge_constructions)). The latter result is hardly evident since the [modular equation](wikipedia://en/439154) for $j$ of order 2 is cubic.[^o24]

## Pi formulas {#Pi_formulas}

The [Chudnovsky brothers](wikipedia://en/15893520) found in 1987,[^o27]

$$\frac{1}{\pi} = \frac{12}{640320^{3/2}} \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(6k)! (163 \cdot 3344418k + 13591409)}{(3k)!\left(k!\right)^3 \left(-640320\right)^{3k}}$$

a proof of which uses the fact that

$$j\left(\frac{1+\sqrt{-163}}{2}\right) = -640320^3.$$

For similar formulas, see the [Ramanujan–Sato series](wikipedia://en/42185330).

:::

## Failure to classify elliptic curves over other fields {#Failure_to_classify_elliptic_curves_over_other_fields}

The $j$-invariant is only sensitive to isomorphism classes of elliptic curves over the complex numbers, or more generally, an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018). Over other fields there exist examples of elliptic curves whose $j$-invariant is the same, but are non-isomorphic. For example, let $E_1,E_2$ be the elliptic curves associated to the polynomials

$$\begin{aligned} E_1: &\text{ } y^2 = x^3 - 25x \\ E_2: &\text{ } y^2 = x^3 - 4x, \end{aligned}$$

both having $j$-invariant $1728$. Then, the rational points of $E_2$ can be computed as

$$E_2(\mathbb{Q}) = \{\infty, (2,0), (-2,0), (0,0) \}$$

since $x^3 - 4x = x(x^2 - 4) = x(x-2)(x+2)$. There are no rational solutions with $y = a \neq 0$. This can be shown using [Cardano's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardano's_formula) to show that in that case the solutions to $x^3 - 4x - a^2$ are all irrational.

On the other hand, on the set of points

$$\{ n(-4,6) : n \in \mathbb{Z} \}$$

the equation for $E_1$ becomes $36n^2 = -64n^3 + 100n$. Dividing by $4n$ to eliminate the $(0,0)$ solution, the quadratic formula gives the rational solutions:

$$n = \frac{ -9 \pm \sqrt{81 - 4\cdot 16\cdot(-25)} }{2\cdot 16} = \frac{-9 \pm 41}{32}.$$

If these curves are considered over $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{10})$, there is an isomorphism $E_1(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{10})) \cong E_2(\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{10}))$ sending

$$(x,y)\mapsto (\mu^2x,\mu^3y) \ \text{ where }\ \mu = \frac{\sqrt{10}}{2}.$$

[^o23]: The equality holds if the arithmetic–geometric mean $\operatorname{M}(a,b)$ of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $a,b$ (such that $a,b\ne 0;a\ne \pm b$) is defined as follows: Let $a_0=a$, $b_0=b$, $a_{n+1}=(a_n+b_n)/2$, $b_{n+1}=\pm\sqrt{a_nb_n}$ where the signs are chosen such that $|a_n-b_n|\le|a_n+b_n|$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}$. If $|a_n-b_n|=|a_n+b_n|$, the sign is chosen such that $\Im (b_n/a_n)>0$. Then $\operatorname{M}(a,b)=\lim_{n\to\infty}a_n=\lim_{n\to\infty}b_n$. When $a,b$ are positive real (with $a\ne b$), this definition coincides with the usual definition of the arithmetic–geometric mean for positive real numbers. See [The Arithmetic-Geometric Mean of Gauss](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/248675540_The_Arithmetic-Geometric_Mean_of_Gauss) by [David A. Cox](wikipedia://en/41248158).
