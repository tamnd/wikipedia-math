# Integral test for convergence

*Image: Integral Test.svg.* The integral test applied to the [harmonic series](wikipedia://en/142488). Since the area under the curve $y = 1/x$ for $x \in [1, \infty)$ is infinite, the total area of the rectangles must be infinite as well.

Series

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Integral test for convergence"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **integral test for convergence** is a [method used to test](wikipedia://en/4576951) infinite [series](wikipedia://en/15287) of [monotonic](wikipedia://en/48260) terms for [convergence](wikipedia://en/1743431). It was developed by [Colin Maclaurin](wikipedia://en/7824) and [Augustin-Louis Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842) and is sometimes known as the **Maclaurin–Cauchy test**.

:::

## Statement of the test {#Statement_of_the_test}

Consider an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $N$ and a function $f$ defined on the unbounded [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) *N*, ∞, on which it is [monotonically decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonically_decreasing). Then the infinite series

$$\sum_{n=N}^\infty f(n)$$

converges to a [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) if and only if the [improper integral](wikipedia://en/346030)

$$\int_N^\infty f(x)\,dx$$

is finite. In particular, if the integral diverges, then the [series diverges](wikipedia://en/876428) as well.

### Remark {#Remark}

If the improper integral is finite, then the proof also gives the [lower and upper bounds](wikipedia://en/42693)

::: {#o4 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\int_N^\infty f(x)\,dx\le\sum_{n=N}^\infty f(n)\le f(N)+\int_N^\infty f(x)\,dx \tag{1}$$

:::

for the infinite series.

Note that if the function $f(x)$ is increasing, then the function $-f(x)$ is decreasing and the above theorem applies.

Many textbooks require the function $f$ to be positive,[^o5][^o6][^o7] but this condition is not really necessary, since when $f$ is negative and decreasing both $\sum_{n=N}^\infty f(n)$ and $\int_N^\infty f(x)\,dx$ diverge.[^o8]

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The proof uses the [comparison test](wikipedia://en/1601407), comparing the term $f(n)$ with the integral of $f$ over the intervals $[n-1,n)$ and $[n,n+1)$ respectively.

The monotonic function $f$ is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688). To show this, let

$$D=\{ x\in [N,\infty)\mid f\text{ is discontinuous at } x\}$$

For every $x\in D$, there exists by the [density](wikipedia://en/23523594) of $\mathbb Q$, a $c(x)\in\mathbb Q$ so that $c(x)\in\left[\lim_{y\downarrow x} f(y), \lim_{y\uparrow x} f(y)\right]$.

Note that this set contains an [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) interval precisely if $f$ is [discontinuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discontinuous) at $x$. We can uniquely identify $c(x)$ as the [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) that has the least index in an [enumeration](wikipedia://en/212115) $\mathbb N\to\mathbb Q$ and satisfies the above property. Since $f$ is [monotone](wikipedia://en/48260), this defines an [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) [mapping](wikipedia://en/185427) $c:D\to\mathbb Q, x\mapsto c(x)$ and thus $D$ is [countable](wikipedia://en/6026). It follows that $f$ is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688). This is [sufficient](wikipedia://en/169319) for [Riemann integrability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_integrability).[^o11]

Since $f$ is a monotone decreasing function, we know that

$$f(x)\le f(n)\quad\text{for all }x\in[n,\infty)$$

and

$$f(n)\le f(x)\quad\text{for all }x\in[N,n].$$

Hence, for every integer $n \ge N$,

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\int_n^{n+1} f(x)\,dx \le\int_{n}^{n+1} f(n)\,dx =f(n) \tag{2}$$

:::

and, for every integer $n \ge N + 1$,

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$f(n)=\int_{n-1}^{n} f(n)\,dx \le\int_{n-1}^n f(x)\,dx. \tag{3}$$

:::

By summation over all $n$ from $N$ to some larger integer $M$, we get from ((2))

$$\int_N^{M+1}f(x)\,dx=\sum_{n=N}^M\underbrace{\int_n^{n+1}f(x)\,dx}_{\le\,f(n)}\le\sum_{n=N}^Mf(n)$$

and from ((3))

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=N}^Mf(n)&=f(N)+\sum_{n=N+1}^Mf(n)\\ &\leq f(N)+\sum_{n=N+1}^M\underbrace{\int_{n-1}^n f(x)\,dx}_{\ge\,f(n)}\\ &=f(N)+\int_N^M f(x)\,dx. \end{aligned}$$

Combining these two estimates yields

$$\int_N^{M+1}f(x)\,dx\le\sum_{n=N}^Mf(n)\le f(N)+\int_N^M f(x)\,dx.$$

Letting $M$ tend to infinity, the bounds in ((1)) and the result follow.

:::

::: {#o15 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The [harmonic series](wikipedia://en/142488)

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac 1 n$$

diverges because, using the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), its [antiderivative](wikipedia://en/2823), and the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696), we get

$$\int_1^M \frac 1 n\,dn = \ln n\Bigr|_1^M = \ln M \to\infty \quad\text{for }M\to\infty.$$

On the other hand, the series

$$\zeta(1+\varepsilon)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac1{n^{1+\varepsilon}}$$

(cf. [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809)) converges for every $\varepsilon > 0$, because by the [power rule](wikipedia://en/147912)

$$\int_1^M\frac1{n^{1+\varepsilon}}\,dn = \left. -\frac 1{\varepsilon n^\varepsilon} \right|_1^M= \frac 1 \varepsilon \left(1-\frac 1 {M^\varepsilon}\right) \le \frac 1 \varepsilon < \infty \quad\text{for all }M\ge1.$$

From ((1)) we get the upper estimate

$$\zeta(1+\varepsilon)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac 1 {n^{1+\varepsilon}} \le \frac{1 + \varepsilon}\varepsilon,$$

which can be compared with some of the [particular values of Riemann zeta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Particular_values_of_Riemann_zeta_function).

:::

## Borderline between divergence and convergence {#Borderline_between_divergence_and_convergence}

The above examples involving the harmonic series raise the question of whether there are monotone sequences such that $f(n)$ decreases to 0 faster than $1/n$ but slower than $1/n^{1+\varepsilon}$ in the sense that

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{1/n}=0 \quad\text{and}\quad \lim_{n\to\infty}\frac{f(n)}{1/n^{1+\varepsilon}}=\infty$$

for every $\varepsilon > 0$, and whether the corresponding series of the $f(n)$ still diverges. Once such a sequence is found, a similar question can be asked with $f(n)$ taking the role of either $1/n$ (if $f(n)$ diverges) or $1/n^{1+\varepsilon}$ (if $f(n)$ converges), and so on. In this way it is possible to investigate the borderline between divergence and convergence of infinite series.

Using the integral test for convergence, one can show (see below) that, for every [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $k$, the series

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="4"}

$$\sum_{n=N_k}^\infty\frac1{n\ln(n)\ln_2(n)\cdots \ln_{k-1}(n)\ln_k(n)} \tag{4}$$

:::

still diverges (cf. [proof that the sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_that_the_sum_of_the_reciprocals_of_the_primes_diverges) for $k = 1$) but

::: {#o18 .equation cue=numblk name="5"}

$$\sum_{n=N_k}^\infty\frac1{n\ln(n)\ln_2(n)\cdots\ln_{k-1}(n)(\ln_k(n))^{1+\varepsilon}} \tag{5}$$

:::

converges for every $\varepsilon > 0$. Here $\ln_{k}$ denotes the $k$-fold [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of the natural logarithm defined [recursively](wikipedia://en/25407) by

$$\ln_k(x)= \begin{cases} \ln(x)&\text{for }k=1,\\ \ln(\ln_{k-1}(x))&\text{for }k\ge2. \end{cases}$$

Furthermore, let $N_{k}$ denote the smallest natural number such that the $k$-fold composition is well-defined and $\ln_{k}(N_{k}) \ge 1$, i.e.

$$N_k\ge \underbrace{e^{e^{\cdot^{\cdot^{e}}}}}_{k\ e\text{s}}=e \uparrow\uparrow k$$

using [tetration](wikipedia://en/482379) or [Knuth's up-arrow notation](wikipedia://en/258733).

To see the divergence of the series ((4)) using the integral test, note that by repeated application of the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113)

$$\frac{d}{dx}\ln_{k+1}(x) =\frac{d}{dx}\ln(\ln_k(x)) =\frac1{\ln_k(x)}\frac{d}{dx}\ln_k(x) =\cdots =\frac1{x\ln(x)\cdots\ln_k(x)},$$

hence

$$\int_{N_k}^\infty\frac{dx}{x\ln(x)\cdots\ln_k(x)} =\ln_{k+1}(x)\bigr|_{N_k}^\infty=\infty.$$

To see the convergence of the series ((5)), note that by the [power rule](wikipedia://en/147912), the chain rule, and the above result,

$$-\frac{d}{dx}\frac1{\varepsilon(\ln_k(x))^\varepsilon} =\frac1{(\ln_k(x))^{1+\varepsilon}}\frac{d}{dx}\ln_k(x) =\cdots =\frac{1}{x\ln(x)\cdots\ln_{k-1}(x)(\ln_k(x))^{1+\varepsilon}},$$

hence

$$\int_{N_k}^\infty\frac{dx}{x\ln(x)\cdots\ln_{k-1}(x)(\ln_k(x))^{1+\varepsilon}} =-\frac1{\varepsilon(\ln_k(x))^\varepsilon}\biggr|_{N_k}^\infty<\infty$$

and ((1)) gives bounds for the infinite series in ((5)).
