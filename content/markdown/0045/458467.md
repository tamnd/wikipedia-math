# Conjugate element (field theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conjugate element"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in particular [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29), the **conjugate elements** or **algebraic conjugates** of an [algebraic element](wikipedia://en/186291) $\alpha$, over a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $L/K$, are the [roots](wikipedia://en/264210) of the [minimal polynomial](wikipedia://en/9667106) $p_{K,\alpha}(x)$ of $\alpha$ over $K$. Conjugate elements are commonly called **conjugates** in contexts where this is not ambiguous. Normally $\alpha$ itself is included in the set of conjugates of $\alpha$.

:::

Equivalently (if $L/K$ is normal), the conjugates of $\alpha$ are the images of $\alpha$ under the [field automorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_automorphism) of $L$ that leave fixed the elements of $K$. The equivalence of the two definitions is one of the starting points of [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316).

The concept generalizes [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation), since the algebraic conjugates over $\mathbb{R}$ of a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) are the number itself and its *complex conjugate*.

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

The cube [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) are:

$$\sqrt[3]{1} = \begin{cases}1 \\[3pt] -\frac{1}{2}+\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i \\[5pt] -\frac{1}{2}-\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}i \end{cases}$$

The latter two roots are conjugate elements in $\mathbf{Q}[i\sqrt{3}]$ with minimal polynomial

$$\left(x+\frac{1}{2}\right)^2+\frac{3}{4}=x^2+x+1.$$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

If *K* is given inside an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018) *C*, then the conjugates can be taken inside *C*. If no such *C* is specified, one can take the conjugates in some relatively small field *L*. The smallest possible choice for *L* is to take a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) over *K* of *p*<sub>*K*,*α*</sub>, containing *α*. If *L* is any [normal extension](wikipedia://en/929925) of *K* containing *α*, then by definition it already contains such a splitting field.

Given then a normal extension *L* of *K*, with [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/12673) Aut(*L*/*K*) = *G*, and containing *α*, any element *g*(*α*) for *g* in *G* will be a conjugate of *α*, since the [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) *g* sends roots of *p* to roots of *p*. Conversely any conjugate *β* of *α* is of this form: in other words, *G* acts [transitively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Types_of_actions) on the conjugates. This follows as *K*(*α*) is *K*-isomorphic to *K*(*β*) by irreducibility of the minimal polynomial, and any isomorphism of fields *F* and *F'* that maps polynomial *p* to *p'* can be extended to an isomorphism of the splitting fields of *p* over *F* and *p'* over *F'*, respectively.

In summary, the conjugate elements of *α* are found, in any normal extension *L* of *K* that contains *K*(*α*), as the set of elements *g*(*α*) for *g* in Aut(*L*/*K*). The number of repeats in that list of each element is the separable degree \[*L*:*K*(*α*)\]<sub>sep</sub>.

A theorem of [Kronecker](wikipedia://en/318450) states that if *α* is a nonzero [algebraic integer](wikipedia://en/98981) such that *α* and all of its conjugates in the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) have [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) at most 1, then *α* is a [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950). There are quantitative forms of this, stating more precisely bounds (depending on degree) on the largest absolute value of a conjugate that imply that an algebraic integer is a root of unity.
