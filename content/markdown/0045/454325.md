# Locally compact abelian group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Locally compact abelian group"}

In several [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) areas, including [harmonic analysis](wikipedia://en/14147), [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **locally compact abelian groups** are [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) which have a particularly convenient topology on them. For example, the group of integers (equipped with the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology)), or the real numbers or the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) (both with their usual topology) are locally compact abelian groups.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition and examples"}

## Definition and examples {#Definition_and_examples}

A [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) is called *[locally compact](wikipedia://en/1651204)* if the underlying topological space is [locally compact](wikipedia://en/48632) and [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637); the topological group is called *abelian* if the underlying group is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974).

Examples of locally compact [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) groups include:

- $\mathbb{R}^n$ for *n* a positive integer, with vector addition as group operation.
- The [positive real numbers](wikipedia://en/47172571) $\mathbb{R}^+$ with multiplication as operation. This group is isomorphic to $(\mathbb{R}, +)$ by the exponential map.
- Any finite abelian group, with the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology). By the [structure theorem for finite abelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Structure_theorem_for_finite_abelian_groups), all such groups are products of cyclic groups.
- The integers $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition, again with the discrete topology.
- The [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177), denoted $\mathbb{T}$ for [torus](wikipedia://en/74800). This is the group of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) of [modulus](wikipedia://en/991) 1. $\mathbb{T}$ is isomorphic as a topological group to the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$.
- The field $\mathbb{Q}_p$ of [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423) under addition, with the usual *p*-adic topology.

:::

## The dual group {#The_dual_group}

If $G$ is a locally compact *abelian* group, a [character](wikipedia://en/293418) of $G$ is a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) from $G$ with values in the [circle group](wikipedia://en/508177) $\mathbb{T}$. The set of all characters on $G$ can be made into a locally compact abelian group, called the *dual group* of $G$ and denoted $\widehat G$. The group operation on the dual group is given by pointwise multiplication of characters, the inverse of a character is its complex conjugate and the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) on the space of characters is that of [uniform convergence](wikipedia://en/50652) on [compact sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) (i.e., the [compact-open topology](wikipedia://en/726335), viewing $\widehat{G}$ as a subset of the space of all continuous functions from $G$ to $\mathbb{T}$.). This topology is in general not metrizable. However, if the group $G$ is a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) locally compact abelian group, then the dual group is metrizable.

This is analogous to the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) in linear algebra: just as for a vector space $V$ over a field $K$, the dual space is $\mathrm{Hom}(V, K)$, so too is the dual group $\mathrm{Hom}(G, \mathbb{T})$. More abstractly, these are both examples of [representable functors](wikipedia://en/844461), being represented respectively by $K$ and $\mathbb{T}$.

A group that is isomorphic (as topological groups) to its dual group is called *self-dual*. While the [reals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) and finite [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327) are self-dual, the group and the dual group are not *naturally* isomorphic, and should be thought of as two different groups.

### Examples of dual groups {#Examples_of_dual_groups}

The dual of $\mathbb{Z}$ is isomorphic to the circle group $\mathbb{T}$. A character on the [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) of integers $\mathbb{Z}$ under addition is determined by its value at the generator 1. Thus for any character $\chi$ on $\mathbb{Z}$, $\chi(n) = \chi(1)^n$. Moreover, this formula defines a character for any choice of $\chi(1)$ in $\mathbb{T}$. The topology of uniform convergence on compact sets is in this case the topology of [pointwise convergence](wikipedia://en/340198). This is the topology of the circle group inherited from the complex numbers.

The dual of $\mathbb{T}$ is canonically isomorphic with $\mathbb{Z}$. Indeed, a character on $\mathbb{T}$ is of the form $z\mapsto z^n$ for $n$ an integer. Since $\mathbb{T}$ is compact, the topology on the dual group is that of uniform convergence, which turns out to be the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology).

The group of real numbers $\mathbb{R}$, is isomorphic to its own dual; the characters on $\mathbb{R}$ are of the form $r\mapsto e^{i\theta r}$ for $\theta$ a real number. With these dualities, the version of the Fourier transform to be introduced next coincides with the classical [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) on $\mathbb{R}$.

Analogously, the group of $p$-adic numbers $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is isomorphic to its dual. (In fact, any finite extension of $\mathbb{Q}_p$ is also self-dual.) It follows that the [adeles](wikipedia://en/247978) are self-dual.

## Pontryagin duality {#Pontryagin_duality}

Pontryagin duality asserts that the [functor](wikipedia://en/10987)

$$G \mapsto \hat G$$

induces an [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051) between the [opposite](wikipedia://en/372399) of the category of locally compact abelian groups (with continuous morphisms) and itself:

$$LCA^{op} \stackrel \cong \longrightarrow LCA.$$

## Categorical properties {#Categorical_properties}

(Clausen 2017) shows that the category LCA of locally compact abelian groups measures, very roughly speaking, the difference between the integers and the reals. More precisely, the [algebraic K-theory](wikipedia://en/598500) spectrum of the category of locally compact abelian groups and the ones of **Z** and **R** lie in a [homotopy fiber sequence](wikipedia://en/19207725)

$$K(\mathbf Z) \to K(\mathbf R) \to K(LCA).$$
