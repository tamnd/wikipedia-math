# Operator algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Operator algebra"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **operator algebra** is an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) of [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) on a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752), with the multiplication given by the [composition of mappings](wikipedia://en/195947).

:::

The results obtained in the study of operator algebras are often phrased in [algebraic](wikipedia://en/18716923) terms, while the techniques used are often highly [analytic](wikipedia://en/48396).[^o2] Although the study of operator algebras is usually classified as a branch of functional analysis, it has direct applications to [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), [quantum statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/712450), [quantum information](wikipedia://en/25274), and [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267).

## Overview {#Overview}

Operator algebras can be used to study arbitrary sets of operators with little algebraic relation *simultaneously*. From this point of view, operator algebras can be regarded as a generalization of [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) of a single operator. In general, operator algebras are [non-commutative](wikipedia://en/24062516) [rings](wikipedia://en/48404).

An operator algebra is typically required to be [closed](wikipedia://en/293802) in a specified operator [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) inside the whole algebra of continuous linear operators. In particular, it is a set of operators with both algebraic and topological closure properties. In some disciplines such properties are [axiomatized](wikipedia://en/928) and algebras with certain topological structure become the subject of the research.

Though algebras of operators are studied in various contexts (for example, algebras of [pseudo-differential operators](wikipedia://en/973479) acting on spaces of [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29)), the term *operator algebra* is usually used in reference to algebras of [bounded operators](wikipedia://en/455961) on a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) or, even more specifically in reference to algebras of operators on a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), endowed with the [operator norm](wikipedia://en/246722) topology.

In the case of operators on a Hilbert space, the [Hermitian adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) map on operators gives a natural [involution](wikipedia://en/293450), which provides an additional algebraic structure that can be imposed on the algebra. In this context, the best studied examples are [self-adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-adjoint) operator algebras, meaning that they are closed under taking adjoints. These include [C\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/7184), [von Neumann algebras](wikipedia://en/294185), and [AW\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/39435930). C\*-algebras can be easily characterized abstractly by a condition relating the norm, involution and multiplication. Such abstractly defined C\*-algebras can be identified to a certain closed [subalgebra](wikipedia://en/45239#Subalgebras_for_algebras_over_a_ring_or_field) of the algebra of the continuous linear operators on a suitable Hilbert space. A similar result holds for von Neumann algebras.

[Commutative](wikipedia://en/245990) self-adjoint operator algebras can be regarded as the algebra of [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers)-valued continuous functions on a [locally compact space](wikipedia://en/48632), or that of [measurable functions](wikipedia://en/44055) on a [standard measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333). Thus, general operator algebras are often regarded as a noncommutative generalizations of these algebras, or the structure of the *base space* on which the functions are defined. This point of view is elaborated as the philosophy of [noncommutative geometry](wikipedia://en/295917), which tries to study various non-classical and/or pathological objects by noncommutative operator algebras.

Examples of operator algebras that are not self-adjoint include:

- [nest algebras](wikipedia://en/460461),
- many [commutative subspace lattice algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_subspace_lattice_algebra),
- many [limit algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_algebra).
