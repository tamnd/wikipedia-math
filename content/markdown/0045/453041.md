# Orthogonal functions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Orthogonal functions"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **orthogonal functions** belong to a [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) that is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) equipped with a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477). When the function space has an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) as the [domain](wikipedia://en/50263), the bilinear form may be the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of the product of functions over the interval:

$$\langle f,g\rangle = \int \overline{f(x)}g(x)\,dx .$$

:::

The functions $f$ and $g$ are [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/71675950) when this integral is zero, i.e. $\langle f, \, g \rangle = 0$ whenever $f \neq g$. As with a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of vectors in a finite-dimensional space, orthogonal functions can form an infinite basis for a function space. Conceptually, the above integral is the equivalent of a vector [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093); two vectors are mutually independent (orthogonal) if their dot-product is zero.

Suppose $\{ f_0, f_1, \ldots\}$ is a sequence of orthogonal functions of nonzero [*L*<sup>2</sup>-norms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L2-norm) $\left\| f_n \right\| _2 = \sqrt{\langle f_n, f_n \rangle} = \left(\int f_n ^2 \ dx \right) ^\frac{1}{2}$. It follows that the sequence $\left\{ f_n / \left\| f_n \right\| _2 \right\}$ is of functions of *L*<sup>2</sup>-norm one, forming an [orthonormal sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal_sequence). To have a defined *L*<sup>2</sup>-norm, the integral must be bounded, which restricts the functions to being [square-integrable](wikipedia://en/28680558).

## Trigonometric functions {#Trigonometric_functions}

Several sets of orthogonal functions have become standard bases for approximating functions. For example, the sine functions sin *nx* and sin *mx* are orthogonal on the interval $x \in (-\pi, \pi)$ when $m \neq n$ and *n* and *m* are positive integers. For then

$$2 \sin \left(mx\right) \sin \left(nx\right) = \cos \left(\left(m - n\right)x\right) - \cos\left(\left(m+n\right) x\right),$$

and the integral of the product of the two sine functions vanishes.[^o3] Together with cosine functions, these orthogonal functions may be assembled into a [trigonometric polynomial](wikipedia://en/591568) to approximate a given function on the interval with its [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038).

## Polynomials {#Polynomials}

If one begins with the [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) sequence $\left\{1, x, x^2, \dots\right\}$ on the interval $[-1,1]$ and applies the [Gram–Schmidt process](wikipedia://en/82361), then one obtains the [Legendre polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legendre_polynomial). Another collection of orthogonal polynomials are the [associated Legendre polynomials](wikipedia://en/1062015).

The study of orthogonal polynomials involves [weight functions](wikipedia://en/245523) $w(x)$ that are inserted in the bilinear form:

$$\langle f,g\rangle = \int w(x) f(x) g(x)\,dx .$$

For [Laguerre polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laguerre_polynomial) on $(0,\infty)$ the weight function is $w(x) = e^{-x}$.

Both physicists and probability theorists use [Hermite polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomial) on $(-\infty,\infty)$, where the weight function is $w(x) = e^{-x^2}$ or $w(x) = e^{- x^2/2}$.

[Chebyshev polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev_polynomial) are defined on $[-1,1]$ and use weights $w(x) = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1 - x^2}}$ or $w(x) = \sqrt{1 - x^2}$.

[Zernike polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zernike_polynomial) are defined on the [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738) and have orthogonality of both radial and angular parts.

## Binary-valued functions {#Binary-valued_functions}

[Walsh functions](wikipedia://en/380839) and [Haar wavelets](wikipedia://en/50905) are examples of orthogonal functions with discrete ranges.

## Rational functions {#Rational_functions}

*Image: ChebychevRational1.png.* Plot of the Chebyshev rational functions of order n=0,1,2,3 and 4 between x=0.01 and 100.

Legendre and Chebyshev polynomials provide orthogonal families for the interval \[−1, 1\] while occasionally orthogonal families are required on \[0, ∞). In this case it is convenient to apply the [Cayley transform](wikipedia://en/882010#Real_homography) first, to bring the argument into \[−1, 1\]. This procedure results in families of [rational](wikipedia://en/361210) orthogonal functions called [Legendre rational functions](wikipedia://en/6329366) and [Chebyshev rational functions](wikipedia://en/6112758).

## In differential equations {#In_differential_equations}

Solutions of linear [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) with boundary conditions can often be written as a weighted sum of orthogonal solution functions (a.k.a. [eigenfunctions](wikipedia://en/228601)), leading to [generalized Fourier series](wikipedia://en/245530).
