# Computer algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Computer algebra"}

*Image: RischIntegration.PNG.* [Symbolic integration](wikipedia://en/1652672) of the [algebraic function](wikipedia://en/974169) $f(x) = \tfrac{x}{\sqrt{x^{4} + 10x^{2} - 96x - 71}}$ using the computer algebra system *[Axiom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axiom_%28computer_algebra_system%29)*

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science),[^o2] **computer algebra**, also called **symbolic computation** or **algebraic computation**, is a scientific area that refers to the study and development of [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) and [software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software) for manipulating [mathematical expressions](wikipedia://en/609125) and other [mathematical objects](wikipedia://en/19453961). Although computer algebra could be considered a subfield of [scientific computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_computing), they are generally considered as distinct fields because scientific computing is usually based on [numerical computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_computation) with approximate [floating point numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_point_number), while symbolic computation emphasizes *exact* computation with expressions containing [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) that have no given value and are manipulated as symbols.

:::

[Software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software) applications that perform symbolic calculations are called *[computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427)*, with the term *system* alluding to the complexity of the main applications that include, at least, a method to represent mathematical data in a computer, a user [programming language](wikipedia://en/23015) (usually different from the language used for the implementation), a dedicated memory manager, a [user interface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/User_interface) for the input/output of mathematical expressions, and a large set of [routines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_%28computer_science%29) to perform usual operations, like simplification of expressions, [differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation_%28mathematics%29) using the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113), [polynomial factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_factorization), [indefinite integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integration), etc.

Computer algebra is widely used to experiment in mathematics and to design the formulas that are used in numerical programs. It is also used for complete scientific computations, when purely numerical methods fail, as in [public key cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key_cryptography), or for some [non-linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-linear) problems.

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

Some authors distinguish *computer algebra* from *symbolic computation*, using the latter name to refer to kinds of symbolic computation other than the computation with mathematical [formulas](wikipedia://en/164040). Some authors use *symbolic computation* for the computer-science aspect of the subject and *computer algebra* for the mathematical aspect.[^o5] In some languages, the name of the field is not a direct translation of its English name. Typically, it is called *calcul formel* in French, which means "formal computation". This name reflects the ties this field has with [formal methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_methods).

Symbolic computation has also been referred to, in the past, as *symbolic manipulation*, *algebraic manipulation*, *symbolic processing*, *symbolic mathematics*, or *symbolic algebra*, but these terms, which also refer to non-computational manipulation, are no longer used in reference to computer algebra.

:::

## Scientific community {#Scientific_community}

There is no [learned society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learned_society) that is specific to computer algebra, but this function is assumed by the [special interest group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_interest_group) of the [Association for Computing Machinery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Association_for_Computing_Machinery) named [SIGSAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SIGSAM) (Special Interest Group on Symbolic and Algebraic Manipulation).[^o7]

There are several annual conferences on computer algebra, the premier being [ISSAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISSAC) (International Symposium on Symbolic and Algebraic Computation), which is regularly sponsored by SIGSAM.[^o8]

There are several journals specializing in computer algebra, the top one being *[Journal of Symbolic Computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Symbolic_Computation)* founded in 1985 by [Bruno Buchberger](wikipedia://en/1924233).[^o9] There are also several other journals that regularly publish articles in computer algebra.[^o10]

## Computer science aspects {#Computer_science_aspects}

### Data representation {#Data_representation}

As [numerical software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_software) is highly efficient for approximate [numerical computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_computation), it is common, in computer algebra, to emphasize *exact* computation with exactly represented data. Such an exact representation implies that, even when the size of the output is small, the intermediate data generated during a computation may grow in an unpredictable way. This behavior is called *expression swell*.[^o13] To alleviate this problem, various methods are used in the representation of the data, as well as in the algorithms that manipulate them.[^o14]

#### Numbers {#Numbers}

The usual number systems used in [numerical computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_computation) are [floating point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_point) numbers and [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) of a fixed, bounded size. Neither of these is convenient for computer algebra, due to expression swell.[^o16] Therefore, the basic numbers used in computer algebra are the integers of the mathematicians, commonly represented by an unbounded signed sequence of [digits](wikipedia://en/280582) in some [base of numeration](wikipedia://en/701207), usually the largest base allowed by the [machine word](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Machine_word). These integers allow one to define the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), which are [irreducible fractions](wikipedia://en/14822) of two integers.

Programming an efficient implementation of the arithmetic operations is a hard task. Therefore, most free [computer algebra systems](wikipedia://en/54427), and some commercial ones such as [Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica) and [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29),[^o17][^o18] use the [GMP library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GNU_Multiple_Precision_Arithmetic_Library), which is thus a *de facto* standard.

#### Expressions {#Expressions}

*Image: Cassidy.1985.015.gif.* Representation of the expression $(8 - 6) \times (3 + 1)$ as a [Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisp_%28programming_language%29) tree, from a 1985 Master's Thesis[^o21]

Except for [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690) and [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), every [mathematical expression](wikipedia://en/609125) may be viewed as the symbol of an operator followed by a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) of operands. In computer-algebra software, the expressions are usually represented in this way. This representation is very flexible, and many things that seem not to be mathematical expressions at first glance, may be represented and manipulated as such. For example, an [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) may be considered as an expression with "=" as its leading operator, and a matrix may be represented as an expression with "matrix" as an operator and its rows as operands.

Even programs may be considered and represented as expressions with operator "procedure" and, at least, two operands, the list of parameters and the body, which is itself an expression with "body" as an operator and a sequence of instructions as operands. Conversely, any mathematical expression may be viewed as a program. For example, the expression $a + b$ may be viewed as a program for the addition, with $a$ and $b$ as parameters. Executing this program consists of *evaluating* the expression for given values of $a$ and $b$; if they are not given any values, then the result of the evaluation is simply its input.

This process of delayed evaluation is fundamental in computer algebra. For example, the operator "=" of equation is also, in most computer algebra systems, the name of the program of the equality test: normally, the evaluation of an equation results in an equation, but, when an equality test is needed, either explicitly asked by the user through an "evaluation to a Boolean" command, or automatically started by the system in the case of a test inside a program, then the evaluation to a Boolean result is executed.

As the size of the operands of an expression is unpredictable and may change during a working session, the sequence of the operands is usually represented as a sequence of either [pointers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pointer_%28computer_programming%29) (like in [Macsyma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macsyma))[^o22] or entries in a [hash table](wikipedia://en/13833) (like in [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29)).

:::

### Simplification {#Simplification}

The raw application of the basic rules of [differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) with respect to $x$ on the expression $a^{x}$ gives the result

$$x\cdot a^{x-1}\cdot 0 + a^x\cdot \left (1\cdot \log a + x\cdot \frac{0}{a} \right).$$

A simpler expression than this is generally desired, and simplification is needed when working with general expressions. This simplification is normally done through [rewriting rules](wikipedia://en/415847).[^o25] There are several classes of rewriting rules to be considered. The simplest are rules that always reduce the size of the expression, like $E - E \to 0$ or $\sin(0) \to 0$. They are systematically applied in computer algebra systems.

A difficulty occurs with [associative operations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative_operation) like addition and multiplication. The standard way to deal with associativity is to consider that addition and multiplication have an arbitrary number of operands; that is, that $a + b + c$ is represented as $''+''(a, b, c)$. Thus $a + (b + c)$ and $(a + b) + c$ are both simplified to $''+''(a, b, c)$, which is displayed $a + b + c$. In the case of expressions such as $a - b + c$, the simplest way is to systematically rewrite $-E$, $E - F$, $E/F$ as, respectively, $(-1)\cdot E$, $E + (-1)\cdot F$, $E\cdot F^{-1}$. In other words, in the internal representation of the expressions, there is no subtraction nor division nor unary minus, outside the representation of the numbers.

Another difficulty occurs with the [commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) of addition and multiplication. The problem is to quickly recognize the [like terms](wikipedia://en/19222837) in order to combine or cancel them. Testing every pair of terms is costly with very long sums and products. To address this, [Macsyma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macsyma) sorts the operands of sums and products into an order that places like terms in consecutive places, allowing easy detection. In [Maple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29), a [hash function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hash_function) is designed for generating collisions when like terms are entered, allowing them to be combined as soon as they are introduced. This allows subexpressions that appear several times in a computation to be immediately recognized and stored only once. This saves memory and speeds up computation by avoiding repetition of the same operations on identical expressions.

Some rewriting rules sometimes increase and sometimes decrease the size of the expressions to which they are applied. This is the case for the [distributive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity) or [trigonometric identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_identity#Product-to-sum_and_sum-to-product_identities). For example, the distributive law allows rewriting $(x+1)^4 \rightarrow x^4+4x^3+6x^2+4x+1$ and $(x-1)(x^4+x^3+x^2+x+1) \rightarrow x^5-1.$ As there is no way to make a good general choice of applying or not such a rewriting rule, such rewriting is done only when explicitly invoked by the user. For the distributive law, the computer function that applies this rewriting rule is typically called "expand". The reverse rewriting rule, called "factor", requires a non-trivial algorithm, which is thus a key function in computer algebra systems (see [Polynomial factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_factorization)).

:::

## Mathematical aspects {#Mathematical_aspects}

Some fundamental mathematical questions arise when one wants to manipulate [mathematical expressions](wikipedia://en/609125) in a computer. We consider mainly the case of the [multivariate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_polynomial) [rational fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_fraction). This is not a real restriction, because, as soon as the [irrational functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_function) appearing in an expression are simplified, they are usually considered as new indeterminates. For example,

$$(\sin(x+y)^2+ \log(z^2-5))^3$$

is viewed as a polynomial in $\sin(x+y)$ and $\log(z^2-5)$.

::: {#o28 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Syntactic equality"}

### Equality {#Equality}

There are two notions of equality for [mathematical expressions](wikipedia://en/609125). *Syntactic equality* is the equality of their representation in a computer. This is easy to test in a program. *Semantic equality* is when two expressions represent the same mathematical object, as in

$$(x+y)^2=x^2+2xy+y^2.$$

It is known from [Richardson's theorem](wikipedia://en/13463844) that there may not exist an algorithm that decides whether two expressions representing numbers are semantically equal if exponentials and logarithms are allowed in the expressions. Accordingly, (semantic) equality may be tested only on some classes of expressions such as the [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) and [rational fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_fraction).

To test the equality of two expressions, instead of designing specific algorithms, it is usual to put expressions in some *[canonical form](wikipedia://en/515096)* or to put their difference in a *normal form*, and to test the syntactic equality of the result.

In computer algebra, "canonical form" and "normal form" are not synonymous.[^o29] A *canonical form* is such that two expressions in canonical form are semantically equal if and only if they are syntactically equal, while a *normal form* is such that an expression in normal form is semantically zero only if it is syntactically zero. In other words, zero has a unique representation as an expression in normal form.

Normal forms are usually preferred in computer algebra for several reasons. Firstly, canonical forms may be more costly to compute than normal forms. For example, to put a polynomial in canonical form, one has to expand every product through the [distributive law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributivity), while it is not necessary with a normal form (see below). Secondly, it may be the case, like for expressions involving radicals, that a canonical form, if it exists, depends on some arbitrary choices and that these choices may be different for two expressions that have been computed independently. This may make the use of a canonical form impractical.

:::

## History {#History}

### Human-driven computer algebra {#Human-driven_computer_algebra}

Early computer algebra systems, such as the [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC) at the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania), relied on [human computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_%28occupation%29) or programmers to reprogram it between calculations, manipulate its many physical modules (or panels), and feed its IBM card reader.[^o33] Female mathematicians handled the majority of ENIAC programming human-guided computation: [Jean Jennings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Bartik), [Marlyn Wescoff](wikipedia://en/5292366), [Ruth Lichterman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Teitelbaum), [Betty Snyder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Holberton), [Frances Bilas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Spence), and [Kay McNulty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathleen_Antonelli) led said efforts.[^o34]

### Foundations and early applications {#Foundations_and_early_applications}

In 1960, [John McCarthy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_McCarthy_%28computer_scientist%29) explored an extension of [primitive recursive functions](wikipedia://en/24829) for computing symbolic expressions through the [Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lisp_%28programming_language%29) programming language while at the [Massachusetts Institute of Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Massachusetts_Institute_of_Technology).[^o36] Though his series on "Recursive functions of symbolic expressions and their computation by machine" remained incomplete,[^o37] McCarthy and his contributions to artificial intelligence programming and computer algebra via Lisp helped establish [Project MAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIT_Computer_Science_and_Artificial_Intelligence_Laboratory) at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and the organization that later became the [Stanford AI Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University_centers_and_institutes) (SAIL) at [Stanford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanford_University), whose competition facilitated significant development in computer algebra throughout the late 20th century.

Early efforts at symbolic computation, in the 1960s and 1970s, faced challenges surrounding the inefficiency of long-known algorithms when ported to computer algebra systems.[^o38] Predecessors to Project MAC, such as [ALTRAN](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALTRAN), sought to overcome algorithmic limitations through advancements in hardware and interpreters, while later efforts turned towards software optimization.[^o39]

### Historic problems {#Historic_problems}

A large part of the work of researchers in the field consisted of revisiting classical [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) to increase its [effectiveness](wikipedia://en/1139338) while developing [efficient algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_efficiency) for use in computer algebra. An example of this type of work is the computation of [polynomial greatest common divisors](wikipedia://en/9927028), a task required to simplify fractions and an essential component of computer algebra. Classical algorithms for this computation, such as [Euclid's algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377), proved inefficient over infinite fields; algorithms from [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) faced similar struggles.[^o41] Thus, researchers turned to discovering methods of reducing polynomials (such as those over a [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) or a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546)) to a variant efficiently computable via a Euclidean algorithm.
