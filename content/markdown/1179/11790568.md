# Percolation threshold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Percolation threshold"}

The **percolation threshold** is a mathematical concept in [percolation theory](wikipedia://en/187805) that describes the formation of long-range connectivity in [random](wikipedia://en/19196523) systems. Below the threshold a giant [connected component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29) does not exist; while above it, there exists a giant component of the order of system size. In engineering and [coffee making](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coffee_making), percolation represents the flow of fluids through [porous media](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porous_media), but in the mathematics and physics worlds it generally refers to simplified [lattice models](wikipedia://en/291472) of random systems or networks ([graphs](wikipedia://en/325806)), and the nature of the connectivity in them. The percolation threshold is the critical value of the occupation probability *p*, or more generally a critical surface for a group of parameters *p*<sub>1</sub>, *p*<sub>2</sub>, ..., such that infinite connectivity (*[percolation](wikipedia://en/299184)*) first occurs.[^o2]

:::

## Percolation models {#Percolation_models}

The most common percolation model is to take a regular lattice, like a [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853), and make it into a random network by randomly "occupying" sites (vertices) or bonds (edges) with a statistically independent probability *p*. At a critical threshold *p<sub>c</sub>*, large clusters and long-range connectivity first appear, and this is called the **percolation threshold**. Depending on the method for obtaining the random network, one distinguishes between the [site percolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Site_percolation) threshold and the [bond percolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bond_percolation) threshold. More general systems have several probabilities *p*<sub>1</sub>, *p*<sub>2</sub>, etc., and the transition is characterized by a *critical surface* or *manifold*. One can also consider continuum systems, such as overlapping disks and spheres placed randomly, or the negative space ([*Swiss-cheese*](wikipedia://en/11415193) models).

To understand the threshold, you can consider a quantity such as the probability that there is a continuous path from one boundary to another along occupied sites or bonds—that is, within a single cluster. For example, one can consider a square system, and ask for the probability *P* that there is a path from the top boundary to the bottom boundary. As a function of the occupation probability *p*, one finds a sigmoidal plot that goes from *P=0* at *p=0* to *P=1* at *p=1*. The larger the square is compared to the lattice spacing, the sharper the transition will be. When the system size goes to infinity, *P(p)* will be a [step function](wikipedia://en/170939) at the threshold value *p<sub>c</sub>*. For finite large systems, *P(p<sub>c</sub>)* is a constant whose value depends upon the shape of the system; for the square system discussed above, *P(p<sub>c</sub>)=$\tfrac{1}{2}$* exactly for any lattice by a simple symmetry argument.

There are other signatures of the critical threshold. For example, the size distribution (number of clusters of size *s*) drops off as a power-law for large *s* at the threshold, *n<sub>s</sub>(p<sub>c</sub>) ~ s<sup>−τ</sup>*, where τ is a dimension-dependent [percolation critical exponents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percolation_critical_exponents). For an infinite system, the critical threshold corresponds to the first point (as *p* increases) where the size of the clusters become infinite.

In the systems described so far, it has been assumed that the occupation of a site or bond is completely random—this is the so-called *[Bernoulli](wikipedia://en/102651) percolation.* For a continuum system, random occupancy corresponds to the points being placed by a [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process). Further variations involve correlated percolation, such as percolation clusters related to Ising and Potts models of ferromagnets, in which the bonds are put down by the Fortuin–[Kasteleyn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pieter_Kasteleyn) method.[^o4] In *bootstrap* or *k-sat* percolation, sites and/or bonds are first occupied and then successively culled from a system if a site does not have at least *k* neighbors. Another important model of percolation, in a different [universality class](wikipedia://en/353890) altogether, is [directed percolation](wikipedia://en/6988866), where connectivity along a bond depends upon the direction of the flow. Another variation of recent interest is [Explosive Percolation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explosive_Percolation), whose thresholds are listed on that page.

Over the last several decades, a tremendous amount of work has gone into finding exact and approximate values of the percolation thresholds for a variety of these systems. Exact thresholds are only known for certain two-dimensional lattices that can be broken up into a self-dual array, such that under a triangle-triangle transformation, the system remains the same. Studies using numerical methods have led to numerous improvements in algorithms and several theoretical discoveries.

Simple duality in two dimensions implies that all fully triangulated lattices (e.g., the triangular, union jack, cross dual, martini dual and asanoha or 3-12 dual, and the Delaunay triangulation) all have site thresholds of $\tfrac{1}{2}$, and self-dual lattices (square, martini-B) have bond thresholds of $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

The notation such as (4,8<sup>2</sup>) comes from [Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976) and [Shephard](wikipedia://en/33354532),[^o5] and indicates that around a given vertex, going in the clockwise direction, one encounters first a square and then two octagons. Besides the eleven [Archimedean lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archimedean_lattices) composed of regular polygons with every site equivalent, many other more complicated lattices with sites of different classes have been studied.

Error bars in the last digit or digits are shown by numbers in parentheses. Thus, 0.729724(3) signifies 0.729724 ± 0.000003, and 0.74042195(80) signifies 0.74042195 ± 0.00000080. The error bars variously represent one or two standard deviations in net error (including statistical and expected systematic error), or an empirical [confidence interval](wikipedia://en/280911), depending upon the source.

## Percolation on networks {#Percolation_on_networks}

For a random [tree-like](wikipedia://en/48560) [network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Network_science) (i.e., a connected network with no cycle) without degree-degree correlation, it can be shown that such network can have a [giant component](wikipedia://en/2379792), and the percolation threshold (transmission probability) is given by

$$p_c = \frac{1}{g_1'(1)} = \frac{\langle k \rangle}{\langle k^2 \rangle - \langle k \rangle}.$$

Where $g_1(z)$ is the [generating function](wikipedia://en/2129782#Generating_functions_method) corresponding to the [excess degree distribution](wikipedia://en/2129782#Generating_functions_method), ${\langle k \rangle}$ is the average degree of the network and ${\langle k^2 \rangle}$ is the second [moment](wikipedia://en/368684) of the [degree distribution](wikipedia://en/2129782). So, for example, for an [ER network](wikipedia://en/10477221), since the degree distribution is a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144), where${\langle k^2 \rangle = \langle k \rangle^2 + \langle k \rangle},$ the threshold is at $p_c = {\langle k \rangle}^{-1}$.

In networks with low [clustering](wikipedia://en/1457636), $0 < C \ll 1$, the critical point gets scaled by $(1-C)^{-1}$ such that:[^o7]

$$p_c = \frac{1}{1-C}\frac{1}{g_1'(1)}.$$

This indicates that for a given degree distribution, the clustering leads to a larger percolation threshold, mainly because for a fixed number of links, the clustering structure reinforces the core of the network with the price of diluting the global connections. For networks with high clustering, strong clustering could induce the core–periphery structure, in which the core and periphery might percolate at different critical points, and the above approximate treatment is not applicable.[^o8]

### Inhomogeneous 2-uniform lattice {#Inhomogeneous_2-uniform_lattice}

*Image: 2uniformLattice37.pdf.* 2-uniform lattice #37

This figure shows something similar to the 2-uniform lattice #37, except the polygons are not all regular—there is a rectangle in the place of the two squares—and the size of the polygons is changed. This lattice is in the isoradial representation in which each polygon is inscribed in a circle of unit radius. The two squares in the 2-uniform lattice must now be represented as a single rectangle in order to satisfy the isoradial condition. The lattice is shown by black edges, and the dual lattice by red dashed lines. The green circles show the isoradial constraint on both the original and dual lattices. The yellow polygons highlight the three types of polygons on the lattice, and the pink polygons highlight the two types of polygons on the dual lattice. The lattice has vertex types ($\tfrac{1}{2}$)(3<sup>3</sup>,4<sup>2</sup>) + ($\tfrac{1}{2}$)(3,4,6,4), while the dual lattice has vertex types ($\tfrac{1}{15}$)(4<sup>6</sup>)+($\tfrac{6}{15}$)(4<sup>2</sup>,5<sup>2</sup>)+($\tfrac{2}{15}$)(5<sup>3</sup>)+($\tfrac{6}{15}$)(5<sup>2</sup>,4). The critical point is where the longer bonds (on both the lattice and dual lattice) have occupation probability p = 2 sin (π/18) = 0.347296... which is the bond percolation threshold on a triangular lattice, and the shorter bonds have occupation probability 1 − 2 sin(π/18) = 0.652703..., which is the bond percolation on a hexagonal lattice. These results follow from the isoradial condition[^o440] but also follow from applying the star-triangle transformation to certain stars on the honeycomb lattice. Finally, it can be generalized to having three different probabilities in the three different directions, p<sub>1</sub>, p<sub>2</sub> and *p*<sub>3</sub> for the long bonds, and 1 − *p*<sub>1</sub>, 1 − *p*<sub>2</sub>, and 1 − *p*<sub>3</sub> for the short bonds, where *p*<sub>1</sub>, *p*<sub>2</sub> and *p*<sub>3</sub> satisfy the critical surface for the inhomogeneous triangular lattice.

For disks, $n_c = 4 r^2 N / L^2$ equals the critical number of disks per unit area, measured in units of the diameter $2r$, where $N$ is the number of objects and $L$ is the system size

For disks, $\eta_c = \pi r^2 N / L^2 = (\pi/4) n_c$ equals critical total disk area.

$4 \eta_c$ gives the number of disk centers within the circle of influence (radius 2 r).

$r_c = L \sqrt{\frac{\eta_c}{\pi N}} = \frac{L}{2} \sqrt{\frac{n_c}{N}}$ is the critical disk radius.

$\eta_c = \pi a b N / L^2$ for ellipses of semi-major and semi-minor axes of a and b, respectively. Aspect ratio $\epsilon = a / b$ with $a > b$.

$\eta_c = \ell m N / L^2$ for rectangles of dimensions $\ell$ and $m$. Aspect ratio $\epsilon = \ell / m$ with $\ell > m$.

$\eta_c = \pi \times N / (4 L^2 (x-2))$ for power-law distributed disks with $\hbox{Prob(radius}\ge R) = R^{-x}$, $R \ge 1$.

$\phi_c = 1 - e^{-\eta_c}$ equals critical area fraction.

For disks, Ref.[^o93] use $\phi_c = 1 - e^{-\pi \times / 2}$ where $x$ is the density of disks of radius $1/\sqrt{2}$.

$n_c = \ell^2 N / L^2$ equals number of objects of maximum length $\ell = 2 a$ per unit area.

For ellipses, $n_c = (4 \epsilon / \pi)\eta_c$

For void percolation, $\phi_c = e^{-\eta_c}$ is the critical void fraction.

### Site-Bond Directed Percolation {#Site-Bond_Directed_Percolation}

p<sub>b</sub> = bond threshold

p<sub>s</sub> = site threshold

Site-bond percolation is equivalent to having different probabilities of connections:

P<sub>0</sub> = probability that no sites are connected = (1-p<sub>s</sub>) + p<sub>s</sub>(1-p<sub>b</sub>)<sup>2</sup>

P<sub>2</sub> = probability that exactly one descendant is connected to the upper vertex (two connected together) = p<sub>s</sub> p<sub>b</sub> (1-p<sub>b</sub>)

P<sub>3</sub> = probability that both descendants are connected to the original vertex (all three connected together)= p<sub>s</sub> p<sub>b</sub><sup>2</sup>

### Isotropic/Directed Percolation {#Isotropic/Directed_Percolation}

Here we have a cross between ordinary bond percolation (OP) and directed percolation (DP)[^o376]. When p<sub>d</sub> = 0 or 1, we have pure DP, while when p<sub>d</sub> = 1/2 we have the random diode model or essentially OP, with the threshold twice the OP value. ### Exact critical manifolds of inhomogeneous systems {#Exact_critical_manifolds_of_inhomogeneous_systems}

Inhomogeneous triangular lattice bond percolation[^o399]

$$1 - p_1 - p_2 - p_3 + p_1 p_2 p_3 = 0$$

Inhomogeneous honeycomb lattice bond percolation = kagome lattice site percolation[^o399]

$$1 - p_1 p_2 - p_1 p_3 - p_2 p_3+ p_1 p_2 p_3 = 0$$

Inhomogeneous (3,12^2) lattice, site percolation[^o386][^o348]

$1 - 3(s_1s_2)^2 + (s_1s_2)^3 = 0,$ or $s_1 s_2 = 1 - 2 \sin(\pi/18)$

Inhomogeneous union-jack lattice, site percolation with probabilities $p_1, p_2, p_3, p_4$[^o349]

$$p_3 = 1 - p_1; \qquad p_4 = 1 - p_2$$

Inhomogeneous martini lattice, bond percolation[^o443][^o350]

$$1 - (p_1 p_2 r_3 + p_2 p_3 r_1 + p_1 p_3 r_2) - (p_1 p_2 r_1 r_2 + p_1 p_3 r_1 r_3 + p_2 p_3 r_2 r_3) + p_1 p_2 p_3 ( r_1 r_2 + r_1 r_3 + r_2 r_3) + r_1 r_2 r_3 (p_1 p_2 + p_1 p_3 + p_2 p_3) - 2 p_1 p_2 p_3 r_1 r_2 r_3 = 0$$

Inhomogeneous martini lattice, site percolation. *r* = site in the star

$$1 - r (p_1 p_2 + p_1 p_3 + p_2 p_3 - p_1 p_2 p_3) = 0$$

Inhomogeneous martini-A (3–7) lattice, bond percolation. Left side (top of "A" to bottom): $r_2,\ p_1$. Right side: $r_1, \ p_2$. Cross bond: $\ r_3$.

$$1 - p_1 r_2 - p_2 r_1 - p_1 p_2 r_3 - p_1 r_1 r_3 - p_2 r_2 r_3 + p_1 p_2 r_1 r_3 + p_1 p_2 r_2 r_3 + p_1 r_1 r_2 r_3+ p_2 r_1 r_2 r_3 - p_1 p_2 r_1 r_2 r_3 = 0$$

Inhomogeneous martini-B (3–5) lattice, bond percolation

Inhomogeneous martini lattice with outside enclosing triangle of bonds, probabilities $y, x, z$ from inside to outside, bond percolation[^o350]

$$1 - 3 z + z^3-(1-z^2) [3 x^2 y (1 + y - y^2)(1 + z) + x^3 y^2 (3 - 2 y)(1 + 2 z) ] = 0$$

Inhomogeneous checkerboard lattice, bond percolation[^o432][^o351]

$$1 - (p_1 p_2 + p_1 p_3 + p_1 p_4 + p_2 p_3 + p_2 p_4 + p_3 p_4) + p_1 p_2 p_3 + p_1 p_2 p_4 + p_1 p_3 p_4 + p_2 p_3 p_4 = 0$$

Inhomogeneous bow-tie lattice, bond percolation[^o431][^o351]

$$1 - (p_1 p_2 + p_1 p_3 + p_1 p_4 + p_2 p_3 + p_2 p_4 + p_3 p_4) + p_1 p_2 p_3 + p_1 p_2 p_4 + p_1 p_3 p_4 + p_2 p_3 p_4 - u(1 - p_1 p_2 - p_3 p_4 + p_1 p_2 p_3 p_4) = 0$$

where $p_1, p_2, p_3, p_4$ are the four bonds around the square and $u$ is the diagonal bond connecting the vertex between bonds $p_4, p_1$ and $p_2, p_3$.

### Rigidity percolation {#Rigidity_percolation}

Assuming a finite graph with unbending bonds, rigidity percolation refers to a situation where the entire graph is rigid everywhere with respect to shear forces being put on it.[^o354][^o355][^o356][^o357] Another way to say this is that constraints are sufficient to eliminate all zero-frequency vibrational modes, transforming a mechanically floppy network into one capable of supporting stress[^o358].

The [Geiringer–Laman theorem](wikipedia://en/66490111) gives a combinatorial characterization of generically rigid graphs in 2-dimensional Euclidean space

Generic lattices have bonds of different lengths, and can be made by randomly displacing the sites of a regular lattice.
