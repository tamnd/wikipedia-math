# Q-Gaussian distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Q-Gaussian distribution"}

The ***q*-Gaussian** is a probability distribution arising from the maximization of the [Tsallis entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsallis_entropy) under appropriate constraints. It is one example of a [Tsallis distribution](wikipedia://en/22342404). The *q*-Gaussian is a generalization of the Gaussian in the same way that Tsallis entropy is a generalization of standard [Boltzmann–Gibbs entropy](wikipedia://en/4701125) or [Shannon entropy](wikipedia://en/15445).[^o2] The [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) is recovered as *q* → 1.

:::

The *q*-Gaussian has been applied to problems in the fields of [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481), [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology), [anatomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatomy), [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy), [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [finance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finance), and [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488). The distribution is often favored for its [heavy tails](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heavy_tails) in comparison to the Gaussian for 1 \< *q* \< 3. For $q <1$ the *q*-Gaussian distribution is the PDF of a bounded [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685). This makes in biology and other domains[^o3] the *q*-Gaussian distribution more suitable than Gaussian distribution to model the effect of external stochasticity. A generalized [*q*-analog](wikipedia://en/1619396) of the classical [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406)[^o4] was proposed in 2008, in which the independence constraint for the [i.i.d. variables](wikipedia://en/30876902) is relaxed to an extent defined by the *q* parameter, with independence being recovered as *q* → 1. However, a proof of such a theorem is still lacking.[^o5]

In the heavy tail regions, the distribution is equivalent to the [Student's *t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) with a direct mapping between *q* and the [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321606). A practitioner using one of these distributions can therefore parameterize the same distribution in two different ways. The choice of the *q*-Gaussian form may arise if the system is [non-extensive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonextensive_entropy), or if there is lack of a connection to small samples sizes.

## Characterization {#Characterization}

### Probability density function {#Probability_density_function}

The standard *q*-Gaussian has the probability density function [^o4]

$$f(x) = {\sqrt{\beta} \over C_q} e_q(-\beta x^2)$$

where

$$e_q(x) = [1+(1-q)x]_+^{1 \over 1-q}$$

is the [*q*-exponential](wikipedia://en/3599748#q-exponential) and the normalization factor $C_q$ is given by

$$C_q = {{2 \sqrt{\pi} \Gamma\left({1 \over 1-q}\right)} \over {(3-q) \sqrt{1-q} \Gamma\left({3-q \over 2(1-q)}\right)}} \text{ for } -\infty < q < 1$$

$$C_q = \sqrt{\pi} \text{ for } q = 1 \,$$

$$C_q = { {\sqrt{\pi} \Gamma\left({3-q \over 2(q-1)}\right)} \over {\sqrt{q-1} \Gamma\left({1 \over q-1}\right)}} \text{ for }1 < q < 3 .$$

Note that for $q <1$ the *q*-Gaussian distribution is the PDF of a bounded [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685).

### Cumulative density function {#Cumulative_density_function}

For $1 < q < 3$ cumulative density function is [^o10]

$$F(x)= \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{q-1}\, \Gamma\left({1 \over q-1}\right) x \sqrt{\beta} \, {}_2F_1\left (\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{q-1};\tfrac{3}{2};-(q-1)\beta x^2 \right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\, \Gamma\left({3-q \over 2(q-1)}\right)} ,$$

where ${}_2F_1(a,b;c;z)$ is the [hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/2055223). As the hypergeometric function is defined for $|z| < 1$ but *x* is unbounded, [Pfaff transformation](wikipedia://en/2055223#Fractional_linear_transformations) could be used.

For $q<1$,

$$F(x)= \begin{cases} 0 & x < - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta(1-q)}}, \\ \frac{1}{2} + \frac{\sqrt{1-q}\, \Gamma\left({5-3q \over 2(1-q)}\right) x \sqrt{\beta} \, {}_2F_1\left (\tfrac{1}{2},\tfrac{1}{q-1};\tfrac{3}{2};-(q-1)\beta x^2 \right)}{\sqrt{\pi}\, \Gamma\left({2-q \over 1-q}\right)} & - \frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta(1-q)}} < x < \frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta(1-q)}}, \\ 1 & x > \frac{1}{\sqrt{\beta(1-q)}}. \end{cases}$$

:::

## Entropy {#Entropy}

Just as the [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) is the maximum [information entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy) distribution for fixed values of the first moment $\operatorname{E}(X)$ and second moment $\operatorname{E}(X^2)$ (with the fixed zeroth moment $\operatorname{E}(X^0)=1$ corresponding to the normalization condition), the *q*-Gaussian distribution is the maximum [Tsallis entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsallis_entropy) distribution for fixed values of these three moments.

## Related distributions {#Related_distributions}

### Student's *t*-distribution {#Student's_t-distribution}

While it can be justified by an interesting alternative form of entropy, statistically it is a scaled reparametrization of the [Student's *t*-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) introduced by W. Gosset in 1908 to describe small-sample statistics. In Gosset's original presentation the degrees of freedom parameter *ν* was constrained to be a positive integer related to the sample size, but it is readily observed that Gosset's density function is valid for all real values of *ν*.[^o14] The scaled reparametrization introduces the alternative parameters *q* and *β* which are related to *ν*.

Given a Student's *t*-distribution with *ν* degrees of freedom, the equivalent *q*-Gaussian has

$$q = \frac{\nu+3}{\nu+1}\text{ with }\beta = \frac{1}{3-q}$$

with inverse

$$\nu = \frac{3-q}{q-1},\text{ but only if }\beta = \frac{1}{3-q}.$$

Whenever $\beta \ne {1 \over {3-q}}$, the function is simply a scaled version of Student's *t*-distribution.

It is sometimes argued that the distribution is a generalization of Student's *t*-distribution to negative and or non-integer degrees of freedom. However, the theory of Student's *t*-distribution extends trivially to all real degrees of freedom, where the support of the distribution is now [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) rather than infinite in the case of *ν* \< 0.

### Three-parameter version {#Three-parameter_version}

As with many distributions centered on zero, the *q*-Gaussian can be trivially extended to include a location parameter *μ*. The density then becomes defined by

$${\sqrt{\beta} \over C_q} e_q({-\beta (x-\mu)^2}) .$$

## Generating random deviates {#Generating_random_deviates}

The [Box–Muller transform](wikipedia://en/60758) has been generalized to allow random sampling from *q*-Gaussians.[^o17][^o18] The standard Box–Muller technique generates pairs of independent normally distributed variables from equations of the following form.

$$Z_1 = \sqrt{-2 \ln(U_1)} \cos(2 \pi U_2)$$

$$Z_2 = \sqrt{-2 \ln(U_1)} \sin(2 \pi U_2)$$

The generalized Box–Muller technique can generates pairs of *q*-Gaussian deviates that are not independent. In practice, only a single deviate will be generated from a pair of uniformly distributed variables. The following formula will generate deviates from a *q*-Gaussian with specified parameter *q* and $\beta = {1 \over {3-q}}$

$$Z = \sqrt{-2 \text{ ln}_{q'}(U_1)} \text{ cos}(2 \pi U_2)$$

where $\text{ ln}_q$ is the [*q*-logarithm](wikipedia://en/3599748#q-logarithm) and $q' = { {1+q} \over {3-q}}$

These deviates can be transformed to generate deviates from an arbitrary *q*-Gaussian by

$$Z' = \mu + {Z \over \sqrt{\beta (3-q)}}$$

## Applications {#Applications}

### Physics {#Physics}

It has been shown that the momentum distribution of cold atoms in dissipative optical lattices is a *q*-Gaussian.[^o22]

The *q*-Gaussian distribution is also obtained as the asymptotic [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of the position of the unidimensional motion of a mass subject to two forces: a deterministic force of the type $F_1(x) = - 2 x/(1-x^2)$ (determining an infinite potential well) and a stochastic white noise force $F_2(t)= \sqrt{2(1-q)} \xi(t)$, where $\xi(t)$ is a [white noise](wikipedia://en/46182). Note that in the overdamped/small mass approximation the above-mentioned convergence fails for $q <0$, as recently shown.[^o23]

### Finance {#Finance}

Financial return distributions in the New York Stock Exchange, NASDAQ and elsewhere have been interpreted as *q*-Gaussians.[^o25][^o26]
