# Splitting lemma

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Splitting lemma"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and more specifically in [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), the **splitting lemma** states that in any [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063), the following statements are [equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) for a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence)

$$0 \longrightarrow A \mathrel{\overset{q}{\longrightarrow}} B \mathrel{\overset{r}{\longrightarrow}} C \longrightarrow 0.$$

::: {#o2 .entry cue=term name="Left split"}

::: {#o3 .entry cue=term name="Right split"}

::: {#o4 .entry cue=term name="Direct sum"}

**Left split** There exists a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) $t: B \to A$ such that $tq$ is the [identity](wikipedia://en/15069) $\operatorname{id}_{A}$ on $A$,; **Right split** There exists a morphism $u: C \to B$ such that $ru$ is the identity $\operatorname{id}_{C}$ on $C$,; **Direct sum** There is an [isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_%28category_theory%29) $h$ from $B$ to the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/59574) of $A$ and $C$, such that $hq$ is the natural injection of $A$ into the direct sum, and $rh^{-1}$ is the natural projection of the direct sum [onto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) $C$.

:::

:::

:::

:::

If any of these statements holds, the sequence is called a **[split exact sequence](wikipedia://en/1531404)**, and the sequence is said to *split*.

In the above short exact sequence, where the sequence splits, it allows one to refine the [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_isomorphism_theorem), which states that:

$C \cong B/\ker r \cong B/q(A)$ (i.e., $C$ isomorphic to the [coimage](wikipedia://en/632685) of $r$ or [cokernel](wikipedia://en/279715) of $q$)

to:

$$B = q(A) \oplus u(C) \cong A \oplus C$$

where the first isomorphism theorem is then just the projection onto $C$.

It is a [categorical](wikipedia://en/5869) generalization of the [rank–nullity theorem](wikipedia://en/330310) (in the form $V \cong \ker\,T \oplus \operatorname{im}\,T)$ in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422).

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proof for the category of abelian groups"}

## Proof for the category of abelian groups {#Proof_for_the_category_of_abelian_groups}

### $3. \Rightarrow 1.$ and $3. \Rightarrow 2.$ {#and}

First, to show that 3. implies both 1. and 2., we assume 3. and take as $t$ the natural projection of the direct sum onto $A$, and take as $u$ the natural injection of $C$ into the direct sum.

### $1. \Rightarrow 3.$

To [prove](wikipedia://en/82285) that 1. implies 3., first note that any member of *B* is in the set ($\ker t + \operatorname{im} q$). This follows since for all $b$ in $B$, $b = (b - qt(b)) + qt(b)$; $qt(b)$ is in $\operatorname{im} q$, and $b - qt(b)$ is in $\ker t$, since

$$t(b - qt(b)) = t(b) - tqt(b) = t(b) - (tq)t(b) = t(b) - t(b) = 0.$$

Next, the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of $\operatorname{im} q$ and $\ker t$ is 0, since if there exists $a$ in $A$ such that $q(a) = b$, and $t(b) = 0$, then $0 = tq(a) = a$; and therefore, $b = 0$.

This proves that $B$ is the direct sum of $\operatorname{im} q$ and $\ker t$. So, for all $b$ in $B$, $b$ can be uniquely identified by some $a$ in $A$, $k$ in $\ker t$, such that $b = q(a) + k$.

By exactness $\ker r = \operatorname{im} q$. The subsequence $B \longrightarrow C \longrightarrow 0$ implies that $r$ is [onto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective); therefore for any $c$ in $C$ there exists some $b = q(a) + k$ such that $c = r(b) = r(q(a) + k) = r(k)$. Therefore, for any *c* in *C*, exists *k* in ker *t* such that *c* = *r*(*k*), and *r*(ker *t*) = *C*.

If $r(k) = 0$, then $k$ is in $\operatorname{im} q$; since the intersection of $\operatorname{im} q$ and $\ker t = 0$, then $k = 0$. Therefore, the [restriction](wikipedia://en/1589032) $r: \ker t \to C$ is an isomorphism; and $\ker t$ is isomorphic to $C$.

Finally, $\operatorname{im} q$ is isomorphic to $A$ due to the exactness of $0 \longrightarrow A \longrightarrow B$; so *B* is isomorphic to the direct sum of $A$ and $C$, which proves (3).

### $2. \Rightarrow 3.$

To show that 2. implies 3., we follow a similar argument. Any member of $B$ is in the set $\ker r + \operatorname{im} u$; since for all $b$ in $B$, $b = (b - ur(b)) + ur(b)$, which is in $\ker r + \operatorname{im} u$. The intersection of $\ker r$ and $\operatorname{im} u$ is $0$, since if $r(b) = 0$ and $u(c) = b$, then $0 = ru(c) = c$.

By exactness, $\operatorname{im} q = \ker r$, and since $q$ is an [injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective), $\operatorname{im} q$ is isomorphic to $A$, so $A$ is isomorphic to $\ker r$. Since $ru$ is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942), $u$ is an injection, and thus $\operatorname{im} u$ is isomorphic to $C$. So $B$ is again the direct sum of $A$ and $C$.

An alternative "[abstract nonsense](wikipedia://en/630022)" [proof of the splitting lemma](https://math.stackexchange.com/q/753182) may be formulated entirely in [category theoretic](wikipedia://en/5869) terms.

:::

## Non-abelian groups {#Non-abelian_groups}

In the form stated here, the splitting lemma does not hold in the full [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), which is not an abelian category.

### Partially true {#Partially_true}

It is partially true: if a short exact sequence of groups is left split or a direct sum (1. or 3.), then all of the conditions hold. For a direct sum this is clear, as one can inject from or project to the summands. For a left split sequence, the map $t \times r: B \to A \times C$ gives an isomorphism, so $B$ is a direct sum (3.), and thus inverting the isomorphism and composing with the natural injection $C \to A \times C$ gives an injection $C \to B$ splitting $r$ (2.).

However, if a short exact sequence of groups is right split (2.), then it need not be left split or a direct sum (neither 1. nor 3. follows): the problem is that the image of the right splitting need not be [normal](wikipedia://en/21918). What is true in this case is that $B$ is a [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246), though not in general a [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015).

::: {#o11 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Counterexample"}

### Counterexample {#Counterexample}

To form a counterexample, take the smallest [non-abelian group](wikipedia://en/4791400) $B \cong S_{3}$, the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) on three letters. Let $A$ denote the [alternating subgroup](wikipedia://en/49726), and let $C = B/A \cong \{\pm1$}. Let $q$ and $r$ denote the inclusion map and the [sign](wikipedia://en/49727) map respectively, so that

$$0 \longrightarrow A \mathrel{\stackrel{q}{\longrightarrow}} B \mathrel{\stackrel{r}{\longrightarrow}} C \longrightarrow 0$$

is a short exact sequence. 3. fails, because $S_{3}$ is not abelian, but 2. holds: we may define $u: C \to B$ by mapping the generator to any [two-cycle](wikipedia://en/449166). Note for completeness that 1. fails: any map $t: B \to A$ must map every two-cycle to the [identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identity_permutation) because the map has to be a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396), while the [order](wikipedia://en/493527) of a two-cycle is 2 which can not be divided by the order of the elements in *A* other than the identity element, which is 3 as $A$ is the alternating subgroup of $S_{3}$, or namely the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) 3. But every [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) is a product of two-cycles, so $t$ is the trivial map, whence $tq: A \to A$ is the trivial map, not the identity.

:::
