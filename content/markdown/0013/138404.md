# Chain complex

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Chain complex"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **chain complex** is an [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) that consists of a sequence of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) (or [modules](wikipedia://en/276410)) and a sequence of [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) between consecutive groups such that the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of each homomorphism is contained in the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240#Group_homomorphisms) of the next. Associated to a chain complex is its [homology](wikipedia://en/142432), which is (loosely speaking) a measure of the failure of a chain complex to be [exact](wikipedia://en/138215).

:::

A **cochain complex** is similar to a chain complex, except that its homomorphisms are in the opposite direction. The homology of a cochain complex is called its [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060).

In [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), the singular chain complex of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) X is constructed using [continuous maps](wikipedia://en/6122#Continuous_functions_between_topological_spaces) from a [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) to X, and the homomorphisms of the chain complex capture how these maps restrict to the boundary of the simplex. The homology of this chain complex is called the [singular homology](wikipedia://en/431041) of X, and is a commonly used [invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_invariant) of a topological space.

Chain complexes are studied in [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), but are used in several areas of mathematics, including [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316), [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). They can be defined more generally in [abelian categories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_categories).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

A **chain complex** $(A_\bullet, d_\bullet)$ is a sequence of abelian groups or modules $\cdots, A_0, A_1, A_2,\dots$ connected by homomorphisms (called **boundary operators** or **differentials**) $d_n:A_n\to A_{n-1}$, such that the composition of any two consecutive maps is the zero map. Explicitly, the differentials satisfy $d_n\circ d_{n+1}=0$ for all $n$, or, concisely, $d^2=0$. The complex may be written out as follows:

$$\cdots \xleftarrow{ d_0 } A_0 \xleftarrow{d_1} A_1 \xleftarrow{d_2} A_2 \xleftarrow{d_3} A_3 \xleftarrow{d_4} A_4 \xleftarrow{d_5} \cdots$$

The **cochain complex** $(A^\bullet, d^\bullet)$ is the [dual](wikipedia://en/310885) notion to a chain complex. It consists of a sequence of abelian groups or modules $\cdots, A^0, A^1, A^2,\dots$ connected by homomorphisms (**coboundary operators**) $d^n:A^n\to A^{n+1}$ satisfying $d^{n+1}\circ d^{n}=0$. The cochain complex may be written out in a similar fashion to the chain complex:

$$\cdots \xrightarrow{ d^{-1} } A^0 \xrightarrow{d^0} A^1 \xrightarrow{d^1} A^2 \xrightarrow{d^2} A^3 \xrightarrow{d^3} A^4 \xrightarrow{d^4} \cdots$$

In both cases, the index $n$ is referred to as the **degree** (or **dimension**). The difference between chain and cochain complexes is that, in chain complexes, the differentials decrease dimension, whereas in cochain complexes they increase dimension. All the concepts and definitions for chain complexes apply to cochain complexes, except that they will follow this different convention for dimension, and often terms will be given the [prefix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix) *co-*. In this article, definitions will be given for chain complexes when the distinction is not required.

A **bounded chain complex** is one in which [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309#cardinality) the $A_n$ are 0; that is, a finite complex extended to the left and right by 0. An example is the chain complex defining the [simplicial homology](wikipedia://en/1060236) of a finite [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686). A chain complex is **bounded above** if all modules above some fixed degree $N$ are 0, and is **bounded below** if all modules below some fixed degree are 0. Clearly, a complex is bounded both above and below if and only if the complex is bounded.

The elements of the individual groups of a (co)chain complex are called **(co)chains**. The elements in the kernel of $d$ are called **(co)cycles** (or **closed** elements), and the elements in the image of *d* are called **(co)boundaries** (or **exact** elements). Right from the definition of the differential, all boundaries are cycles. The ***n*-th (co)homology group** *H*<sub>*n*</sub> (*H*<sup>*n*</sup>) is the group of (co)cycles [modulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_%28jargon%29#structures) (co)boundaries in degree *n*, that is,

$$H_n = \ker d_{n}/\mbox{im } d_{n+1} \quad \left(H^n = \ker d^{n}/\mbox{im } d^{n-1} \right)$$

### Exact sequences {#Exact_sequences}

An **exact sequence** (or **exact** complex) is a chain complex whose homology groups are all zero. This means all closed elements in the complex are exact. A **short exact sequence** is a bounded exact sequence in which only the groups *A*<sub>*k*</sub>, *A*<sub>*k*+1</sub>, *A*<sub>*k*+2</sub> may be nonzero. For example, the following chain complex is a short exact sequence.

$$\cdots \xrightarrow{} \; 0 \; \xrightarrow{} \; \mathbf{Z} \; \xrightarrow{\times p} \; \mathbf{Z} \twoheadrightarrow \mathbf{Z}/p\mathbf{Z} \; \xrightarrow{} \; 0 \; \xrightarrow{} \cdots$$

In the middle group, the closed elements are the elements p**Z**; these are clearly the exact elements in this group.

### Chain maps {#Chain_maps}

A **chain map** *f* between two chain complexes $(A_\bullet, d_{A,\bullet})$ and $(B_\bullet, d_{B,\bullet})$ is a sequence $f_\bullet$ of homomorphisms $f_n : A_n \rightarrow B_n$ for each *n* that commutes with the boundary operators on the two chain complexes, so $d_{B,n} \circ f_n = f_{n-1} \circ d_{A,n}$. This is written out in the following [commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484).

*Image: Chain map.svg.* 650 px

A chain map sends cycles to cycles and boundaries to boundaries, and thus induces a map on homology $(f_\bullet)_*:H_\bullet(A_\bullet, d_{A,\bullet}) \rightarrow H_\bullet(B_\bullet, d_{B,\bullet})$.

A continuous map *f* between topological spaces *X* and *Y* induces a chain map between the singular chain complexes of *X* and *Y*, and hence induces a map *f*<sub>\*</sub> between the singular homology of *X* and *Y* as well. When *X* and *Y* are both equal to the [*n*-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782), the map induced on homology defines the [degree](wikipedia://en/3094450#From_Sn_to_Sn) of the map *f*.

The concept of chain map reduces to the one of boundary through the construction of the [cone](wikipedia://en/10125391) of a chain map.

:::

### Chain homotopy {#Chain_homotopy}

A chain homotopy offers a way to relate two chain maps that induce the same map on homology groups, even though the maps may be different. Given two chain complexes *A* and *B*, and two chain maps *f*, *g* : *A* → *B*, a **chain homotopy** is a sequence of homomorphisms *h*<sub>*n*</sub> : *A*<sub>*n*</sub> → *B*<sub>*n*+1</sub> such that *hd*<sub>*A*</sub> + *d*<sub>*B*</sub>*h* = *f* − *g*. The maps may be written out in a diagram as follows, but this diagram is not commutative.

*Image: Chain homotopy between chain complexes.svg.* 650 px

The map *hd*<sub>*A*</sub> + *d*<sub>*B*</sub>*h* is easily verified to induce the zero map on homology, for any *h*. It immediately follows that *f* and *g* induce the same map on homology. One says *f* and *g* are **chain homotopic** (or simply **homotopic**), and this property defines an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) between chain maps.

Let *X* and *Y* be topological spaces. In the case of singular homology, a [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) between continuous maps *f*, *g* : *X* → *Y* induces a chain homotopy between the chain maps corresponding to *f* and *g*. This shows that two homotopic maps induce the same map on singular homology. The name "chain homotopy" is motivated by this example.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Singular homology {#Singular_homology}

Let *X* be a topological space. Define *C*<sub>*n*</sub>(*X*) for [natural](wikipedia://en/21474) *n* to be the [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) formally generated by [singular n-simplices](wikipedia://en/431041) in *X*, and define the boundary map $\partial_n: C_n(X) \to C_{n-1}(X)$ to be

$$\partial_n : \, (\sigma: [v_0,\ldots,v_n] \to X) \mapsto (\sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i \sigma: [v_0,\ldots, \hat v_i, \ldots, v_n] \to X)$$

where the hat denotes the omission of a [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417). That is, the boundary of a singular simplex is the alternating sum of restrictions to its faces. It can be shown that ∂<sup>2</sup> = 0, so $(C_\bullet, \partial_\bullet)$ is a chain complex; the **singular homology** $H_\bullet(X)$ is the homology of this complex.

Singular homology is a useful invariant of topological spaces up to [homotopy equivalence](wikipedia://en/212250#homotopy_equivalence). The degree zero homology group is a free abelian group on the [path-components](wikipedia://en/6233#Path_connectedness) of *X*.

### de Rham cohomology {#de_Rham_cohomology}

The [differential *k*-forms](wikipedia://en/221519) on any [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) *M* form a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) called Ω<sup>*k*</sup>(*M*) under addition. The [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) *d* maps Ω<sup>*k*</sup>(*M*) to Ω<sup>*k*+1</sup>(*M*), and *d*${}^{2}$ = 0 follows essentially from [symmetry of second derivatives](wikipedia://en/412094), so the vector spaces of *k*-forms along with the exterior derivative are a cochain complex.

$$0\stackrel{\subset}{\to}\ {\Re^{c}} \stackrel{\subset}{\to}\ {\Omega^0(M)} \stackrel{d}{\to}\ {\Omega^1(M)} \stackrel{d}{\to}\ {\Omega^2(M)} \stackrel{d}{\to}\ \Omega^3(M) \to \cdots$$

The cohomology of this complex is called the **de Rham cohomology** of *M*. [Locally constant functions](wikipedia://en/295629) are designated with its isomorphism $\Re^c$ with c the count of mutually disconnected components of *M*. This way the complex was extended to leave the complex exact at zero-form level using the subset operator.

[Smooth maps](wikipedia://en/1408000#Smooth_functions_on_and_between_manifolds) between manifolds induce chain maps, and smooth homotopies between maps induce chain homotopies.

:::

## Category of chain complexes {#Category_of_chain_complexes}

Chain complexes of $K$-modules with chain maps as [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) form a [category](wikipedia://en/53759) $\mathbf{Ch}_K$, where $K$ is a commutative ring.

If $V=V_*$ and $W=W_*$ are chain complexes, their **tensor product** $V \otimes W$ is a chain complex with degree $n$ elements given by

$$(V \otimes W)_n = \bigoplus_{\{i,j\mid i+j=n\}} V_i \otimes W_j$$

and differential given by

$$\partial (a \otimes b) = \partial a \otimes b + (-1)^{\left|a\right|} a \otimes \partial b$$

where $a$ and $b$ are any two homogeneous vectors in $V$ and $W$ respectively, and $\left|a\right|$ denotes the degree of $a$.

This tensor product makes the category $\mathbf{Ch}_K$ into a [symmetric monoidal category](wikipedia://en/3865249). The identity object with respect to this monoidal product is the base ring $K$ viewed as a chain complex in degree $0$. The [braiding](wikipedia://en/1470466) is given on simple tensors of homogeneous elements by

$$a \otimes b \mapsto (-1)^{\left|a\right|\left|b\right|} b \otimes a$$

The sign is necessary for the braiding to be a chain map.

Moreover, the category of chain complexes of $K$-modules also has [internal Hom](wikipedia://en/1396924): given chain complexes $V$ and $W$, the internal Hom of $V$ and $W$, denoted $\mathrm{Hom}(V,W)$, is the chain complex with degree $n$ elements given by $\Pi_{i}\text{Hom}_K (V_i,W_{i+n})$ and differential given by

$$(\partial f)(v) = \partial(f(v)) - (-1)^{\left|f\right|} f(\partial(v)).$$

We have a [natural isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism)

$$\text{Hom}(A\otimes B, C) \cong \text{Hom}(A,\text{Hom}(B,C))$$
