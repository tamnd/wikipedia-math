# Homological algebra

*Image: Snake lemma origin.svg.* A diagram used in the [snake lemma](wikipedia://en/87089), a basic result in homological algebra.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Homological algebra"}

**Homological algebra** is the branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) that studies [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) in a general algebraic setting. It is a relatively young discipline, whose origins can be traced to investigations in [combinatorial topology](wikipedia://en/363400) (a precursor to [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801)) and [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) (theory of [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) and [syzygies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syzygy_%28mathematics%29)) at the end of the 19th century, chiefly by [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) and [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302).

:::

Homological algebra is the study of homological [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) and the intricate algebraic structures that they entail; its development was closely intertwined with the emergence of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869). A central concept is that of [chain complexes](wikipedia://en/138404), which can be studied through their homology and [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060).

Homological algebra affords the means to extract information contained in these complexes and present it in the form of homological [invariants](wikipedia://en/1126638) of [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), modules, [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), and other "tangible" mathematical objects. A [spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/803894) is a powerful tool for this.

It has played an enormous role in algebraic topology. Its influence has gradually expanded and presently includes [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990), [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200), [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416), [operator algebras](wikipedia://en/455987), [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), and the theory of [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564). [*K*-theory](wikipedia://en/246748) is an independent discipline which draws upon methods of homological algebra, as does the [noncommutative geometry](wikipedia://en/295917) of [Alain Connes](wikipedia://en/340).

## History {#History}

Homological algebra began to be studied in its most basic form in the late 19th century as a branch of topology and in the 1940s became an independent subject with the study of objects such as the [ext functor](wikipedia://en/840758) and the [tor functor](wikipedia://en/856347), among others.[^o3]

## Chain complexes and homology {#Chain_complexes_and_homology}

The notion of [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404) is central in homological algebra. An abstract **chain complex** is a sequence $(C_\bullet, d_\bullet)$ of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) and [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396), with the property that the composition of any two consecutive [maps](wikipedia://en/516931) is zero:

$$C_\bullet: \cdots \longrightarrow C_{n+1} \stackrel{d_{n+1}}{\longrightarrow} C_n \stackrel{d_n}{\longrightarrow} C_{n-1} \stackrel{d_{n-1}}{\longrightarrow} \cdots, \quad d_n \circ d_{n+1}=0.$$

The elements of *C*<sub>*n*</sub> are called *n*-**chains** and the homomorphisms *d*<sub>*n*</sub> are called the **boundary maps** or **differentials**. The **chain groups** *C*<sub>*n*</sub> may be endowed with extra structure; for example, they may be [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) or [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over a fixed [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R*. The differentials must preserve the extra structure if it exists; for example, they must be [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102) or homomorphisms of *R*-modules. For notational convenience, restrict attention to abelian groups (more correctly, to the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) **Ab** of abelian groups); a celebrated [theorem by Barry Mitchell](wikipedia://en/453755) implies the results will generalize to any [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063). Every chain complex defines two further sequences of abelian groups, the **cycles** *Z*<sub>*n*</sub> = Ker *d*<sub>*n*</sub> and the **boundaries** *B*<sub>*n*</sub> = Im *d*<sub>*n*+1</sub>, where Ker *d* and Im *d* denote the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) and the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of *d*. Since the composition of two consecutive boundary maps is zero, these groups are embedded into each other as

$$B_n \subseteq Z_n \subseteq C_n.$$

[Subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011) of abelian groups are automatically [normal](wikipedia://en/21918); therefore we can define the *n*th **homology group** *H*<sub>*n*</sub>(*C*) as the [factor group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_group) of the *n*-cycles by the *n*-boundaries,

$$H_n(C) = Z_n/B_n = \operatorname{Ker}\, d_n/ \operatorname{Im}\, d_{n+1}.$$

A chain complex is called **acyclic** or an **[exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215)** if all its homology groups are zero.

Chain complexes arise in abundance in [algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) and [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801). For example, if *X* is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) then the [singular chains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_chain) *C*<sub>*n*</sub>(*X*) are formal [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) of [continuous maps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map) from the standard *n*-[simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) into *X*; if *K* is a [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686) then the [simplicial chains](wikipedia://en/224765) *C*<sub>*n*</sub>(*K*) are formal linear combinations of the *n*-simplices of *K*; if *A* = *F*/*R* is a presentation of an abelian group *A* by [generators and relations](wikipedia://en/99494), where *F* is a [free abelian group](wikipedia://en/247296) spanned by the generators and *R* is the subgroup of relations, then letting *C*<sub>1</sub>(*A*) = *R*, *C*<sub>0</sub>(*A*) = *F*, and *C*<sub>*n*</sub>(*A*) = 0 for all other *n* defines a sequence of abelian groups. In all these cases, there are natural differentials *d*<sub>*n*</sub> making *C*<sub>*n*</sub> into a chain complex, whose homology reflects the structure of the topological space *X*, the simplicial complex *K*, or the abelian group *A*. In the case of topological spaces, we arrive at the notion of [singular homology](wikipedia://en/431041), which plays a fundamental role in investigating the properties of such spaces, for example, [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470).

On a philosophical level, homological algebra teaches us that certain chain complexes associated with algebraic or geometric objects (topological spaces, simplicial complexes, *R*-modules) contain a lot of valuable algebraic information about them, with the homology being only the most readily available part. On a technical level, homological algebra provides the tools for manipulating complexes and extracting this information. Here are two general illustrations.

- Two objects *X* and *Y* are connected by a map *f* between them. Homological algebra studies the relation, induced by the map *f*, between chain complexes associated with *X* and *Y* and their homology. This is generalized to the case of several objects and maps connecting them. Phrased in the language of [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), homological algebra studies the [functorial properties](wikipedia://en/10987) of various constructions of chain complexes and of the homology of these complexes.
- An object *X* admits multiple descriptions (for example, as a topological space and as a simplicial complex) or the complex $C_\bullet(X)$ is constructed using some 'presentation' of *X*, which involves non-canonical choices. It is important to know the effect of change in the description of *X* on chain complexes associated with *X*. Typically, the complex and its homology $H_\bullet(C)$ are functorial with respect to the presentation; and the homology (although not the complex itself) is actually independent of the presentation chosen, thus it is an [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) of *X*.

:::

## Foundational aspects {#Foundational_aspects}

Cohomology theories have been defined for many different objects such as [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450), [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466), [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [rings](wikipedia://en/48404), [Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/17944), and [C\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/7184). The study of modern [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) would be almost unthinkable without [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357).

Central to homological algebra is the notion of [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215); these can be used to perform actual calculations. A classical tool of homological algebra is that of [derived functor](wikipedia://en/433875); the most basic examples are functors [Ext](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ext_functors) and [Tor](wikipedia://en/856347).

With a diverse set of applications in mind, it was natural to try to put the whole subject on a uniform basis. There were several attempts before the subject settled down. An approximate history can be stated as follows:

- [Cartan](wikipedia://en/362536)–[Eilenberg](wikipedia://en/362501): In their 1956 book "Homological Algebra", these authors used projective and injective [module resolutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resolution_of_a_module).
- 'Tohoku': The approach in a [celebrated paper](wikipedia://en/44572732) by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) which appeared in the Second Series of the *[Tohoku Mathematical Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tohoku_Mathematical_Journal)* in 1957, using the [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063) concept (to include [sheaves](wikipedia://en/245466) of abelian groups).
- The [derived category](wikipedia://en/985897) of [Grothendieck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grothendieck) and [Verdier](wikipedia://en/9168893). Derived categories date back to Verdier's 1967 thesis. They are examples of [triangulated categories](wikipedia://en/1679700) used in a number of modern theories.

These move from computability to generality.

The computational sledgehammer *par excellence* is the [spectral sequence](wikipedia://en/803894); these are essential in the Cartan-Eilenberg and Tohoku approaches where they are needed, for instance, to compute the derived functors of a composition of two functors. Spectral sequences are less essential in the derived category approach, but still play a role whenever concrete computations are necessary.

There have been attempts at 'non-commutative' theories which extend first cohomology as *[torsors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsor)* (important in [Galois cohomology](wikipedia://en/1111315)).

## Standard tools {#Standard_tools}

### Exact sequences {#Exact_sequences}

In the context of [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), a sequence

$$G_0 \;\xrightarrow{f_1}\; G_1 \;\xrightarrow{f_2}\; G_2 \;\xrightarrow{f_3}\; \cdots \;\xrightarrow{f_n}\; G_n$$

of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) and [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) is called **exact** if the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of each homomorphism is equal to the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of the next:

$$\mathrm{im}(f_k) = \mathrm{ker}(f_{k+1}).\!$$

Note that the sequence of groups and homomorphisms may be either finite or infinite.

A similar definition can be made for certain other [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364). For example, one could have an exact sequence of [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) and [linear maps](wikipedia://en/18102), or of [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) and [module homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/474118). More generally, the notion of an exact sequence makes sense in any [category](wikipedia://en/53759) with [kernels](wikipedia://en/142616) and [cokernels](wikipedia://en/279715).

#### Short {#Short}

The most common type of exact sequence is the **short exact sequence**. This is an exact sequence of the form

$$A \;\overset{f}{\hookrightarrow}\; B \;\overset{g}{\twoheadrightarrow}\; C$$

where ƒ is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) and *g* is an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539). In this case, *A* is a [subobject](wikipedia://en/540476) of *B*, and the corresponding [quotient](wikipedia://en/332640) is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) to *C*:

$$C \cong B/f(A).$$

(where *f(A)* = im(*f*)).

A short exact sequence of abelian groups may also be written as an exact sequence with five terms:

$$0 \;\xrightarrow{}\; A \;\xrightarrow{f}\; B \;\xrightarrow{g}\; C \;\xrightarrow{}\; 0$$

where 0 represents the [zero object](wikipedia://en/59405), such as the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) or a zero-dimensional vector space. The placement of the 0's forces ƒ to be a monomorphism and *g* to be an epimorphism (see below).

#### Long {#Long}

A long exact sequence is an exact sequence indexed by the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474).

### Abelian categories {#Abelian_categories}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **abelian category** is a [category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_%28category_theory%29) in which [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) and objects can be added and in which [kernels](wikipedia://en/142616) and [cokernels](wikipedia://en/279715) exist and have desirable properties. The motivating prototype example of an abelian category is the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748), **Ab**. The theory originated in a tentative attempt to unify several [cohomology theories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_theory) by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042). Abelian categories are very *stable* categories, for example they are [regular](wikipedia://en/9790950) and they satisfy the [snake lemma](wikipedia://en/87089). The class of Abelian categories is closed under several categorical constructions, for example, the category of [chain complexes](wikipedia://en/138404) of an Abelian category, or the category of [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) from a [small category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_category) to an Abelian category are Abelian as well. These stability properties make them inevitable in homological algebra and beyond; the theory has major applications in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) and pure [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869). Abelian categories are named after [Niels Henrik Abel](wikipedia://en/21573).

More concretely, a category is **abelian** if

- it has a [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object),
- it has all binary [products](wikipedia://en/237868) and binary [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889), and
- it has all [kernels](wikipedia://en/142616) and [cokernels](wikipedia://en/279715).
- all [monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59538) and [epimorphisms](wikipedia://en/59539) are [normal](wikipedia://en/142626).

### Derived functors {#Derived_functors}

Suppose we are given a covariant [left exact functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_exact_functor) *F* : **A** → **B** between two [abelian categories](wikipedia://en/45063) **A** and **B**. If 0 → *A* → *B* → *C* → 0 is a short exact sequence in **A**, then applying *F* yields the exact sequence 0 → *F*(*A*) → *F*(*B*) → *F*(*C*) and one could ask how to continue this sequence to the right to form a long exact sequence. Strictly speaking, this question is ill-posed, since there are always numerous different ways to continue a given exact sequence to the right. But it turns out that (if **A** is "nice" enough) there is one [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) way of doing so, given by the right derived functors of *F*. For every *i*≥1, there is a functor *R<sup>i</sup>F*: **A** → **B**, and the above sequence continues like so: 0 → *F*(*A*) → *F*(*B*) → *F*(*C*) → *R*<sup>1</sup>*F*(*A*) → *R*<sup>1</sup>*F*(*B*) → *R*<sup>1</sup>*F*(*C*) → *R*<sup>2</sup>*F*(*A*) → *R*<sup>2</sup>*F*(*B*) → ... . From this we see that *F* is an exact functor if and only if *R*<sup>1</sup>*F* = 0; so in a sense the right derived functors of *F* measure "how far" *F* is from being exact.

### Ext functor {#Ext_functor}

Let *R* be a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) and let Mod<sub>*R*</sub> be the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) over *R*. Let *B* be in Mod<sub>*R*</sub> and set *T*(*B*) = Hom<sub>*R*</sub>(*A,B*), for fixed *A* in Mod<sub>*R*</sub>. This is a [left exact functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_exact_functor) and thus has right [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) *R<sup>n</sup>T*. The Ext functor is defined by

$$\operatorname{Ext}_R^n(A,B)=(R^nT)(B).$$

This can be calculated by taking any [injective resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_resolution)

$$0 \rightarrow B \rightarrow I^0 \rightarrow I^1 \rightarrow \cdots,$$

and computing

$$0 \rightarrow \operatorname{Hom}_R(A,I^0) \rightarrow \operatorname{Hom}_R(A,I^1) \rightarrow \cdots.$$

Then (*R<sup>n</sup>T*)(*B*) is the [cohomology](wikipedia://en/138404) of this complex. Note that Hom<sub>*R*</sub>(*A,B*) is excluded from the complex.

An alternative definition is given using the functor *G*(*A*)=Hom<sub>*R*</sub>(*A,B*). For a fixed module *B*, this is a [contravariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covariance_and_contravariance_of_functors) [left exact functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_exact_functor), and thus we also have right [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) *R<sup>n</sup>G*, and can define

$$\operatorname{Ext}_R^n(A,B)=(R^nG)(A).$$

This can be calculated by choosing any [projective resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_resolution)

$$\dots \rightarrow P^1 \rightarrow P^0 \rightarrow A \rightarrow 0,$$

and proceeding dually by computing

$$0\rightarrow\operatorname{Hom}_R(P^0,B)\rightarrow \operatorname{Hom}_R(P^1,B) \rightarrow \cdots.$$

Then (*R<sup>n</sup>G*)(*A*) is the cohomology of this complex. Again note that Hom<sub>*R*</sub>(*A,B*) is excluded.

These two constructions turn out to yield [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) results, and so both may be used to calculate the Ext functor.

### Tor functor {#Tor_functor}

Suppose *R* is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), and denoted by *R*-**Mod** the [category](wikipedia://en/5869) of [left *R*-modules](wikipedia://en/276410) and by **Mod**-*R* the category of right *R*-modules (if *R* is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346), the two categories coincide). Fix a module *B* in *R*-**Mod**. For *A* in **Mod**-*R*, set *T*(*A*) = *A*⊗<sub>*R*</sub>*B*. Then *T* is a [right exact functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_exact_functor) from **Mod**-*R* to the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748) **Ab** (in the case when *R* is commutative, it is a right exact functor from **Mod**-*R* to **Mod**-*R*) and its [left derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) *L<sub>n</sub>T* are defined. We set

$$\mathrm{Tor}_n^R(A,B)=(L_nT)(A)$$

i.e., we take a [projective resolution](wikipedia://en/364488#Projective_resolutions)

$$\cdots\rightarrow P_2 \rightarrow P_1 \rightarrow P_0 \rightarrow A\rightarrow 0$$

then remove the *A* term and tensor the projective resolution with *B* to get the complex

$$\cdots \rightarrow P_2\otimes_R B \rightarrow P_1\otimes_R B \rightarrow P_0\otimes_R B \rightarrow 0$$

(note that *A*⊗<sub>*R*</sub>*B* does not appear and the last arrow is just the zero map) and take the [homology](wikipedia://en/142432) of this complex.

### Spectral sequences {#Spectral_sequences}

Fix an [abelian category](wikipedia://en/45063), such as a category of modules over a ring. A **spectral sequence** is a choice of a nonnegative integer *r*<sub>0</sub> and a collection of three sequences:

1. For all integers *r* ≥ *r*<sub>0</sub>, an object *E<sub>r</sub>*, called a *sheet* (as in a sheet of [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper)), or sometimes a *page* or a *term*,
1. Endomorphisms *d<sub>r</sub>* : *E<sub>r</sub>* → *E<sub>r</sub>* satisfying *d<sub>r</sub>* o *d<sub>r</sub>* = 0, called *boundary maps* or *differentials*,
1. Isomorphisms of *E<sub>r+1</sub>* with *H*(*E<sub>r</sub>*), the homology of *E<sub>r</sub>* with respect to *d<sub>r</sub>*.

*Image: SpectralSequence.png.* The E<sub>2</sub> sheet of a cohomological spectral sequence

A doubly graded spectral sequence has a tremendous amount of data to keep track of, but there is a common visualization technique which makes the structure of the spectral sequence clearer. We have three indices, *r*, *p*, and *q*. For each *r*, imagine that we have a sheet of graph paper. On this sheet, we will take *p* to be the horizontal direction and *q* to be the vertical direction. At each lattice point we have the object $E_r^{p,q}$.

It is very common for *n* = *p* + *q* to be another natural index in the spectral sequence. *n* runs diagonally, northwest to southeast, across each sheet. In the homological case, the differentials have bidegree (−*r*, *r* − 1), so they decrease *n* by one. In the cohomological case, *n* is increased by one. When *r* is zero, the differential moves objects one space down or up. This is similar to the differential on a chain complex. When *r* is one, the differential moves objects one space to the left or right. When *r* is two, the differential moves objects just like a [knight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knight_%28chess%29)'s move in [chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess). For higher *r*, the differential acts like a generalized knight's move.

## Functoriality {#Functoriality}

A [continuous map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_map) of topological spaces gives rise to a homomorphism (also called [pushforward](wikipedia://en/16649410)) between their *n*th [homology groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_group) for all *n*. This basic fact of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) finds a natural explanation through certain properties of chain complexes. Since it is very common to study several topological spaces simultaneously, in homological algebra one is led to simultaneous consideration of multiple chain complexes.

A **morphism** between two chain complexes, $F: C_\bullet\to D_\bullet,$ is a family of homomorphisms of abelian groups $F_n: C_n \to D_n$ that commute with the differentials, in the sense that $F_{n-1} \circ d_n^C = d_n^D \circ F_n$ for all *n*. A morphism of chain complexes induces a morphism $H_\bullet(F)$ of their homology groups, consisting of the homomorphisms $H_n(F) : H_n(C) \to H_n(D)$ for all *n*. A morphism *F* is called a **[quasi-isomorphism](wikipedia://en/3653039)** if it induces an isomorphism on the *n*th homology for all *n*.

Many constructions of chain complexes arising in algebra and geometry, including [singular homology](wikipedia://en/431041), have the following [functoriality](wikipedia://en/10987) property: if two objects *X* and *Y* are connected by a map *f*, then the associated chain complexes are connected by a morphism $F=C(f) : C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(Y),$ and moreover, the composition $g\circ f$ of maps *f*: *X* → *Y* and *g*: *Y* → *Z* induces the morphism $C(g\circ f): C_\bullet(X) \to C_\bullet(Z)$ that coincides with the composition $C(g) \circ C(f).$ It follows that the homology groups $H_\bullet(C)$ are functorial as well, so that morphisms between algebraic or topological objects give rise to compatible maps between their homology.

The following definition arises from a typical situation in algebra and topology. A triple consisting of three chain complexes $L_\bullet, M_\bullet, N_\bullet$ and two morphisms between them, $f:L_\bullet\to M_\bullet, g: M_\bullet\to N_\bullet,$ is called an **exact triple**, or a **short exact sequence of complexes**, and written as

$$0 \longrightarrow L_\bullet \overset{f}{\longrightarrow} M_\bullet \overset{g}{\longrightarrow} N_\bullet \longrightarrow 0,$$

if for any *n*, the sequence

$$0 \longrightarrow L_n \overset{f_n}{\longrightarrow} M_n \overset{g_n}{\longrightarrow} N_n \longrightarrow 0$$

is a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of abelian groups. By definition, this means that *f*<sub>*n*</sub> is an [injection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injection_%28mathematics%29), *g*<sub>*n*</sub> is a [surjection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection), and Im *f*<sub>*n*</sub> = Ker *g*<sub>*n*</sub>. One of the most basic theorems of homological algebra, sometimes known as the [zig-zag lemma](wikipedia://en/19382017), states that, in this case, there is a **long exact sequence in homology**

$$\cdots \longrightarrow H_n(L) \overset{H_n(f)}{\longrightarrow} H_n(M) \overset{H_n(g)}{\longrightarrow} H_n(N) \overset{\delta_n}{\longrightarrow} H_{n-1}(L) \overset{H_{n-1}(f)}{\longrightarrow} H_{n-1}(M) \longrightarrow \cdots,$$

where the homology groups of *L*, *M*, and *N* cyclically follow each other, and *δ*<sub>*n*</sub> are certain homomorphisms determined by *f* and *g*, called the **[connecting homomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connecting_homomorphism)**. Topological manifestations of this theorem include the [Mayer–Vietoris sequence](wikipedia://en/378677) and the long exact sequence for [relative homology](wikipedia://en/1248647).
