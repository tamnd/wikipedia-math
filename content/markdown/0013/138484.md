# Commutative diagram

*Image: 5 lemma.svg.* The commutative diagram used in the proof of the [five lemma](wikipedia://en/138214)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Commutative diagram"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), and especially in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a **commutative diagram** is a [diagram](wikipedia://en/7651625) such that all directed paths in the diagram with the same start and endpoints lead to the same result.[^o2] It is said that commutative diagrams play the role in category theory that [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations) play in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923).[^o3]

:::

## Description {#Description}

A commutative diagram often consists of three parts:

- [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) (also known as *vertices*)
- [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) (also known as *arrows* or *edges*)
- paths or composites

::: {#o6 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="↪"}

### Arrow symbols {#Arrow_symbols}

In algebra texts, the type of morphism can be denoted with different arrow usages:

- A [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538) may be labeled with a $\hookrightarrow$[^o7] or a $\rightarrowtail$.[^o8]
- An [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539) may be labeled with a $\twoheadrightarrow$.
- An [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) may be labeled with a $\overset{\sim}{\rightarrow}$.
- The dashed arrow typically represents the claim that the indicated morphism exists (whenever the rest of the diagram holds); the arrow may be optionally labeled as $\exists$.
  - If the morphism is in addition unique, then the dashed arrow may be labeled $!$ or $\exists!$.
- If the morphism acts between two arrows (such as in the case of [higher category theory](wikipedia://en/5274013)), it's called preferably a [natural transformation](wikipedia://en/45022) and may be labelled as $\Rightarrow$ (as seen below in this article).

The meanings of different arrows are not entirely standardized: the arrows used for monomorphisms, epimorphisms, and isomorphisms are also used for [injections](wikipedia://en/45196), [surjections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjection), and [bijections](wikipedia://en/3942), as well as the cofibrations, fibrations, and weak equivalences in a [model category](wikipedia://en/2895304).

:::

### Verifying commutativity {#Verifying_commutativity}

Commutativity makes sense for a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) of any finite number of sides (including just 1 or 2), and a diagram is commutative if every polygonal subdiagram is commutative.

Note that a diagram may be non-commutative, i.e., the composition of different paths in the diagram may not give the same result.

## Diagram chasing {#Diagram_chasing}

**Diagram chasing** (also called **diagrammatic search**) is a method of [mathematical proof](wikipedia://en/82285) used especially in [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410), where one establishes a property of some morphism by tracing the elements of a commutative diagram. A proof by diagram chasing typically involves the formal use of the properties of the diagram, such as [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) or [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) maps, or [exact sequences](wikipedia://en/138215).[^o16] A [syllogism](wikipedia://en/48046) is constructed, for which the graphical display of the diagram is just a visual aid. It follows that one ends up "chasing" elements around the diagram, until the desired element or result is constructed or verified.

Examples of proofs by diagram chasing include those typically given for the [five lemma](wikipedia://en/138214), the [snake lemma](wikipedia://en/87089), the [zig-zag lemma](wikipedia://en/19382017), and the [nine lemma](wikipedia://en/454673).

:::

## In higher category theory {#In_higher_category_theory}

In higher category theory, one considers not only objects and arrows, but arrows between the arrows, arrows between arrows between arrows, and so on ad infinitum. For example, the category of small categories **Cat** is naturally a 2-category, with [functors](wikipedia://en/10987) as its arrows and [natural transformations](wikipedia://en/45022) as the arrows between functors. In this setting, commutative diagrams may include these higher arrows as well, which are often depicted in the following style: $\Rightarrow$. For example, the following (somewhat trivial) diagram depicts two categories **$C$** and **$D$**, together with two functors $F$, $G$ : **$C$** → **$D$** and a natural transformation $\alpha$ : $F$ ⇒ $G$:

## Diagrams as functors {#Diagrams_as_functors}

A commutative diagram in a category *C* can be interpreted as a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) from an index category *J* to *C;* one calls the functor a **[diagram](wikipedia://en/7651625).**

More formally, a commutative diagram is a visualization of a diagram indexed by a [poset category](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Posetal_category). Such a diagram typically includes:

- a node for every object in the index category,
- an arrow for a generating set of morphisms (omitting identity maps and morphisms that can be expressed as compositions),
- the commutativity of the diagram (the equality of different compositions of maps between two objects), corresponding to the uniqueness of a map between two objects in a poset category.

Conversely, given a commutative diagram, it defines a poset category, where:

- the objects are the nodes,
- there is a morphism between any two objects if and only if there is a (directed) path between the nodes,
- with the relation that this morphism is unique (any composition of maps is defined by its domain and target: this is the commutativity axiom).

However, not every diagram commutes (the notion of diagram strictly generalizes commutative diagram). As a simple example, the diagram of a single object with an endomorphism ($f\colon X \to X$), or with two parallel arrows ($\bullet \rightrightarrows \bullet$, that is, $f,g\colon X \to Y$, sometimes called the [free quiver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_quiver)), as used in the definition of [equalizer](wikipedia://en/310767) need not commute. Further, diagrams may be messy or impossible to draw, when the number of objects or morphisms is large (or even infinite).
