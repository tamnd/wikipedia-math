# Fourier transform on finite groups

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fourier transform on finite groups"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Fourier transform on finite groups** is a generalization of the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) from [cyclic](wikipedia://en/52327) to arbitrary [finite groups](wikipedia://en/323707).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

The **Fourier transform** of a function $f : G \to \mathbb{C}$ at a [representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_of_a_finite_group) $\varrho : G \to \mathrm{GL}_{d_\varrho}(\mathbb{C})$ of $G$ is

$$\widehat{f}(\varrho) = \sum_{a \in G} f(a) \varrho(a).$$

For each representation $\varrho$ of $G$, $\widehat{f}(\varrho)$ is a $d_\varrho \times d_\varrho$ matrix, where $d_\varrho$ is the degree of $\varrho$.

Let $\widehat{G}$ be the complete set of inequivalent [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of $G$. Then the **inverse Fourier transform** at an element $a$ of $G$ is given by

$$f(a) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{\varrho \in \widehat{G}} d_{\varrho} \mathrm{Tr}\left(\varrho(a^{-1})\widehat{f}(\varrho)\right).$$

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

### Transform of a convolution {#Transform_of_a_convolution}

The **convolution** of two functions $f, g : G \to \mathbb{C}$ is defined as

$$(f \ast g)(a) = \sum_{b \in G} f\!\left(ab^{-1}\right) g(b).$$

The Fourier transform of a convolution at any representation $\varrho$ of $G$ is given by

$$\widehat{f \ast g}(\varrho) = \hat{f}(\varrho)\hat{g}(\varrho).$$

::: {#o8 .statement cue=named-heading name="Plancherel formula"}

### Plancherel formula {#Plancherel_formula}

For functions $f, g : G \to \mathbb{C}$, the Plancherel formula states

$$\sum_{a \in G} f(a^{-1}) g(a) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_i d_{\varrho_i} \text{Tr}\left(\hat{f}(\varrho_i)\hat{g}(\varrho_i)\right),$$

where $\varrho_i$ are the irreducible representations of $G$.

:::

:::

## Fourier transform for finite abelian groups {#Fourier_transform_for_finite_abelian_groups}

If the group *G* is a finite [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974), the situation simplifies considerably:

- all irreducible representations $\varrho_i$ are of degree 1 and hence equal to the irreducible characters of the group. Thus the matrix-valued Fourier transform becomes scalar-valued in this case.
- The set of irreducible *G*-representations has a natural group structure in its own right, which can be identified with the group $\widehat G := \mathrm{Hom}(G, S^1)$ of [group homomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_homomorphisms) from *G* to $S^1 = \{z \in \mathbb C, |z|=1\}$. This group is known as the [Pontryagin dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontryagin_dual) of *G*.

The Fourier transform of a function $f : G \to \mathbb{C}$ is the function $\widehat{f}: \widehat{G}\to \mathbb{C}$ given by

$$\widehat{f}(\chi) = \sum_{a \in G} f(a) \bar{\chi}(a).$$

The inverse Fourier transform is then given by

$$f(a) = \frac{1}{|G|} \sum_{\chi \in \widehat{G}} \widehat{f}(\chi) \chi(a).$$

For $G = \mathbb Z/n \mathbb Z$, a choice of a primitive *n*-th [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) $\zeta$ yields an isomorphism

$$G \to \widehat G,$$

given by $m \mapsto (r \mapsto \zeta^{mr})$. In the literature, the common choice is $\zeta = e^{2 \pi i /n}$, which explains the formula given in the article about the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811). However, such an isomorphism is not canonical, similarly to the situation that a finite-dimensional vector space is isomorphic to its [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_vector_space), but giving an isomorphism requires choosing a basis.

A property that is often useful in probability is that the Fourier transform of the uniform distribution is simply $\delta_{a,0}$, where 0 is the group identity and $\delta_{i,j}$ is the [Kronecker delta](wikipedia://en/182890).

Fourier Transform can also be done on cosets of a group.

## Relationship with representation theory {#Relationship_with_representation_theory}

There is a direct relationship between the Fourier transform on finite groups and the [representation theory of finite groups](wikipedia://en/874400). The set of complex-valued functions on a finite group, $G$, together with the operations of pointwise addition and convolution, form a ring that is naturally identified with the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) of $G$ over the complex numbers, $\mathbb{C}[G]$. [Modules](wikipedia://en/276410) of this ring are the same thing as representations. [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007) implies that $\mathbb{C}[G]$ is a [semisimple ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_ring), so by the [Artin–Wedderburn theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artin–Wedderburn_theorem) it decomposes as a [direct product](wikipedia://en/25063) of [matrix rings](wikipedia://en/487541). The Fourier transform on finite groups explicitly exhibits this decomposition, with a matrix ring of dimension $d_\varrho$ for each irreducible representation. More specifically, the [Peter-Weyl theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter-Weyl_theorem) (for finite groups) states that there is an isomorphism

$$\mathbb C[G] \cong \bigoplus_{i} \mathrm{End}(V_i)$$

given by

$$\sum_{g \in G} a_g g \mapsto \left(\sum a_g \rho_i(g): V_i \to V_i\right)$$

The left hand side is the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223) of *G*. The direct sum is over a complete set of inequivalent irreducible *G*-representations $\varrho_i : G \to \mathrm{GL}(V_i)$.

The Fourier transform for a finite group is just this isomorphism. The product formula mentioned above is equivalent to saying that this map is a [ring isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_isomorphism).

## Over other fields {#Over_other_fields}

The above representation theoretic decomposition can be generalized to fields $k$ other than $\mathbb{C}$ as long as $\text{char}(k) \nmid |G|$ via [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007). That is, the group algebra $k[G]$ is semisimple. The same formulas may be used for the Fourier transform and its inverse, as crucially $\frac{1}{|G|}$ is defined in $k$.

## Modular case {#Modular_case}

When $\text{char}(k) \mid |G|$, $k[G]$ is no longer semisimple and one must consider the modular representation theory of $G$ over $k$. We can still decompose the group algebra into blocks via the [Peirce decomposition](wikipedia://en/30189901) using idempotents. That is

$$k[G] \cong \bigoplus_i k[G]e_i$$

and $1 = \sum_i e_i$ is a decomposition of the identity into central, primitive, orthogonal idempotents. Choosing a basis for the blocks $\text{span}_k \{g e_i | g \in G\}$ and writing the projection maps $v \mapsto v e_i$ as a matrix yields the modular [DFT matrix](wikipedia://en/1090930).[^o13]

For example, for the symmetric group the idempotents of $F_p[S_n]$ are computed in Murphy[^o14] and explicitly in SageMath.[^o15]

## Unitarity {#Unitarity}

One can normalize the above definition to obtain

$$\hat{f}(\rho)=\sqrt{\frac{d_\rho}{|G|}}\sum_{g \in G}f(g)\rho(g)$$

with inverse

$f(g)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{|G|}}\sum_{\rho \in \widehat{G}}\sqrt{d_\rho}\mathrm{Tr}(\hat{f}(\rho)\rho^{-1}(g))$.[^o17]

Two representations are considered equivalent if one may be obtained from the other by a change of basis. This is an equivalence relation, and each equivalence class contains a unitary representation. The unitary representations can be obtained via Weyl's [unitarian trick](wikipedia://en/1569065) in characteristic zero. If $\widehat{G}$ consists of unitary representations, then the corresponding DFT will be unitary.

Over finite fields $F_{q^2}$, it is possible to find a change of basis in certain cases, for example the symmetric group, by decomposing the matrix $U$ associated to a $G$-invariant [symmetric bilinear form](wikipedia://en/1803375) as $U=AA^*$, where $^*$ denotes conjugate-transpose with respect to $x \mapsto x^q$ conjugation. The unitary representation is given by $A^*\rho(g)A^{* -1}$. To obtain the unitary DFT, note that as defined above $DFT.DFT^* = S$, where $S$ is a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) consisting of +1's and -1's. We can factor $S=RR^*$ by factoring each sign $c_i = z_i z_i^*$. $uDFT = R^{-1}.DFT$ is unitary.

## Applications {#Applications}

This generalization of the discrete Fourier transform is used in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506). A [circulant matrix](wikipedia://en/889936) is a matrix where every column is a [cyclic shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_shift) of the previous one. Circulant matrices can be [diagonalized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_diagonalization) quickly using the [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512), and this yields a fast method for solving [systems of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087) with circulant matrices. Similarly, the Fourier transform on arbitrary groups can be used to give fast algorithms for matrices with other symmetries (Åhlander Munthe-Kaas 2005). These algorithms can be used for the construction of [numerical methods for solving partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_partial_differential_equations) that preserve the symmetries of the equations (Munthe-Kaas 2006).

When applied to the [Boolean group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_group) $(\mathbb Z / 2 \mathbb Z)^n$, the Fourier transform on this group is the [Hadamard transform](wikipedia://en/634765), which is commonly used in [quantum computing](wikipedia://en/25220) and other fields. [Shor's algorithm](wikipedia://en/42674) uses both the Hadamard transform (by applying a [Hadamard gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard_gate) to every qubit) as well as the [quantum Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/30872292). The former considers the qubits as indexed by the group $(\mathbb Z / 2 \mathbb Z)^n$ and the later considers them as indexed by $\mathbb Z / 2^n \mathbb Z$ for the purpose of the Fourier transform on finite groups.[^o22]
