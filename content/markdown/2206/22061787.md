# Magnus expansion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Magnus expansion"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the **Magnus expansion**, named after [Wilhelm Magnus](wikipedia://en/8337666) (1907–1990), provides an exponential representation of the [product integral](wikipedia://en/11602384) solution of a first-order homogeneous [linear differential equation](wikipedia://en/379868) for a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator). In particular, it furnishes the [fundamental matrix](wikipedia://en/10125619) of a system of linear [ordinary differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_differential_equations) of order $n$ with varying coefficients. The exponent is aggregated as an infinite series, whose terms involve multiple integrals and nested commutators.

:::

## The deterministic case {#The_deterministic_case}

### Magnus approach and its interpretation {#Magnus_approach_and_its_interpretation}

Given the $n \times n$ [coefficient matrix](wikipedia://en/4636611) $A(t)$, one wishes to solve the [initial-value problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial-value_problem) associated with the linear ordinary differential equation

$$Y'(t) = A(t) Y(t), \quad Y(t_0) = Y_0$$

for the unknown $n$-dimensional vector function $Y(t)$.

When *n* = 1, the solution is given as a [product integral](wikipedia://en/11602384)

$$Y(t) = \exp \left( \int_{t_0}^t A(s)\,ds \right) Y_0.$$

This is still valid for *n* > 1 if the matrix $A(t)$ satisfies $A(t_{1}) A(t_{2}) = A(t_{2}) A(t_{1})$ for any pair of values of *t*, *t*<sub>1</sub> and *t*<sub>2</sub>. In particular, this is the case if the matrix $A$ is independent of $t$. In the general case, however, the expression above is no longer the solution of the problem.

The approach introduced by Magnus to solve the matrix initial-value problem is to express the solution by means of the exponential of a certain $n \times n$ matrix function $\Omega(t, t_{0})$:

$$Y(t) = \exp\big(\Omega(t, t_0)\big) \, Y_0,$$

which is subsequently constructed as a [series](wikipedia://en/15287) expansion:

$$\Omega(t) = \sum_{k=1}^\infty \Omega_k(t),$$

where, for simplicity, it is customary to write $\Omega(t)$ for $\Omega(t, t_{0})$ and to take *t*<sub>0</sub> = 0.

Magnus appreciated that, since $\tfrac{d}{dt} (e^{\Omega}) e^{-\Omega} = A(t)$, using a [Poincaré−Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/44135401) matrix identity, he could relate the [time derivative](wikipedia://en/1783069) of $\Omega$ to the [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) of [Bernoulli numbers](wikipedia://en/4964#Generating_function) and the [adjoint endomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_algebra) of $\Omega$,

$$\Omega' = \frac{\operatorname{ad}_\Omega}{\exp(\operatorname{ad}_\Omega) - 1} A,$$

to solve for $\Omega$ recursively in terms of $A$ "in a continuous analog of the [BCH expansion](wikipedia://en/285422)", as outlined in a subsequent section.

The equation above constitutes the **Magnus expansion**, or **Magnus series**, for the solution of matrix linear initial-value problem. The first four terms of this series read

$$\begin{aligned} \Omega_1(t) &= \int_0^t A(t_1)\,dt_1, \\ \Omega_2(t) &= \frac{1}{2} \int_0^t dt_1 \int_0^{t_1} dt_2 \, [A(t_1), A(t_2)], \\ \Omega_3(t) &= \frac{1}{6} \int_0^t dt_1 \int_0^{t_1} dt_2 \int_0^{t_2} dt_3 \, \Bigl(\big[A(t_1), [A(t_2), A(t_3)]\big] + \big[A(t_3), [A(t_2), A(t_1)]\big]\Bigr), \\ \Omega_4(t) &= \frac{1}{12} \int_0^t dt_1 \int_0^{t_1}d t_2 \int_0^{t_2} dt_3 \int_0^{t_3} dt_4\, \left(\Big[\big[[A_1, A_2], A_3\big], A_4\Big]\right. \\ &\qquad + \Big[A_1, \big[[A_2, A_3], A_4\big]\Big] + \Big[A_1, \big[A_2, [A_3, A_4]\big]\Big] +\left. \Big[A_2, \big[A_3, [A_4, A_1]\big]\Big]\right), \end{aligned}$$

where $[A, B] \equiv A B - B A$ is the matrix [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193) of *A* and *B*.

These equations may be interpreted as follows: $\Omega_{1}(t)$ coincides exactly with the exponent in the scalar ($n$ = 1) case, but this equation cannot give the whole solution. If one insists in having an exponential representation ([Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945)), the exponent needs to be corrected. The rest of the Magnus series provides that correction systematically: $\Omega$ or parts of it are in the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of the [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) on the solution.

In applications, one can rarely sum exactly the Magnus series, and one has to truncate it to get approximate solutions. The main advantage of the Magnus proposal is that the truncated series very often shares important qualitative properties with the exact solution, similarly to other conventional [perturbation](wikipedia://en/102140) theories. For instance, in [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) the [symplectic](wikipedia://en/294298) character of the [time evolution](wikipedia://en/937739) is preserved at every order of approximation. Similarly, the [unitary](wikipedia://en/225999) character of the time evolution operator in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) is also preserved (in contrast, e.g., to the [Dyson series](wikipedia://en/1409609) solving the same problem).

### Convergence of the expansion {#Convergence_of_the_expansion}

From a mathematical point of view, the [convergence problem](wikipedia://en/8658125) is the following: given a certain matrix $A(t)$, when can the exponent $\Omega(t)$ be obtained as the sum of the Magnus series?

A sufficient condition for this series to [converge](wikipedia://en/1743431) for $t \in [0,T)$ is

$$\int_0^T \|A(s)\|_2 \, ds < \pi,$$

where $\| \cdot \|_2$ denotes a [matrix norm](wikipedia://en/1543735). This result is generic in the sense that one may construct specific matrices $A(t)$ for which the series diverges for any $t > T$.

### Magnus generator {#Magnus_generator}

A recursive procedure to generate all the terms in the Magnus expansion utilizes the matrices $S_{n}^{(k)}$ defined recursively through

$$S_n^{(j)} = \sum_{m=1}^{n-j} \left[\Omega_m, S_{n-m}^{(j-1)}\right], \quad 2 \leq j \leq n - 1,$$

$$S_n^{(1)} = \left[\Omega_{n-1}, A\right], \quad S_n^{(n-1)} = \operatorname{ad}_{\Omega_1}^{n-1}(A),$$

which then furnish

$$\Omega_1 = \int_0^t A(\tau) \, d\tau,$$

$$\Omega_n = \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \frac{B_j}{j!} \int_0^t S_n^{(j)}(\tau) \, d\tau , \quad n \geq 2.$$

Here ad<sup>*k*</sup><sub>Ω</sub> is a shorthand for an iterated commutator (see [adjoint endomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_algebra)):

$$\operatorname{ad}_{\Omega}^0 A = A, \quad \operatorname{ad}_{\Omega}^{k+1} A = [\Omega, \operatorname{ad}_\Omega^k A],$$

while $B_{j}$ are the [Bernoulli numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernoulli_numbers) with $B_{1} = -1/2$.

Finally, when this recursion is worked out explicitly, it is possible to express $\Omega_{n}(t)$ as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of *n*-fold integrals of *n* − 1 nested commutators involving $n$ matrices $A$:

$$\Omega_n(t) = \sum_{j=1}^{n-1} \frac{B_j}{j!} \sum_{k_1 + \cdots + k_j = n-1 \atop k_1 \ge 1, \ldots, k_j \ge 1} \int_0^t \operatorname{ad}_{\Omega_{k_1}(\tau)} \operatorname{ad}_{\Omega_{k_2}(\tau )} \cdots \operatorname{ad}_{\Omega_{k_j}(\tau)} A(\tau) \, d\tau, \quad n \ge 2,$$

which becomes increasingly intricate with $n$.

### Canonical Representation {#Canonical_Representation}

A non-recursive expression for all the terms in the Magnus expansion can also be formulated by encoding the combinatorics of the recursion in a single integral. This was found by Bialynicki-Birula, Mielnik, and Plebañski in 1969.[^o7] The [iterative solution](wikipedia://en/15237) Y(t,t\_0) to the initial value problem can be written as:$Y(t,t_0) = I + \sum_{n=1}^\infty \int_{t_0}^t dt_n \dots \int_{t_0}^{t}A(t_n)\theta_{n,n-1} dt_1 A(t_{n-1}) \dots \theta_{2,1} A(t_1) \qquad \theta_{k,l} = \begin{cases}1, \quad t_k \geq t_l \\ 0, \quad t_k < t_l\end{cases}$Then, this representation can be used to derive the [canonical representation](wikipedia://en/515096) of $\Omega_n$, using a function $\Theta_n(t_1, \dots, t_n)$ which counts the time ordering, and thus encodes the combinatorial structure of the expansion:

$$\Theta_n(t_1,t_2, \dots, t_n) = \theta_{n,n-1} + \theta_{n-1,n-2} + \dots \theta_{2,1}$$

$$\Omega_n = \frac{1}{n!} \int_{t_0}^t dt_n \dots \int_{t_0}^{t} dt_1 (-1)^{n-\Theta - 1} \Theta! (n-\Theta-1)! A(t_n)\cdots A(t_1)$$

This format is advantageous in many applications due to its independent limits of integration and closed form for an arbitrary degree.[^o8][^o9]

## The stochastic case {#The_stochastic_case}

### Extension to stochastic ordinary differential equations {#Extension_to_stochastic_ordinary_differential_equations}

For the extension to the stochastic case let $\left(W_t\right)_{t\in [0,T]}$ be a $\mathbb{R}^q$-dimensional [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436), $q\in \mathbb{N}_{>0}$, on the [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) $\left(\Omega,\mathcal{F},\mathbb{P}\right)$ with finite time horizon $T>0$ and [natural filtration](wikipedia://en/5864648). Now, consider the linear matrix-valued stochastic Itô differential equation (with Einstein's [summation convention](wikipedia://en/195407) over the index $j$)

$$dX_t = B_t X_t dt + A_t^{(j)} X_t dW_t^j,\quad X_0=I_d,\qquad d\in\mathbb{N}_{>0},$$

where $B_{\cdot},A_{\cdot}^{(1)},\dots,A_{\cdot}^{(j)}$ are progressively measurable $d\times d$-valued bounded [stochastic processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_processes) and $I_d$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). Following the same approach as in the deterministic case with alterations due to the stochastic setting[^o12] the corresponding matrix logarithm will turn out as an Itô-process, whose first two expansion orders are given by $Y_t^{(1)}=Y_t^{(1,0)}+Y_t^{(0,1)}$ and $Y_t^{(2)}=Y_t^{(2,0)}+Y_t^{(1,1)}+Y_t^{(0,2)}$, where with Einstein's summation convention over $i$ and $j$

$$\begin{aligned} Y^{(0,0)}_t &= 0,\\ Y^{(1,0)}_t &= \int_0^t A^{(j)}_s \, d W^j_s ,\\ Y^{(0,1)}_t &= \int_0^t B_s \, d s,\\ Y^{(2,0)}_t &= - \frac{1}{2} \int_0^t \big(A^{(j)}_s\big)^2 \, d s + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^t \Big[ A^{(j)}_s , \int_0^s A^{(i)}_r \, d W^i_r \Big] d W^j_s ,\\ Y^{(1,1)}_t &= \frac{1}{2} \int_0^t \Big[ B_s , \int_0^s A^{(j)}_r \, d W_r \Big] \, ds + \frac{1}{2} \int_0^t \Big[ A^{(j)}_s ,\int_0^s B_r \, dr \Big] \, dW^j_s,\\ Y^{(0,2)}_t &= \frac{1}{2} \int_0^t \Big[ B_s , \int_0^s B_r \, dr \Big] \, ds. \end{aligned}$$

### Convergence of the expansion {#Convergence_of_the_expansion}

In the stochastic setting the convergence will now be subject to a [stopping time](wikipedia://en/1202193) $\tau$ and a first convergence result is given by:[^o14]

Under the previous assumption on the coefficients there exists a strong solution $X=(X_t)_{t\in[0,T]}$, as well as a strictly positive stopping time $\tau\leq T$ such that:

1. $X_t$ has a real logarithm $Y_t$ up to time $\tau$, i.e.

$$X_t = e^{Y_t},\qquad 0\leq t<\tau;$$

1. the following representation holds $\mathbb{P}$-[almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908):

$$Y_t = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} Y^{(n)}_t,\qquad 0\leq t<\tau,$$

where $Y^{(n)}$ is the $n$-th term in the stochastic Magnus expansion as defined below in the subsection Magnus expansion formula;

1. there exists a positive constant $C$, only dependent on $\|A^{(1)}\|_{T},\dots,\|A^{(q)}\|_{T}, \|B\|_{T}, T, d$, with $\|A_{\cdot}\|_T=\|\|A_t\|_{F}\|_{L^{\infty}(\Omega\times [0,T])}$, such that

$$\mathbb{P} (\tau \leq t) \leq C t,\qquad t\in[0,T].$$

### Magnus expansion formula {#Magnus_expansion_formula}

The general expansion formula for the stochastic Magnus expansion is given by:

$$Y_t = \sum_{n=0}^{\infty} Y^{(n)}_t \quad \text{with}\quad Y^{(n)}_t := \sum_{r=0}^{n} Y^{(r,n-r)}_t,$$

where the general term $Y^{(r,n-r)}$ is an Itô-process of the form:

$$Y^{(r,n-r)}_t = \int_0^t \mu^{r,n-r}_s d s + \int_0^t \sigma^{r,n-r,j}_s d W^j_s, \qquad n\in \mathbb{N}_0, \ r=0,\dots,n,$$

The terms $\sigma^{r,n-r,j},\mu^{r,n-r}$ are defined recursively as

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma^{r,n-r,j}_s &:= \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\beta_i}{i!} S^{r-1,n-r,i}_s\big(A^{(j)}\big),\\ \mu^{r,n-r}_s &:= \sum_{i=0}^{n-1}\frac{\beta_i}{i!} S^{r,n-r-1,i}_s(B) - \frac{1}{2} \sum_{j=1}^q \sum_{i=0}^{{ n-2}}\frac{\beta_i}{i!} \sum_{q_1=2}^{{ r }} \sum_{q_2=0}^{{ n-r}} S^{r-q_1,n-r-q_2,i} \big( Q^{q_1,q_2,j} \big), \end{aligned}$$

with

$$\begin{aligned} Q^{q_1,q_2,j}_s := \sum_{i_1=2}^{q_1}\sum_{i_2=0}^{q_2} \sum_{h_1=1}^{i_1-1} \sum_{h_2=0}^{i_2} &\sum_{p_1=0}^{q_1-i_1} \sum_{{p_2}=0}^{q_2-i_2}\ \sum_{m_1=0}^{p_1+p_2} \ \sum_{{m_2}=0}^{q_1-i_1-p_1+q_2-i_2-p_2} \\ & \Bigg({ \frac{S_s^{p_1,p_2,m_1}\big(\sigma^{h_1,h_2,j}_s\big)}{({m_1}+1)!} \frac{ S_s^{q_1-i_1-p_1,q_2-i_2-p_2,m_2} \big(\sigma^{i_1-h_1,i_2-h_2,j}_s\big)}{({m_2}+1)!} } \\ & \qquad\qquad + {\frac{ \big[S_s^{p_1,p_2,m_1}\big(\sigma^{i_1-h_1,i_2-h_2,j}_s\big),S_s^{q_1-i_1-p_1,q_2-i_2-p_2,m_2}\big(\sigma^{h_1,h_2,j}_s\big)\big] }{ ({m_1}+{m_2}+2)({m_1}+1)!{m_2}! } } \Bigg), \end{aligned}$$

and with the operators $S$ being defined as

$$\begin{aligned} S^{r-1,n-r,0}_s(A) &:= \begin{cases} A & \text{if } r=n=1,\\ 0 & \text{otherwise}, \end{cases}\\ S^{r-1,n-r,i}_s(A) &:= \sum_{\begin{array}{c}(j_1,k_1),\dots,(j_i,k_i) \in\mathbb{N}_0^2 \\ j_1 + \cdots + j_i = r-1 \\ k_1+ \cdots +k_{i} = n-r \end{array}} \big[Y^{(j_1,k_1)}_s , \big[ \dots , \big[ Y^{(j_i,k_i)}_s, A_s \big] \dots \big] \big] \\ &= \sum_{\begin{array}{c}(j_1,k_1),\dots,(j_i,k_i) \in\mathbb{N}_0^2 \\ j_1 + \cdots + j_i = r-1 \\ k_1+ \cdots k_{i} = n-r \end{array}} \operatorname{ad}_{Y^{(j_1,k_1)}_s} \circ \cdots \circ \operatorname{ad}_{Y^{(j_i,k_i)}_s}(A_s) , \qquad i\in\mathbb{N}. \end{aligned}$$

## Applications {#Applications}

Since the 1960s, the Magnus expansion has been successfully applied as a perturbative tool in numerous areas of physics and chemistry, from [atomic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_physics) and [molecular physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_physics) to [nuclear magnetic resonance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_magnetic_resonance)[^o19] and [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268).[^o20] It has been also used since 1998 as a tool to construct practical algorithms for the [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089) of matrix linear differential equations. As they inherit from the Magnus expansion the preservation of qualitative traits of the problem, the corresponding schemes are prototypical examples of [geometric numerical integrators](wikipedia://en/2208188).
