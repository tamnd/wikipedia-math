# Knowledge space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Knowledge space"}

In [mathematical psychology](wikipedia://en/2081516) and [education theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Education_theory), a **knowledge space** is a [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) [structure](wikipedia://en/8519) used to formulate [mathematical models](wikipedia://en/20590) describing the progression of a human [learner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning).[^o2] Knowledge spaces were introduced in 1985 by [Jean-Paul Doignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Paul_Doignon) and [Jean-Claude Falmagne](wikipedia://en/31885421),[^o3] and remain in extensive use in the education theory.[^o4][^o5] Modern applications include two [computerized tutoring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_tutoring) systems, [ALEKS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ALEKS)[^o6] and the defunct [RATH](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RATH).[^o7]

:::

Formally, a knowledge space assumes that a domain of knowledge is a [collection](wikipedia://en/26691) of concepts or skills, each of which must be eventually [mastered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mastery). Not all concepts are interchangeable; some require other concepts as prerequisites. Conversely, [competency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Competence_%28human_resources%29) at one skill may ease the acquisition of another through similarity. A knowledge space marks out which collections of skills are *feasible*: they can be learned without mastering any other skills. Under reasonable assumptions, the collection of feasible competencies forms the mathematical structure known as an [antimatroid](wikipedia://en/667063).

Researchers and educators usually explore the structure of a discipline's knowledge space as a [latent class model](wikipedia://en/4094720).[^o8]

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Knowledge Space Theory attempts to address shortcomings of [standardized testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standardized_test) when used in educational [psychometry](wikipedia://en/24982). Common tests, such as the [SAT](wikipedia://en/144716) and [ACT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ACT_%28test%29), compress a student's knowledge into a very small range of [ordinal](wikipedia://en/52904244) [ranks](wikipedia://en/1482394), in the process effacing the conceptual dependencies between questions. Consequently, the tests cannot distinguish between true understanding and [guesses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guessing), nor can they identify a student's particular weaknesses, only the general proportion of skills mastered. The goal of knowledge space theory is to provide a language by which [exams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exam) can communicate[^o10]

- *What the student can do* and
- *What the student is ready to learn*.

## Model structure {#Model_structure}

Knowledge Space Theory-based models presume that an [educational subject](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject_%28documents%29) $S$ can be modeled as a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) $Q$ of [concepts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concept), skills, or topics. Each *feasible state of knowledge* about $S$ is then a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $Q$; the set of all such feasible states is $K$. The precise term for the information $(Q, K)$ depends on the extent to which $K$ satisfies certain [axioms](wikipedia://en/928):

- A **knowledge structure** assumes that $K$ contains the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) (a student may know nothing about $S$) and $Q$ itself (a student may have fully mastered $S$).
- A **knowledge space** is a knowledge structure that is closed under [set union](wikipedia://en/47949): if, for each topic, there is an expert in a [class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Form_%28education%29) on that topic, then it is possible, with enough time and effort, for each student in the class to become an expert on all those topics simultaneously.
- A **quasi-ordinal knowledge space** is a knowledge space that is also closed under [set intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429): if student $a$ knows topics $A$ and $B$; and student $c$ knows topics $B$ and $C$; then it is possible for another student $b$ to know only topic $B$.
- A **well-graded knowledge space** or **learning space** is a knowledge space satisfying the following axiom: > If $S\in K$, then there exists $x\in S$ such that $S\backslash\{x\}\in K$ In educational terms, any feasible body of knowledge can be learned one concept at a time.

### Prerequisite partial order {#Prerequisite_partial_order}

The more contentful axioms associated with quasi-ordinal and well-graded knowledge spaces each imply that the knowledge space forms a well-understood (and heavily studied) mathematical structure:

- A quasi-ordinal knowledge space can be associated with a [distributive lattice](wikipedia://en/310883) under set union and set intersection. The name "quasi-ordinal" arises from [Birkhoff's representation theorem](wikipedia://en/20489385), which explains that distributive lattices [uniquely correspond](wikipedia://en/3942) to [partial orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order).
- A well-graded knowledge space is an [antimatroid](wikipedia://en/667063), a type of mathematical structure that describes certain problems solvable with a [greedy algorithm](wikipedia://en/89247).

In either case, the mathematical structure implies that [set inclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Set_inclusion) defines [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_Order) on $K$, interpretable as an [educational prerequirement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curriculum#Higher_education): if $a(\preceq)b$ in this partial order, then $a$ must be learned before $b$.

### Inner and outer fringe {#Inner_and_outer_fringe}

The prerequisite partial order does not uniquely identify a [curriculum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curriculum); some concepts may lead to a variety of other possible topics. But the [covering relation](wikipedia://en/14835049) associated with the prerequisite partial does control curricular structure: if students know $a$ before a lesson and $b$ immediately after, then $b$ must cover $a$ in the partial order. In such a circumstance, the new topics covered between $a$ and $b$ constitute the **outer fringe** of $a$ ("what the student was ready to learn") and the **inner fringe** of $b$ ("what the student just learned").

## Construction of knowledge spaces {#Construction_of_knowledge_spaces}

In practice, there exist several methods to construct knowledge spaces. The most frequently used method is querying experts. There exist several querying algorithms that allow one or several experts to construct a knowledge space by answering a sequence of simple questions.[^o15][^o16][^o17]

Another method is to construct the knowledge space by explorative data analysis (for example by [item tree analysis](wikipedia://en/16569923)) from data.[^o18][^o19] A third method is to derive the knowledge space from an analysis of the problem solving processes in the corresponding domain.[^o20]
