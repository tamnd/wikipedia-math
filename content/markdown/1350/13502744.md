# Babuška–Lax–Milgram theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Babuška–Lax–Milgram theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Babuška–Lax–Milgram theorem** is a generalization of the famous [Lax–Milgram theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lax–Milgram_theorem), which gives conditions under which a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) can be "inverted" to show the existence and uniqueness of a [weak solution](wikipedia://en/3555416) to a given [boundary value problem](wikipedia://en/436779). The result was proved by J. Nečas in 1962, and is a generalization of the famous Lax-Milgram theorem by [Peter Lax](wikipedia://en/486365) and [Arthur Milgram](wikipedia://en/21029542).

:::

## Background {#Background}

In the modern, [functional-analytic](wikipedia://en/11180) approach to the study of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations), one does not attempt to solve a given partial differential equation directly, but by using the structure of the [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of possible solutions, e.g. a [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) *W*<sup>*k*,*p*</sup>. Abstractly, consider two [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [normed spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) *U* and *V* with their [continuous dual spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) *U*<sup>∗</sup> and *V*<sup>∗</sup> respectively. In many applications, *U* is the space of possible solutions; given some [partial differential operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) Λ : *U* → *V*<sup>∗</sup> and a specified element *f* ∈ *V*<sup>∗</sup>, the objective is to find a *u* ∈ *U* such that

$$\Lambda u = f.$$

However, in the [weak formulation](wikipedia://en/3368981), this equation is only required to hold when "tested" against all other possible elements of *V*. This "testing" is accomplished by means of a bilinear function *B* : *U* × *V* → **R** which encodes the differential operator Λ; a *weak solution* to the problem is to find a *u* ∈ *U* such that

$$B(u, v) = \langle f, v \rangle \mbox{ for all } v \in V.$$

The achievement of Lax and Milgram in their 1954 result was to specify sufficient conditions for this weak formulation to have a unique solution that depends [continuously](wikipedia://en/6122) upon the specified datum *f* ∈ *V*<sup>∗</sup>: it suffices that *U* = *V* is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), that *B* is continuous, and that *B* is strongly [coercive](wikipedia://en/8971904), i.e.

$$| B(u, u) | \geq c \| u \|^{2}$$

for some constant *c* > 0 and all *u* ∈ *U*.

For example, in the solution of the [Poisson equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_equation) on a [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258), [open](wikipedia://en/39358) domain Ω ⊂ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>,

$$\begin{cases} - \Delta u(x) = f(x), & x \in \Omega; \\ u(x) = 0, & x \in \partial \Omega; \end{cases}$$

the space *U* could be taken to be the Sobolev space *H*<sub>0</sub><sup>1</sup>(Ω) with dual *H*<sup>−1</sup>(Ω); the former is a subspace of the [*L*<sup>*p*</sup> space](wikipedia://en/45194) *V* = *L*<sup>2</sup>(Ω); the bilinear form *B* associated to −Δ is the *L*<sup>2</sup>(Ω) [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) of the derivatives:

$$B(u, v) = \int_{\Omega} \nabla u(x) \cdot \nabla v(x) \, \mathrm{d} x.$$

Hence, the weak formulation of the Poisson equation, given *f* ∈ *L*<sup>2</sup>(Ω), is to find *u*<sub>*f*</sub> such that

$$\int_{\Omega} \nabla u_{f}(x) \cdot \nabla v(x) \, \mathrm{d} x = \int_{\Omega} f(x) v(x) \, \mathrm{d} x \mbox{ for all } v \in H_{0}^{1} (\Omega).$$

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of the theorem"}

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

In 1962, J. Nečas provided the following generalization of Lax and Milgram's earlier result, which begins by dispensing with the requirement that *U* and *V* be the same space. Let *U* and *V* be two real Hilbert spaces and let *B* : *U* × *V* → **R** be a continuous bilinear functional. Suppose also that *B* is weakly coercive: for some constant *c* > 0 and all *u* ∈ *U*,

$$\sup_{\| v \| = 1} | B(u, v) | \geq c \| u \|$$

and, for all 0 ≠ *v* ∈ *V*,

$$\sup_{\| u \| = 1} | B(u, v) | > 0$$

Then, for all *f* ∈ *V*<sup>∗</sup>, there exists a unique solution *u* = *u*<sub>*f*</sub> ∈ *U* to the weak problem

$$B(u_{f}, v) = \langle f, v \rangle \mbox{ for all } v \in V.$$

Moreover, the solution depends continuously on the given data:

$$\| u_{f} \| \leq \frac{1}{c} \| f \|.$$

Nečas' proof extends directly to the situation where $U$ is a Banach space and $V$ a reflexive Banach space.
