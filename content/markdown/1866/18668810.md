# Supernatural number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Supernatural number"}

*Image: Steinitz numbers svg.svg.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of the [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) of supernatural numbers; primes other than 2 and 3 are omitted for simplicity.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **supernatural numbers**, sometimes called **generalized natural numbers** or **Steinitz numbers**, are a generalization of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). They were used by [Ernst Steinitz](wikipedia://en/3966685)[^o2] in 1910 as a part of his work on [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29).

:::

A supernatural number $\omega$ is a [formal](wikipedia://en/3729927) [product](wikipedia://en/24977):

$$\omega = \prod_p p^{n_p},$$

where $p$ runs over all [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), and each $n_p$ is either zero, a natural number or [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824). This can be thought of as the [prime factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factorization) of the supernatural number. In the special case where only finitely many values $n_p$ are nonzero and no index $n_p$ is $\infty$, we get the positive natural numbers; for example, $60 = 2^2 \cdot 3 \cdot 5$ can be represented as

$$2^2 3^1 5^1 7^0 11^0 13^0\dots$$

There are also many supernatural numbers with no natural counterpart, such as

$$2^1 3^1 5^1 7^1 11^1 13^1\dots$$

and

$$2^1 3^\infty 5^0 7^0 11^0 13^0\dots$$

Sometimes the notation $v_p(\omega)$ is used instead of $n_p$. $v_p(\omega)$ can be seen as the [p-adic valuation](wikipedia://en/16047618) of $\omega$, and it agrees with the standard p-adic valuation on the natural numbers.

There is no natural way to add supernatural numbers, but they can be multiplied, with $\prod_p p^{n_p}\cdot\prod_p p^{m_p}=\prod_p p^{n_p+m_p}$. Similarly, the notion of divisibility extends to the supernaturals with $\omega_1\mid\omega_2$ if $v_p(\omega_1)\leq v_p(\omega_2)$ for all $p$. The notion of the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961) and [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) can also be generalized for supernatural numbers, by defining

$$\displaystyle \operatorname{lcm}(\{\omega_i\}) \displaystyle =\prod_p p^{\sup(v_p(\omega_i))}$$

and

$$\displaystyle \operatorname{gcd}(\{\omega_i\}) \displaystyle =\prod_p p^{\inf(v_p(\omega_i))}$$

.

With these definitions, the gcd or lcm of infinitely many natural numbers (or supernatural numbers) is a supernatural number.

Supernatural numbers are used to define orders and indices of [profinite groups](wikipedia://en/24266) and subgroups, in which case many of the theorems from [finite group theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_group_theory) carry over exactly. They are used to encode the [algebraic extensions](wikipedia://en/2125) of a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615).[^o3]

Supernatural numbers also arise in the classification of [uniformly hyperfinite algebras](wikipedia://en/16587536).
