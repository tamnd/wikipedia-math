# Lattice problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lattice problem"}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), **lattice problems** are a class of [optimization](wikipedia://en/52033) problems related to mathematical objects called *[lattices](wikipedia://en/469246)*. The conjectured [intractability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intractable_problem) of such problems is central to the construction of secure [lattice-based cryptosystems](wikipedia://en/18657553): lattice problems are an example of [NP-hard](wikipedia://en/54681) problems which have been shown to be [average-case hard](wikipedia://en/6158383), providing a test case for the security of cryptographic algorithms. In addition, some lattice problems which are worst-case hard can be used as a basis for extremely secure cryptographic schemes. The use of worst-case hardness in such schemes makes them among the very few schemes that are very likely secure even against [quantum computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computers). For applications in such [cryptosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptosystems), lattices over [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) (often $\mathbb{Q}^n$) or [free modules](wikipedia://en/326454) (often $\mathbb{Z}^n$) are generally considered.

:::

For all the problems below, assume that we are given (in addition to other more specific inputs) a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for the vector space *V* and a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) *N*. The norm usually considered is the Euclidean norm [*L*<sup>2</sup>](wikipedia://en/990534#Euclidean_norm). However, other norms (such as [*L*<sup>*p*</sup>](wikipedia://en/990534#p-norm)) are also considered and show up in a variety of results.[^o2]

Throughout this article, let $\lambda(L)$ denote the length of the shortest non-zero vector in the lattice *L*: that is,

$$\lambda(L) = \min_{v \in L \smallsetminus \{\mathbf{0}\}} \|v\|_N.$$

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Shortest vector problem"}

## Shortest vector problem (SVP) {#Shortest_vector_problem_(SVP)}

*Image: SVP.svg.* This is an illustration of the shortest vector problem (basis vectors in blue, shortest vector in red).

In the SVP, a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* and a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) *N* (often [*L*<sup>2</sup>](wikipedia://en/990534#Euclidean_norm)) are given for a lattice *L* and one must find the shortest non-zero vector in *V*, as measured by *N*, in *L*. In other words, the algorithm should output a non-zero vector *v* such that $\|v\|_N=\lambda(L)$. In the following, the size of the problem is specified by *n*, the [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of the vector space *V*.

In the γ-approximation version SVP<sub>γ</sub>, one must find a non-zero lattice vector of length at most $\gamma \cdot \lambda(L)$ for given $\gamma \geq 1$.

### Hardness results {#Hardness_results}

In 1998, [Miklós Ajtai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miklós_Ajtai) proved that exact SVP in the Euclidean norm is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) under randomized polynomial-time reductions.[^o57][^o6] Subsequent work established randomized hardness of approximation for every constant factor, as well as larger factors under stronger complexity assumptions. The corresponding problem with respect to the [uniform norm](wikipedia://en/349755) is known unconditionally to be [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard).[^o7]

In 2025, Isaac Hair and [Amit Sahai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amit_Sahai) proved that, for every fixed $p>2$ and $\varepsilon>0$, SVP in the $\ell_p$ norm is NP-hard under deterministic polynomial-time reductions even when approximation within a factor $2^{(\log n)^{1-\varepsilon}}$ is permitted.[^o8]

A 2026 preprint by [Daqing Wan](wikipedia://en/22098796) proves NP-hardness of exact Euclidean SVP and of Euclidean approximation within every factor smaller than $\sqrt 2$.[^o9]

### Algorithms for the Euclidean norm {#Algorithms_for_the_Euclidean_norm}

To solve the exact version of the SVP under the Euclidean norm, several different approaches are known, which can be split into two classes: algorithms requiring superexponential time ($2^{\omega(n)}$) and $\operatorname{poly}(n)$ memory, and algorithms requiring both exponential time and space ($2^{\Theta(n)}$) in the lattice dimension. The former class of algorithms most notably includes lattice enumeration[^o11][^o37][^o12] and random sampling reduction,[^o13][^o14] while the latter includes lattice sieving,[^o46][^o15][^o16] computing the Voronoi cell of the lattice,[^o39][^o17] and discrete Gaussian sampling.[^o18] An open problem is whether algorithms for solving exact SVP exist running in single exponential time ($2^{O(n)}$) and requiring memory scaling polynomially in the lattice dimension.[^o19]

To solve the γ-approximation version SVP<sub>γ</sub> for $\gamma > 1$ for the Euclidean norm, the best known approaches are based on using [lattice basis reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_basis_reduction). For large $\gamma = 2^{\Omega(n)}$, the [Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász (LLL) algorithm](wikipedia://en/1735228) can find a solution in time polynomial in the lattice dimension. For smaller values $\gamma$, the Block Korkine-Zolotarev algorithm (BKZ)[^o20][^o21][^o22] is commonly used, where the input to the algorithm (the blocksize $\beta$) determines the time complexity and output quality: for large approximation factors $\gamma$, a small block size $\beta$ suffices, and the algorithm terminates quickly. For small $\gamma$, larger $\beta$ are needed to find sufficiently short lattice vectors, and the algorithm takes longer to find a solution. The BKZ algorithm internally uses an exact SVP algorithm as a subroutine (running in lattices of dimension at most $\beta$), and its overall complexity is closely related to the costs of these SVP calls in dimension $\beta$.

## GapSVP {#GapSVP}

The problem GapSVP<sub>β</sub> consists of distinguishing between the instances of SVP in which the length of the shortest vector is at most $1$ or larger than $\beta$, where $\beta$ can be a fixed function of the dimension of the lattice $n$. Given a basis for the lattice, the algorithm must decide whether $\lambda(L) \leq 1$ or $\lambda(L)>\beta$. Like other [promise problems](wikipedia://en/1471429), the algorithm is allowed to err on all other cases.

Yet another version of the problem is GapSVP<sub>ζ,γ</sub> for some functions ζ and γ. The input to the algorithm is a basis $B$ and a number $d$. It is assured that all the vectors in the [Gram–Schmidt orthogonalization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram–Schmidt_orthogonalization) are of length at least 1, and that $\lambda(L(B)) \leq \zeta(n)$ and that $1 \leq d \leq \zeta(n)/\gamma(n)$, where $n$ is the dimension. The algorithm must accept if $\lambda(L(B)) \leq d$, and reject if $\lambda(L(B)) \geq \gamma(n) \cdot d$. For large $\zeta$ (i.e. $\zeta(n)>2^{n/2}$), the problem is equivalent to GapSVP<sub>γ</sub> because[^o25] a preprocessing done using the [LLL algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LLL_algorithm) makes the second condition (and hence, $\zeta$) redundant.

## Closest vector problem (CVP) {#Closest_vector_problem_(CVP)}

*Image: CVP.svg.* This is an illustration of the closest vector problem (basis vectors in blue, external vector in green, closest vector in red).

In CVP, a basis of a vector space *V* and a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29) *M* (often [*L*<sup>2</sup>](wikipedia://en/53932)) are given for a lattice *L*, as well as a vector *v* in *V* but not necessarily in *L*. It is desired to find the vector in *L* closest to *v* (as measured by *M*). In the $\gamma$-approximation version CVP<sub>γ</sub>, one must find a lattice vector at distance at most $\gamma$.

### Relationship with SVP {#Relationship_with_SVP}

The closest vector problem is a generalization of the shortest vector problem. It is easy to show that given an [oracle](wikipedia://en/22431) for CVP<sub>γ</sub> (defined below), one can solve SVP<sub>γ</sub> by making some queries to the oracle.[^o29] The naive method to find the shortest vector by calling the CVP<sub>γ</sub> oracle to find the closest vector to 0 does not work because 0 is itself a lattice vector and the algorithm could potentially output 0.

The reduction from SVP<sub>γ</sub> to CVP<sub>γ</sub> is as follows: Suppose that the input to the SVP<sub>γ</sub> is the basis for lattice $B=[b_1,b_2,\ldots,b_n]$. Consider the basis $B^i=[b_1,\ldots,2b_i,\ldots,b_n]$ and let $x_i$ be the vector returned by CVP<sub>γ</sub>(*B*<sup>*i*</sup>, *b*<sub>*i*</sub>). The claim is that the shortest vector in the set $\{x_i-b_i\}$ is the shortest vector in the given lattice.

### Hardness results {#Hardness_results}

Goldreich et al. showed that any hardness of SVP implies the same hardness for CVP.[^o31] Using [PCP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistically_checkable_proof_%28complexity%29) tools, [Arora](wikipedia://en/21513331) et al. showed that CVP is hard to approximate within factor $2^{\log^{1-\epsilon}(n)}$ unless $\operatorname{NP} \subseteq \operatorname{DTIME}(2^{\operatorname{poly}(\log n)})$.[^o32] Dinur et al. strengthened this by giving a NP-hardness result with $\epsilon=(\log \log n)^c$ for $c<1/2$.[^o33]

In 2026, [OpenAI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenAI) announced an unconditional proof that approximating the Euclidean CVP in dimension $n$ within a factor of $n^{1/400}$ is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard), and released a formalization of the result in [Lean](wikipedia://en/62889984).[^o34]

::: {#o36 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="LAMBDA method"}

### Sphere decoding {#Sphere_decoding}

Algorithms for CVP, especially the Fincke and Pohst variant,[^o37] have been used for data detection in multiple-input multiple-output ([MIMO](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MIMO)) wireless communication systems (for coded and uncoded signals).[^o38][^o39] In this context it is called *sphere decoding* due to the radius used internal to many CVP solutions.[^o40]

#### Integer ambiguity resolution of carrier-phase GNSS {#Integer_ambiguity_resolution_of_carrier-phase_GNSS}

It has been applied in the field of the integer ambiguity resolution of carrier-phase GNSS (GPS).[^o42] It is called the *[LAMBDA method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_J._G._Teunissen)* in that field. In the same field, the general CVP problem is referred to as *Integer Least Squares*.

## GapCVP {#GapCVP}

This problem is similar to the GapSVP problem. For GapSVP<sub>β</sub>, the input consists of a lattice basis and a vector $v$, and the algorithm must answer whether one of the following holds:

- there is a lattice vector such that the distance between it and $v$ is at most 1, and
- every lattice vector is at a distance greater than $\beta$ away from $v$.

The opposite condition is that the closest lattice vector is at a distance $1 < \lambda(L) \leq \beta$, hence the name *Gap*CVP.

### Known results {#Known_results}

The problem is trivially contained in [NP](wikipedia://en/21562) for any approximation factor.

[Schnorr](wikipedia://en/9439931), in 1987, showed that deterministic polynomial time algorithms can solve the problem for $\beta=2^{O(n(\log \log n)^2/\log n)}$.[^o45] Ajtai et al. showed that probabilistic algorithms can achieve a slightly better approximation factor of $\beta=2^{O(n \log \log n/\log n)}$.[^o46]

In 1993, Banaszczyk showed that GapCVP<sub>n</sub> is in $\mathsf{NP \cap coNP}$.[^o47] In 2000, Goldreich and Goldwasser showed that $\beta=\sqrt{n/\log n}$ puts the problem in both NP and [coAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CoAM).[^o48] In 2005, Aharonov and Regev showed that for some constant $c$, the problem with $\beta=c\sqrt{n}$ is in $\mathsf{NP \cap coNP}$.[^o49]

For lower bounds, Dinur et al. showed in 1998 that the problem is NP-hard for $\beta=n^{o(1/\log{\log{n}})}$.[^o50]

## Shortest independent vectors problem (SIVP) {#Shortest_independent_vectors_problem_(SIVP)}

Given a lattice L of dimension *n*, the algorithm must output *n* [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) $v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_n$ so that $\max \|v_i\| \leq \max_B \|b_i\|$, where the right-hand side considers all bases $B=\{b_1,\ldots,b_n\}$ of the lattice.

In the $\gamma$-approximate version, given a lattice L with dimension *n*, one must find *n* [linearly independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_independent) vectors $v_1, v_2,\ldots, v_n$ of length $\max \|v_i\| \le \gamma \lambda_n(L)$, where $\lambda_n(L)$ is the $n$<sup>th</sup> successive minimum of $L$.

## Bounded distance decoding {#Bounded_distance_decoding}

This problem is similar to CVP. Given a vector such that its distance from the lattice is at most $\lambda(L)/2$, the algorithm must output the closest lattice vector to it.

## Covering radius problem {#Covering_radius_problem}

Given a basis for the lattice, the algorithm must find the largest distance (or in some versions, its approximation) from any vector to the lattice.

## Shortest basis problem {#Shortest_basis_problem}

Many problems become easier if the input basis consists of short vectors. An algorithm that solves the Shortest Basis Problem (SBP) must, given a lattice basis $B$, output an equivalent basis $B'$ such that the length of the longest vector in $B'$ is as short as possible.

The approximation version SBP<sub>γ</sub> problem consist of finding a basis whose longest vector is at most $\gamma$ times longer than the longest vector in the shortest basis.

## Use in cryptography {#Use_in_cryptography}

[Average-case](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average_case) hardness of problems forms a basis for proofs-of-security for most cryptographic schemes. However, experimental evidence suggests that most NP-hard problems lack this property: they are probably only worst case hard. Many lattice problems have been conjectured or proven to be average-case hard, making them an attractive class of problems to base cryptographic schemes on. Moreover, worst-case hardness of some lattice problems have been used to create secure cryptographic schemes. The use of worst-case hardness in such schemes makes them among the very few schemes that are very likely secure even against [quantum computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computers).

The above lattice problems are easy to solve if the algorithm is provided with a "good" basis. [Lattice reduction](wikipedia://en/4786593) algorithms aim, given a basis for a lattice, to output a new basis consisting of relatively short, nearly [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) vectors. The [Lenstra–Lenstra–Lovász lattice basis reduction algorithm](wikipedia://en/1735228) (LLL) was an early efficient algorithm for this problem which could output an almost reduced lattice basis in polynomial time.[^o56] This algorithm and its further refinements were used to break several cryptographic schemes, establishing its status as a very important tool in [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715). The success of LLL on experimental data led to a belief that lattice reduction might be an easy problem in practice; however, this belief was challenged in the late 1990s, when several new results on the hardness of lattice problems were obtained, starting with the result of [Ajtai](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Miklós_Ajtai).[^o57]

In his seminal papers, Ajtai showed that the SVP problem was NP-hard and discovered some connections between the [worst-case complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worst-case_complexity) and [average-case complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Average-case_complexity) of some lattice problems.[^o57][^o6] Building on these results, Ajtai and [Dwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cynthia_Dwork) created a [public-key cryptosystem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public-key_cryptosystem) whose security could be proven using only the worst case hardness of a certain version of SVP,[^o58] thus making it the first result to have used worst-case hardness to create secure systems.[^o59]
