# Lam's problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lam's problem"}

In [finite geometry](wikipedia://en/267061), **Lam's problem** is the problem of determining if a [finite projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350#Finite_projective_planes) of order ten exists. The order ten case is the first theoretically uncertain case, as all smaller orders can be resolved by purely theoretical means.[^o2] Lam's problem is named after [Clement W. H. Lam](wikipedia://en/45187642) who experimentally determined that projective planes of order ten do not exist via exhaustive computational searches.[^o3]

:::

## Introduction {#Introduction}

A finite projective plane of order $n$ is a collection of points and lines such that

- any two points define a unique line,
- any two lines meet at a unique point,
- there are exactly $n+1$ points on every line, and
- there are exactly $n+1$ lines through every point.

A consequence of this definition is that a projective plane of order $n$ will contain $n^2+n+1$ points and $n^2+n+1$ lines. The incidence relation between points and lines may equivalently be described using an [incidence matrix](wikipedia://en/420919). In this context a projective plane of order $n$ is equivalent to a $(n^2+n+1)\times(n^2+n+1)$ [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) with $\{0,1\}$ entries such that every row and column has $n+1$ ones and the inner product between any two rows or columns is exactly $1$.

Using the incidence matrix representation, Lam's problem is equivalent to determining if there is a way of placing 0s and 1s in a $111\times111$ matrix such that there are exactly eleven 1s in each row and column and any pair of rows share a single 1 in the same column.[^o5]

[Lam](wikipedia://en/45187642) considered studying the existence of a projective plane of order ten in his PhD thesis but was dissuaded by his thesis advisor [H. J. Ryser](wikipedia://en/20825197) who believed the problem was too difficult.[^o3]

## Resolution {#Resolution}

Edward Assmus presented a connection between projective planes and [coding theory](wikipedia://en/321869) at the conference *Combinatorial Aspects of Finite Geometries* in 1970.[^o7] He studied the code generated by the rows of the incidence matrix of a hypothetical projective plane of order ten and derived a number of restrictive properties that such a code must satisfy. In particular, the [enumerator polynomial](wikipedia://en/1864484) of the code is completely determined by the number of words of [weights](wikipedia://en/1127884) 12, 15, and 16 in the code.

Over the next two decades a number of computer searches showed that the hypothetical code associated with the projective plane of order ten does not contain words of weights 15,[^o8] 12,[^o9] and 16[^o10]—which implied that it must contain words of weight 19. Finally, Clement Lam, Larry Thiel and Stanley Swiercz used about three months of time on a [Cray-1A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cray-1) supercomputer to show that words of weight 19 are also not present in the code.[^o11] This resolved Lam's problem in the negative. Their result was independently verified in 2021 by using a [SAT solver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SAT_solver) to generate computer-verifiable certificates for the correctness of the exhaustive searches.[^o12]
