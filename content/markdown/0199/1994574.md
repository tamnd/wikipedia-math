# D. G. Champernowne

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="D. G. Champernowne"}

**David Gawen Champernowne**, (9 July 1912 – 19 August 2000)[^o3][^o13][^o4] was an English [economist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economist) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902).

:::

## Early life and family {#Early_life_and_family}

Champernowne was the only child of Francis Gawayne Champernowne (1866–1921), [M.A. (Oxon.)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts_%28Oxford,_Cambridge,_and_Dublin%29), a barrister and [bursar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bursar) of [Keble College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keble_College,_Oxford), and his wife Isabel Mary, daughter of George Rashleigh, of Riseley, [Horton Kirby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horton_Kirby), [Kent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kent).[^o6] The Champernowne family were landed gentry, of [Dartington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartington), [Devon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devon); Francis Gawayne Champernowne was a grandson of Arthur Champernowne (1767–1819), [M.P.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saltash_%28UK_Parliament_constituency%29#MPs_1640–1832) for [Saltash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saltash) in 1806, who, born to Rev. Richard Harington, second son of [Sir James Harington, 6th Baronet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harington_baronets), had taken his maternal grandfather's name on inheriting his estates.[^o7][^o8]

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Champernowne was educated at [Winchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winchester_College) and [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge), where he was a contemporary and friend of [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208). After academic work there and at the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics), he was drafted into the statistical section of the prime minister's office at the beginning of the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War) to supply quantitative information to help [Winston Churchill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winston_Churchill) make decisions; then, in 1941, he moved on to become a programme director in the Ministry of Aircraft Production.

He was a Fellow of [Nuffield College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuffield_College,_Oxford), Director of the Oxford Institute of Statistics during 1945–1948, and [Professor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Professor) of Statistical Economics at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford) (1948–1959), and Professor of Economics and Statistics (later Emeritus) at the [University of Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge) (1970–2000).[^o3][^o13]

In 1953, he published a model of income distribution that generates the [Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057).[^o10][^o11]

He published work on what is now called the [Champernowne constant](wikipedia://en/541351) in 1933, whilst still an undergraduate at Cambridge.[^o3][^o13] In 1948, working with his old college friend [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208), he helped develop one of the first [chess-playing computer programs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess-playing_computer_program), *[Turochamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turochamp)*.[^o12] The book for which he is most renowned, synthesising a life's work, *Economic Inequality and Income Distribution* ([Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press)), was published in 1998.[^o3]

His co-editors at the *[Economic Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economic_Journal)* found him to be "modest, quirky and humorous".[^o13]

## Death {#Death}

Champernowne's grave is at the new church at [Dartington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartington) in [Devon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Devon), built by his family in the 1870s to replace the ancient church at [Dartington Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dartington_Hall), the family seat.
