# Subtended angle

*Image: subtended\_angle.svg.* Example of the angles subtended by an arc from two points

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Subtended angle"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) **subtended** (from [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin) for "stretched under") by a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) at an arbitrary [vertex](wikipedia://en/11308417) is formed by the two [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ray_%28geometry%29) between the vertex and each [endpoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endpoint_%28geometry%29) of the segment. For example, a [side](wikipedia://en/12158034) of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) *subtends* the opposite angle.

:::

More generally, an angle subtended by an [arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc_%28geometry%29) of a [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) is the angle subtended by the corresponding [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) of the arc. For example, a [circular arc](wikipedia://en/5578920) *subtends* the [central angle](wikipedia://en/462705) formed by the two [radii](wikipedia://en/1780815) through the arc endpoints.

If an angle is subtended by a straight or curved segment, the segment is said to ***subtend*** the angle. Sometimes the term "subtend" is applied in the opposite sense, and the angle is said to *subtend* the segment. Alternately, the angle can be said to ***intercept*** or ***enclose*** the segment.

The above definition of a subtended [plane angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_angle) remains valid in [three-dimensional space](wikipedia://en/3054853) (3D), as one vertex and two endpoints (assumed non-collinear) define an [Euclidean plane in 3D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_plane_in_3D). For example, an arc of a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) on a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) subtends a central plane angle, formed by the two radii between the center of the sphere and each of the two arc endpoints.

More generally, a [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970) *subtends* a [solid angle](wikipedia://en/94102) if its boundary defines the [cone](wikipedia://en/782427) of the angle.[^o2]

Many [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977) in geometry relate to subtended angles. If two sides of a triangle are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330), then the angles they subtend are also congruent, and conversely if two angles are congruent then they are subtended by congruent sides (propositions I.5–6 in [Euclid's *Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107)), forming an [isosceles triangle](wikipedia://en/315428). More generally, the [law of sines](wikipedia://en/113222) states that the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) of each angle of a triangle is proportional to the side subtending it. The [inscribed angle theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inscribed_angle_theorem) states that when the vertex of an angle inscribed in a circle lies on the same side of the chord subtending it as the center of the circle, then the central angle subtended by the same chord is twice the inscribed angle.

By extension, an angle subtended by a more complex geometric figure may be defined in terms of the figure's [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) and its [diameter](wikipedia://en/68283333); for example, the angle subtended by a tree as viewed in a camera (see [angular size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_size)).[^o3] A subtended plane angle can also be defined for any two arbitrary [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557) and a vertex, as in two [lines of sight](wikipedia://en/70797458) from a particular viewer; for example, the angle subtended by two stars as seen from Earth (see [angular separation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_separation)).[^o4]
