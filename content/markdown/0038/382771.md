# Seifert–Van Kampen theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Seifert–Van Kampen theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Seifert–Van Kampen theorem** of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801) (named after [Herbert Seifert](wikipedia://en/637024) and [Egbert van Kampen](wikipedia://en/8156533)), sometimes just called **Van Kampen's theorem**, expresses the structure of the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$ in terms of the fundamental groups of two open, [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected) [subspaces](wikipedia://en/910926) that [cover](wikipedia://en/317552) $X$. It can therefore be used for computations of the fundamental group of spaces that are constructed out of simpler ones.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Van Kampen's theorem for fundamental groups"}

## Van Kampen's theorem for fundamental groups {#Van_Kampen's_theorem_for_fundamental_groups}

Let *X* be a topological space which is the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of two open and path connected subspaces *U*<sub>1</sub>, *U*<sub>2</sub>. Suppose *U*<sub>1</sub> ∩ *U*<sub>2</sub> is path connected and [nonempty](wikipedia://en/9566), and let *x*<sub>0</sub> be a point in *U*<sub>1</sub> ∩ *U*<sub>2</sub> that will be used as the base of all fundamental groups. The inclusion maps of *U*<sub>1</sub> and *U*<sub>2</sub> into *X* [induce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_homomorphism_%28fundamental_group%29) [group homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) $j_1:\pi_1(U_1,x_0)\to \pi_1(X,x_0)$ and $j_2:\pi_1(U_2,x_0)\to \pi_1(X,x_0)$. Then *X* is path connected and $j_1$ and $j_2$ form a commutative [pushout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibered_coproduct) diagram:

*Image: VanKampen-01.png.*

The natural morphism *k* is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). That is, the fundamental group of *X* is the [free product](wikipedia://en/360036) of the fundamental groups of *U*<sub>1</sub> and *U*<sub>2</sub> with amalgamation of $\pi_1(U_1\cap U_2, x_0)$.[^o4]

Usually the morphisms induced by inclusion in this theorem are not themselves [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_map), and the more precise version of the statement is in terms of [pushouts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibered_coproduct) of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447).

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Van Kampen's theorem for fundamental groupoids"}

### Van Kampen's theorem for fundamental groupoids {#Van_Kampen's_theorem_for_fundamental_groupoids}

Unfortunately, the theorem as given above does not compute the fundamental group of the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) – which is the most important basic example in algebraic topology – because the circle cannot be realised as the union of two open sets with connected [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429). This problem can be resolved by working with the [fundamental groupoid](wikipedia://en/55264190) $\pi_1(X,A)$ on a *set A* of base points, chosen according to the geometry of the situation. Thus for the circle, one uses two base points.[^o7]

This [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) consists of [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) classes relative to the end points of [paths](wikipedia://en/1189553) in *X* joining points of *A* ∩ *X*. In particular, if *X* is a [contractible space](wikipedia://en/1752414), and *A* consists of two distinct points of *X*, then $\pi_1(X,A)$ is easily seen to be isomorphic to the groupoid often written $\mathcal I$ with two vertices and exactly one morphism between any two vertices. This groupoid plays a role in the theory of groupoids analogous to that of the group of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties) in the theory of groups.[^o8] The groupoid $\mathcal I$ also allows for groupoids a notion of homotopy: it is a *unit interval object* in the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) of groupoids.

*Image: Fig6 10c.jpg.* A connected union of two non connected spaces, with set of base points

The category of groupoids admits all [colimits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colimit), and in particular all pushouts.

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Let the topological space *X* be covered by the [interiors](wikipedia://en/55610) of two subspaces *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub> and let *A* be a set which meets each [path component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_component) of *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub> and *X*<sub>0</sub> = *X*<sub>1</sub> ∩ *X*<sub>2</sub>. Then *A* meets each path component of *X* and the diagram **P** of morphisms induced by inclusion

*Image: SeifertvanKampenPO.PNG.*

is a pushout diagram in the category of groupoids.[^o10]

:::

This theorem gives the transition from [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) to [algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), in determining completely the fundamental groupoid $\pi_1(X,A)$; one then has to use algebra and [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) to determine a fundamental group at some basepoint.

One interpretation of the theorem is that it computes homotopy 1-types. To see its utility, one can easily find cases where *X* is connected but is the union of the interiors of two subspaces, each with say 402 path components and whose intersection has say 1004 path components. The interpretation of this theorem as a calculational tool for "fundamental groups" needs some development of 'combinatorial groupoid theory'.[^o11][^o12] This theorem implies the calculation of the fundamental group of the circle as the group of integers, since the group of integers is obtained from the groupoid $\mathcal I$ by identifying, in the category of groupoids, its two vertices.

There is a version of the last theorem when *X* is covered by the union of the interiors of a family $\{U_\lambda : \lambda \in \Lambda\}$ of subsets.[^o13][^o14]

The conclusion is that if *A* meets each path component of all 1,2,3-fold intersections of the sets $U_\lambda$, then *A* meets all path components of *X* and the diagram

$$\bigsqcup_{(\lambda,\mu) \in \Lambda^2} \pi_1(U_\lambda \cap U_\mu, A) \rightrightarrows \bigsqcup_{\lambda \in \Lambda} \pi_1(U_\lambda, A)\rightarrow \pi_1(X,A)$$

of morphisms induced by inclusions is a [coequaliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coequaliser) in the category of groupoids.

:::

:::

## Equivalent formulations {#Equivalent_formulations}

In the language of [combinatorial group theory](wikipedia://en/979834), if $X$ is a topological space; $U$ and $V$ are open, path connected subspaces of $X$; $U\cap V$ is nonempty and path-connected; and $w\in U\cap V$; then $\pi_1(X,w)$ is the [free product with amalgamation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_product_with_amalgamation) of $\pi_1(U,w)$ and $\pi_1(V,w)$, with respect to the (not necessarily injective) homomorphisms $I:\pi_1(U\cap V, w)\to \pi_1(U,w)$ and $J:\pi_1(U\cap V, w)\to \pi_1(V,w)$. Given [group presentations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation):

$$\begin{aligned} \pi_1(U,w) &= \langle u_1,\dots,u_k \mid\alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_l\rangle \\ \pi_1(V,w) &= \langle v_1,\dots,v_m \mid \beta_1,\dots,\beta_n\rangle \\ \pi_1(U\cap V,w) &= \langle w_1,\dots,w_p \mid \gamma_1,\dots,\gamma_q\rangle \end{aligned}$$

the amalgamation can be presented[^n1] as

$$\pi_1(X,w) = \left\langle u_1,\dots,u_k, v_1,\dots,v_m \left | \alpha_1,\dots,\alpha_l, \beta_1,\dots,\beta_n, I(w_1)J(w_1)^{-1},\dots,I(w_p)J(w_p)^{-1} \right. \right\rangle.$$

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), $\pi_1(X,w)$ is the [pushout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pushout_%28mathematics%29), in the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), of the diagram:

$$\pi_1(U,w)\gets\pi_1(U\cap V,w)\to\pi_1(V,w).$$

## Examples {#Examples}

### 2-sphere {#2-sphere}

One can use Van Kampen's theorem to calculate fundamental groups for topological spaces that can be decomposed into simpler spaces. For example, consider the [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) $S^2$. Pick open sets $A = S^2\setminus \{n\}$ and $B = S^2\setminus \{s\}$ where *n* and *s* denote the north and south poles respectively. Then we have the property that *A*, *B* and *A* ∩ *B* are open path connected sets. Thus we can see that there is a [commutative diagram](wikipedia://en/138484) including *A* ∩ *B* into *A* and *B* and then another inclusion from *A* and *B* into $S^2$ and that there is a corresponding diagram of homomorphisms between the fundamental groups of each subspace. Applying Van Kampen's theorem gives the result

$$\pi_1(S^2)=\pi_1(A)\cdot\pi_1(B)/\ker(\Phi).$$

However, *A* and *B* are both [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to **R**<sup>2</sup> which is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected), so both *A* and *B* have [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059) fundamental groups. It is clear from this that the fundamental group of $S^2$ is trivial.

### Wedge sum of spaces {#Wedge_sum_of_spaces}

Given two [pointed spaces](wikipedia://en/1236666) $(X,x)$ and $(Y,y)$ we can form their [wedge sum](wikipedia://en/684143), $(X\vee Y,p)$, by taking the [quotient](wikipedia://en/237213) of $X \coprod Y$ by identifying their two basepoints.

If $x$ admits a contractible open [neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighborhood_%28mathematics%29) $U \subset X$ and $y$ admits a contractible open neighborhood $V \subset Y$ (which is the case if, for instance, $X$ and $Y$ are [CW complexes](wikipedia://en/353022)), then we can apply the Van Kampen theorem to $X \vee Y$ by taking $X \vee V$ and $U \vee Y$ as the two open sets and we conclude that the fundamental group of the wedge is the [free product](wikipedia://en/360036) of the fundamental groups of the two spaces we started with:

$$\pi_1(X\vee Y, p)\cong \pi_1(X,x)*\pi_1(Y,y).$$

### Orientable genus-*g* surfaces {#Orientable_genus-g_surfaces}

A more complicated example is the calculation of the fundamental group of a [genus-*n* orientable surface](wikipedia://en/61891#Orientable_surface) *S*, otherwise known as the *genus-n surface group*. One can construct *S* using its [standard fundamental polygon](wikipedia://en/1668732#Standard_fundamental_polygons). For the first open set *A*, pick a disk within the center of the polygon. Pick *B* to be the complement in *S* of the center point of *A*. Then the intersection of *A* and *B* is an [annulus](wikipedia://en/356158), which is known to be [homotopy equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_equivalent) to (and so has the same fundamental group as) a circle. Then $\pi_1(A \cap B)=\pi_1(S^1)$, which is the integers, and $\pi_1(A) = \pi_1(D^2) = {1}$. Thus the inclusion of $\pi_1(A \cap B)$ into $\pi_1(A)$ sends any generator to the trivial element. However, the inclusion of $\pi_1(A \cap B)$ into $\pi_1(B)$ is not trivial. In order to understand this, first one must calculate $\pi_1(B)$. This is easily done as one can [deformation retract](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_retract) *B* (which is *S* with one point deleted) onto the edges labeled by

$$A_1 B_1 A_1^{-1} B_1^{-1} A_2 B_2 A_2^{-1} B_2^{-1} \cdots A_n B_n A_n^{-1} B_n^{-1}.$$

This space is known to be the [wedge sum](wikipedia://en/684143) of 2*n* circles (also called a [bouquet of circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bouquet_of_circles)), which further is known to have fundamental group isomorphic to the [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) with 2*n* generators, which in this case can be represented by the edges themselves: $\{A_1,B_1,\dots,A_n,B_n\}$. We now have enough information to apply Van Kampen's theorem. The generators are the [loops](wikipedia://en/1189560) $\{A_1,B_1,\dots,A_n,B_n\}$ (*A* is simply connected, so it contributes no generators) and there is exactly one relation:

$$A_1 B_1 A_1^{-1} B_1^{-1} A_2 B_2 A_2^{-1} B_2^{-1} \cdots A_n B_n A_n^{-1} B_n^{-1} = 1.$$

Using generators and relations, this group is denoted

$$\left \langle A_1,B_1,\dots,A_n,B_n\left | A_1B_1A_1^{-1}B_1^{-1}\cdots A_nB_nA_n^{-1}B_n^{-1}\right. \right \rangle.$$

### Simple-connectedness {#Simple-connectedness}

If *X* is a space that can be written as the union of two open [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) sets *U* and *V* with *U* ∩ *V* non-empty and [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected), then *X* is simply connected.[^o23]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

As explained above, this theorem was extended by [Ronald Brown](wikipedia://en/27079705) to the non-connected case by using the fundamental groupoid $\pi_1(X,A)$ on a set *A* of base points. The theorem for arbitrary covers, with the restriction that *A* meets all threefold intersections of the sets of the cover, is given in the paper by Brown and Abdul Razak Salleh.[^o26] The theorem and [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) for the fundamental group, but using some groupoid methods, are also given in [J. Peter May](wikipedia://en/14644098)'s book.[^o27] The version that allows more than two overlapping sets but with *A* a [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) is also given in [Allen Hatcher](wikipedia://en/1112366)'s book below, theorem 1.20.

Applications of the fundamental groupoid on a set of base points to the [Jordan curve theorem](wikipedia://en/267444), [covering spaces](wikipedia://en/250323), and [orbit spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_space) are given in Ronald Brown's book.[^o28] In the case of orbit spaces, it is convenient to take *A* to include all the fixed points of the action. An example here is the conjugation action on the circle.

References to [higher-dimensional](wikipedia://en/5274013) versions of the theorem which yield some information on homotopy types are given in an article on higher-dimensional group theories and groupoids.[^o29] Thus a 2-dimensional Van Kampen theorem which computes nonabelian second relative [homotopy groups](wikipedia://en/297506) was given by Ronald Brown and Philip J. Higgins.[^o30] A full account and extensions to all dimensions are given by Brown, Higgins, and Rafael Sivera,[^o31] while an extension to *n*-cubes of spaces is given by Ronald Brown and [Jean-Louis Loday](wikipedia://en/36183327).[^o32]

Fundamental groups also appear in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) and are the main topic of [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042)'s first [Séminaire de géométrie algébrique](wikipedia://en/394508) (SGA1). A version of Van Kampen's theorem appears there, and is proved along quite different lines than in algebraic topology, namely by [descent theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Descent_theory). A similar proof works in algebraic topology.[^o33]
