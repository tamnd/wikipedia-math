# Spectral radius

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spectral radius"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **spectral radius** of a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is the maximum of the absolute values of its [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalues).[^o2] More generally, the spectral radius of a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) is the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of the absolute values of the elements of its [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206137). The spectral radius is often denoted by $\rho(\cdot)$.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

### Matrices {#Matrices}

Let $\lambda_{1}, ..., \lambda_{n}$ be the eigenvalues of a matrix $A \in \mathbf{C}^{n\times n}$. The spectral radius of $A$ is defined as

$$\rho(A) = \max \left \{ |\lambda_1|, \dotsc, |\lambda_n| \right \}.$$

The spectral radius can be thought of as an infimum of all norms of a matrix. Indeed, on the one hand, $\rho(A) \leqslant \|A\|$ for every [natural matrix norm](wikipedia://en/1543735#Matrix_norms_induced_by_vector_norms) $\|\cdot\|$; and on the other hand, Gelfand's formula states that $\rho(A) = \lim_{k\to\infty} \|A^k\|^{1/k}$. Both of these results are shown below.

However, the spectral radius does not necessarily satisfy $\|A\mathbf{v}\| \leqslant \rho(A) \|\mathbf{v}\|$ for arbitrary vectors $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{C}^n$. To see why, let $r > 1$ be arbitrary and consider the matrix

$$C_r = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & r^{-1} \\ r & 0 \end{pmatrix}.$$

The [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of $C_r$ is $\lambda^2 - 1$, so its eigenvalues are $\{-1, 1\}$ and thus $\rho(C_r) = 1$. However, $C_r \mathbf{e}_1 = r \mathbf{e}_2$. As a result,

$$\| C_r \mathbf{e}_1 \| = r > 1 = \rho(C_r) \|\mathbf{e}_1\|.$$

As an illustration of Gelfand's formula, note that $\|C_r^k\|^{1/k} \to 1$ as $k \to \infty$, since $C_r^k = I$ if $k$ is even and $C_r^k = C_r$ if $k$ is odd.

A special case in which $\|A\mathbf{v}\| \leqslant \rho(A) \|\mathbf{v}\|$ for all $\mathbf{v} \in \mathbb{C}^n$ is when $A$ is a [Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/189682) and $\|\cdot\|$ is the [Euclidean norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_norm). This is because any Hermitian Matrix is [diagonalizable](wikipedia://en/246325) by a [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222), and unitary matrices preserve vector length. As a result,

$$\|A\mathbf{v}\| = \|U^*DU\mathbf{v}\| = \|DU\mathbf{v}\| \leqslant \rho(A) \|U\mathbf{v}\| = \rho(A) \|\mathbf{v}\| .$$

### Bounded linear operators {#Bounded_linear_operators}

In the context of a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) $A$ on a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), the eigenvalues need to be replaced with the elements of the [spectrum of the operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_an_operator), i.e. the values $\lambda$ for which $A - \lambda I$ is not bijective. We denote the spectrum by

$$\sigma(A) = \left\{ \lambda \in \mathbb{C}: A - \lambda I \; \text{is not bijective} \right\}$$

The spectral radius is then defined as the supremum of the magnitudes of the elements of the spectrum:

$$\rho(A) = \sup_{\lambda \in \sigma(A)} |\lambda|$$

Gelfand's formula, also known as the spectral radius formula, also holds for bounded linear operators: letting $\|\cdot\|$ denote the [operator norm](wikipedia://en/246722), we have

$$\rho(A) = \lim_{k \to \infty}\|A^k\|^{\frac{1}{k}}=\inf_{k\in\mathbb{N}^*} \|A^k\|^{\frac{1}{k}}.$$

A bounded operator (on a complex Hilbert space) is called a **spectraloid operator** if its spectral radius coincides with its [numerical radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_radius). An example of such an operator is a [normal operator](wikipedia://en/190167).

### Graphs {#Graphs}

The spectral radius of a finite [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) is defined to be the spectral radius of its [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463).

This definition extends to the case of infinite graphs with bounded degrees of vertices (i.e. there exists some real number $C$ such that the degree of every vertex of the graph is smaller than $C$). In this case, for the graph $G$ define:

$$\ell^2(G) = \left \{ f : V(G) \to \mathbf{R} \ : \ \sum\nolimits_{v \in V(G)} \left \|f(v)^2 \right \| < \infty \right \}.$$

Let $\gamma$ be the adjacency operator of $G$:

$$\begin{cases} \gamma : \ell^2(G) \to \ell^2(G) \\ (\gamma f)(v) = \sum_{(u,v) \in E(G)} f(u) \end{cases}$$

The spectral radius of $G$ is defined to be the spectral radius of the bounded linear operator $\gamma$.

:::

## Upper bounds {#Upper_bounds}

### Upper bounds on the spectral radius of a matrix {#Upper_bounds_on_the_spectral_radius_of_a_matrix}

The following proposition gives simple yet useful upper bounds on the spectral radius of a matrix.

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition.** Let $A \in \mathbf{C}^{n\times n}$ with spectral radius $\rho(A)$ and a [sub-multiplicative matrix norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-multiplicative_norm) $||\cdot||$. Then for each integer $k \geqslant 1$:

$$\rho(A)\leq \|A^k\|^{\frac{1}{k}}.$$

:::

::: {#o11 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**

Let $(\mathbf{v}, \lambda)$ be an [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector)-[eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) pair for a matrix *A*. By the sub-multiplicativity of the matrix norm, we get:

$$|\lambda|^k\|\mathbf{v}\| = \|\lambda^k \mathbf{v}\| = \|A^k \mathbf{v}\| \leq \|A^k\|\cdot\|\mathbf{v}\|.$$

Since $\mathbf{v} \ne 0$, we have

$$|\lambda|^k \leq \|A^k\|$$

and therefore

$$\rho(A)\leq \|A^k\|^{\frac{1}{k}}.$$

concluding the proof.

:::

### Upper bounds for spectral radius of a graph {#Upper_bounds_for_spectral_radius_of_a_graph}

There are many upper bounds for the spectral radius of a graph in terms of its number *n* of vertices and its number *m* of edges. For instance, if

$$\frac{(k-2)(k-3)}{2} \leq m-n \leq \frac{k(k-3)}{2}$$

where $3 \le k \le n$ is an integer, then[^o13]

$$\rho(G) \leq \sqrt{2 m-n-k+\frac{5}{2}+\sqrt{2 m-2 n+\frac{9}{4}}}$$

## Symmetric matrices {#Symmetric_matrices}

For real-valued matrices $A$ the inequality $\rho(A) \leq {\|A\|}_{2}$ holds in particular, where ${\|\cdot\|}_{2}$ denotes the [spectral norm](wikipedia://en/1543735#Spectral_norm). In the case where $A$ is symmetric, this inequality is tight:

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.** Let $A \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times n}$ be symmetric, i.e., $A = A^T.$ Then it holds that $\rho(A) = {\|A\|}_{2}.$

:::

::: {#o16 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**

Let $(v_i, \lambda_i)_{i=1}^{n}$ be the eigenpairs of *A*. Due to the symmetry of *A*, all $v_i$ and $\lambda_i$ are real-valued and the eigenvectors $v_i$ are [orthonormal](wikipedia://en/195063). By the definition of the spectral norm, there exists an $x \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ with ${\|x\|}_{2} = 1$ such that ${\|A\|}_{2} = {\| A x \|}_{2}.$ Since the eigenvectors $v_i$ form a basis of $\mathbb{R}^{n},$ there exists factors $\delta_{1}, \ldots, \delta_{n} \in \mathbb{R}^{n}$ such that $\textstyle x = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \delta_{i} v_{i}$ which implies that

$$A x = \sum_{i = 1}^{n}\delta_{i} A v_{i} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \delta_{i} \lambda_{i} v_{i}.$$

From the orthonormality of the eigenvectors $v_i$ it follows that

$${\| A x\|}_{2} = \| \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \delta_{i} \lambda_{i} v_{i}\|_{2} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} {|\delta_{i}|} \cdot {|\lambda_{i}|} \cdot {\| v_{i}\|}_{2} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} {|\delta_{i}|} \cdot {|\lambda_{i}|}$$

and

$${\|x\|}_{2} = \| \sum_{i = 1}^{n} \delta_{i} v_{i} \|_{2} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} {|\delta_{i}|} \cdot {\| v_{i} \|}_{2} = \sum_{i = 1}^{n} {|\delta_{i}|}.$$

Since $x$ is chosen such that it maximizes ${\|Ax\|}_{2}$ while satisfying ${\|x\|}_{2} = 1,$ the values of $\delta_{i}$ must be such that they maximize $\textstyle \sum_{i = 1}^{n} {|\delta_{i}|} \cdot {|\lambda_{i}|}$ while satisfying $\textstyle \sum_{i = 1}^{n} {|\delta_{i}|} = 1.$ This is achieved by setting $\delta_{k} = 1$ for $k = \mathrm{arg\,max}_{i=1}^{n} {|\lambda_i|}$ and $\delta_{i} = 0$ otherwise, yielding a value of ${\|Ax\|}_{2} = {|\lambda_k|} = \rho(A).$

:::

## Power sequence {#Power_sequence}

The spectral radius is closely related to the behavior of the convergence of the power sequence of a matrix; namely as shown by the following theorem.

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.** Let $A \in \mathbf{C}^{n\times n}$ with spectral radius $\rho(A)$. Then $\rho(A) < 1$ if and only if

$$\lim_{k \to \infty} A^k = 0.$$

On the other hand, if $\rho(A) > 1$, $\lim_{k \to \infty} \|A^k\| = \infty$. The statement holds for any choice of matrix norm on $\mathbf{C}^{n\times n}$.

:::

::: {#o19 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof**

Assume that $A^k$ goes to zero as $k$ goes to infinity. We will show that $\rho(A) < 1$. Let $(\mathbf{v}, \lambda)$ be an [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector)-[eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) pair for *A*. Since $A^{k}\mathbf{v} = \lambda^{k}\mathbf{v}$, we have

$$\begin{aligned} 0 &= \left(\lim_{k \to \infty} A^k \right) \mathbf{v} \\ &= \lim_{k \to \infty} \left(A^k\mathbf{v} \right ) \\ &= \lim_{k \to \infty} \lambda^k\mathbf{v} \\ &= \mathbf{v} \lim_{k \to \infty} \lambda^k \end{aligned}$$

Since $\mathbf{v} \ne 0$ by hypothesis, we must have

$$\lim_{k \to \infty}\lambda^k = 0,$$

which implies $|\lambda| < 1$. Since this must be true for any eigenvalue $\lambda$, we can conclude that $\rho(A) < 1$.

Now, assume the radius of $A$ is less than $1$. From the [Jordan normal form](wikipedia://en/312877) theorem, we know that for all $A \in \mathbf{C}^{n\times n}$, there exist $V, J \in \mathbf{C}^{n\times n}$ with $V$ non-singular and $J$ block diagonal such that:

$$A = VJV^{-1}$$

with

$$J=\begin{bmatrix} J_{m_1}(\lambda_1) & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & J_{m_2}(\lambda_2) & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \cdots & \ddots & \cdots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & J_{m_{s-1}}(\lambda_{s-1}) & 0 \\ 0 & \cdots & \cdots & 0 & J_{m_s}(\lambda_s) \end{bmatrix}$$

where

$$J_{m_i}(\lambda_i)=\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_i & 1 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & \lambda_i & 1 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_i & 1 \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \lambda_i \end{bmatrix}\in \mathbf{C}^{m_i \times m_i}, 1\leq i\leq s.$$

It is easy to see that

$$A^k=VJ^kV^{-1}$$

and, since $J$ is block-diagonal,

$$J^k=\begin{bmatrix} J_{m_1}^k(\lambda_1) & 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ 0 & J_{m_2}^k(\lambda_2) & 0 & \cdots & 0 \\ \vdots & \cdots & \ddots & \cdots & \vdots \\ 0 & \cdots & 0 & J_{m_{s-1}}^k(\lambda_{s-1}) & 0 \\ 0 & \cdots & \cdots & 0 & J_{m_s}^k(\lambda_s) \end{bmatrix}$$

Now, a standard result on the $k$-power of an $m_i \times m_i$ Jordan block states that, for $k \geq m_i-1$:

$$J_{m_i}^k(\lambda_i)=\begin{bmatrix} \lambda_i^k & {k \choose 1}\lambda_i^{k-1} & {k \choose 2}\lambda_i^{k-2} & \cdots & {k \choose m_i-1}\lambda_i^{k-m_i+1} \\ 0 & \lambda_i^k & {k \choose 1}\lambda_i^{k-1} & \cdots & {k \choose m_i-2}\lambda_i^{k-m_i+2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \ddots & \vdots \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & \lambda_i^k & {k \choose 1}\lambda_i^{k-1} \\ 0 & 0 & \cdots & 0 & \lambda_i^k \end{bmatrix}$$

Thus, if $\rho(A) < 1$ then for all $i$ $|\lambda_i| < 1$. Hence for all $i$ we have:

$$\lim_{k \to \infty}J_{m_i}^k=0$$

which implies

$$\lim_{k \to \infty} J^k = 0.$$

Therefore,

$$\lim_{k \to \infty}A^k=\lim_{k \to \infty}VJ^kV^{-1}=V \left (\lim_{k \to \infty}J^k \right )V^{-1}=0$$

On the other side, if $\rho(A)>1$, there is at least one element in $J$ that does not remain bounded as $k$ increases, thereby proving the second part of the statement.

:::

::: {#o21 .statement cue=named-heading name="Gelfand's formula"}

## Gelfand's formula {#Gelfand's_formula}

Gelfand's formula, named after [Israel Gelfand](wikipedia://en/364643), gives the spectral radius as a limit of matrix norms.

::: {#o23 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

### Theorem {#Theorem}

For any [matrix norm](wikipedia://en/1543735) $||\cdot||,$ we have[^o24]

$$\rho(A)=\lim_{k \to \infty} \left \|A^k \right \|^{\frac{1}{k}}.$$

Moreover, in the case of a [consistent](wikipedia://en/1543735) matrix norm $\lim_{k \to \infty} \left \|A^k \right \|^{\frac{1}{k}}$ approaches $\rho(A)$ from above (indeed, in that case $\rho(A) \leq \left \|A^k \right \|^{\frac{1}{k}}$ for all $k$).

:::

::: {#o26 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

For any $\varepsilon > 0$, let us define the two following matrices:

$$A_{\pm}= \frac{1}{\rho(A) \pm\varepsilon}A.$$

Thus,

$$\rho \left (A_{\pm} \right ) = \frac{\rho(A)}{\rho(A) \pm \varepsilon}, \qquad \rho (A_+) < 1 < \rho (A_-).$$

We start by applying the previous theorem on limits of power sequences to $A_{+}$:

$$\lim_{k \to \infty} A_+^k=0.$$

This shows the existence of $N_{+} \in \mathbf{N}$ such that, for all $k \ge N_{+}$,

$$\left\|A_+^k \right \| < 1.$$

Therefore,

$$\left \|A^k \right \|^{\frac{1}{k}} < \rho(A)+\varepsilon.$$

Similarly, the theorem on power sequences implies that $\|A_-^k\|$ is not bounded and that there exists $N_{-} \in \mathbf{N}$ such that, for all $k \ge N_{-}$,

$$\left\|A_-^k \right \| > 1.$$

Therefore,

$$\left\|A^k \right\|^{\frac{1}{k}} > \rho(A)-\varepsilon.$$

Let $N = \max\{N_{+}, N_{-}$}. Then,

$$\forall \varepsilon>0\quad \exists N\in\mathbf{N} \quad \forall k\geq N \quad \rho(A)-\varepsilon < \left \|A^k \right \|^{\frac{1}{k}} < \rho(A)+\varepsilon,$$

that is,

$$\lim_{k \to \infty} \left \|A^k \right \|^{\frac{1}{k}} = \rho(A).$$

This concludes the proof.

:::

::: {#o28 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=named-heading name="Corollary"}

### Corollary {#Corollary}

Gelfand's formula yields a bound on the spectral radius of a product of commuting matrices: if $A_1, \ldots, A_n$ are matrices that all commute, then

$$\rho(A_1 \cdots A_n) \leq \rho(A_1) \cdots \rho(A_n).$$

:::
