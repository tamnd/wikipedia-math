# Thermohaline circulation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thermohaline circulation"}

*Image: Thermohaline Circulation 2.png.* A summary of the path of the thermohaline circulation. Blue paths represent deep-water currents, while red paths represent surface currents.

*Image: Thermohaline Circulation using Improved Flow Field.ogv.* Thermohaline circulation

**Thermohaline circulation** (**THC**) is a part of the large-scale [ocean circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_current) driven by global [density gradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density_gradient) formed by surface heat and freshwater [fluxes](wikipedia://en/43590).[^o16][^o2] The name *thermohaline* is derived from *thermo-*, referring to temperature, and *haline*, referring to [salt content](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity)—factors which together determine the [density of sea water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_%28molecule%29#Density_of_saltwater_and_ice).

:::

Wind-driven surface currents (such as the [Gulf Stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gulf_Stream)) travel [polewards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_regions_of_Earth) from the equatorial Atlantic Ocean, cooling and sinking en-route to higher [latitudes](wikipedia://en/17616) - eventually becoming part of the [North Atlantic Deep Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Atlantic_Deep_Water) - before flowing into the [ocean basins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_basin).[^o4] While the bulk of thermohaline water [upwells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwelling) in the [Southern Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Ocean), the oldest waters (with a transit time of approximately 1000 years) upwell in the North Pacific;[^o3] extensive mixing takes place between the ocean basins, reducing the difference in their densities, forming the [Earth's oceans a global system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_Ocean).[^o4] The water in these circuits transport energy (as heat) and mass (as dissolved solids and gases) around the globe. Consequently, the state of the circulation greatly impacts the [climate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate) of Earth.

The thermohaline circulation is often referred to as the*ocean conveyor belt*, *great ocean conveyor*, or "*global conveyor belt*" - a term coined by climate scientist [Wallace Smith Broecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_Smith_Broecker).[^o5][^o6] It is also known as the [meridional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zonal_and_meridional) overturning circulation, or *MOC*; a name used to signify that circulation patterns caused by temperature and salinity gradients are not necessarily part of a *single* global circulation. This is due, in part, to the difficulty in separating parts of the circulation driven by temperature and [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity) from those affected by factors such as wind and [tidal force](wikipedia://en/30719).[^o7]

This global circulation comprises two major "limbs;" the [Atlantic meridional overturning circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_meridional_overturning_circulation) (*AMOC*) centered in the north Atlantic Ocean, and the [Southern Ocean overturning circulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Ocean_overturning_circulation), or *Southern Ocean meridional circulation* (*SMOC*) located near [Antarctica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctica). Since 90% of the human population occupies the [Northern Hemisphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northern_Hemisphere),[^o8] more extensive research has been undertaken on the AMOC, however the SMOC is of equal importance to the global climate. Evidence suggests both circulations are slowing due to [climate change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Climate_change) in line with increasing rates of dilution from melting [ice sheets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_sheet) - critically affecting the salinity of [Antarctic bottom water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_bottom_water).[^o9][^o10] In addition, the potential for outright collapse of either circulation to a much weaker state exemplifies [tipping points in the climate system](wikipedia://en/16817594). If either hemisphere experiences collapse of its circulation, the likelihood of prolonged dry spells and droughts would increase as [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation) decreases, while the other hemisphere will become wetter. [Marine ecosystems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_ecosystem) are then more likely to receive fewer [nutrients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nutrient) and experience greater [ocean deoxygenation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_deoxygenation). In the Northern Hemisphere, the collapse of AMOC would lead to substantially lower temperatures in many European countries, while the east coast of North America is predicted to see accelerated [sea level rise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_level_rise). The collapse of these circulations is generally accepted to be more than a century away, and may only occur in the event of rapid and high sea-temperature increases. However, these projections are marked by significant uncertainty.[^o10][^o11]

## History of research {#History_of_research}

*Image: Sea water freezing temperature and density maximum.png.* Effect of temperature and salinity upon sea water density maximum and sea water freezing temperature

It has long been known that wind drives ocean currents, but only at the surface.[^o13] In the 19th century, some [oceanographers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanographer) suggested that the [convection of heat](wikipedia://en/9662955) could drive deeper currents. In 1908, [Johan Sandström](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johan_Sandström) performed a series of experiments at a [Bornö Marine Research Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bornö_Marine_Research_Station) which proved that the currents driven by [thermal energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_energy) transfer can exist, but require that "heating occurs at a greater depth than cooling".[^o16][^o14] Normally, the opposite occurs, because ocean water is heated from above by the Sun and becomes less dense, so the surface layer floats on the surface above the cooler, denser layers, resulting in [ocean stratification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ocean_stratification). However, wind and [tides](wikipedia://en/30718) cause mixing between these water layers, with [diapycnal mixing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diapycnal_mixing) caused by tidal currents being one example.[^o15] This mixing is what enables the convection between ocean layers, and thus, deep water currents.[^o16]

In the 1920s, Sandström's framework was expanded by accounting for the role of [salinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salinity) in ocean layer formation.[^o16] Salinity is important because like temperature, it affects water [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density). Water becomes less dense as its temperature increases and the distance between its [molecules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecule) expands, but more dense as the salinity increases, since there is a larger mass of salts dissolved within that water.[^o17] Further, while fresh water is at its most dense at 4 °C, [seawater](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seawater) only gets denser as it cools, up until it reaches the freezing point. That freezing point is also lower than for fresh water due to salinity, and can be below −2 °C, depending on salinity and pressure.[^o18]

## Structure {#Structure}

*Image: Conveyor belt.svg.* The global conveyor belt on a continuous-ocean map \[ (animation)\]

These density differences caused by temperature and salinity ultimately separate ocean water into distinct [water masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_mass), such as the [North Atlantic Deep Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Atlantic_Deep_Water) (NADW) and [Antarctic Bottom Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Bottom_Water) (AABW). These two waters are the main drivers of the circulation, which was established in 1960 by [Henry Stommel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Stommel) and Arnold B. Arons.[^o20] They have chemical, temperature and isotopic ratio signatures (such as [<sup>231</sup>Pa / <sup>230</sup>Th](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isotope_geochemistry) ratios) which can be traced, their flow rate calculated, and their age determined. NADW is formed because North Atlantic is a rare place in the ocean where [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation), which adds fresh water to the ocean and so reduces its salinity, is outweighed by [evaporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporation), in part due to high windiness. When water evaporates, it leaves salt behind, and so the surface waters of the North Atlantic are particularly salty. North Atlantic is also an already cool region, and [evaporative cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporative_cooling) reduces water temperature even further. Thus, this water sinks downward in the [Norwegian Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_Sea), fills the Arctic Ocean Basin and spills southwards through the Greenland-Scotland-Ridge – crevasses in the [submarine sills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submarine_sill) that connect [Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland), [Iceland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iceland) and Great Britain. It cannot flow towards the Pacific Ocean due to the narrow shallows of the [Bering Strait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bering_Strait), but it does slowly flow into the deep [abyssal plains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abyssal_plain) of the south Atlantic.[^o21]

In the [Southern Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southern_Ocean), strong [katabatic winds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katabatic_wind) blowing from the Antarctic continent onto the [ice shelves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ice_shelves) will blow the newly formed [sea ice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sea_ice) away, opening [polynyas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynyas) in locations such as [Weddell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weddell_Sea) and [Ross Seas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Sea), off the [Adélie Coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adélie_Coast) and by [Cape Darnley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cape_Darnley_%28Mac._Robertson_Land%29). The ocean, no longer protected by sea ice, suffers a brutal and strong cooling (see [polynya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynya)). Meanwhile, sea ice starts reforming, so the surface waters also get saltier, hence very dense. In fact, the formation of sea ice contributes to an increase in surface seawater salinity; saltier [brine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine) is left behind as the sea ice forms around it (pure water preferentially being frozen). Increasing salinity lowers the freezing point of seawater, so cold liquid brine is formed in inclusions within a honeycomb of ice. The brine progressively melts the ice just beneath it, eventually dripping out of the ice matrix and sinking. This process is known as [brine rejection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brine_rejection). The resulting Antarctic bottom water sinks and flows north and east. It is denser than the NADW, and so flows beneath it. AABW formed in the [Weddell Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weddell_Sea) will mainly fill the Atlantic and Indian Basins, whereas the AABW formed in the [Ross Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ross_Sea) will flow towards the Pacific Ocean. At the Indian Ocean, a vertical exchange of a lower layer of cold and salty water from the Atlantic and the warmer and fresher upper ocean water from the tropical Pacific occurs, in what is known as **overturning**. In the Pacific Ocean, the rest of the cold and salty water from the Atlantic undergoes haline forcing, and becomes warmer and fresher more quickly.[^o22][^o23][^o24][^o25][^o26]

*Image: Thermohaline conveyor belt (NASA).webm.* Surface water flows north and sinks in the dense ocean near Iceland and Greenland. It joins the global thermohaline circulation into the Indian Ocean, and the [Antarctic Circumpolar Current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Circumpolar_Current).[^o27]

The out-flowing undersea of cold and salty water makes the sea level of the Atlantic slightly lower than the Pacific and salinity or halinity of water at the Atlantic higher than the Pacific. This generates a large but slow flow of warmer and fresher upper ocean water from the tropical Pacific to the Indian Ocean through the [Indonesian Archipelago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesian_Archipelago) to replace the cold and salty [Antarctic Bottom Water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antarctic_Bottom_Water). This is also known as 'haline forcing' (net high latitude freshwater gain and low latitude evaporation). This warmer, fresher water from the Pacific flows up through the South Atlantic to [Greenland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greenland), where it cools off and undergoes [evaporative cooling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evaporative_cooling) and sinks to the ocean floor, providing a continuous thermohaline circulation.[^o28][^o29]

## Upwelling {#Upwelling}

As the deep waters sink into the ocean basins, they displace the older deep-water masses, which gradually become less dense due to continued ocean mixing. Thus, some water is rising, in what is known as [upwelling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upwelling). Its speeds are very slow even compared to the movement of the bottom water masses. It is therefore difficult to measure where upwelling occurs using current speeds, given all the other wind-driven processes going on in the surface ocean. Deep waters have their own chemical signature, formed from the breakdown of particulate matter falling into them over the course of their long journey at depth. A number of scientists have tried to use these tracers to infer where the upwelling occurs. [Wallace Broecker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_S._Broecker), using box models, has asserted that the bulk of deep upwelling occurs in the North Pacific, using as evidence the high values of silicon found in these waters. Other investigators have not found such clear evidence.[^o31]

Computer models of ocean circulation increasingly place most of the deep upwelling in the Southern Ocean, associated with the strong winds in the open latitudes between South America and Antarctica.[^o32] Direct estimates of the strength of the thermohaline circulation have also been made at 26.5°N in the North Atlantic, by the UK-US RAPID programme. It combines direct estimates of ocean transport using current meters and subsea cable measurements with estimates of the [geostrophic current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geostrophic_current) from temperature and salinity measurements to provide continuous, full-depth, basin-wide estimates of the meridional overturning circulation. However, it has only been operating since 2004, which is too short when the timescale of the circulation is measured in centuries.[^o33]

## Effects on global climate {#Effects_on_global_climate}

The thermohaline circulation plays an important role in supplying heat to the polar regions, and thus in regulating the amount of sea ice in these regions, although poleward heat transport outside the tropics is considerably larger in the atmosphere than in the ocean.[^o35] Changes in the thermohaline circulation are thought to have significant impacts on the Earth's [radiation budget](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiation_budget).

Large influxes of low-density meltwater from [Lake Agassiz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lake_Agassiz) and [deglaciation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deglaciation) in North America are thought to have led to a shifting of deep water formation and subsidence in the extreme North Atlantic and caused the climate period in Europe known as the [Younger Dryas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Younger_Dryas).[^o36]
