# History of computing

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="History of computing"}

The **history of computing** extends beyond the [history of computing hardware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_computing_hardware) and [modern computing technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) including earlier methods that relied on pen and paper or chalk and slate, with or without the aid of tables.

:::

## Concrete devices {#Concrete_devices}

Digital [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing) is intimately tied to the representation of [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690).[^o3] But long before [abstractions](wikipedia://en/2972) like *the number* arose, there were mathematical concepts to serve the purposes of civilization. These concepts are implicit in concrete practices such as:

- *[One-to-one correspondence](wikipedia://en/3942)*,[^o4] a rule to [count](wikipedia://en/275768) *how many* items, e.g. on a [tally stick](wikipedia://en/30872676), eventually abstracted into *numbers*.
- *Comparison to a standard*,[^o5] a method for assuming *[reproducibility](wikipedia://en/47651)* in a [measurement](wikipedia://en/19022), for example, the number of [coins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coin).
- The *3-4-5* right triangle was a device for assuring a *right angle*, using [ropes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rope) with 12 evenly spaced [knots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knot), for example.[^o6]

## Numbers {#Numbers}

Eventually, the concept of numbers became concrete and familiar enough for counting to arise, at times with sing-song [mnemonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnemonic) to teach [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) to others. All known human languages, except the [Piraha language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piraha_language), have words for at least the [numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_%28linguistics%29) "one" and "two", and even some animals like the [blackbird](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_blackbird) can distinguish a surprising number of items.[^o8]

Advances in the [numeral system](wikipedia://en/21170) and [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) eventually led to the discovery of mathematical operations such as addition, subtraction, multiplication, division, squaring, square root, and so forth. Eventually, the operations were formalized, and concepts about the operations became understood well enough to be [stated formally](wikipedia://en/30977), and even [proven](wikipedia://en/82285). See, for example, [Euclid's algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) for finding the greatest common divisor of two numbers.

By the [High Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_Middle_Ages), the [positional](wikipedia://en/437052) [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](wikipedia://en/3393371) had reached [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe), which allowed for the systematic computation of numbers. During this period, the representation of a calculation on [paper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paper) allowed the calculation of [mathematical expressions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_expression), and the tabulation of [mathematical functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_function) such as the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) and the [common logarithm](wikipedia://en/174482) (for use in multiplication and division), and the [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367). By the time of [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s research, paper or vellum was an important [computing resource](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing_resource), and even in our present time, researchers like [Enrico Fermi](wikipedia://en/10264) would cover random scraps of paper with calculation, to satisfy their curiosity about an equation.[^o9] Even into the period of programmable calculators, [Richard Feynman](wikipedia://en/25523) would unhesitatingly compute any steps that overflowed the [memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_memory) of the calculators, by hand, just to learn the answer; by 1976 Feynman had purchased an [HP-25](wikipedia://en/4402245) calculator with a 49 program-step capacity; if a differential equation required more than 49 steps to solve, he could just continue his computation by hand.[^o10]

## Early computation {#Early_computation}

Timeline of computing hardware before 1950

Mathematical statements need not be abstract only; when a statement can be illustrated with actual numbers, the numbers can be communicated and a community can arise. This allows the repeatable, verifiable statements which are the hallmark of mathematics and science. These kinds of statements have existed for thousands of years, and across multiple civilizations, as shown below:

The earliest known tool used for computation is the [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer) [abacus](wikipedia://en/655), believed to have been invented in [Babylon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylon) c. 2700–2300 BC. Its original style of usage was by lines drawn in sand with pebbles.

In c. 1050–771 BC, the [south-pointing chariot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South-pointing_chariot) was invented in [ancient China](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_China#Ancient_China). It was the first known [geared](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gear) mechanism to use a [differential gear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_gear), which was later used in [analog computers](wikipedia://en/2428). The [Chinese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/China) also invented a more sophisticated abacus from around the 2nd century BC known as the [Chinese abacus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinese_abacus).[^o12][^o13]

In the 3rd century BC, [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) used the mechanical principle of balance (see Archimedes Palimpsest) to calculate mathematical problems, such as the number of grains of sand in the universe (*[The sand reckoner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_sand_reckoner)*), which also required a recursive notation for numbers (e.g., the [myriad](wikipedia://en/20088) [myriad](wikipedia://en/20088)).

The [Antikythera mechanism](wikipedia://en/89732) is believed to be the earliest known geared computing device. It was designed to calculate astronomical positions. It was discovered in 1901 in the [Antikythera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antikythera) wreck off the Greek island of Antikythera, between Kythera and [Crete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crete), and has been dated to *circa* 100 BC.[^o14]

According to [Simon Singh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Singh), [Muslim mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_mathematics) also made important advances in [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), such as the development of [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) and [frequency analysis](wikipedia://en/157934) by [Alkindus](wikipedia://en/272065).[^o15][^o16] [Programmable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Program_%28machine%29) machines were also invented by [Muslim engineers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inventions_in_medieval_Islam), such as the automatic [flute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flute) player by the [Banū Mūsā brothers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banū_Mūsā_brothers).[^o17]

The 9th-century mathematician [Al-Khwārizmī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Khwārizmī) introduced the systematic concepts of [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) and [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923); indeed, the very term "algorithm" is derived from the Latinized form of his name, *Algoritmi*.[^o18] His work, translated into Latin as *Algoritmi de numero Indorum*, provided the mathematical foundation for sequential step-by-step procedures, which are the fundamental basis of all modern computing and programming logic.

During the Middle Ages, several European philosophers made attempts to produce analog computer devices. Influenced by the Arabs and [Scholasticism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism), Majorcan philosopher [Ramon Llull](wikipedia://en/69677) (1232–1315) devoted a great part of his life to defining and designing several *logical machines* that, by combining simple and undeniable philosophical truths, could produce all possible knowledge. These machines were never actually built, as they were more of a [thought experiment](wikipedia://en/49535) to produce new knowledge in systematic ways; although they could make simple logical operations, they still needed a human being for the interpretation of results. Moreover, they lacked a versatile architecture, each machine serving only very concrete purposes. Despite this, Llull's work had a strong influence on [Gottfried Leibniz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gottfried_Leibniz) (early 18th century), who developed his ideas further and built several calculating tools using them.

The apex of this early era of mechanical computing can be seen in the [Difference Engine](wikipedia://en/8324) and its successor the [Analytical Engine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analytical_engine) both by [Charles Babbage](wikipedia://en/5698). Babbage never completed constructing either engine, but in 2002 [Doron Swade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doron_Swade) and a group of other engineers at the [Science Museum in London](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Museum,_London) completed Babbage's Difference Engine using only materials that would have been available in the 1840s.[^o19] By following Babbage's detailed design they were able to build a functioning engine, allowing historians to say, with some confidence, that if Babbage had been able to complete his Difference Engine it would have worked.[^o20] The additionally advanced Analytical Engine combined concepts from his previous work and that of others to create a device that, if constructed as designed, would have possessed many properties of a modern electronic computer, such as an internal "scratch memory" equivalent to [RAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random-access_memory), multiple forms of output including a bell, a graph-plotter, and simple printer, and a programmable input-output "hard" memory of [punched cards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Punched_card) which it could modify as well as read. The key advancement that Babbage's devices possessed beyond those created before him was that each component of the device was independent of the rest of the machine, much like the components of a modern electronic computer. This was a fundamental shift in thought; previous computational devices served only a single purpose but had to be at best disassembled and reconfigured to solve a new problem. Babbage's devices could be reprogrammed to solve new problems by the entry of new data and act upon previous calculations within the same series of instructions. [Ada Lovelace](wikipedia://en/974) took this concept one step further, by creating a program for the Analytical Engine to calculate [Bernoulli numbers](wikipedia://en/4964), a complex calculation requiring a recursive algorithm. This is considered to be the first example of a true computer program, a series of instructions that act upon data not known in full until the program is run.

Following Babbage, although unaware of his earlier work, [Percy Ludgate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Percy_Ludgate)[^n1][^o22] in 1909 published the 2nd of the only two designs for mechanical analytical engines in history.[^o23] Two other inventors, [Leonardo Torres Quevedo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_Torres_Quevedo)[^n2] and [Vannevar Bush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vannevar_Bush),[^n3] also did follow-on research based on Babbage's work. In his *Essays on Automatics* (1914) Torres presented the design of an electromechanical calculating machine and introduced the idea of [Floating-point arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11376).[^o26][^o27] In 1920, to celebrate the 100th anniversary of the invention of the [arithmometer](wikipedia://en/1212596), Torres presented in Paris the [Electromechanical Arithmometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_Torres_y_Quevedo#Analytical_machines), an arithmetic unit connected to a remote typewriter, on which commands could be typed and the results printed automatically.[^o28][^n4] Bush's paper *Instrumental Analysis* (1936) discussed using existing IBM punch card machines to implement Babbage's design. In the same year, he started the Rapid Arithmetical Machine project to investigate the problems of constructing an electronic digital computer.

Several examples of analog computation survived into recent times. A [planimeter](wikipedia://en/1103376) is a device that does integrals, using [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) as the analog quantity. Until the 1980s, [HVAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HVAC) systems used [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air) both as the analog quantity and the controlling element. Unlike modern digital computers, analog computers are not very flexible and need to be reconfigured (i.e., reprogrammed) manually to switch them from working on one problem to another. Analog computers had an advantage over early digital computers in that they could be used to solve complex problems using behavioral analogues while the earliest attempts at digital computers were quite limited.

*Image: Visual Smith Chart.png.* A [Smith Chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith_Chart) is a well-known [nomogram](wikipedia://en/271164).

Since computers were rare in this era, the solutions were often *hard-coded* into paper forms such as [nomograms](wikipedia://en/271164),[^o30] which could then produce analog solutions to these problems, such as the distribution of pressures and temperatures in a heating system.

## Digital electronic computers {#Digital_electronic_computers}

The "brain" \[computer\] may one day come down to our level \[of the common people\] and help with our income-tax and book-keeping calculations. But this is speculation and there is no sign of it so far.

In an 1886 letter, [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) described how logical operations could be carried out by electrical switching circuits.[^o33] During 1880-81 he showed that [NOR gates alone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOR_logic) (or [NAND gates alone](wikipedia://en/3521614)) can be used to reproduce the functions of all the other [logic gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_gate), but this work on it was unpublished until 1933.[^o34] The first published proof was by [Henry M. Sheffer](wikipedia://en/2993325) in 1913, so the NAND logical operation is sometimes called [Sheffer stroke](wikipedia://en/29388); the [logical NOR](wikipedia://en/209154) is sometimes called *Peirce's arrow*.[^o35] Consequently, these gates are sometimes called *universal logic gates*.[^o36]

Eventually, [vacuum tubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_tube) replaced relays for logic operations. [Lee de Forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lee_de_Forest)'s modification, in 1907, of the [Fleming valve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fleming_valve) can be used as a logic gate. [Ludwig Wittgenstein](wikipedia://en/17741) introduced a version of the 16-row [truth table](wikipedia://en/25512250) as proposition 5.101 of *[Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus](wikipedia://en/30062)* (1921). [Walther Bothe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walther_Bothe), inventor of the [coincidence circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coincidence_circuit), got part of the 1954 [Nobel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize) in physics, for the first modern electronic AND gate in 1924. [Konrad Zuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse) designed and built electromechanical logic gates for his computer [Z1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z1_%28computer%29) (from 1935 to 1938).

The first recorded idea of using [digital electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_electronics) for computing was the 1931 paper "The Use of Thyratrons for High Speed Automatic Counting of Physical Phenomena" by [C. E. Wynn-Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._E._Wynn-Williams).[^o37] From 1934 to 1936, [NEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NEC) engineer [Akira Nakashima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Akira_Nakashima), [Claude Shannon](wikipedia://en/5693), and [Victor Shestakov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Shestakov) published papers introducing [switching circuit theory](wikipedia://en/32182026), using digital electronics for [Boolean algebraic](wikipedia://en/54476844) operations.[^o38][^o39][^o40][^o41]

In 1936 [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) published his paper [On Computable Numbers, with an Application to the Entscheidungsproblem](wikipedia://en/3739933).[^o42] In it, Turing described an abstract computing machine operating on a one-dimensional tape and a [universal machine](wikipedia://en/71435) capable of imitating any other such machine.[^o43]

The first digital electronic computer was developed between April 1936 and June 1939 at the IBM Patent Department in Endicott, New York, by Arthur Halsey Dickinson.[^o44][^o45][^o46] In this computer, IBM introduced a calculating device with a keyboard, processor and electronic output (display). The competitor to IBM was the digital electronic computer NCR3566, developed in NCR, Dayton, Ohio by Joseph Desch and Robert Mumma in the period April 1939 - August 1939.[^o47][^o48] The IBM and NCR machines were decimal, executing addition and subtraction in binary position code.

In December 1939 [John Atanasoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Vincent_Atanasoff) and [Clifford Berry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_Berry) completed their experimental model to prove the concept of the [Atanasoff–Berry computer (ABC)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atanasoff–Berry_computer) which began development in 1937.[^o49] This experimental model is binary, executed addition and subtraction in octal binary code and is the first binary digital [electronic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) computing device. The Atanasoff–Berry computer was intended to solve systems of linear equations, though it was not programmable. The computer was never truly completed due to Atanasoff's departure from [Iowa State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iowa_State_University) in 1942 to work for the United States Navy.[^o50][^o51] Many people credit ABC with many of the ideas used in later developments during the age of early electronic computing.[^o52]

The [Z3 computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z3_%28computer%29), built by [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) inventor [Konrad Zuse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse) in 1941, was the first programmable, fully automatic computing machine, but it was not electronic.

During World War II, ballistics computing was done by women, who were hired as "computers." The term computer remained one that referred to mostly women (now seen as "operator") until 1945, after which it took on the modern definition of machinery it presently holds.[^o53]

The [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC) (Electronic Numerical Integrator And Computer) was the first electronic general-purpose computer, announced to the public in 1946. It was Turing-complete,[^o54] digital, and capable of being reprogrammed to solve a full range of computing problems. Women implemented the programming for machines like the ENIAC, and men created the hardware.[^o53]

The [Manchester Baby](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_Baby) was the first electronic [stored-program computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stored-program_computer). It was built at the [Victoria University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victoria_University_of_Manchester) by [Frederic C. Williams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederic_Calland_Williams), [Tom Kilburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Kilburn) and [Geoff Tootill](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoff_Tootill), and ran its first program on 21 June 1948.[^o55]

[William Shockley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shockley), [John Bardeen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Bardeen) and [Walter Brattain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Brattain) at [Bell Labs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_Labs) invented the first working [transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor), the [point-contact transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point-contact_transistor), in 1947, followed by the [bipolar junction transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bipolar_junction_transistor) in 1948.[^o56][^o57] At the [University of Manchester](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Manchester) in 1953, a team under the leadership of [Tom Kilburn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tom_Kilburn) designed and built the first [transistorized computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistorized_computer), called the [Transistor Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manchester_computers), a machine using the newly developed transistors instead of valves.[^o58] The first stored-program transistor computer was the ETL Mark III, developed by Japan's Electrotechnical Laboratory[^o59][^o60][^o61] from 1954[^o62] to 1956.[^o60] However, early junction transistors were relatively bulky devices that were difficult to manufacture on a [mass-production](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass-production) basis, which limited them to a number of specialized applications.[^o63]

In 1954, 95% of computers in service were being used for engineering and scientific purposes.[^o64]

Computers other than the E101 are divided into four classes: [desk calculators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_%28occupation%29); general-purpose punched-card computers such as the [International Business Machines Corporation CPC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_CPC) or [Remington Rand 409](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remington_Rand_409); [magnetic-drum computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_memory#History); and [electronic-memory computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_memory#History).

### Personal computers {#Personal_computers}

The [metal–oxide–silicon field-effect transistor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOSFET) (MOSFET), also known as the MOS transistor, was invented at Bell Labs between 1955 and 1960,[^o67][^o68][^o69][^o70][^o71][^o72][^o73] It was the first truly compact transistor that could be [miniaturised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MOSFET_scaling) and [mass-produced](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore's_law) for a wide range of uses.[^o63] The MOSFET made it possible to build [high-density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_large-scale_integration) [integrated circuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit) chips.[^o74][^o75] The MOSFET is the most widely used transistor in computers,[^o76][^o77] and is the fundamental building block of [digital electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_electronics).[^o78]

The [silicon-gate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicon-gate) MOS integrated circuit was developed by [Federico Faggin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federico_Faggin) at [Fairchild Semiconductor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fairchild_Semiconductor) in 1968.[^o79] This led to the development of the first single-chip [microprocessor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microprocessor), the [Intel 4004](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_4004).[^o80] The Intel 4004 was developed as a single-chip microprocessor from 1969 to 1970, led by Intel's Federico Faggin, [Marcian Hoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcian_Hoff), and [Stanley Mazor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley_Mazor), and Busicom's Masatoshi Shima.[^o81] The chip was mainly designed and realized by Faggin, with his silicon-gate MOS technology.[^o80] The microprocessor led to the microcomputer revolution, with the development of the [microcomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcomputer), which would later be called the [personal computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_computer) (PC).

Most early microprocessors, such as the [Intel 8008](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_8008) and [Intel 8080](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel_8080), were [8-bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8-bit). Texas Instruments released the first fully [16-bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/16-bit) microprocessor, the [TMS9900](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TMS9900) processor, in June 1976.[^o82] They used the microprocessor in the TI-99/4 and [TI-99/4A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/TI-99/4A) computers.

The 1980s brought about significant advances with microprocessors that greatly impacted the fields of engineering and other sciences. The [Motorola 68000](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motorola_68000) microprocessor had a processing speed that was far superior to the other microprocessors being used at the time. Because of this, having a newer, faster microprocessor allowed for the newer [microcomputers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microcomputer) that came along after to be more efficient in the amount of computing they were able to do. This was evident in the 1983 release of the [Apple Lisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Lisa). The Lisa was one of the first personal computers with a [graphical user interface (GUI)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphical_user_interface) that was sold commercially. It ran on the Motorola 68000 CPU and used both dual floppy disk drives and a 5 MB hard drive for storage. The machine also had 1MB of [RAM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random-access_memory) used for running software from disk without rereading the disk persistently.[^o83] After the failure of the Lisa in terms of sales, Apple released its [first Macintosh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_128K) computer, still running on the Motorola 68000 microprocessor, but with only 128KB of RAM, one floppy drive, and no hard drive to lower the price.

In the late 1980s and early 1990s, computers became more useful for personal and work purposes, such as [word processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word_processing).[^o84] In 1989, Apple released the [Macintosh Portable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macintosh_Portable), it weighed 7.3 and was extremely expensive, costing US\$7,300. At launch, it was one of the most powerful laptops available, but due to the price and weight, it was not met with great success and was discontinued only two years later. That same year Intel introduced the Touchstone Delta [supercomputer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercomputer), which had 512 microprocessors. This technological advancement was very significant, as it was used as a model for some of the fastest multi-processor systems in the world. It was even used as a prototype for Caltech researchers, who used the model for projects like real-time processing of satellite images and simulating molecular models for various fields of research.

### Supercomputers {#Supercomputers}

In terms of supercomputing, the first widely acknowledged supercomputer was the [Control Data Corporation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_Data_Corporation) (CDC) [6600](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDC_6600)[^o86] built in 1964 by [Seymour Cray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seymour_Cray). Its maximum speed was 40 MHz or 3 million floating point operations per second ([FLOPS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FLOPS)). The CDC 6600 was replaced by the [CDC 7600](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CDC_7600) in 1969;[^o87] although its normal clock speed was not faster than the 6600, the 7600 was still faster due to its peak clock speed, which was approximately 30 times faster than that of the 6600. Although CDC was a leader in supercomputers, their relationship with Seymour Cray (which had already been deteriorating) completely collapsed. In 1972, Cray left CDC and began his own company, [Cray Research Inc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cray_Research).[^o88] With support from investors in Wall Street, an industry fueled by the Cold War, and without the restrictions he had within CDC, he created the [Cray-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cray-1) supercomputer. With a clock speed of 80 MHz or 136 megaFLOPS, Cray developed a name for himself in the computing world. By 1982, Cray Research produced the [Cray X-MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cray_X-MP) equipped with multiprocessing and in 1985 released the [Cray-2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cray-2), which continued with the trend of multiprocessing and clocked at 1.9 gigaFLOPS. Cray Research developed the [Cray Y-MP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cray_Y-MP) in 1988, however afterward struggled to continue to produce supercomputers. This was largely because the Cold War had ended, and the demand for cutting-edge computing by colleges and the government declined drastically and the demand for microprocessing units increased.

In 1998, [David Bader](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_A._Bader) developed the first [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux) supercomputer using commodity parts.[^o89] While at the University of New Mexico, Bader sought to build a supercomputer running Linux using consumer off-the-shelf parts and a high-speed low-latency interconnection network. The prototype utilized an Alta Technologies "AltaCluster" of eight dual, 333 MHz, Intel Pentium II computers running a modified Linux kernel. Bader ported a significant amount of software to provide Linux support for necessary components as well as code from members of the National Computational Science Alliance (NCSA) to ensure interoperability, as none of it had been run on Linux previously.[^o90] Using the successful prototype design, he led the development of "RoadRunner," the first Linux supercomputer for open use by the national science and engineering community via the National Science Foundation's National Technology Grid. RoadRunner was put into production use in April 1999. At the time of its deployment, it was considered one of the 100 fastest supercomputers in the world.[^o90][^o91] Though Linux-based clusters using consumer-grade parts, such as [Beowulf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beowulf_cluster), existed before the development of Bader's prototype and RoadRunner, they lacked the scalability, bandwidth, and [parallel computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_computing) capabilities to be considered "true" supercomputers.[^o90]

Today, supercomputers are still used by the governments of the world and educational institutions for computations such as simulations of natural disasters, genetic variant searches within a population relating to disease, and more. , the fastest supercomputer is [LineShine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LineShine).[^o92]

## Navigation and astronomy {#Navigation_and_astronomy}

Starting with known special cases, the calculation of logarithms and trigonometric functions can be performed by looking up numbers in a [mathematical table](wikipedia://en/239112), and [interpolating](wikipedia://en/14569) between known cases. For small enough differences, this linear operation was accurate enough for use in [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation) and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) in the [Age of Exploration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_Exploration). The uses of interpolation have thrived in the past 500 years: by the twentieth century [Leslie Comrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leslie_Comrie) and [W. J. Eckert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wallace_John_Eckert) systematized the use of interpolation in tables of numbers for punch card calculation.

## Weather prediction {#Weather_prediction}

The numerical solution of differential equations, notably the [Navier-Stokes equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navier-Stokes_equations) was an important stimulus to computing, with [Lewis Fry Richardson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lewis_Fry_Richardson)'s numerical approach to solving differential equations. The first computerized weather forecast was performed in 1950 by a team composed of American meteorologists [Jule Charney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jule_Charney), [Philip Duncan Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philip_Duncan_Thompson), Larry Gates, and Norwegian meteorologist [Ragnar Fjørtoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ragnar_Fjørtoft), applied mathematician [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942), and [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC) programmer [Klara Dan von Neumann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Klara_Dan_von_Neumann).[^o95][^o96][^o97] To this day, some of the most powerful computer systems on Earth are used for [weather forecasts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weather_forecast).[^o98]

## Symbolic computations {#Symbolic_computations}

By the late 1960s, computer systems could perform [symbolic algebraic manipulations](wikipedia://en/23659805) well enough to pass college-level [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) courses.

## Important women and their contributions {#Important_women_and_their_contributions}

Women are often underrepresented in [STEM fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/STEM_fields) when compared to their male counterparts.[^o101] In the modern era before the 1960s, computing was widely seen as "women's work" since it was associated with the operation of [tabulating machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tabulating_machine) and other mechanical office work.[^o102][^o103] The accuracy of this association varied from place to place. In America, [Margaret Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Hamilton_%28software_engineer%29) recalled an environment dominated by men,[^o104] while [Elsie Shutt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elsie_Shutt) recalled surprise at seeing even half of the computer operators at Raytheon were men.[^o105] Machine operators in Britain were mostly women into the early 1970s.[^o106] As these perceptions changed and computing became a high-status career, the field became more dominated by men.[^o107][^o108][^o109] Professor [Janet Abbate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Janet_Abbate), in her book *Recoding Gender*, writes:

> Yet women were a significant presence in the early decades of computing. They made up the majority of the first computer programmers during World War II; they held positions of responsibility and influence in the early computer industry; and they were employed in numbers that, while a small minority of the total, compared favorably with women's representation in many other areas of science and engineering. Some female programmers of the 1950s and 1960s would have scoffed at the notion that programming would ever be considered a masculine occupation, yet these women's experiences and contributions were forgotten all too quickly.[^o110]

Some notable examples of women in the history of computing are:

- [Ada Lovelace](wikipedia://en/974): wrote the addendum to Babbage's Analytical Machine. Detailing, in poetic style, the first computer algorithm; a description of exactly how The Analytical Machine should have worked based on its design.
- [Grace Murray Hopper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grace_Hopper): a pioneer of computing. She worked alongside [Howard H. Aiken](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Howard_H._Aiken) on IBM's Mark I. Hopper and also came up with the term "[debugging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debugging)."
- [Hedy Lamarr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hedy_Lamarr): invented a "[frequency hopping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency-hopping_spread_spectrum)" technology that the Navy used during World War II to control torpedoes via radio signals. This same technology is also used today in creating [Bluetooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluetooth) and [Wi-Fi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wi-Fi) signals.
- [Frances Elizabeth "Betty" Holberton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betty_Holberton): invented "[breakpoints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breakpoint)" which are mini pauses put into lines of computer code to help programmers easily detect, troubleshoot, and solve problems.
- The women who originally programmed the [ENIAC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ENIAC): [Kay McNulty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kathleen_Antonelli), [Betty Jennings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Bartik), [Marlyn Meltzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marlyn_Wescoff), [Fran Bilas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Spence), [Ruth Lichterman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruth_Teitelbaum), and Betty Holberton (see above.)
- [Jean E. Sammet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_E._Sammet): co-designed [COBOL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/COBOL), a widely used programming language.
- [Frances Allen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frances_Allen): [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist) and pioneer in the field of [optimizing compilers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimizing_compiler), first woman to win the [Turing Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_Award).
- [Karen Spärck Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karen_Spärck_Jones): responsible for "[inverse document frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tf–idf)" - a concept that is most commonly used by search engines.
- [Dana Angluin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dana_Angluin): made fundamental contributions to [computational learning theory](wikipedia://en/387537).
- [Margaret Hamilton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Hamilton_%28software_engineer%29): the director of the Software Engineering Division at MIT, which developed on-board flight software for the [Apollo's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_space_program) Missions to Space.
- [Barbara Liskov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Liskov): developed the "[Liskov substitution principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liskov_substitution_principle)."
- [Radia Perlman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radia_Perlman): invented the "[Spanning Tree Protocol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanning_Tree_Protocol)", a key network protocol used in [Ethernet networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethernet_network).
- [Stephanie "Steve" Shirley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steve_Shirley): started [F International](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F_International), a highly successful freelance software company.
- [Sophie Wilson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sophie_Wilson): helped design [ARM processor architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ARM_architecture) widely used in many products such as smartphones and video games.
- [Ann Hardy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ann_Hardy): pioneered computer [time-sharing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-sharing) systems.
- [Lynn Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lynn_Conway): revolutionised microchip design and production by co-introducing [structured VLSI design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Very_Large_Scale_Integration#Structured_design) among other inventions.
- The [women at Bletchley Park](wikipedia://en/54254691): around 8,000 women who worked in numerous capacities with British [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) during World War II. Many came from the [Women's Royal Naval Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_Royal_Naval_Service) (who were called "wrens") as well as the [Women's Auxiliary Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Women's_Auxiliary_Air_Force) ("WAAFs.") They were instrumental in cracking the ["Enigma" cipher](wikipedia://en/872175) and helping the Allies win the war.
