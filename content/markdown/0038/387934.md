# Metalogic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Metalogic"}

**Metalogic** is the [metatheory](wikipedia://en/4823199) of [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065). Whereas *logic* studies how [logical systems](wikipedia://en/396102) can be used to construct [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) and [sound](wikipedia://en/26819) [arguments](wikipedia://en/13018310), metalogic studies the properties of [logical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_system).[^o2] Logic concerns the truths that may be derived using a logical system; metalogic concerns the truths that may be derived *about* the [languages](wikipedia://en/10939) and systems that are used to express truths.

:::

The basic objects of metalogical study are formal languages, formal systems, and their [interpretations](wikipedia://en/14511671). The study of interpretation of formal systems is the branch of [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) that is known as [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858), and the study of [deductive systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_system) is the branch that is known as [proof theory](wikipedia://en/183478).

## Overview {#Overview}

### Formal language {#Formal_language}

A *formal language* is an organized set of [symbols](wikipedia://en/24028642), the symbols of which precisely define it by shape and place. Such a language therefore can be defined without [reference](wikipedia://en/20110874) to the [meanings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meaning_%28linguistics%29) of its expressions; it can exist before any [interpretation](wikipedia://en/14511671) is assigned to it—that is, before it has any meaning. [First-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) is expressed in some formal language. A [formal grammar](wikipedia://en/18020716) determines which symbols and sets of symbols are [formulas](wikipedia://en/404582) in a formal language.

A formal language can be formally defined as a set *A* of strings (finite sequences) on a fixed alphabet α. Some authors, including [Rudolf Carnap](wikipedia://en/57987), define the language as the ordered pair \<α, *A*>.[^o6] Carnap also requires that each element of α must occur in at least one string in *A*.

### Formation rules {#Formation_rules}

*Formation rules* (also called *formal grammar*) are a precise description of the [well-formed formulas](wikipedia://en/404582) of a formal language. They are synonymous with the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of [strings](wikipedia://en/27701) over the [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet) of the formal language that constitute well formed formulas. However, it does not describe their [semantics](wikipedia://en/29107) (i.e. what they mean).

### Formal systems {#Formal_systems}

A *formal system* (also called a *logical calculus*, or a *logical system*) consists of a formal language together with a [deductive apparatus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_system) (also called a *deductive system*). The deductive apparatus may consist of a set of [transformation rules](wikipedia://en/252311) (also called *inference rules*) or a set of [axioms](wikipedia://en/928), or have both. A formal system is used to [derive](wikipedia://en/183478) one expression from one or more other expressions.

A *formal system* can be formally defined as an ordered triple \<α,$\mathcal{I}$,$\mathcal{D}$d>, where $\mathcal{D}$d is the relation of direct derivability. This relation is understood in a comprehensive [sense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_and_reference) such that the primitive sentences of the formal system are taken as directly [derivable](wikipedia://en/1197184) from the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) of sentences. Direct derivability is a relation between a sentence and a finite, possibly empty set of sentences. Axioms are so chosen that every first place member of $\mathcal{D}$d is a member of $\mathcal{I}$ and every second place member is a finite subset of $\mathcal{I}$.

A *formal system* can also be defined with only the relation $\mathcal{D}$d. Thereby can be omitted $\mathcal{I}$ and α in the definitions of *interpreted formal language*, and *interpreted formal system*. However, this method can be more difficult to understand and use.[^o6]

### Formal proofs {#Formal_proofs}

A *formal proof* is a sequence of well-formed formulas of a formal language, the last of which is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) of a formal system. The theorem is a [syntactic consequence](wikipedia://en/37531624) of all the well formed formulae that precede it in the proof system. For a well formed formula to qualify as part of a proof, it must result from applying a rule of the deductive apparatus of some formal system to the previous well formed formulae in the proof sequence.

### Interpretations {#Interpretations}

An *interpretation* of a formal system is the assignment of meanings to the symbols and [truth-values](wikipedia://en/161711) to the sentences of the formal system. The study of interpretations is called [Formal semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_semantics_%28logic%29). *Giving an interpretation* is synonymous with *constructing a [model](wikipedia://en/4055928)*.

## Important distinctions {#Important_distinctions}

### Metalanguage–object language {#Metalanguage–object_language}

In metalogic, formal languages are sometimes called *object languages*. The language used to make statements about an object language is called a *metalanguage*. This distinction is a key difference between logic and metalogic. While logic deals with *proofs in a formal system*, expressed in some formal language, metalogic deals with *proofs about a formal system* which are expressed in a metalanguage about some object language.

### Syntax–semantics {#Syntax–semantics}

In metalogic, 'syntax' has to do with formal languages or formal systems without regard to any interpretation of them, whereas, 'semantics' has to do with interpretations of formal languages. The term 'syntactic' has a slightly wider scope than 'proof-theoretic', since it may be applied to properties of formal languages without any deductive systems, as well as to formal systems. 'Semantic' is synonymous with 'model-theoretic'.

### Use–mention {#Use–mention}

In metalogic, the words *use* and *mention*, in both their noun and verb forms, take on a technical sense in order to identify an important distinction.[^o3] The *use–mention distinction* (sometimes referred to as the *words-as-words distinction*) is the distinction between *using* a word (or phrase) and *mentioning* it. Usually it is indicated that an expression is being mentioned rather than used by enclosing it in quotation marks, printing it in italics, or setting the expression by itself on a line. The enclosing in quotes of an expression gives us the [name](wikipedia://en/164226) of an expression, for example:

"Metalogic" is the name of this article. This article is about metalogic.

### Type–token {#Type–token}

The *type-token distinction* is a distinction in metalogic, that separates an abstract concept from the objects which are particular instances of the concept. For example, the particular bicycle in your garage is a token of the [type](wikipedia://en/14934822) of thing known as "The bicycle." Whereas, the bicycle in your garage is in a particular place at a particular time, that is not true of "the bicycle" as used in the sentence: "*The bicycle* has become more popular recently." This distinction is used to clarify the meaning of [symbols](wikipedia://en/24028642) of [formal languages](wikipedia://en/10939).

## History {#History}

Metalogical questions have been asked since the time of [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308).[^o18] However, it was only with the rise of formal languages in the late 19th and early 20th century that investigations into the foundations of logic began to flourish. In 1904, [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) observed that in investigating the [foundations of mathematics](wikipedia://en/169358) that logical notions are presupposed, and therefore a simultaneous account of metalogical and [metamathematical](wikipedia://en/183508) principles was required. Today, metalogic and metamathematics are largely synonymous with each other, and both have been substantially subsumed by [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) in academia. A possible alternate, less mathematical model may be found in the writings of [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) and other [semioticians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotics).

## Results {#Results}

section Results in metalogic primally involve demonstrating properties of particular [formal systems](wikipedia://en/396102). Key properties include [consistency](wikipedia://en/75802), [completeness](wikipedia://en/31271451), and [decidability](wikipedia://en/913118). Other results are important to metalogic more for providing a tool utilized frequently in proving subsequent results. For example, the proof the [uncountability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountability) of the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) ([Cantor's theorem](wikipedia://en/341442) 1891) which introduced the technique of [diagonalization](wikipedia://en/51426).

Major completeness or incompleteness results include:

- Completeness of truth-functional [propositional logic](wikipedia://en/18154) ([Paul Bernays](wikipedia://en/2556580) 1918),[^o21] (Emil Post 1920)[^o3]
- Completeness of first-order monadic predicate logic (Leopold Löwenheim 1915)
- Completeness of first-order [predicate logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predicate_logic) ([Gödel's completeness theorem](wikipedia://en/12450) 1930)
- [Gödel's first incompleteness theorem](wikipedia://en/58863#First_incompleteness_theorem) 1931
- [Gödel's second incompleteness theorem](wikipedia://en/58863#Second_incompleteness_theorem) 1931

Major consistency results include:

- Consistency of truth-functional [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) ([Emil Post](wikipedia://en/362382) 1920),
- Consistency of first-order [monadic predicate logic](wikipedia://en/8015680) ([Leopold Löwenheim](wikipedia://en/755668) 1915)
- Consistency of first-order predicate logic ([David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) and [Wilhelm Ackermann](wikipedia://en/62727) 1928)

Major decidability results include:

- Decidability of truth-functional propositional logic (Emil Post 1920)[^o3]
- Decidability of first-order monadic predicate logic (Leopold Löwenheim 1915)
- Undecidability of first-order predicate logic ([Church's theorem](wikipedia://en/9672) 1936)

Other important meta logic results include:

- Existence of models of arbitrary infinite cardinality for countable first-order theories with an infinite model, [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](wikipedia://en/341482) ([Leopold Löwenheim](wikipedia://en/755668) 1915 and [Thoralf Skolem](wikipedia://en/453765) 1919)
- Proof of the [cut-elimination theorem](wikipedia://en/910234) for the [sequent calculus](wikipedia://en/252329) ([Gentzen](wikipedia://en/46027)'s *Hauptsatz* 1934)
- [Tarski's undefinability theorem](wikipedia://en/583785) (Gödel and Tarski in the 1930s)
