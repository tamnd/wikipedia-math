# Cantor distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cantor distribution"}

The **Cantor distribution** is the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) whose [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) is the [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819).

:::

This distribution has neither a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) nor a [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725), since although its cumulative distribution function is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122), the distribution is not [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670) with respect to [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), nor does it have any point-masses. It is thus neither a discrete nor an absolutely continuous probability distribution, nor is it a mixture of these. Rather it is an example of a [singular distribution](wikipedia://en/7820200).

Its cumulative distribution function is continuous everywhere but horizontal almost everywhere, so is sometimes referred to as the [Devil's staircase](wikipedia://en/51453), although that term has a more general meaning.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

The [support](wikipedia://en/381013) of the Cantor distribution is the [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172), itself the intersection of the (countably infinitely many) sets:

$$\begin{aligned} C_0 = {} & [0,1] \\[8pt] C_1 = {} & [0,1/3]\cup[2/3,1] \\[8pt] C_2 = {} & [0,1/9]\cup[2/9,1/3]\cup[2/3,7/9]\cup[8/9,1] \\[8pt] C_3 = {} & [0,1/27]\cup[2/27,1/9]\cup[2/9,7/27]\cup[8/27,1/3]\cup \\[4pt] {} & [2/3,19/27]\cup[20/27,7/9]\cup[8/9,25/27]\cup[26/27,1] \\[8pt] C_4 = {} & \cdots \end{aligned}$$

The Cantor distribution is the unique probability distribution for which for any *C*<sub>*t*</sub> (*t* ∈ { 0, 1, 2, 3, ... }), the probability of a particular interval in *C*<sub>*t*</sub> containing the Cantor-distributed random variable is identically 2<sup>−*t*</sup> on each one of the 2<sup>*t*</sup> intervals.

:::

## Moments {#Moments}

It is easy to see by symmetry and being bounded that for a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) *X* having this distribution, its [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) E(*X*) = 1/2, and that all odd central moments of *X* are 0.

The [law of total variance](wikipedia://en/312408) can be used to find the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) var(*X*), as follows. For the above set *C*<sub>1</sub>, let *Y* = 0 if *X* ∈ \[0,1/3\], and 1 if *X* ∈ \[2/3,1\]. Then:

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{var}(X) & = \operatorname{E}(\operatorname{var}(X\mid Y)) + \operatorname{var}(\operatorname{E}(X\mid Y)) \\ & = \frac{1}{9}\operatorname{var}(X) + \operatorname{var} \left\{ \begin{matrix} 1/6 & \mbox{with probability}\ 1/2 \\ 5/6 & \mbox{with probability}\ 1/2 \end{matrix} \right\} \\ & = \frac{1}{9}\operatorname{var}(X) + \frac{1}{9} \end{aligned}$$

From this we get:

$$\operatorname{var}(X)=\frac{1}{8}.$$

A closed-form expression for any even [central moment](wikipedia://en/19983) can be found by first obtaining the even [cumulants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulants)[^o5]

$$\kappa_{2n} = \frac{2^{2n-1} (2^{2n}-1) B_{2n}} {n\, (3^{2n}-1)}, \,\!$$

where *B*<sub>2*n*</sub> is the 2*n*th [Bernoulli number](wikipedia://en/4964), and then [expressing the moments as functions of the cumulants](wikipedia://en/359684#Cumulants_and_moments).
