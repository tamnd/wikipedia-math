# Tomography

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tomography"}

*Image: TomographyPrinciple Illustration.png.* **Fig.1**: Basic principle of tomography: superposition free tomographic cross sections S<sub>1</sub> and S<sub>2</sub> compared with the (not tomographic) projected image P

*Image: Sagittal brain MRI.jpg.* [Median plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Median_plane) [sagittal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sagittal_plane) tomography of the head by [magnetic resonance imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging)

**Tomography** is [imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaging) by sections or sectioning that uses any kind of penetrating [wave](wikipedia://en/33516). The method is used in [radiology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radiology), [archaeology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archaeology), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), [atmospheric science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atmospheric_science), [geophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geophysics), [oceanography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oceanography), [plasma physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_physics), [materials science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science), [cosmochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmochemistry), [astrophysics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrophysics), [quantum information](wikipedia://en/25274), and other areas of [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science).

:::

The word *tomography* is derived from [Ancient Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek) τόμος, grc slice, section and γράφω, grc to write or, in this context as well, to describe. A device used in tomography is called a **tomograph**, while the image produced is a **tomogram**.

In many cases, the production of these images is based on the mathematical procedure [tomographic reconstruction](wikipedia://en/995908), such as [X-ray computed tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_computed_tomography) technically being produced from multiple [projectional radiographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectional_radiograph). Many different [reconstruction algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reconstruction_algorithm) exist. Most algorithms fall into one of two categories: [filtered back projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtered_back_projection) (FBP) and [iterative reconstruction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iterative_reconstruction) (IR). These procedures give inexact results: they represent a compromise between accuracy and computation time required. FBP demands fewer computational resources, while IR generally produces fewer artifacts (errors in the reconstruction) at a higher computing cost.[^o2]

Although MRI ([magnetic resonance imaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_resonance_imaging)), [optical coherence tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_coherence_tomography) and [ultrasound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultrasound) are transmission methods, they typically do not require movement of the transmitter to acquire data from different directions. In MRI, both projections and higher spatial harmonics are sampled by applying spatially varying magnetic fields; no moving parts are necessary to generate an image. On the other hand, since ultrasound and optical coherence tomography uses time-of-flight to spatially encode the received signal, it is not strictly a tomographic method and does not require multiple image acquisitions.

Some recent advances rely on using simultaneously integrated physical phenomena, e.g. X-rays for both [CT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computed_tomography) and [angiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angiography), combined [CT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan)/[MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRI) and combined CT/[PET](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positron_emission_tomography).

[Discrete tomography](wikipedia://en/6778984) and [Geometric tomography](wikipedia://en/32445296), on the other hand, are research areas that deal with the reconstruction of objects that are discrete (such as crystals) or homogeneous. They are concerned with reconstruction methods, and as such they are not restricted to any of the particular (experimental) tomography methods listed above.

### Synchrotron X-ray tomographic microscopy {#Synchrotron_X-ray_tomographic_microscopy}

A new technique called synchrotron X-ray tomographic microscopy ([SRXTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SRXTM)) allows for detailed three-dimensional scanning of fossils.[^o64][^o65]

The construction of third-generation [synchrotron sources](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Synchrotron_light_source) combined with the tremendous improvement of detector technology, data storage and processing capabilities since the 1990s has led to a boost of high-end synchrotron tomography in materials research with a wide range of different applications, e.g. the visualization and quantitative analysis of differently absorbing phases, microporosities, cracks, precipitates or grains in a specimen. Synchrotron radiation is created by accelerating free particles in high vacuum. By the laws of electrodynamics this acceleration leads to the emission of electromagnetic radiation (Jackson, 1975). Linear particle acceleration is one possibility, but apart from the very high electric fields one would need it is more practical to hold the charged particles on a closed trajectory in order to obtain a source of continuous radiation. Magnetic fields are used to force the particles onto the desired orbit and prevent them from flying in a straight line. The radial acceleration associated with the change of direction then generates radiation.[^o66]

## Volume rendering {#Volume_rendering}

*Image: Image of 3D volumetric QCT scan.jpg.* Multiple X-ray [computed tomographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan) (with [quantitative mineral density calibration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantitative_computed_tomography)) stacked to form a 3D model

Volume rendering is a set of techniques used to display a 2D projection of a 3D discretely [sampled](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampling_%28signal_processing%29) [data set](wikipedia://en/8495), typically a 3D [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879). A typical 3D data set is a group of 2D slice images acquired, for example, by a [CT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan), [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MRI), or [MicroCT](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MicroCT) [scanner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_scanner). These are usually acquired in a regular pattern (e.g., one slice every millimeter) and usually have a regular number of image [pixels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pixel) in a regular pattern. This is an example of a regular volumetric grid, with each volume element, or [voxel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voxel) represented by a single value that is obtained by sampling the immediate area surrounding the voxel.

To render a 2D projection of the 3D data set, one first needs to define a [camera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_camera) in space relative to the volume. Also, one needs to define the [opacity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opacity_%28optics%29) and color of every voxel. This is usually defined using an [RGBA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RGBA_color_model) (for red, green, blue, alpha) [transfer function](wikipedia://en/31146) that defines the RGBA value for every possible voxel value.

For example, a volume may be viewed by extracting [isosurfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isosurface) (surfaces of equal values) from the volume and rendering them as [polygonal meshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon_mesh) or by rendering the volume directly as a block of data. The [marching cubes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marching_cubes) algorithm is a common technique for extracting an isosurface from volume data. Direct volume rendering is a computationally intensive task that may be performed in several ways.

## History {#History}

[Focal plane tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focal_plane_tomography) was developed in the 1930s by the radiologist [Alessandro Vallebona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Vallebona), and proved useful in reducing the problem of superimposition of structures in [projectional radiography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projectional_radiography).

In a 1953 article in the medical journal [*Chest*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chest_%28journal%29), B. Pollak of the [Fort William Sanatorium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fort_William_Sanatorium) described the use of planography, another term for tomography.[^o70]

Focal plane tomography remained the conventional form of tomography until being largely replaced by mainly [computed tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computed_tomography) in the late 1970s.[^o71] Focal plane tomography uses the fact that the focal plane appears sharper, while structures in other planes appear blurred. By moving an X-ray source and the film in opposite directions during the exposure, and modifying the direction and extent of the movement, operators can select different focal planes which contain the structures of interest.
