# Cofiniteness

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cofiniteness"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **cofinite** [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of a set $X$ is a subset $A$ whose [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) in $X$ is a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742). In other words, $A$ contains all but finitely many elements of $X.$ If the complement is not finite, but is countable, then one says the set is [cocountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cocountable).

:::

These arise naturally when generalizing structures on finite sets to infinite sets, particularly on infinite products, as in the product topology or direct sum.

This use of the prefix "**co**" to describe a property possessed by a set's [**co**mplement](wikipedia://en/54347) is consistent with its use in other terms such as "[**co**meagre set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comeagre_set)".

## Boolean algebras {#Boolean_algebras}

The set of all subsets of $X$ that are either finite or cofinite forms a [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/3959), which means that it is closed under the operations of [union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Union_%28mathematics%29), [intersection](wikipedia://en/41780044), and complementation. This Boolean algebra is the **finite–cofinite algebra** on $X.$

In the other direction, a Boolean algebra $A$ has a unique non-principal [ultrafilter](wikipedia://en/31911) (that is, a [maximal filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_filter) not generated by a single element of the algebra) if and only if there exists an infinite set $X$ such that $A$ is isomorphic to the finite–cofinite algebra on $X.$ In this case, the non-principal ultrafilter is the set of all cofinite subsets of $X$.

## Cofinite topology {#Cofinite_topology}

The **cofinite topology** or the **finite complement topology** is a [topology](wikipedia://en/30450) that can be defined on every set $X.$ It has precisely the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) and all cofinite subsets of $X$ as open sets. As a consequence, in the cofinite topology, the only closed subsets are finite sets, or the whole of $X.$ For this reason, the cofinite topology is also known as the **finite-closed topology**. Symbolically, one writes the topology as

$$\mathcal{T} = \{A \subseteq X : A = \varnothing \mbox{ or } X \setminus A \mbox{ is finite} \}.$$

This topology occurs naturally in the context of the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744). Since [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) in one variable over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$ are zero on finite sets, or the whole of $K,$ the Zariski topology on $K$ (considered as *affine line*) is the cofinite topology. The same is true for any *[irreducible](wikipedia://en/2652476)* [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260); it is not true, for example, for $XY = 0$ in the plane.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

- Subspaces: Every [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926) of the cofinite topology is also a cofinite topology.
- Compactness: Since every [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) contains all but finitely many points of $X,$ the space $X$ is [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) and [sequentially compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequentially_compact).
- Separation: The cofinite topology is the [coarsest topology](wikipedia://en/393069) satisfying the [T<sub>1</sub> axiom](wikipedia://en/148721); that is, it is the smallest topology for which every [singleton set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set) is closed. In fact, an arbitrary topology on $X$ satisfies the T<sub>1</sub> axiom if and only if it contains the cofinite topology. If $X$ is finite then the cofinite topology is simply the [discrete topology](wikipedia://en/56061). If $X$ is not finite then this topology is not [Hausdorff (T<sub>2</sub>)](wikipedia://en/13637), [regular](wikipedia://en/53058) or [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) because no two nonempty open sets are disjoint (that is, it is [hyperconnected](wikipedia://en/7556651)).

:::

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Double-pointed cofinite topology"}

### Double-pointed cofinite topology {#Double-pointed_cofinite_topology}

The **double-pointed cofinite topology** is the cofinite topology with every point doubled; that is, it is the [topological product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_product) of the cofinite topology with the [indiscrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_topology) on a two-element set. It is not [T<sub>0</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/T0_space) or [T<sub>1</sub>](wikipedia://en/148721), since the points of each doublet are [topologically indistinguishable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_indistinguishable). It is, however, [R<sub>0</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R0_space) since topologically distinguishable points are [separated](wikipedia://en/148911). The space is [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_%28topology%29) as the product of two compact spaces; alternatively, it is compact because each nonempty open set contains all but finitely many points.

For an example of the countable double-pointed cofinite topology, the set $\mathbb{Z}$ of integers can be given a topology such that every [even number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) $2n$ is [topologically indistinguishable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topologically_indistinguishable) from the following [odd number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) $2n+1$. The closed sets are the unions of finitely many pairs $2n,2n+1,$ or the whole set. The open sets are the complements of the closed sets; namely, each open set consists of all but a finite number of pairs $2n,2n+1,$ or is the empty set.

:::

## Other examples {#Other_examples}

### Product topology {#Product_topology}

The [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910) on a product of topological spaces $\prod X_i$ has [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29) $\prod U_i$ where $U_i \subseteq X_i$ is open, and cofinitely many $U_i = X_i.$

The analog without requiring that cofinitely many factors are the whole space is the [box topology](wikipedia://en/954333).

### Direct sum {#Direct_sum}

The elements of the [direct sum of modules](wikipedia://en/58899) $\bigoplus M_i$ are sequences $\alpha_i \in M_i$ where cofinitely many $\alpha_i = 0.$

The analog without requiring that cofinitely many summands are zero is the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887).
