# Support (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Support"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **support** of a [real-valued](wikipedia://en/20646438) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ is the [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the function's [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) consisting of those elements that are not mapped to zero. If the domain of $f$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450), then the support of $f$ is instead defined as the smallest [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) containing all points not mapped to zero. This concept is used widely in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formulation"}

## Formulation {#Formulation}

Suppose that $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is a real-valued function whose [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) is an arbitrary set $X.$ The **set-theoretic support** of $f,$ written $\operatorname{supp}(f),$ is the set of points in $X$ where $f$ is non-zero:

$$\operatorname{supp}(f) = \{ x \in X \,:\, f(x) \neq 0\}.$$

The support of $f$ is the smallest subset of $X$ with the property that $f$ is zero on the subset's complement. If $f(x) = 0$ for all but a finite number of points $x \in X,$ then $f$ is said to have **finite support**.

If the set $X$ has an additional structure (for example, a [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_%28structure%29)), then the support of $f$ is defined in an analogous way as the smallest subset of $X$ of an appropriate type such that $f$ vanishes in an appropriate sense on its complement. The notion of support also extends in a natural way to functions taking values in more general sets than $\mathbb{R}$ and to other objects, such as [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) or [distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).

:::

## Closed support {#Closed_support}

The most common situation occurs when $X$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) (such as the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) or $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697)) and $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ is a [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) real- (or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-) valued function. In this case, the **support of $f$**, $\operatorname{supp}(f)$, or the **closed support** **of** **$f$**, is defined topologically as the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) (taken in $X$) of the subset of $X$ where $f$ is non-zero[^o5][^o6][^o7] that is,

$$\operatorname{supp}(f) := \operatorname{cl}_X\left(\{x \in X \,:\, f(x) \neq 0 \}\right) = \overline{f^{-1}\left(\{ 0 \}^{\mathrm{c}}\right)}.$$

Since the intersection of closed sets is closed, $\operatorname{supp}(f)$ is the intersection of all closed sets that contain the set-theoretic support of $f.$ Note that if the function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \supseteq X \to \mathbb{R}$ is defined on an open subset $X \subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$, then the closure is still taken with respect to $X$ and not with respect to the ambient $\mathbb{R}^n$.

For example, if $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ is the function defined by

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1 - x^2 & \text{if } |x| < 1 \\ 0 & \text{if } |x| \geq 1 \end{cases}$$

then $\operatorname{supp}(f)$, the support of $f$, or the closed support of $f$, is the closed interval $[-1, 1],$ since $f$ is non-zero on the open interval $(-1, 1)$ and the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of this set is $[-1, 1].$

The notion of closed support is usually applied to continuous functions, but the definition makes sense for arbitrary real or complex-valued functions on a topological space, and some authors do not require that $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ (or $f : X \to \mathbb{C}$) be continuous.[^o8]

::: {#o10 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Compact support"}

## Compact support {#Compact_support}

Functions with **compact support** on a topological space $X$ are those whose closed support is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) subset of $X.$ If $X$ is the real line, or $n$-dimensional Euclidean space, then a function has compact support if and only if it has **bounded support**, since a subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is compact if and only if it is closed and bounded.

For example, the function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined above is a continuous function with compact support $[-1, 1].$ If $f : \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ is a smooth function then because $f$ is identically $0$ on the open subset $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \operatorname{supp}(f),$ all of $f$'s partial derivatives of all orders are also identically $0$ on $\mathbb{R}^n \setminus \operatorname{supp}(f).$

The condition of compact support is stronger than the condition of [vanishing at infinity](wikipedia://en/461986). For example, the function $f : \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb{R}$ defined by

$$f(x) = \frac{1}{1+x^2}$$

vanishes at infinity, since $f(x) \to 0$ as $|x| \to \infty,$ but its support $\mathbb{R}$ is not compact.

Real-valued compactly supported [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) on a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) are sometimes called [bump functions](wikipedia://en/815799). [Mollifiers](wikipedia://en/1596063) are an important special case of bump functions as they can be used in [distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) to create [sequences](wikipedia://en/27838) of smooth functions approximating nonsmooth (generalized) functions, via [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519).

In a locally compact Hausdorff space, continuous functions with compact support are [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in the space of continuous functions that vanish at infinity. As an intuition for more complex examples, and in the language of [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602), for any $\varepsilon > 0,$ any function $f$ on the real line $\mathbb{R}$ that vanishes at infinity can be approximated by choosing an appropriate compact subset $C$ of $\mathbb{R}$ such that

$$\left|f(x) - I_C(x) f(x)\right| < \varepsilon$$

for all $x \in X,$ where $I_C$ is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $C$. One then can then choose a continuous cutoff function that vanishes outside a small thickening of $C$.

In the theory of [distributions](wikipedia://en/51955), it is also true that the smooth compactly supported [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) are dense in the space of [Schwartz functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwartz_functions). They are also dense in every [Lp space](wikipedia://en/45194) for $p<\infty$. Thus in most good cases, the compactly supported functions of the appropriate class are dense in many different topologies, and so they are fundamental in analysis.

Every continuous function on a compact topological space has compact support since every closed subset of a compact space is indeed compact.

:::

## Essential support {#Essential_support}

If $X$ is a topological [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270) with a [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) $\mu$ (such as $\mathbb{R}^n,$ or a [Lebesgue measurable](wikipedia://en/18198) subset of $\mathbb{R}^n,$ equipped with Lebesgue measure), then one typically identifies functions that are equal $\mu$-almost everywhere. In that case, the **essential support** of a measurable function $f : X \to \mathbb{R}$ written **$\operatorname{ess\,supp}(f),$** is defined to be the smallest closed subset $F$ of $X$ such that $f = 0$ $\mu$-almost everywhere outside $F.$ Equivalently, $\operatorname{ess\,supp}(f)$ is the complement of the largest [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) on which $f = 0$ $\mu$-almost everywhere[^o12]

$$\operatorname{ess\,supp}(f) := X \setminus \bigcup \left\{\Omega \subseteq X : \Omega\text{ is open and } f = 0\, \mu\text{-almost everywhere in } \Omega \right\}.$$

The essential support of a function $f$ depends on the [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) $\mu$ as well as on $f,$ and it may be strictly smaller than the closed support. For example, if $f : [0, 1] \to \mathbb{R}$ is the [Dirichlet function](wikipedia://en/630005) that is $0$ on irrational numbers and $1$ on rational numbers, and $[0, 1]$ is equipped with Lebesgue measure, then the support of $f$ is the entire interval $[0, 1],$ but the essential support of $f$ is empty, since $f$ is equal almost everywhere to the zero function.

In analysis one nearly always wants to use the essential support of a function, rather than its closed support, when the two sets are different, so $\operatorname{ess\,supp}(f)$ is often written simply as $\operatorname{supp}(f)$ and referred to as the support.[^o12][^o13]

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

If $M$ is an arbitrary set containing zero, the concept of support is immediately generalizable to functions $f : X \to M.$ Support may also be defined for any [algebraic structure](wikipedia://en/106364) with [identity](wikipedia://en/14962) (such as a [group](wikipedia://en/19447), [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652), or [composition algebra](wikipedia://en/3861635)), in which the identity element assumes the role of zero. For instance, the family $\mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}}$ of functions from the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) to the [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) is the [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable) set of integer sequences. The subfamily $\left\{ f \in \mathbb{Z}^{\mathbb{N}} : f \text{ has finite support } \right\}$ is the countable set of all integer sequences that have only finitely many nonzero entries.

Functions of finite support are used in defining algebraic structures such as [group rings](wikipedia://en/349223) and [free abelian groups](wikipedia://en/247296).[^o16]

:::

## In probability and measure theory {#In_probability_and_measure_theory}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the support of a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) can be loosely thought of as the [closure](wikipedia://en/293802) of the set of possible values of a random variable having that distribution. There are, however, some subtleties to consider when dealing with general distributions defined on a [sigma algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra), rather than on a topological space.

More formally, if $X : \Omega \to \mathbb{R}$ is a random variable on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ then the support of $X$ is the smallest closed set $R_X \subseteq \mathbb{R}$ such that $P\left(X \in R_X\right) = 1.$

In practice however, the support of a [discrete random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_random_variable) $X$ is often defined as the set $R_X = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : P(X = x) > 0 \}$ and the support of a [continuous random variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_random_variable) $X$ is defined as the set $R_X = \{x \in \mathbb{R} : f_X(x) > 0 \}$ where $f_X(x)$ is a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of $X$ (the set-theoretic support).[^o18]

Note that the word support can refer to the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of the [likelihood](wikipedia://en/44968) of a probability density function.[^o19]

## Support of a distribution {#Support_of_a_distribution}

It is possible also to talk about the support of a [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29), such as the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021) $\delta(x)$ on the real line. In that example, we can consider test functions $F,$ which are [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) with support not including the point $0.$ Since $\delta(F)$ (the distribution $\delta$ applied as [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) to $F$) is $0$ for such functions, we can say that the support of $\delta$ is $\{ 0 \}$ only. Since measures (including [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437)) on the real line are special cases of distributions, we can also speak of the support of a measure in the same way.

Suppose that $f$ is a distribution, and that $U$ is an open set in Euclidean space such that, for all test functions $\phi$ such that the support of $\phi$ is contained in $U,$ $f(\phi) = 0.$ Then $f$ is said to vanish on $U.$ Now, if $f$ vanishes on an arbitrary family $U_{\alpha}$ of open sets, then for any test function $\phi$ supported in $\bigcup U_{\alpha},$ a simple argument based on the compactness of the support of $\phi$ and a partition of unity shows that $f(\phi) = 0$ as well. Hence we can define the support of $f$ as the complement of the largest open set on which $f$ vanishes. For example, the support of the Dirac delta is $\{ 0 \}.$

::: {#o22 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Singular support"}

## Singular support {#Singular_support}

In [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659) in particular, it is interesting to study the **singular support** of a distribution. This has the intuitive interpretation as the set of points at which a distribution fails to be a smooth function.

For example, the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of the [Heaviside step function](wikipedia://en/87299) can, up to constant factors, be considered to be $1/x$ (a function) except at $x = 0.$ While $x = 0$ is clearly a special point, it is more precise to say that the transform of the distribution has singular support $\{ 0 \}$: it cannot accurately be expressed as a function in relation to test functions with support including $0.$ It can be expressed as an application of a [Cauchy principal value](wikipedia://en/408195) improper integral.

For distributions in several variables, singular supports allow one to define [wave front sets](wikipedia://en/1904007) and understand [Huygens' principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huygens'_principle) in terms of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396). Singular supports may also be used to understand phenomena special to distribution theory, such as attempts to 'multiply' distributions (squaring the Dirac delta function fails – essentially because the singular supports of the distributions to be multiplied should be disjoint).

:::

## Family of supports {#Family_of_supports}

An abstract notion of **family of supports** on a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X,$ suitable for [sheaf theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheaf_theory), was defined by [Henri Cartan](wikipedia://en/362536). In extending [Poincaré duality](wikipedia://en/540732) to [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) that are not compact, the 'compact support' idea enters naturally on one side of the duality; see for example [Alexander–Spanier cohomology](wikipedia://en/694843).

Bredon, *Sheaf Theory* (2nd edition, 1997) gives these definitions. A family $\Phi$ of closed subsets of $X$ is a family of supports, if it is [down-closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Down-closed) and closed under [finite union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_union). Its extent is the union over $\Phi.$ A paracompactifying family of supports that satisfies further that any $Y$ in $\Phi$ is, with the [subspace topology](wikipedia://en/910926), a [paracompact space](wikipedia://en/48631); and has some $Z$ in $\Phi$ which is a [neighbourhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neighbourhood_%28topology%29). If $X$ is a [locally compact space](wikipedia://en/48632), assumed [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637), the family of all [compact subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_subset) satisfies the further conditions, making it paracompactifying.
