# Proof that 22/7 exceeds π

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proof that 22/7 exceeds π"}

*Image: 22 Divided by 7 Circle.png.* This is not a perfect 22/7 circle, because 22/7 is not a perfect representation of pi.

[Proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) of the mathematical result that the [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) $\tfrac{22}{7}$ is greater than [$\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601) (pi) date back to antiquity. One of these proofs, more recently developed but requiring only elementary techniques from calculus, has attracted attention in modern mathematics due to its [mathematical elegance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_elegance) and its connections to the theory of [Diophantine approximations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diophantine_approximations). Stephen Lucas calls this proof "one of the more beautiful results related to approximating $\pi$".[^o2] Julian Havil ends a discussion of [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) approximations of $\pi$ with the result, describing it as "impossible to resist mentioning" in that context.[^o3]

:::

The purpose of the proof is not primarily to convince its readers that $\tfrac{22}{7}$ (or $3\tfrac{1}{7}$) is indeed bigger than $\pi$. Systematic methods of computing the value of $\pi$ exist. If one knows that $\pi$ is approximately 3.14159, then it trivially follows that $\pi$ \< $\tfrac{22}{7}$, which is approximately 3.142857. But it takes much less work to show that $\pi$ \< $\tfrac{22}{7}$ by the method used in this proof than to show that $\pi$ is approximately 3.14159.

## Background {#Background}

$\tfrac{22}{7}$ is a widely used [Diophantine approximation](wikipedia://en/251558) of $\pi$. It is a convergent in the simple [continued fraction](wikipedia://en/739199) expansion of $\pi$. It is greater than $\pi$, as can be readily seen in the [decimal expansions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_expansion) of these values:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{22}{7} & = 3. \overline{142\,857}, \\ \pi\, & = 3.141 \,592\,65\ldots \end{aligned}$$

The approximation has been known since antiquity. [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844) wrote the first known proof that $\tfrac{22}{7}$ is an overestimate in the 3rd century BCE, although he may not have been the first to use that approximation. His proof proceeds by showing that $\tfrac{22}{7}$ is greater than the ratio of the [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) with 96 sides to the diameter of a circle it circumscribes.

::: {#o6 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

The proof first devised by British electrical engineer Donald Percy Dalzell (1898–1988) in 1944[^o7] can be expressed very succinctly:

$$0 < \int_0^1 \frac{x^4\left(1-x\right)^4}{1+x^2} \, dx = \frac{22}{7} - \pi.$$

Therefore, $\tfrac{22}{7}$ > $\pi$.

The evaluation of this integral was the first problem in the 1968 [Putnam Competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lowell_Putnam_Mathematical_Competition).[^o8]

:::

## Details of evaluation of the integral {#Details_of_evaluation_of_the_integral}

That the [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) is positive follows from the fact that the [integrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrand) is [non-negative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-negative); the denominator is positive and the numerator is a product of nonnegative numbers. One can also easily check that the integrand is strictly positive for at least one point in the range of integration, say at $\tfrac{1}{2}$. Since the integrand is continuous at that point and nonnegative elsewhere, the integral from 0 to 1 must be strictly positive.

It remains to show that the integral in fact evaluates to the desired quantity:

$$\begin{aligned} 0 & < \int_0^1\frac{x^4\left(1-x\right)^4}{1+x^2}\, dx \\[8pt] & = \int_0^1\frac{x^4-4x^5+6x^6-4x^7+x^8}{1+x^2}\, dx & \text{expansion of terms in the numerator} \\[8pt] & = \int_0^1 \left(x^6-4x^5+5x^4-4x^2+4-\frac{4}{1+x^2}\right) \, dx & \text{ using polynomial long division} & \\[8pt] & = \left.\left(\frac{x^7}{7}-\frac{2x^6}{3}+ x^5- \frac{4x^3}{3}+4x-4\arctan{x}\right)\,\right|_0^1 & \text{definite integration} \\[6pt] & = \frac{1}{7}-\frac{2}{3}+1-\frac{4}{3}+4-\pi\quad & \text{with }\arctan(1) = \frac{\pi}{4} \text{ and } \arctan(0) = 0 \\[8pt] & = \frac{22}{7}-\pi. & \text{addition} \end{aligned}$$

(See [polynomial long division](wikipedia://en/314205).)

## Quick upper and lower bounds {#Quick_upper_and_lower_bounds}

In (Dalzell 1944), it is pointed out that if 1 is substituted for $x$ in the denominator, one gets a lower bound on the integral, and if 0 is substituted for $x$ in the denominator, one gets an upper bound:[^o12]

$$\frac{1}{1260} = \int_0^1\frac{x^4 \left(1-x\right)^4}{2}\,dx < \int_0^1\frac{x^4 \left(1-x\right)^4}{1+x^2}\,dx < \int_0^1\frac{x^4 \left(1-x\right)^4}{1}\,dx = {1 \over 630}.$$

Thus we have

$$\frac{22}{7} - \frac{1}{630} < \pi < \frac{22}{7} - \frac{1}{1260},$$

hence 3.1412 \< $\pi$ \< 3.1421 in decimal expansion. The bounds deviate by less than 0.015% from $\pi$. See also (Dalzell 1971).[^o14]

## Proof that 355/113 exceeds $\pi$ {#Proof_that_355/113_exceeds}

As discussed in (Lucas 2005), the well-known Diophantine approximation and far better upper estimate [$\tfrac{355}{113}$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/355/113) for $\pi$ follows from the relation

$$0<\int_0^1\frac{x^8\left(1-x\right)^8\left(25+816x^2\right)}{3164\left(1+x^2\right)}\,dx=\frac{355}{113}-\pi.$$

$$\frac{355}{113}= 3.141\,592\,92\ldots,$$

where the first six digits after the decimal point agree with those of $\pi$. Substituting 1 for $x$ in the denominator, we get the lower bound

$$\int_0^1\frac{x^8\left(1-x\right)^8\left(25+816x^2\right)}{6328}\,dx =\frac{911}{5\,261\,111\,856} = 0.000\,000\,173\ldots,$$

substituting 0 for $x$ in the denominator, we get twice this value as an upper bound, hence

$$\frac{355}{113}-\frac{911}{2\,630\,555\,928}<\pi<\frac{355}{113}-\frac{911}{5\,261\,111\,856}\,.$$

In decimal expansion, this means **\3.141** **592** 57 \< $\pi$ \< **\3.141** **592** 74, where the bold digits of the lower and upper bound are those of $\pi$.

:::

## Extensions {#Extensions}

The above ideas can be generalized to get better approximations of $\pi$.[^o20][^o2] For explicit calculations, consider, for every integer $n \ge 1$,

$$\frac1{2^{2n-1}}\int_0^1 x^{4n}(1-x)^{4n}\,dx <\frac1{2^{2n-2}}\int_0^1\frac{x^{4n}(1-x)^{4n}}{1+x^2}\,dx <\frac1{2^{2n-2}}\int_0^1 x^{4n}(1-x)^{4n}\,dx,$$

where the middle integral evaluates to

$$\begin{aligned} \frac1{2^{2n-2}} & \int_0^1\frac{x^{4n}(1-x)^{4n}}{1+x^2}\,dx\\[6pt] = {} & \sum_{j=0}^{2n-1}\frac{(-1)^j}{2^{2n-j-2}(8n-j-1)\binom{8n-j-2}{4n+j}} +(-1)^n\left(\pi-4\sum_{j=0}^{3n-1}\frac{(-1)^j}{2j+1}\right) \end{aligned}$$

involving $\pi$. The last sum also appears in [Leibniz' formula for $\pi$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_formula_for_pi). The correction term and [error bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Error_bound) is given by

$$\begin{aligned}\frac1{2^{2n-1}}\int_0^1 x^{4n}(1-x)^{4n}\,dx &=\frac{1}{2^{2n-1}(8n+1)\binom{8n}{4n}}\\[6pt] &\sim\frac{\sqrt{\pi n}}{2^{10n-2}(8n+1)}, \end{aligned}$$

where the approximation (the tilde means that the quotient of both sides tends to one for large $n$) of the [central binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/2128244) follows from [Stirling's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stirling's_formula) and shows the fast convergence of the integrals to $\pi$.

Calculation of these integrals: For all integers $k \ge 0$ and $\ell \ge 2$ we have

$$\begin{aligned} x^k(1-x)^\ell&=(1-2x+x^2)x^k(1-x)^{\ell-2}\\[6pt] &=(1+x^2)\,x^k(1-x)^{\ell-2}-2x^{k+1}(1-x)^{\ell-2}. \end{aligned}$$

Applying this formula recursively $2n$ times yields

$$x^{4n}(1-x)^{4n} =\left(1+x^2\right)\sum_{j=0}^{2n-1}(-2)^jx^{4n+j}(1-x)^{4n-2(j+1)}+(-2)^{2n}x^{6n}.$$

Furthermore,

$$\begin{aligned} x^{6n}-(-1)^{3n} &=\sum_{j=1}^{3n}(-1)^{3n-j}x^{2j}-\sum_{j=0}^{3n-1}(-1)^{3n-j}x^{2j}\\[6pt] &=\sum_{j=0}^{3n-1}\left((-1)^{3n-(j+1)} x^{2(j+1)}-(-1)^{3n-j}x^{2j}\right)\\[6pt] &=-(1+x^2)\sum_{j=0}^{3n-1} (-1)^{3n-j}x^{2j}, \end{aligned}$$

where the first equality holds, because the terms for $1 \le j \le 3n - 1$ cancel, and the second equality arises from the index shift $j \to j + 1$ in the first sum.

Application of these two results gives

$$\begin{aligned}\frac{x^{4n}(1-x)^{4n}}{2^{2n-2}(1+x^2)} =\sum_{j=0}^{2n-1} & \frac{(-1)^j}{2^{2n-j-2}}x^{4n+j}(1-x)^{4n-2j-2}\\[6pt] & {} -4\sum_{j=0}^{3n-1}(-1)^{3n-j}x^{2j}+(-1)^{3n}\frac4{1+x^2}.\qquad(1) \end{aligned}$$

For integers $k, \ell \ge 0$, using [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) $\ell$ times, we obtain

$$\begin{aligned} \int_0^1x^k(1-x)^\ell\,dx &=\frac \ell{k+1}\int_0^1x^{k+1}(1-x)^{\ell-1}\,dx\\[6pt] &\,\,\,\vdots\\[6pt] &=\frac \ell{k+1} \frac{\ell-1}{k+2}\cdots\frac1{k+\ell}\int_0^1x^{k+\ell}\,dx\\[6pt] &=\frac{1}{(k+\ell+1)\binom{k+\ell}{k}}.\qquad(2) \end{aligned}$$

Setting $k = \ell = 4n$, we obtain

$$\int_0^1 x^{4n} (1-x)^{4n}\,dx = \frac{1}{(8n+1)\binom{8n}{4n}}.$$

Integrating equation (1) from 0 to 1 using equation (2) and $\arctan(1) = \tfrac{\pi}{4}$, we get the claimed equation involving $\pi$.

The results for $n = 1$ are given above. For $n = 2$ we get

$$\frac14\int_0^1\frac{x^8(1-x)^8}{1+x^2}\,dx=\pi -\frac{47\,171}{15\,015}$$

and

$$\frac18\int_0^1 x^8(1-x)^8\,dx=\frac1{1\,750\,320},$$

hence **\3.141** **592** 31 \< $\pi$ \< **\3.141** **592** 89, where the bold digits of the lower and upper bound are those of $\pi$. Similarly for $n = 3$,

$$\frac1{16}\int_0^1\frac{x^{12}\left(1-x\right)^{12}}{1+x^2}\,dx= \frac{431\,302\,721}{137\,287\,920}-\pi$$

with correction term and error bound

$$\frac1{32}\int_0^1 x^{12} (1-x)^{12}\,dx=\frac1{2\,163\,324\,800},$$

hence **\3.141** **592** **653** 40 \< $\pi$ \< **\3.141** **592** **653** 87. The next step for $n = 4$ is

$$\frac1{64}\int_0^1\frac{x^{16} (1-x)^{16}}{1+x^2}\,dx= \pi-\frac{741\,269\,838\,109}{235\,953\,517\,800}$$

with

$$\frac1{128}\int_0^1 x^{16} (1-x)^{16}\,dx=\frac1{2\,538\,963\,567\,360},$$

which gives **\3.141** **592** **653** **589** 55 \< $\pi$ \< **\3.141** **592** **653** **589** 96.

:::
