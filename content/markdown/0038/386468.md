# Discrete geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Discrete geometry"}

*Image: Unit disk graph.svg.* A collection of [circles](wikipedia://en/6220) and the corresponding [unit disk graph](wikipedia://en/3686677)

**Discrete geometry** and **combinatorial geometry** are branches of [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) that study [combinatorial](wikipedia://en/5170) properties and constructive methods of [discrete](wikipedia://en/8492) geometric objects. Most questions in discrete geometry involve [finite](wikipedia://en/11742) or [discrete](wikipedia://en/56061) [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of basic geometric objects, such as [points](wikipedia://en/593693), [lines](wikipedia://en/946975), [planes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29), [circles](wikipedia://en/6220), [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859), [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621), and so forth. The subject focuses on the combinatorial properties of these objects, such as how they [intersect](wikipedia://en/23476429) one another, or how they may be arranged to cover a larger object.

:::

Discrete geometry has a large overlap with [convex geometry](wikipedia://en/4165882) and [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), and is closely related to subjects such as [finite geometry](wikipedia://en/267061), [combinatorial optimization](wikipedia://en/420555), [digital geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_geometry), [discrete differential geometry](wikipedia://en/17700015), [geometric graph theory](wikipedia://en/3313527), [toric geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toric_geometry), and [combinatorial topology](wikipedia://en/363400).

## History {#History}

[Polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra) and [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671) had been studied for many years by people such as [Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) and [Cauchy](wikipedia://en/1842), modern discrete geometry has its origins in the late 19th century. Early topics studied were: the density of [circle packings](wikipedia://en/16879665) by [Thue](wikipedia://en/635533), [projective configurations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_configuration) by Reye and [Steinitz](wikipedia://en/3966685), the [geometry of numbers](wikipedia://en/250153) by Minkowski, and [map colourings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four_colour_theorem) by Tait, Heawood, and [Hadwiger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadwiger).

[László Fejes Tóth](wikipedia://en/2388442), [H.S.M. Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter), and [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) laid the foundations of *discrete geometry*.[^o3][^o4][^o5]

## Topics {#Topics}

### Polyhedra and polytopes {#Polyhedra_and_polytopes}

A **polytope** is a geometric object with flat sides, which exists in any general number of dimensions. A [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) is a polytope in two dimensions, a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) in three dimensions, and so on in higher dimensions (such as a [4-polytope](wikipedia://en/24979) in four dimensions). Some theories further generalize the idea to include such objects as unbounded polytopes ([apeirotopes](wikipedia://en/12768648) and [tessellations](wikipedia://en/321671)), and [abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056).

The following are some of the aspects of polytopes studied in discrete geometry:

- [Polyhedral combinatorics](wikipedia://en/19696519)
- [Lattice polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_lattice_polytope)
- [Ehrhart polynomials](wikipedia://en/316904)
- [Pick's theorem](wikipedia://en/328252)
- [Hirsch conjecture](wikipedia://en/10412371)
- [Opaque set](wikipedia://en/41162601)

### Packings, coverings and tilings {#Packings,_coverings_and_tilings}

Packings, coverings, and tilings are all ways of arranging uniform objects (typically circles, spheres, or tiles) in a regular way on a surface or [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470).

A **sphere packing** is an arrangement of non-overlapping [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859) within a containing space. The spheres considered are usually all of identical size, and the space is usually three-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697). However, sphere [packing problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Packing_problem) can be generalised to consider unequal spheres, *n*-dimensional Euclidean space (where the problem becomes [circle packing](wikipedia://en/16879665) in two dimensions, or [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) packing in higher dimensions) or to [non-Euclidean](wikipedia://en/58610) spaces such as [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742).

A **tessellation** of a flat surface is the tiling of a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852) using one or more geometric shapes, called tiles, with no overlaps and no gaps. In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), tessellations can be generalized to higher dimensions.

Specific topics in this area include:

- [Circle packings](wikipedia://en/16879665)
- [Sphere packings](wikipedia://en/368621)
- [Kepler conjecture](wikipedia://en/368311)
- [Quasicrystals](wikipedia://en/25350)
- [Aperiodic tilings](wikipedia://en/868145)
- [Periodic graph](wikipedia://en/29008597)
- [Finite subdivision rules](wikipedia://en/36799559)

### Structural rigidity and flexibility {#Structural_rigidity_and_flexibility}

*Image: Structural rigidity basic examples.svg.* Graphs are drawn as rods connected by rotating hinges. The [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) C<sub>4</sub> drawn as a square can be tilted over by the blue force into a parallelogram, so it is a flexible graph. K<sub>3</sub>, drawn as a triangle, cannot be altered by any force that is applied to it, so it is a rigid graph.

**Structural rigidity** is a [combinatorial theory](wikipedia://en/5170) for predicting the flexibility of ensembles formed by [rigid bodies](wikipedia://en/455769) connected by flexible [linkages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linkage_%28mechanical%29) or [hinges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hinge).

Topics in this area include:

- [Cauchy's theorem](wikipedia://en/8317687)
- [Flexible polyhedra](wikipedia://en/9070022)

### Incidence structures {#Incidence_structures}

*Image: Fano plane with nimber labels.svg.* Seven points are elements of seven lines in the [Fano plane](wikipedia://en/390404), an example of an incidence structure.

Incidence structures generalize planes (such as [affine](wikipedia://en/649724), [projective](wikipedia://en/24350), and [Möbius planes](wikipedia://en/38245519)) as can be seen from their axiomatic definitions. Incidence structures also generalize the higher-dimensional analogs and the finite structures are sometimes called [finite geometries](wikipedia://en/267061).

Formally, an **incidence structure** is a triple

$$C=(P,L,I).\,$$

where *P* is a set of "points", *L* is a set of "lines" and $I \subseteq P \times L$ is the [incidence](wikipedia://en/387224) relation. The elements of $I$ are called **flags.** If

$$(p,l) \in I,$$

we say that point *p* "lies on" line $l$.

Topics in this area include:

- [Configurations](wikipedia://en/7200539)
- [Line arrangements](wikipedia://en/864149)
- [Hyperplane arrangements](wikipedia://en/864438)
- [Buildings](wikipedia://en/275015)

### Oriented matroids {#Oriented_matroids}

An **oriented matroid** is a [mathematical structure](wikipedia://en/1809181) that abstracts the properties of [directed graphs](wikipedia://en/19721986) and of arrangements of vectors in a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over an [ordered field](wikipedia://en/22430) (particularly for [partially ordered vector spaces](wikipedia://en/8099221)).[^o12] In comparison, an ordinary (i.e., non-oriented) [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) abstracts the [dependence](wikipedia://en/101863) properties that are common both to [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806), which are not necessarily *directed*, and to arrangements of vectors over [fields](wikipedia://en/10603), which are not necessarily *ordered*.[^o13][^o14]

### Geometric graph theory {#Geometric_graph_theory}

A **geometric graph** is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) in which the [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) or [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) are associated with [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) objects. Examples include Euclidean graphs, the 1-[skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skeleton_%28topology%29) of a [polyhedron](wikipedia://en/23470) or [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471), [unit disk graphs](wikipedia://en/3686677), and [visibility graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visibility_graphs).

Topics in this area include:

- [Graph drawing](wikipedia://en/325813)
- [Polyhedral graphs](wikipedia://en/24656270)
- [Random geometric graphs](wikipedia://en/12106740)
- [Voronoi diagrams](wikipedia://en/177668) and [Delaunay triangulations](wikipedia://en/8864)

### Simplicial complexes {#Simplicial_complexes}

A **simplicial complex** is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) of a certain kind, constructed by "gluing together" [points](wikipedia://en/593693), [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860), [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654), and their [*n*-dimensional counterparts](wikipedia://en/39781) (see illustration). Simplicial complexes should not be confused with the more abstract notion of a [simplicial set](wikipedia://en/1027784) appearing in modern simplicial homotopy theory. The purely combinatorial counterpart to a simplicial complex is an [abstract simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/723105). See also [random geometric complexes](wikipedia://en/13056778).

### Topological combinatorics {#Topological_combinatorics}

The discipline of combinatorial topology used combinatorial concepts in [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and in the early 20th century this turned into the field of [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).

In 1978, the situation was reversed – methods from algebraic topology were used to solve a problem in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) – when [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938) proved the [Kneser conjecture](wikipedia://en/2724154), thus beginning the new study of **topological combinatorics**. Lovász's proof used the [Borsuk-Ulam theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borsuk-Ulam_theorem) and this theorem retains a prominent role in this new field. This theorem has many equivalent versions and analogs and has been used in the study of [fair division](wikipedia://en/669899) problems.

Topics in this area include:

- [Sperner's lemma](wikipedia://en/465067)
- [Regular maps](wikipedia://en/21533914)

### Lattices and discrete groups {#Lattices_and_discrete_groups}

A **discrete group** is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* equipped with the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology). With this topology, *G* becomes a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315). A **discrete subgroup** of a topological group *G* is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) *H* whose [relative topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_topology) is the discrete one. For example, the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), **Z**, form a discrete subgroup of the [reals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers), **R** (with the standard [metric topology](wikipedia://en/20018)), but the [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), **Q**, do not.

A **lattice** in a [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315) is a [discrete subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_subgroup) with the property that the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) has finite [invariant measure](wikipedia://en/7635201). In the special case of subgroups of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, this amounts to the usual geometric notion of a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246), and both the algebraic structure of lattices and the geometry of the totality of all lattices are relatively well understood. Deep results of [Borel](wikipedia://en/365672), [Harish-Chandra](wikipedia://en/373190), [Mostow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Mostow), [Tamagawa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsuneo_Tamagawa), [M. S. Raghunathan](wikipedia://en/18730540), [Margulis](wikipedia://en/1104948), [Zimmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Zimmer_%28mathematician%29) obtained from the 1950s through the 1970s provided examples and generalized much of the theory to the setting of [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/144569) [Lie groups](wikipedia://en/17945) and [semisimple algebraic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semisimple_algebraic_group) over a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375). In the 1990s, [Bass](wikipedia://en/7085910) and [Lubotzky](wikipedia://en/22754130) initiated the study of *tree lattices*, which remains an active research area.

Topics in this area include:

- [Reflection groups](wikipedia://en/2422023)
- [Triangle groups](wikipedia://en/2069342)

### Digital geometry {#Digital_geometry}

**Digital geometry** deals with [discrete](wikipedia://en/56061) sets (usually discrete [point](wikipedia://en/593693) sets) considered to be [digitized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digitizing) [models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_model) or [images](wikipedia://en/71925) of objects of the 2D or 3D [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697).

Simply put, **digitizing** is replacing an object by a discrete set of its points. The images we see on the TV screen, the [raster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raster_graphics) display of a computer, or in newspapers are in fact [digital](wikipedia://en/8276) images.

Its main application areas are [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics) and [image analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_analysis).[^o20]

### Discrete differential geometry {#Discrete_differential_geometry}

**Discrete differential geometry** is the study of discrete counterparts of notions in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625). Instead of smooth curves and surfaces, there are [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621), [meshes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mesh), and [simplicial complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicial_complexes). It is used in the study of [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics) and [topological combinatorics](wikipedia://en/17633579).

Topics in this area include:

- [Discrete Laplace operator](wikipedia://en/1394358)
- [Discrete exterior calculus](wikipedia://en/11015552)
- [Discrete calculus](wikipedia://en/61660335)
- [Discrete Morse theory](wikipedia://en/17637383)
- [Topological combinatorics](wikipedia://en/17633579)
- [Spectral shape analysis](wikipedia://en/34878672)
- [Analysis on fractals](wikipedia://en/18744973)
