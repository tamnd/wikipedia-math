# Log-logistic distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Log-logistic distribution"}

In [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **log-logistic distribution** (known as the **Fisk distribution** in [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics)) is a [continuous probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_probability_distribution) for a non-negative [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685). It is used in [survival analysis](wikipedia://en/419259) as a [parametric model](wikipedia://en/2883137) for events whose rate increases initially and decreases later, as, for example, [mortality rate](wikipedia://en/191305) from cancer following diagnosis or treatment. It has also been used in [hydrology](wikipedia://en/13435) to model stream flow and [precipitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precipitation), in [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics) as a simple model of the [distribution of wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_of_wealth) or [income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_distribution), and in [networking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_network) to model the transmission times of data considering both the network and the software.

:::

The log-logistic distribution is the probability distribution of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) whose [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) has a [logistic](wikipedia://en/542666) distribution.↵ It is similar in shape to the [log-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/102476) but has [heavier tails](wikipedia://en/8092200) . Unlike the log-normal, its [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) can be written in [closed form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_form_expression).

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

There are several different parameterizations of the distribution in use. The one shown here gives reasonably interpretable parameters and a simple form for the [cumulative distribution](wikipedia://en/5793) function \[1\].[^o7][^o8] The parameter $\alpha>0$ is a [scale parameter](wikipedia://en/769503) and is also the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) of the distribution. The parameter $\beta>0$ is a [shape parameter](wikipedia://en/9210865). The distribution is [unimodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodal) when $\beta>1$ its [dispersion](wikipedia://en/23773247) decreases as it $\beta$ increases.

The [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) is

$$\begin{aligned} F(x; \alpha, \beta) & = { 1 \over 1+(x/\alpha)^{-\beta} } \\[5pt] & = {(x/\alpha)^\beta \over 1+(x/\alpha)^ \beta } \\[5pt] & = {x^\beta \over \alpha^\beta+x^\beta} \end{aligned}$$

where $x>0$, $\alpha>0$, $\beta>0.$

The [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) is

$$f(x; \alpha, \beta) = \frac{ (\beta/\alpha)(x/\alpha)^{\beta-1} } { \left( 1+(x/\alpha)^{\beta} \right)^2 }$$

### Alternative parameterization {#Alternative_parameterization}

An alternative parametrization is given by the pair $\mu, s$ in analogy with the logistic distribution:

$$\mu = \ln (\alpha)$$

$$s = 1 / \beta$$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Moments {#Moments}

The $k$throw [moment](wikipedia://en/368684) exists only when $k<\beta,$ when it is given by \[3\]\[4\].[^o13]

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}(X^k) & = \alpha^k\operatorname{B}(1-k/\beta, 1+k/\beta) \\[5pt] & = \alpha^k\, {k\pi/\beta \over \sin(k\pi/\beta)} \end{aligned}$$

where B is the [beta](wikipedia://en/245660) function. Expressions for the [mean](wikipedia://en/9653), [variance](wikipedia://en/32344), [skewness](wikipedia://en/28212), and [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848) can be derived from this. Writing $b=\pi/\beta$ for convenience, the mean is

$$\operatorname{E}(X) = \alpha b / \sin b , \quad \beta>1,$$

and the variance is

$$\operatorname{Var}(X) = \alpha^2 \left( 2b / \sin 2b -b^2 / \sin^2 b \right), \quad \beta>2.$$

Explicit expressions for the skewness and kurtosis are lengthy.[^o14] As $\beta$ tends to infinity, the mean tends to the variance and skewness tend to zero, and the excess kurtosis tends to 6/5 (see also related distributions below).

### Quantiles {#Quantiles}

The [quantile function](wikipedia://en/10167616) (inverse cumulative distribution function) is

$$F^{-1}(p;\alpha, \beta) = \alpha\left( \frac{p}{1-p} \right)^{1/\beta}.$$

It follows that the [median](wikipedia://en/18837) is, the lower [quartile](wikipedia://en/25170) is, and the upper quartile is $3^{1/\beta} \alpha$.

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Loglogistichaz.svg.* [Hazard function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_function). $\alpha=1,$ values of $\beta$ as shown in legend

### Survival analysis {#Survival_analysis}

The log-logistic distribution provides one [parametric model](wikipedia://en/2883137) for [survival analysis](wikipedia://en/419259). Unlike the more commonly used [Weibull distribution](wikipedia://en/206948), it can have a non-[monotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonic) [hazard function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_function): when $\beta>1,$ the hazard function is [unimodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodal) (when $\beta$ ≤ 1, the hazard decreases monotonically). The fact that the cumulative distribution function can be written in closed form is particularly useful for analysis of survival data with censoring.[^o19] The log-logistic distribution can be used as the basis of an [accelerated failure time model](wikipedia://en/15704862) by allowing it $\alpha$ to differ between groups, or more generally by introducing covariates that affect it $\alpha$ but not $\beta$ by modelling $\log(\alpha)$ as a linear function of the covariates.[^o20]

The [survival function](wikipedia://en/30876757) is

$$S(t) = 1 - F(t) = [1+(t/\alpha)^{\beta}]^{-1},\,$$

and so the [hazard function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hazard_function) is

$$h(t) = \frac{f(t)}{S(t)} = \frac{(\beta/\alpha)(t/\alpha)^{\beta-1}} {1+(t/\alpha)^\beta}.$$

The log-logistic distribution with shape parameter $\beta = 1$ is the marginal distribution of the inter-times in a geometric-distributed [counting](wikipedia://en/20814853) process \[8\].[^o21]

### Hydrology {#Hydrology}

*Image: FitLog-logisticdistr.tif.* Fitted cumulative log-logistic distribution to maximum one-day October rainfalls

The log-logistic distribution has been used in hydrology for modeling stream flow rates and precipitation.[^o7][^o8]

Extreme values like maximum one-day rainfall and river discharge per month or per year often follow a [log-normal](wikipedia://en/102476) distribution \[9\]. The log-normal distribution, however, needs a numeric approximation. As the log-logistic distribution, which can be solved analytically, is similar to the log-normal distribution, it can be used instead.

The blue picture illustrates an example of fitting the log-logistic distribution to ranked maximum one-day October rainfalls, and it shows the 90% [confidence belt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_belt) based on the [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876). The rainfall data are represented by the [plotting position](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotting_position) *r*/(*n*+1) as part of the [cumulative frequency analysis](wikipedia://en/45064451) .

### Economics {#Economics}

The log-logistic has been used as a simple model of the [distribution of wealth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_of_wealth) or [income](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Income_distribution) in [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), where it is known as the Fisk distribution.[^o24] Its [Gini coefficient](wikipedia://en/12883) is \[11\].[^o25]

The Gini coefficient for a continuous probability distribution takes the form:

$$G = {1\over{\mu}}\int_{0}^{\infty}F(1-F)dx$$

where $F$ is the CDF of the distribution and $\mu$ is the expected value. For the log-logistic distribution, the formula for the Gini coefficient becomes:

$$G = {\sin(\pi/\beta) \over{\alpha\pi/\beta}} \int_{0}^{\infty}{dx\over{[1+(x/\alpha)^{-\beta}][1+(x/\alpha)^{\beta}]}}$$

Defining the substitution $z = x/\alpha$ leads to the simpler equation:

$$G = {\sin(\pi/\beta) \over{\pi/\beta}} \int_{0}^{\infty}{dz\over{(1+z^{-\beta})(1+z^{\beta})}}$$

And making the substitution $u = 1/(1 + z^{\beta})$ further simplifies the Gini coefficient formula to:

$$G = {\sin(\pi/\beta)\over{\pi}} \int_{0}^{1}u^{-1/\beta}(1-u)^{1/\beta}du$$

The integral component is equivalent to the standard [beta function](wikipedia://en/245660) $\text{B}(1-1/\beta,1+1/\beta)$. The beta function may also be written as:

$$\text{B}(x,y) = {\Gamma(x)\Gamma(y)\over{\Gamma(x+y)}}$$

where $\Gamma(\cdot)$ is the [gamma function](wikipedia://en/12316). Using the properties of the gamma function, it can be shown that:

$$\text{B}(1-1/\beta,1+1/\beta) = {1\over{\beta}}\Gamma(1-1/\beta)\Gamma(1/\beta)$$

From [Euler's reflection formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler's_reflection_formula), the expression can be simplified further:

$$\text{B}(1-1/\beta,1+1/\beta) = {1\over{\beta}} {\pi\over{\sin(\pi/\beta)}}$$

Finally, we may conclude that the Gini coefficient for the log-logistic distribution $G = 1/\beta$.

### Networking {#Networking}

The log-logistic has been used as a model for the period of time beginning when some data leaves a software user application in a computer and the response is received by the same application after traveling through and being processed by other computers, applications, and network segments, most or all of them without hard [real-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real-time_computing) guarantees (for example, when an application is displaying data coming from a remote [sensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor) connected to the Internet). It has been shown to be a more accurate probabilistic model for that than the [log-normal distribution](wikipedia://en/102476) or others, as long as abrupt changes of regime in the sequences of those times are properly detected.[^o27]

## Related distributions {#Related_distributions}

- If $X \sim \operatorname{LL}(\alpha,\beta)$ then $kX \sim \operatorname{LL}(k \alpha, \beta).$
- If $X \sim \operatorname{LL}(\alpha, \beta)$ then $X^k \sim \operatorname{LL}(\alpha^k, \beta/|k|).$
- $\operatorname{LL}(\alpha,\beta) \sim \textrm{Dagum}(1,\alpha,\beta)$ ([Dagum distribution](wikipedia://en/31102537)).
- $\operatorname{LL}(\alpha,\beta) \sim \textrm{SinghMaddala}(1,\alpha,\beta)$ ([Singh–Maddala distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singh–Maddala_distribution) ).
- $\textrm{LL}(\gamma,\sigma) \sim \beta'(1,1,\gamma,\sigma)$ ([Beta prime distribution](wikipedia://en/3126406)).
- If *X* has a log-logistic distribution with scale parameter $\alpha$ and shape parameter, $\beta$ then *Y* = log(*X*) has a [logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/542666) with location parameter $\log(\alpha)$ and scale parameter $1/\beta.$
- As the shape parameter $\beta$ of the log-logistic distribution increases, its shape increasingly resembles that of a (very narrow) [logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/542666). Informally:

$$\operatorname{LL}(\alpha, \beta) \to L(\alpha,\alpha/\beta) \quad \text{as} \quad \beta \to \infty.$$

- The log-logistic distribution with shape parameter $\beta=1$ and scale parameter $\alpha$ is the same as the [generalized Pareto distribution](wikipedia://en/53057#Generalized_Pareto_distributions) with location parameter $\mu=0$, shape parameter $\xi=1$ and scale parameter $\alpha:$

$$\operatorname{LL}(\alpha,1) = \operatorname{GPD}(1,\alpha,1).$$

- The addition of another parameter (a shift parameter) formally results in a [shifted log-logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/15784796), but this is usually considered in a different parameterization so that the distribution can be bounded above or bounded below.

::: {#o30 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Several different distributions are sometimes referred to as the **generalized log-logistic distribution**, as they contain the log-logistic as a special case.[^o25] These include the [Burr Type XII distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Burr_Type_XII_distribution) (also known as the *Singh–Maddala distribution*) and the [Dagum distribution](wikipedia://en/31102537), both of which include a second shape parameter. Both are in turn special cases of the even more general *generalized beta distribution of the second kind*. Another more straightforward generalization of the log-logistic is the [shifted log-logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/15784796) .

Another **generalized log-logistic distribution** is the log-transform of the [metalog distribution](wikipedia://en/66708690), in which power series expansions in terms of $p$ are substituted for [logistic distribution](wikipedia://en/542666) parameters $\mu$. The resulting [log-metalog distribution](wikipedia://en/66708690#/Unbounded,_semibounded,_and_bounded_metalog_distributions) is highly shape flexible, has a simple closed-form [PDF](wikipedia://en/43487) and [quantile function](wikipedia://en/10167616), can be fit to data with linear least squares, and subsumes the log-logistic distribution as a special case.

:::
