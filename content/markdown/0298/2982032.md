# Boxology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Boxology"}

A **boxology** is a representation of an organized structure as a [graph](wikipedia://en/12401) of labeled nodes ("[boxes](wikipedia://en/70048)") and connections between them (as [lines](wikipedia://en/946975) or [arrows](wikipedia://en/4912832)). The concept is useful because many problems in systems design are reducible to modular "black boxes" and connections or flow channels between them. The term is somewhat tongue-in-cheek [^o2] and refers to the generic nature of diagrams containing labelled nodes and (sometimes directed) paths between them.[^o3]

:::

The archetypical example of a boxology is a corporate ["org chart"](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Organizational_chart), which describes lines of control through the corporation. Other boxologies include programming [flow charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_chart),[^o4][^o5] system-level [circuit diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_diagram) for designing large complex circuits, and even economic models. [Feynman diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_diagrams) are useful because they reduce the complicated [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) to a simple boxology of particle interactions.

Depending on application, the boxes are optional; for example, the global [carbon cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_cycle) is modeled as a boxology, but many figures explaining the model include only labels and directed connections.
