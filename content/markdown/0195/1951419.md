# Critical point (thermodynamics)

*Image: CriticalPointMeasurementEthane.jpg.* Subcritical [ethane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ethane), liquid and gas phase coexist.; Critical point (32.17 °C, 48.72 bar), displaying [critical opalescence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Critical_opalescence).; Supercritical ethane, [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid).[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Critical point"}

In [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952), a **critical point** (or **critical state**) is the end point of a phase [equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_%28thermodynamics%29) curve. One example is the liquid–vapor critical point, the end point of the pressure–temperature curve that designates conditions under which a [liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid) and its [vapor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vapor) can coexist. At higher temperatures, the gas comes into a [supercritical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_fluid) phase, and so cannot be liquefied by pressure alone. At the critical point, defined by a *critical temperature* *T*<sub>c</sub> and a *critical pressure* *p*<sub>c</sub>, [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28matter%29) boundaries vanish. Other examples include the [liquid–liquid critical points in mixtures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_critical_solution_temperature), and the ferromagnet–paramagnet transition ([Curie temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie_temperature)) in the absence of an external magnetic field.[^o3]

:::

## Liquid–vapor critical point {#Liquid–vapor_critical_point}

### Overview {#Overview}

*Image: phase-diag2.svg.* The liquid–vapor critical point in a pressure–temperature [phase diagram](wikipedia://en/56398) is at the high-temperature extreme of the liquid–gas phase boundary. The dashed green line shows the anomalous behavior of water.

The liquid–vapor critical point was the first critical point to be discovered, and it remains the best known and most studied one.

The figure shows the schematic [*P-T* diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PT_diagram) of a *pure substance* (as opposed to mixtures, which have additional state variables and richer phase diagrams, discussed below). The commonly known [phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28matter%29) *solid*, *liquid* and *vapor* are separated by phase boundaries, i.e. pressure–temperature combinations where two phases can coexist. At the [triple point](wikipedia://en/30462), all three phases can coexist. However, the liquid–vapor boundary terminates in an endpoint at some *critical temperature* *T*<sub>c</sub> and *critical pressure* *p*<sub>c</sub>. This is the *critical point*.

The critical point of water occurs at \647.096 K and \22.064 MPa.[^o6]

In the *vicinity* of the critical point, the physical properties of the liquid and the vapor change dramatically, with both phases becoming even more similar. For instance, liquid water under normal conditions is nearly incompressible, has a low thermal expansion coefficient, has a high [dielectric constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric_constant), and is an excellent solvent for electrolytes. Near the critical point, all these properties change into the exact opposite: water becomes compressible, expandable, a poor [dielectric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric), a bad solvent for electrolytes, and mixes more readily with nonpolar gases and organic molecules.[^o7]

*At* the critical point, only one phase exists. The [heat of vaporization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heat_of_vaporization) is zero. There is a [stationary](wikipedia://en/885651) [inflection point](wikipedia://en/379845) in the constant-temperature line (*critical isotherm*) on a [PV diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PV_diagram). This means that at the critical point:[^o8][^o9][^o10]

$$\begin{aligned} \left(\frac{\partial p}{\partial V}\right)_T &= 0\\ \left(\frac{\partial^2p}{\partial V^2}\right)_T &= 0 \end{aligned}$$

*Image: Real Gas Isotherms.svg.* Isotherms of a gas. The red line is the critical isotherm, with critical point K. The dashed lines represent parts of isotherms which are forbidden since the gradient would be positive, giving the gas in this region a [negative compressibility](wikipedia://en/166084#Negative_compressibility).

*Above* the critical point there exists a state of matter that is continuously connected with (can be transformed without phase transition into) both the liquid and the gaseous state. It is called [supercritical fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supercritical_fluid). The common textbook knowledge that all distinction between liquid and vapor disappears beyond the critical point has been challenged by [Fisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Fisher) and [Widom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benjamin_Widom),[^o11] who identified a *p*–*T* line that separates states with different asymptotic statistical properties ([Fisher–Widom line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisher–Widom_line)). Note that even at temperatures above the critical point temperature, sufficient pressure can still potentially compress the matter into a [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid).[^o12]

Sometimes the critical point does not manifest in most thermodynamic or mechanical properties, but is "hidden" and reveals itself in the onset of inhomogeneities in elastic moduli, marked changes in the appearance and local properties of non-affine droplets, and a sudden enhancement in defect pair concentration.[^o13]

### History {#History}

*Image: Critical carbon dioxide.jpg.* Critical [carbon dioxide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_dioxide) exuding [fog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fog) while cooling from supercritical to critical temperature.

The existence of a critical point was first discovered by [Charles Cagniard de la Tour](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Cagniard_de_la_Tour) in 1822[^o15][^o16] and named by [Dmitri Mendeleev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dmitri_Mendeleev) in 1860[^o17][^o18] and [Thomas Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Andrews_%28scientist%29) in 1869.[^o19] Cagniard showed that CO<sub>2</sub> could be liquefied at 31 °C at a pressure of 73 atm, but not at a slightly higher temperature, even under pressures as high as 3000 atm.

### Theory {#Theory}

Solving the above condition $(\partial p / \partial V)_T = 0$ for the [van der Waals equation](wikipedia://en/206064), one can compute the critical point as[^o8]

$$T_\text{c} = \frac{8a}{27Rb}, \quad V_\text{c} = 3nb, \quad p_\text{c} = \frac{a}{27b^2}.$$

However, the van der Waals equation, based on a [mean-field theory](wikipedia://en/555768), does not hold near the critical point. In particular, it predicts wrong [scaling laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scaling_law).

To analyse properties of fluids near the critical point, reduced state variables are sometimes defined relative to the critical properties[^o21]

$$T_\text{r} = \frac{T}{T_\text{c}}, \quad p_\text{r} = \frac{p}{p_\text{c}}, \quad V_\text{r} = \frac{V}{RT_\text{c} / p_\text{c}}.$$

The [principle of corresponding states](wikipedia://en/7464700) indicates that substances at equal reduced pressures and temperatures have equal reduced volumes. This relationship is approximately true for many substances, but becomes increasingly inaccurate for large values of *p*<sub>r</sub>.

For some gases, there is an additional correction factor, called *Newton's correction*, added to the critical temperature and critical pressure calculated in this manner. These are empirically derived values and vary with the pressure range of interest.[^o22]

## Mixtures: liquid–liquid critical point {#Mixtures:_liquid–liquid_critical_point}

*Image: LCST-UCST plot.svg.* A plot of typical polymer solution phase behavior including two critical points: a [LCST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/LCST) and an [UCST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_critical_solution_temperature)

The [liquid–liquid critical point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid–liquid_critical_point) of a solution, which occurs at the *critical solution temperature*, occurs at the limit of the two-phase region of the phase diagram. In other words, it is the point at which an infinitesimal change in some thermodynamic variable (such as temperature or pressure) leads to separation of the mixture into two distinct liquid phases, as shown in the polymer–solvent phase diagram to the right. Two types of liquid–liquid critical points are the [upper critical solution temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_critical_solution_temperature) (UCST), which is the hottest point at which cooling induces phase separation, and the [lower critical solution temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_critical_solution_temperature) (LCST), which is the coldest point at which heating induces phase separation.

### Mathematical definition {#Mathematical_definition}

From a theoretical standpoint, the liquid–liquid critical point represents the temperature–concentration extremum of the [spinodal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinodal) curve (as can be seen in the figure to the right). Thus, the liquid–liquid critical point in a two-component system must satisfy two conditions: the condition of the spinodal curve (the *second* derivative of the [free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) with respect to concentration must equal zero), and the extremum condition (the *third* derivative of the free energy with respect to concentration must also equal zero or the derivative of the spinodal temperature with respect to concentration must equal zero).
