# Graph isomorphism problem

*Image: Graph isomorphisms.svg.* Two isomorphic graphs

::: {#o1 .problem cue=unsolved}

Can the graph isomorphism problem be solved in polynomial time?

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Graph isomorphism problem"}

The **graph isomorphism problem** is the [computational problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_problem) of determining whether two finite [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806) are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577).[^o3]

:::

The problem is not known to be solvable in [polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time) nor to be [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete), and therefore may be in the computational [complexity class](wikipedia://en/502426) [NP-intermediate](wikipedia://en/4637081). It is known that the graph isomorphism problem is in the [low hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Low_hierarchy) of [class NP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_NP), which implies that it is not NP-complete unless the [polynomial time hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time_hierarchy) collapses to its second level.[^n1] At the same time, isomorphism for many special classes of graphs can be solved in polynomial time, and in practice graph isomorphism can often be solved efficiently.[^o5][^n2]

This problem is a special case of the [subgraph isomorphism problem](wikipedia://en/450062),[^n3] which asks whether a given graph *G* contains a subgraph that is isomorphic to another given graph *H*; this problem is known to be NP-complete. It is also known to be a special case of the [non-abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [hidden subgroup problem](wikipedia://en/3728000) over the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901).[^n4]

In the area of [image recognition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_recognition) it is known as the **exact graph matching problem**.[^o9]

## State of the art {#State_of_the_art}

In November 2015, [László Babai](wikipedia://en/3461604) announced a [quasi-polynomial time](wikipedia://en/25269049) algorithm for all graphs, that is, one with running time $2^{O((\log n)^c)}$ for some fixed $c > 0$.[^o11][^o12][^o13][^o14] On January 4, 2017, Babai retracted the quasi-polynomial claim and stated a [sub-exponential time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-exponential_time) bound instead after [Harald Helfgott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Helfgott) discovered a flaw in the proof. On January 9, 2017, Babai announced a correction (published in full on January 19) and restored the quasi-polynomial claim, with Helfgott confirming the fix.[^o15][^o16] Helfgott further claims that one can take $c = 3$, so the running time is $2^{O((\log n)^{3})}$.[^o17][^o18] Babai published a "preliminary report" on related work at the 2019 [Symposium on Theory of Computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symposium_on_Theory_of_Computing), describing a quasipolynomial algorithm for [graph canonization](wikipedia://en/20199287),[^o19] but the full version of these algorithms remains unpublished.

Prior to this, the best accepted theoretical algorithm was due to (Babai Luks 1983), and was based on the earlier work by (Luks 1982) combined with a *subfactorial* algorithm of V. N. Zemlyachenko (Zemlyachenko Korneenko Tyshkevich 1985). The algorithm has run time 2<sup>O($\sqrt{n \log n}$)</sup> for graphs with *n* vertices and relies on the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445). Without this [classification theorem](wikipedia://en/4576710), a slightly weaker bound $2^{O(\sqrt{n} \log^{2} n)}$ was obtained first for [strongly regular graphs](wikipedia://en/1399856) by (txt), and then extended to general graphs by (Babai Luks 1983). Improvement of the exponent $\sqrt{n}$ for strongly regular graphs was done by (Spielman 1996). For [hypergraphs](wikipedia://en/232535) of bounded rank, a [subexponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sub-exponential_time) upper bound matching the case of graphs was obtained by (Babai Codenotti 2008).

There are several competing practical algorithms for graph isomorphism, such as those due to (McKay 1981), (Schmidt Druffel 1976), (Ullman 1976), and (Stoichev 2019). While they seem to perform well on [random graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_graphs), a major drawback of these algorithms is their exponential time performance in the [worst case](wikipedia://en/37956).[^n5]

The graph isomorphism problem is computationally equivalent to the problem of computing the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of a graph,[^n6][^o33][^o34] and is weaker than the [permutation group](wikipedia://en/24634) isomorphism problem and the permutation group intersection problem. For the latter two problems, (Babai Kantor Luks 1983) obtained complexity bounds similar to that for graph isomorphism.

## Solved special cases {#Solved_special_cases}

A number of important special cases of the graph isomorphism problem have efficient, polynomial-time solutions:

- [Trees](wikipedia://en/48560)[^n7][^n8]
- [Planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314)[^n9] (In fact, planar graph isomorphism is in [log space](wikipedia://en/1149999),[^n10] a class contained in [P](wikipedia://en/658550))
- [Interval graphs](wikipedia://en/363423)[^n11]
- [Permutation graphs](wikipedia://en/17625375)[^n12]
- [Circulant graphs](wikipedia://en/3498787)[^n13]
- Bounded-parameter graphs
  - Graphs of bounded [treewidth](wikipedia://en/3987086)[^n14]
  - Graphs of bounded [genus](wikipedia://en/61891)[^o45] (Planar graphs are graphs of genus 0.)
  - Graphs of bounded [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448)[^n15]
  - Graphs with bounded [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) multiplicity[^n16]
  - *k*-Contractible graphs (a generalization of bounded degree and bounded genus)[^n17]
  - Color-preserving isomorphism of [colored graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colored_graph) with bounded color multiplicity (i.e., at most *k* vertices have the same color for a fixed *k*) is in class [NC](wikipedia://en/22073), which is a subclass of [P](wikipedia://en/658550).[^n18]

## Complexity class GI {#Complexity_class_GI}

Since the graph isomorphism problem is neither known to be NP-complete nor known to be tractable, researchers have sought to gain insight into the problem by defining a new class **GI**, the set of problems with a [polynomial-time Turing reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial-time_Turing_reduction) to the graph isomorphism problem.[^o52] If in fact the graph isomorphism problem is solvable in polynomial time, **GI** would equal **[P](wikipedia://en/658550)**. On the other hand, if the problem is NP-complete, **GI** would equal **[NP](wikipedia://en/21562)** and all problems in **NP** would be solvable in quasi-polynomial time.

As is common for [complexity classes](wikipedia://en/502426) within the [polynomial time hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_time_hierarchy), a problem is called **GI-hard** if there is a [polynomial-time Turing reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial-time_Turing_reduction) from any problem in **GI** to that problem, i.e., a polynomial-time solution to a GI-hard problem would yield a polynomial-time solution to the graph isomorphism problem (and so all problems in **GI**). A problem $X$ is called [complete](wikipedia://en/1176530) for **GI**, or **GI-complete**, if it is both GI-hard and a polynomial-time solution to the GI problem would yield a polynomial-time solution to $X$.

The graph isomorphism problem is contained in both **NP** and co-**[AM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AM_%28complexity%29)**. GI is contained in and [low](wikipedia://en/2006468) for [Parity P](wikipedia://en/2120422), as well as contained in the potentially much smaller class **SPP**.[^o53] That it lies in Parity P means that the graph isomorphism problem is no harder than determining whether a polynomial-time [nondeterministic Turing machine](wikipedia://en/21935) has an even or odd number of accepting paths. GI is also contained in and low for [ZPP](wikipedia://en/54772)<sup>NP</sup>.[^n19] This essentially means that an efficient [Las Vegas algorithm](wikipedia://en/537519) with access to an NP [oracle](wikipedia://en/22431) can solve graph isomorphism so easily that it gains no power from being given the ability to do so in constant time.

### GI-complete and GI-hard problems {#GI-complete_and_GI-hard_problems}

#### Isomorphism of other objects {#Isomorphism_of_other_objects}

There are a number of classes of mathematical objects for which the problem of isomorphism is a GI-complete problem. A number of them are graphs endowed with additional properties or restrictions:[^o57]

- [digraphs](wikipedia://en/19721986)[^o57]
- [labelled graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Labelled_graph), with the proviso that an isomorphism is not required to preserve the labels,[^o57] but only the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) consisting of pairs of vertices with the same label
- "polarized graphs" (made of a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) K<sub>m</sub> and an [empty graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_graph) K<sub>n</sub> plus some edges connecting the two; their isomorphism must preserve the partition)[^o57]
- 2-[colored graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colored_graph)[^o57]
- explicitly given finite [structures](wikipedia://en/4055928)[^o57]
- [multigraphs](wikipedia://en/1774970)[^o57]
- [hypergraphs](wikipedia://en/232535)[^o57]
- [finite automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_automata)[^o57]
- [Markov Decision Processes](wikipedia://en/1125883)[^n20]
- [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) class 3 [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) (i.e., *xyz* = 0 for every elements *x*, *y*, *z*) [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799)[^o57]
- finite rank [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788) over a fixed algebraically closed field with zero squared radical and commutative factor over the radical.[^o57][^n21]
- [context-free grammars](wikipedia://en/6759)[^o57]
- [normal-form games](wikipedia://en/1863612)[^o60]
- [balanced incomplete block designs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Balanced_incomplete_block_design)[^o57]
- Recognizing [combinatorial isomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_isomorphism) of [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885) represented by vertex-facet incidences.[^o61]

#### GI-complete classes of graphs {#GI-complete_classes_of_graphs}

A class of graphs is called GI-complete if recognition of isomorphism for graphs from this subclass is a GI-complete problem. The following classes are GI-complete:[^o57]

- [connected graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_graph)[^o57]
- graphs of [diameter](wikipedia://en/2979341) 2 and [radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radius_%28graph_theory%29) 1[^o57]
- [directed acyclic graphs](wikipedia://en/204002)[^o57]
- [regular graphs](wikipedia://en/85821)[^o57]
- [bipartite graphs](wikipedia://en/244431) without non-trivial [strongly regular subgraphs](wikipedia://en/1399856)[^o57]
- bipartite [Eulerian graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulerian_graph)[^o57]
- bipartite regular graphs[^o57]
- [line graphs](wikipedia://en/675231)[^o57]
- [split graphs](wikipedia://en/9928681)[^n11]
- [chordal graphs](wikipedia://en/744165)[^o57]
- regular [self-complementary graphs](wikipedia://en/15093884)[^o57]
- [polytopal graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polytopal_graph) of general, [simple](wikipedia://en/8853286), and [simplicial](wikipedia://en/8853373) [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885) in arbitrary dimensions.[^n22]

Many classes of digraphs are also GI-complete.

#### Other GI-complete problems {#Other_GI-complete_problems}

There are other nontrivial GI-complete problems in addition to isomorphism problems.

- Finding a [graph's automorphism group](wikipedia://en/15094186).[^n6]
- Counting [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/15094186) of a graph.[^n6]
- The recognition of self-complementarity of a graph or digraph.[^n23]
- A [clique problem](wikipedia://en/249254) for a class of so-called *M*-graphs. It is shown that finding an isomorphism for *n*-vertex graphs is equivalent to finding an *n*-clique in an *M*-graph of size *n*<sup>2</sup>. This fact is interesting because the problem of finding a clique of order (1 − *ε*)*n* in a *M*-graph of size *n*<sup>2</sup> is NP-complete for arbitrarily small positive ε.[^n24]
- The problem of homeomorphism of 2-complexes.[^n25]
- The [definability problem for first-order logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_logic_definability_problem). The input of this problem is a relational database instance *I* and a relation *R*, and the question to answer is whether there exists a [first-order query](wikipedia://en/10983) *Q* (without constants) such that *Q* evaluated on *I* gives R as the answer.[^n26]

#### GI-hard problems {#GI-hard_problems}

- The problem of counting the number of isomorphisms between two graphs is polynomial-time equivalent to the problem of telling whether even one exists.[^o73]
- The problem of deciding whether two [convex polytopes](wikipedia://en/1817885) given by either the [V-description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/V-description) or [H-description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-description) are projectively or affinely isomorphic. The latter means existence of a projective or affine map between the spaces that contain the two polytopes (not necessarily of the same dimension) which induces a bijection between the polytopes.[^n22]

## Program checking {#Program_checking}

(txt) have shown a probabilistic checker for programs for graph isomorphism. Suppose *P* is a claimed polynomial-time procedure that checks if two graphs are isomorphic, but it is not trusted. To check if graphs *G* and *H* are isomorphic:

- Ask *P* whether *G* and *H* are isomorphic.
  - If the answer is "yes":
    - Attempt to construct an isomorphism using *P* as subroutine. Mark a vertex *u* in *G* and *v* in *H*, and modify the graphs to make them distinctive (with a small local change). Ask *P* if the modified graphs are isomorphic. If no, change *v* to a different vertex. Continue searching.
    - Either the isomorphism will be found (and can be verified), or *P* will contradict itself.
  - If the answer is "no":
    - Perform the following 100 times. Choose randomly *G* or *H*, and randomly permute its vertices. Ask *P* if the graph is isomorphic to *G* and *H*. (As in [AM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AM_%28complexity%29) protocol for graph nonisomorphism).
    - If any of the tests are failed, judge *P* as invalid program. Otherwise, answer "no".

This procedure is polynomial-time and gives the correct answer if *P* is a correct program for graph isomorphism. If *P* is not a correct program, but answers correctly on *G* and *H*, the checker will either give the correct answer, or detect invalid behaviour of *P*. If *P* is not a correct program, and answers incorrectly on *G* and *H*, the checker will detect invalid behaviour of *P* with high probability, or answer wrong with probability 2<sup>−100</sup>.

Notably, *P* is used only as a blackbox.

## Applications {#Applications}

Graphs are commonly used to encode structural information in many fields, including [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision) and [pattern recognition](wikipedia://en/126706), and [graph matching](wikipedia://en/54414446), i.e., identification of similarities between graphs, is an important tools in these areas. In these areas graph isomorphism problem is known as the exact graph matching.[^o79]

In [cheminformatics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cheminformatics) and in [mathematical chemistry](wikipedia://en/1570072), graph isomorphism testing is used to identify a [chemical compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_compound) within a [chemical database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_database).[^n27] Also, in organic mathematical chemistry graph isomorphism testing is useful for generation of [molecular graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_graph) and for [computer synthesis](wikipedia://en/36680).

Chemical database search is an example of graphical [data mining](wikipedia://en/42253), where the [graph canonization](wikipedia://en/20199287) approach is often used.[^n28] In particular, a number of [identifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identifier) for [chemical substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance), such as [SMILES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMILES) and [InChI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InChI), designed to provide a standard and human-readable way to encode molecular information and to facilitate the search for such information in databases and on the web, use canonization step in their computation, which is essentially the canonization of the graph which represents the molecule.[^o82]

In [electronic design automation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_design_automation) graph isomorphism is the basis of the [Layout Versus Schematic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Layout_Versus_Schematic) (LVS) circuit design step, which is a verification whether the [electric circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_circuit) represented by a [circuit schematic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circuit_diagram) and an [integrated circuit layout](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrated_circuit_layout) are the same.[^n29]
