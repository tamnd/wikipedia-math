# Alison Harcourt

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alison Harcourt"}

**Alison Grant Harcourt** (**Doig**; born 24 November 1929)[^o3] is an Australian mathematician and statistician most well-known for co-defining the [branch and bound](wikipedia://en/456580) algorithm along with [Ailsa Land](wikipedia://en/58693284) whilst carrying out research at the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics).[^o4][^o5][^o6] She was also part of the team which developed a [poverty line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_line) as part of the Henderson Inquiry into [poverty in Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_in_Australia) and helped to introduce the double randomisation method of ordering candidates used in Australian elections.[^o5][^o7]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Harcourt was born Alison Doig in [Colac, Victoria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colac,_Victoria), in 1929. Her father was [Keith Doig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Keith_Doig), a physician and Australian rules footballer who received the [Military Cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Cross) during [World War I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_I). Her mother, Louie Grant, was of Scottish descent and was sister to physicist Sir [Kerr Grant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerr_Grant).[^o9]

She was schooled at Colac West State School, Colac High School and [Fintona Girls' School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fintona_Girls'_School).[^o3] After her schooling, she enrolled at the [University of Melbourne](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Melbourne), gaining a [Bachelor of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) with a major in mathematics, and then a [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science) majoring in physics. While specialising in statistics undertaking a [Master of Arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) degree, she developed a technique for [integer linear programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_linear_programming).[^o3]

## London School of Economics {#London_School_of_Economics}

On the basis of her work in linear programming, she started work at the [London School of Economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_School_of_Economics) (LSE) in the late 1950s. In 1960, Doig and fellow LSE mathematician [Ailsa Land](wikipedia://en/58693284), published a landmark paper in the economics journal *[Econometrica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Econometrica)* ("An Automatic Method for Solving Discrete Programming Problems"), which outlined a [branch and bound](wikipedia://en/456580) optimisation algorithm for solving [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) problems.[^o3][^o11] The algorithm is the backbone idea behind all modern [Integer programming](wikipedia://en/411215) solvers such as [Gurobi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gurobi), [Cplex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cplex).[^o4]

## University of Melbourne {#University_of_Melbourne}

In 1963, Doig returned to Melbourne, where she took up a position as a senior lecturer in statistics at the University of Melbourne.[^o4]

In the mid-1960s, she joined a team headed by the sociologist Ronald Henderson which was attempting to quantify the extent of [poverty in Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_in_Australia). The team developed the Henderson Poverty Line in 1973, which was the disposable income required to support the basic needs of a family of two adults and two dependent children. The techniques developed by the Henderson team have been used by the [Melbourne Institute of Applied Economic and Social Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melbourne_Institute_of_Applied_Economic_and_Social_Research) to regularly update the [poverty line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poverty_line) for Australia since 1979.[^o3]

In 1970, Harcourt took study leave in Sweden, where she co-authored two papers on theoretical chemistry—"A simple demonstration of Hund’s Rule for the helium 2S and 2P States"[^o13] and "Wavefunctions for 4-electron 3-centre bonding"[^o14]—with her husband, the chemist Richard Harcourt.[^o3]

In 1975, following the [dismissal of the Whitlam government](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975_Australian_constitutional_crisis), Harcourt and fellow statistician Malcolm Clark noticed irregularities in the distribution of party ordering on the Senate ballot papers for the [1975 federal election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1975_Australian_federal_election) which was determined by drawing envelopes from a box, with [Coalition parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coalition_%28Australia%29) holding one of the first two positions in every state. Harcourt and Clark made a submission to the Joint Select Committee on Electoral Reform, which resulted in a 1984 amendment to the *[Commonwealth Electoral Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commonwealth_Electoral_Act_1918)* to introduce a more rigorous double randomisation method.[^o15] Harcourt and Clark published a paper about their analysis and recommendations for the *Australian & New Zealand Journal of Statistics* in 1991.[^o16]

Harcourt retired as an academic from the University of Melbourne in 1994, but continued to work there as a sessional tutor in statistics.[^o3] She finally retired completely aged 90 in 2019.[^o17] She pointed out in an interview that she had "tutored or lectured in every decade from the 1940s to the 2010s".[^o17]

In October 2018 Harcourt was named as 2019 [Senior Victorian Australian of the Year](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_of_the_Year#Senior_Australian_of_the_Year).[^o18] In early December 2018, the University of Melbourne awarded Harcourt with an honorary Doctor of Science degree.[^o19]

In June 2019, Harcourt was made an [Officer of the Order of Australia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Officer_of_the_Order_of_Australia) in recognition of her "distinguished service to mathematics and computer science through pioneering research and development of integer linear programming".[^o20]
