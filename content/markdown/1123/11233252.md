# Multimodal logic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multimodal logic"}

A **multimodal logic** is a [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365) that has more than one primitive [modal operator](wikipedia://en/3832714). They find substantial applications in [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

A [modal logic](wikipedia://en/333365) with *n* primitive unary modal operators $\Box_i, i\in \{1,\ldots, n\}$ is called an *n*-modal logic. Given these operators and [negation](wikipedia://en/161019), one can always add $\Diamond_i$ modal operators defined as $\Diamond_i P$ if and only if $\lnot \Box_i \lnot P$, to give a [classical multimodal logic](wikipedia://en/14763077) if it is in addition stable under necessitation (or "possibilization", therefore) of both members of provable equivalences.

Perhaps the first substantive example of a two-modal logic is [Arthur Prior](wikipedia://en/308764)'s [tense logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tense_logic), with two modalities, F and P, corresponding to "sometime in the future" and "sometime in the past". A logic[^o3] with infinitely many modalities is [dynamic logic](wikipedia://en/31631871), introduced by [Vaughan Pratt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vaughan_Pratt) in 1976 and having a separate modal operator for every [regular expression](wikipedia://en/25717). A version of [temporal logic](wikipedia://en/321481) introduced in 1977 and intended for [program verification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Program_verification) has two modalities, corresponding to dynamic logic's \[*A*\] and \[*A*\*\] modalities for a single program *A*, understood as the whole universe taking one step forwards in time. The term *multimodal logic* itself was not introduced until 1980. Another example of a multimodal logic is the [Hennessy–Milner logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hennessy–Milner_logic), itself a fragment of the more expressive [modal μ-calculus](wikipedia://en/6158953), which is also a [fixed-point logic](wikipedia://en/50026488).

Multimodal logic can be used also to formalize a kind of [knowledge representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge_representation): the motivation of [epistemic logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemic_logic) is allowing several agents (they are regarded as [subjects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subject_%28philosophy%29) capable of forming beliefs, knowledge); and managing the belief or knowledge of each agent, so that [epistemic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemic) assertions can be formed about them. The modal operator $\Box$ must be capable of bookkeeping the cognition of each agent, thus $\Box_i$ must be indexed on the set of the agents. The motivation is that $\Box_i \alpha$ should assert "The subject *i* has knowledge about $\alpha$ being true". But it can be used also for formalizing "the subject *i* believes $\alpha$". For formalization of meaning based on the [possible world semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Possible_world_semantics) approach, a [multimodal generalization of Kripke semantics](wikipedia://en/911960#Multimodal_logics) can be used: instead of a single "common" [accessibility relation](wikipedia://en/3708585), there is a series of them indexed on the set of agents.[^n1]
