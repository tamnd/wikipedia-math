# Hamburger moment problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hamburger moment problem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hamburger [moment problem](wikipedia://en/1244992)**, named after [Hans Ludwig Hamburger](wikipedia://en/11964977), is formulated as follows: given a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(m_{0}, m_{1}, m_{2}, ...)$, does there exist a positive [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) $\mu$ (for instance, the measure determined by the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685)) on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) such that

:::

$m_n = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^n\,d\mu(x)$?

In other words, an affirmative answer to the problem means that $(m_{0}, m_{1}, m_{2}, ...)$ is the sequence of [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) of some positive Borel measure $\mu$.

The [Stieltjes moment problem](wikipedia://en/3242790), [Vorobyev moment problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vorobyev_moment_problem), and the [Hausdorff moment problem](wikipedia://en/3216387) are similar but replace the real line by $[0,+\infty)$ (Stieltjes and Vorobyev; but Vorobyev formulates the problem in the terms of matrix theory), or a [bounded interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_interval) (Hausdorff).

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization"}

## Characterization {#Characterization}

The Hamburger moment problem is solvable (that is, $(m_{n})$ is a sequence of [moments](wikipedia://en/368684)) if and only if the corresponding [Hankel kernel](wikipedia://en/377537) on the nonnegative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563)

$$A = \left(\begin{matrix} m_0 & m_1 & m_2 & \cdots \\ m_1 & m_2 & m_3 & \cdots \\ m_2 & m_3 & m_4 & \cdots \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots \end{matrix}\right)$$

is [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite_kernel), i.e.,

$$\sum_{j,k\ge0}m_{j+k}c_j\overline{c_k}\ge0$$

for every arbitrary sequence $(c_{j})_{j \ge 0}$ of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) that are finitary (i.e., $c_{j} = 0$ except for finitely many values of $j$).

For the "only if" part of the claims simply note that

$$\sum_{j,k\ge0}m_{j+k}c_j \overline{c_k} = \int_{-\infty}^\infty \left|\sum_{j\geq 0} c_j x^j\right|^2\,d \mu(x),$$

which is non-negative if $\mu$ is non-negative.

We sketch an argument for the converse. Let $\mathbf{Z}^{+}$ be the nonnegative integers and $F_{0}(\mathbf{Z}^{+})$ denote the family of complex valued sequences with finitary support. The positive Hankel kernel $A$ induces a (possibly degenerate) [sesquilinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sesquilinear) product on the family of complex-valued sequences with finite support. This in turn gives a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932)

$$(\mathcal{H}, \langle\; , \; \rangle)$$

whose typical element is an [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) denoted by $[f]$.

Let $e_{n}$ be the element in $F_{0}(\mathbf{Z}^{+})$ defined by $e_{n}(m) = \delta_{nm}$. One notices that

$$\langle [e_{n+1}], [e_m] \rangle = A_{m,n+1} = m_{m+n+1} = \langle [e_n], [e_{m+1}]\rangle.$$

Therefore, the [shift operator](wikipedia://en/467384) $T$ on $\mathcal{H}$, with $T[e_{n}] = [e_{n + 1}]$, is [symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetric_operator).

On the other hand, the desired expression

$$m_n = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^n\,d \mu(x)$$

suggests that $\mu$ is the [spectral measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectral_measure) of a [self-adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/187408). (More precisely stated, $\mu$ is the spectral measure for an operator $\overline{T}$ defined below and the vector \[1\], (Reed Simon 1975, 145)). If we can find a "function model" such that the symmetric operator $T$ is [multiplication by $x$](wikipedia://en/976642), then the spectral resolution of a [self-adjoint extension](wikipedia://en/10858909) of $T$ proves the claim.

A function model is given by the natural isomorphism from *F*<sub>0</sub>(**Z**<sup>\+</sup>) to the family of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), in one single real variable and complex coefficients: for *n* ≥ 0, identify *e<sub>n</sub>* with *x<sup>n</sup>*. In the model, the operator *T* is multiplication by *x* and a densely defined symmetric operator. It can be shown that *T* always has self-adjoint extensions. Let $\overline{T}$ be one of them and *μ* be its spectral measure. So

$$\langle \overline{T}^n [1], [1] \rangle = \int x^n d \mu(x).$$

On the other hand,

$$\langle \overline{T}^n [1], [1] \rangle = \langle T^n [e_0], [e_0] \rangle = m_n.$$

For an alternative proof of the existence that only uses [Stieltjes integrals](wikipedia://en/330491), see also,[^n1] in particular theorem 3.2.

### Uniqueness of solutions {#Uniqueness_of_solutions}

The solutions form a convex set, so the problem has either infinitely many solutions or a unique solution.

Consider the $(n + 1) \times (n + 1)$ [Hankel matrix](wikipedia://en/377537)

$$\Delta_n = \left[\begin{matrix} m_0 & m_1 & m_2 & \cdots & m_{n} \\ m_1 & m_2 & m_3 & \cdots & m_{n+1} \\ m_2 & m_3 & m_4 & \cdots & m_{n+2} \\ \vdots & \vdots & \vdots & \ddots & \vdots \\ m_{n} & m_{n+1} & m_{n+2} & \cdots & m_{2n} \end{matrix}\right].$$

Positivity of $A$ means that, for each $n$, $\det(\Delta_{n}) \ge 0$. If $\det(\Delta_{n}) = 0$, for some $n$, then

$$(\mathcal{H}, \langle \; , \; \rangle)$$

is finite-dimensional and $T$ is self-adjoint. So in this case the solution to the Hamburger moment problem is unique and $\mu$, being the spectral measure of $T$, has finite support.

More generally, the solution is unique if there are constants $C$ and $D$ such that, for all $n$, $\lvert m_{n}\rvert \le CD^{n}n!$ (Reed Simon 1975, 205). This follows from the more general [Carleman's condition](wikipedia://en/9442947).

There are examples where the solution is not unique; see e.g.[^n2]

## Polynomials {#Polynomials}

The Hamburger moment problem is intimately related to [orthogonal polynomials](wikipedia://en/32811718) on the real line. That is, assume $\{m_{n}\}_{n \in \mathbb{N}_{0}}$ is the moment sequence of some positive measure $\mu$ on $\mathbb{R}$. Then for any polynomial

$$p(x) = \sum_{j=0}^{n} a_j x^j \in \mathbb{R},$$

it holds that

$$\int p(x)^2 d\mu = \int\left( \sum_{j,k=0}^{n} a_j a_k x^{j+k} \right) d\mu = \sum_{j,k=0}^{n} a_j a_k m_{j+k} \geq 0, \quad \forall n\in \mathbb{N}_{0},$$

such that the Hankel matrix is positive semidefinite. This is a necessary condition for a sequence to be a moment sequence and a sufficient condition for the existence of a positive measure.[^n3]

The [Gram–Schmidt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gram–Schmidt) procedure gives a basis of orthogonal polynomials in which the operator: $\overline{T}$ has a tridiagonal *Jacobi matrix representation*. This in turn leads to a *tridiagonal model* of positive Hankel kernels.

An explicit calculation of the [Cayley transform](wikipedia://en/882010) of $T$ shows the connection with what is called the *[Nevanlinna class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nevanlinna_class)* of analytic functions on the left half plane. Passing to the non-commutative setting, this motivates *Krein's formula* which parametrizes the extensions of partial isometries.

The cumulative distribution function and the probability density function can often be found by applying the inverse [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) to the moment generating function

$$m(t) = \sum_{n=0}m_n\frac{t^n}{n!},$$

provided that this function converges.
