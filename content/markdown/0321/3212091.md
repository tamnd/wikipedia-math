# Local cohomology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Local cohomology"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), **local cohomology** is an algebraic analogue of [relative cohomology](wikipedia://en/1248647). [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042) introduced it in seminars in Harvard in 1961 written up by (Hartshorne 1967), and in 1961–2 at IHES written up as [SGA2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SGA2) - (Grothendieck 1968), republished as (Grothendieck 2005). Given a function (more generally, a section of a [quasicoherent sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicoherent_sheaf)) defined on an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) (or [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754)), local cohomology measures the obstruction to extending that function to a larger [domain](wikipedia://en/50263). The [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) $1/x$, for example, is defined only on the complement of $0$ on the [affine line](wikipedia://en/298834) $\mathbb{A}^1_K$ over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$, and cannot be extended to a function on the entire space. The local cohomology module $H^1_{(x)}(K[x])$ (where $K[x]$ is the [coordinate ring](wikipedia://en/320468) of $\mathbb{A}^1_K$) detects this in the nonvanishing of a [cohomology class](wikipedia://en/856614) $[1/x]$. In a similar manner, $1/xy$ is defined away from the $x$ and $y$ [axes](wikipedia://en/7706) in the [affine plane](wikipedia://en/298834), but cannot be extended to either the complement of the $x$-axis or the complement of the $y$-axis alone (nor can it be expressed as a sum of such functions); this obstruction corresponds precisely to a nonzero class $[1/xy]$ in the local cohomology module $H^2_{(x,y)}(K[x,y])$.[^o5]

:::

Outside of algebraic geometry, local cohomology has found applications in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990),[^o6][^o7][^o8] [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170),[^o9][^o10][^o11] and certain kinds of [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations).[^o12]

::: {#o14 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

In the most general geometric form of the theory, sections $\Gamma_Y$ are considered of a [sheaf](wikipedia://en/245466) $F$ of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), on a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) $X$, with [support](wikipedia://en/381013) in a [closed subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subset) $Y$, The [derived functors](wikipedia://en/433875) of $\Gamma_Y$ form **local cohomology groups**

$$H_Y^i(X,F)$$

In the theory's algebraic form, the space *X* is the [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291) Spec(*R*) of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R* (assumed to be [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129) throughout this article) and the sheaf *F* is the [quasicoherent sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicoherent_sheaf) associated to an *R*-[module](wikipedia://en/276410) *M*, denoted by $\tilde M$. The [closed subscheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subscheme) *Y* is defined by an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) *I*. In this situation, the functor Γ<sub>*Y*</sub>(*F*) corresponds to the ***I*-torsion** functor, a union of [annihilators](wikipedia://en/328684)

$$\Gamma_I(M) := \bigcup_{n \ge 0} (0 :_M I^n),$$

i.e., the elements of *M* which are annihilated by some power of *I*. As a [right derived functor](wikipedia://en/433875), the *i*<sup>th</sup> **local cohomology module** with respect to *I* is the *i*<sup>th</sup> [cohomology group](wikipedia://en/194060) $H^i(\Gamma_I(E^\bullet))$ of the [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404) $\Gamma_I(E^\bullet)$ obtained from taking the *I*-torsion part $\Gamma_I(-)$ of an [injective resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_resolution) $E^\bullet$ of the module $M$.[^o15] Because $E^\bullet$ consists of *R*-modules and *R*-module [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13564), the local cohomology groups each have the natural structure of an *R*-module.

The *I*-torsion part $\Gamma_I(M)$ may alternatively be described as

$$\Gamma_I(M) := \varinjlim_{n \in N} \operatorname {Hom}_R(R/I^n, M),$$

and for this reason, the local cohomology of an *R*-module *M* agrees[^o16] with a [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) of [*Ext* modules](wikipedia://en/840758),

$$H_I^i(M) := \varinjlim_{n \in N} \operatorname {Ext}_R^i(R/I^n, M).$$

It follows from either of these definitions that $H^i_I(M)$ would be unchanged if $I$ were replaced by another ideal having the same [radical](wikipedia://en/252009).[^o17] It also follows that local cohomology does not depend on any choice of generators for *I*, a fact which becomes relevant in the following definition involving the Čech complex.

### Using Koszul and Čech complexes {#Using_Koszul_and_Čech_complexes}

The [derived functor](wikipedia://en/433875) definition of local cohomology requires an [injective resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective_resolution) of the module $M$, which can make it inaccessible for use in explicit computations. The [Čech complex](wikipedia://en/856614) is seen as more practical in certain contexts. (Iyengar Leuschke Leykin Miller Miller), for example, state that they "essentially ignore" the "problem of actually producing any one of these \[injective\] kinds of resolutions for a given module"[^o20] prior to presenting the Čech complex definition of local cohomology, and (Hartshorne 1977) describes Čech cohomology as "giv\[ing\] a practical method for computing cohomology of quasi-coherent sheaves on a scheme."[^o22] and as being "well suited for computations."[^o23]

The **Čech complex** can be defined as a colimit of [Koszul complexes](wikipedia://en/519992) $K^\bullet(f_1,\ldots,f_m)$ where $f_1,\ldots, f_n$ generate $I$. The local cohomology modules can be described[^o24] as:

$$H_I^i(M) \cong \varinjlim_m H^i \left (\operatorname{Hom}_R \left (K^\bullet \left (f_1^m, \dots, f_n^m \right ), M \right ) \right )$$

Koszul complexes have the property that multiplication by $f_i$ induces a chain complex morphism $\cdot f_i : K^\bullet(f_1,\ldots, f_n) \to K^\bullet(f_1,\ldots, f_n)$ that is homotopic to zero,[^o25] meaning $H^i(K^\bullet(f_1,\ldots, f_n))$ is annihilated by the $f_i$. A non-zero map in the colimit of the $\operatorname{Hom}$ sets contains maps from the all but finitely many Koszul complexes, and which are not annihilated by some element in the ideal.

This colimit of Koszul complexes is isomorphic to[^o26] the [Čech complex](wikipedia://en/856614), denoted $\check{C}^\bullet(f_1,\ldots,f_n;M)$, below.

> $$0\to M \to \bigoplus_{i_0} M_{f_i} \to \bigoplus_{i_0 < i_1} M_{f_{i_0}f_{i_1}} \to \cdots \to M_{f_1\cdots f_n}\to 0$$

where the *i*<sup>th</sup> local cohomology module of $M$ with respect to $I=(f_1,\ldots,f_n)$ is isomorphic to[^o27] the *i*<sup>th</sup> [cohomology group](wikipedia://en/194060) of the above [chain complex](wikipedia://en/138404),

$$H^i_I(M)\cong H^i(\check{C}^\bullet(f_1,\ldots,f_n;M)).$$

The broader issue of computing local cohomology modules (in [characteristic zero](wikipedia://en/1066621)) is discussed in (Leykin 2002) and (Iyengar Leuschke Leykin Miller Miller, Lecture 23).

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

Since local cohomology is defined as [derived functor](wikipedia://en/433875), for any short exact sequence of *R*-modules $0\to M_1\to M_2\to M_3\to 0$, there is, by definition, a natural [long exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence) in local cohomology

$$\cdots\to H^i_I(M_1)\to H^i_I(M_2)\to H^i_I(M_3)\to H^{i+1}_I(M_1)\to\cdots$$

There is also a [long exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_exact_sequence) of [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357) linking the ordinary sheaf cohomology of *X* and of the [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) *U* = *X* \\*Y*, with the local cohomology modules. For a [quasicoherent sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicoherent_sheaf) *F* defined on *X*, this has the form

$$\cdots\to H^i_Y(X,F)\to H^i(X,F)\to H^i(U,F)\to H^{i+1}_Y(X,F)\to\cdots$$

In the setting where *X* is an [affine scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme) $\text{Spec}(R)$ and *Y* is the vanishing set of an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) *I*, the cohomology groups $H^i(X,F)$ vanish for $i>0$.[^o32] If $F=\tilde{M}$, this leads to an exact sequence

$$0 \to H_I^0(M) \to M \stackrel {\text{res}} \to H^0(U, \tilde M) \to H^1_I(M) \to 0,$$

where the middle map is the restriction of sections. The target of this restriction map is also referred to as the [ideal transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_transform). For *n* ≥ 1, there are isomorphisms

$$H^{n}(U, \tilde M) \stackrel \cong \to H^{n+1}_I(M).$$

Because of the above isomorphism with [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357), local cohomology can be used to express a number of meaningful [topological](wikipedia://en/38801) constructions on the scheme $X=\operatorname{Spec}(R)$ in purely algebraic terms. For example, there is a natural analogue in local cohomology of the [Mayer–Vietoris sequence](wikipedia://en/378677) with respect to a pair of open sets *U* and *V* in *X*, given by the complements of the closed subschemes corresponding to a pair of ideal *I* and *J*, respectively.[^o33] This sequence has the form

$$\cdots H^i_{I+J}(M)\to H^i_I(M)\oplus H^i_J(M)\to H^i_{I\cap J}(M)\to H^{i+1}_{I+J}(M)\to\cdots$$

for any $R$-module $M$.

The vanishing of local cohomology can be used to bound the least number of equations (referred to as the [arithmetic rank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_rank)) needed to (set theoretically) define the algebraic set $V(I)$ in $\operatorname{Spec}(R)$. If $J$ has the same radical as $I$, and is generated by $n$ elements, then the Čech complex on the generators of $J$ has no terms in degree $i > n$. The least number of generators among all ideals $J$ such that $\sqrt{J}=\sqrt{I}$ is the arithmetic rank of $I$, denoted $\operatorname{ara}(I)$.[^o34] Since the local cohomology with respect to $I$ may be computed using any such ideal, it follows that $H^i_I(M)=0$ for $i>\operatorname{ara}(I)$.[^o35]

### Graded local cohomology and projective geometry {#Graded_local_cohomology_and_projective_geometry}

When $R$ is [graded](wikipedia://en/103094) by $\mathbb{N}$, $I$ is generated by homogeneous elements, and $M$ is a graded module, there is a natural grading on the local cohomology module $H^i_I(M)$ that is compatible with the gradings of $M$ and $R$.[^o37] All of the basic properties of local cohomology expressed in this article are compatible with the graded structure.[^o38] If $M$ is finitely generated and $I=\mathfrak{m}$ is the ideal generated by the elements of $R$ having positive degree, then the graded components $H^i_{\mathfrak{m}}(M)_n$ are finitely generated over $R$ and vanish for sufficiently large $n$.[^o39]

The case where $I=\mathfrak m$ is the ideal generated by all elements of positive degree (sometimes called the [irrelevant ideal](wikipedia://en/24621384)) is particularly special, due to its relationship with [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849).[^o40] In this case, there is an isomorphism

$$H^{i+1}_{\mathfrak m}(M)\cong \bigoplus_{k \in \mathbf Z} H^i(\text{Proj}(R), \tilde M(k))$$

where $\text{Proj}(R)$ is the [projective scheme](wikipedia://en/2285000) associated to $R$, and $(k)$ denotes the [Serre twist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serre_twist). This isomorphism is graded, giving

$$H^{i+1}_{\mathfrak m}(M)_n \cong H^i(\text{Proj}(R), \tilde M(n))$$

in all degrees $n$.[^o41]

This isomorphism relates local cohomology with the [global cohomology of projective schemes](wikipedia://en/23535337). For example, the [Castelnuovo–Mumford regularity](wikipedia://en/24560170) can be formulated using local cohomology[^o42] as

$$\text{reg}(M) = \text{sup}\{\text{end}(H^i_{\mathfrak{m}}(M))+i\,|\, 0\leq i\leq \text{dim}(M)\}$$

where $\text{end}(N)$ denotes the highest degree $t$ such that $N_t\neq 0$. Local cohomology can be used to prove certain upper bound results concerning the regularity.[^o43]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Top local cohomology {#Top_local_cohomology}

Using the Čech complex, if $I=(f_1,\ldots,f_n)R$ the local cohomology module $H^n_I(M)$ is generated over $R$ by the images of the [formal fractions](wikipedia://en/252216)

$$\left[\frac{m}{f_1^{t_1}\cdots f_n^{t_n}}\right]$$

for $m\in M$ and $t_1,\ldots,t_n\geq 1$.[^o47] This fraction corresponds to a nonzero element of $H^n_I(M)$ if and only if there is no $k\geq 0$ such that $(f_1\cdots f_t)^k m \in (f_1^{t_1+k},\ldots,f_t^{t_n+k})M$.[^o48] For example, if $t_i=1$, then

$$f_i\cdot \left[\frac{m}{f_1^{t_1}\cdots f_i\cdots f_n^{t_n}}\right]=0.$$

- If $K$ is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) and $R=K[x_1,\ldots,x_n]$ is a [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) over $K$ in $n$ variables, then the local cohomology module $H^n_{(x_1,\ldots,x_n)}(K[x_1,\ldots,x_n])$ may be regarded as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over $K$ with basis given by (the [Čech cohomology](wikipedia://en/856614) classes of) the inverse monomials $\left[x_1^{-t_1}\cdots x_n^{-t_n}\right]$ for $t_1,\ldots,t_n\geq 1$.[^o49] As an $R$-module, multiplication by $x_i$ lowers $t_i$ by 1, subject to the condition $x_i\cdot \left[x_1^{-t_1}\cdots x_i^{-1}\cdots x_n^{-t_n}\right]=0.$ Because the powers $t_i$ cannot be increased by multiplying with elements of $R$, the module $H^n_{(x_1,\ldots,x_n)}(K[x_1,\ldots,x_n])$ is not [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430).

### Examples of H<sup>1</sup> {#Examples_of_H1}

If $H^0(U,\tilde R)$ is known (where $U=\operatorname{Spec}(R)-V(I)$), the module $H^1_I(R)$ can sometimes be computed explicitly using the sequence

$$0 \to H_I^0(R) \to R \to H^0(U, \tilde R) \to H^1_I(R) \to 0.$$

In the following examples, $K$ is any [field](wikipedia://en/10603).

- If $R=K[X,Y^2,XY,Y^3]$ and $I=(X,Y^2)R$, then $H^0(U,\tilde R)=K[X,Y]$ and as a vector space over $K$, the first local cohomology module $H^1_I(R)$ is $K[X,Y]/K[X,Y^2,XY,Y^3]$, a 1-dimensional $K$ vector space generated by $Y$.[^o51]
- If $R=K[X,Y]/(X^2,XY)$ and $\mathfrak{m}=(X,Y)R$, then $\Gamma_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=xR$ and $H^0(U,\tilde R)=K[Y,Y^{-1}]$, so $H^1_{\mathfrak{m}}(R)=K[Y,Y^{-1}]/K[Y]$ is an infinite-dimensional $K$ vector space with basis $Y^{-1},Y^{-2},Y^{-3},\ldots$[^o52]

:::

## Relation to invariants of modules {#Relation_to_invariants_of_modules}

The dimension dim<sub>*R*</sub>(M) of a module (defined as the [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) of its support) provides an upper bound for local cohomology modules:[^o54]

$$H_I^n(M) = 0 \text{ for all }n>\dim_R(M).$$

If *R* is [local](wikipedia://en/153391) and *M* [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430), then this bound is sharp, i.e., $H^n_\mathfrak{m}(M) \ne 0$.

The [depth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Depth_of_a_module) (defined as the maximal length of a [regular *M*-sequence](wikipedia://en/505119); also referred to as the grade of *M*) provides a sharp lower bound, i.e., it is the smallest integer *n* such that[^o55]

$$H^n_I(M) \ne 0.$$

These two bounds together yield a characterisation of [Cohen–Macaulay modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen–Macaulay_module) over local rings: they are precisely those modules where $H^n_\mathfrak{m}(M)$ vanishes for all but one *n*.

## Local duality {#Local_duality}

The [local duality theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_duality_theorem) is a local analogue of [Serre duality](wikipedia://en/359175). For a [Cohen-Macaulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen-Macaulay) [local ring](wikipedia://en/153391) $R$ of dimension $d$ that is a homomorphic image of a [Gorenstein local ring](wikipedia://en/515613)[^o57] (for example, if $R$ is [complete](wikipedia://en/9477975)[^o58]), it states that the natural pairing

$$H^n_\mathfrak m(M) \times \operatorname{Ext}_R^{d-n}(M, \omega_R) \to H^d_\mathfrak m(\omega_R)$$

is a [perfect pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_pairing), where $\omega_R$ is a [dualizing module](wikipedia://en/38513558) for $R$.[^o59] In terms of the [Matlis duality functor](wikipedia://en/38077774) $D(-)$, the local duality theorem may be expressed as the following isomorphism.[^o60]

$$H^n_\mathfrak m(M) \cong D(\operatorname{Ext}_R^{d-n}(M,\omega_R))$$

The statement is simpler when $\omega_R \cong R$, which is equivalent[^o61] to the hypothesis that $R$ is [Gorenstein](wikipedia://en/515613). This is the case, for example, if $R$ is [regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_ring).

## Applications {#Applications}

The initial applications were to analogues of the [Lefschetz hyperplane theorems](wikipedia://en/3459657). In general such theorems state that homology or cohomology is supported on a [hyperplane section](wikipedia://en/3459767) of an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808), except for some 'loss' that can be controlled. These results applied to the [algebraic fundamental group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_fundamental_group) and to the [Picard group](wikipedia://en/1850134).

Another type of application are connectedness theorems such as [Grothendieck's connectedness theorem](wikipedia://en/13993691) (a local analogue of the [Bertini theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertini_theorem)) or the [Fulton–Hansen connectedness theorem](wikipedia://en/5287504) due to (Fulton Hansen 1979) and (Faltings 1979). The latter asserts that for two [projective varieties](wikipedia://en/320469) *V* and *W* in **P**<sup>*r*</sup> over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018), the [connectedness dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connectedness_dimension) of *Z* = *V* ∩ *W* (i.e., the minimal dimension of a closed subset *T* of *Z* that has to be removed from *Z* so that the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) *Z* \\ *T* is [disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disconnected_space)) is bound by

c(*Z*) ≥ dim *V* + dim *W* − *r* − 1.

For example, *Z* is connected if dim *V* + dim *W* > *r*.[^o66]

In polyhedral geometry, a key ingredient of Stanley's 1975 proof of the simplicial form of McMullen's [Upper bound theorem](wikipedia://en/40964551) involves showing that the [Stanley-Reisner ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stanley-Reisner_ring) of the corresponding [simplicial complex](wikipedia://en/252686) is [Cohen-Macaulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen-Macaulay), and local cohomology is an important tool in this computation, via Hochster's formula.[^o67][^o10][^o68]
