# Symmetric derivative

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Symmetric derivative"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **symmetric derivative** is an [operation](wikipedia://en/22213) generalizing the ordinary [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921).

:::

It is defined as:[^o2][^o3]

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - f(x - h)}{2h}.$$

The expression under the limit is sometimes called the **symmetric [difference quotient](wikipedia://en/241863)**.[^o4][^o5] A function is said to be **symmetrically differentiable** at a point *x* if its symmetric derivative exists at that point.

If a function is [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) (in the usual sense) at a point, then it is also symmetrically differentiable, but the converse is not true. A well-known [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) function $f(x) = |x|$, which is not differentiable at $x = 0$, but is symmetrically differentiable there with symmetric derivative 0. For differentiable functions, the [symmetric difference](wikipedia://en/262288) quotient does provide a better [numerical approximation of the derivative](wikipedia://en/1254566) than the usual difference quotient.[^o4]

The symmetric derivative at a given point equals the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of the [left and right derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_and_right_derivative) at that point, if the latter two both exist.[^o2][^o3]p. 6

Neither [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305) nor the [mean-value theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean-value_theorem) hold for the symmetric derivative; some similar but weaker statements have been proved.

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### The absolute value function {#The_absolute_value_function}

*Image: Modulusfunction.png.* [Graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of the absolute value function. Note the sharp turn at $x = 0$, leading to non-differentiability of the curve at $x = 0$. The function hence possesses no ordinary derivative at $x = 0$. The symmetric derivative, however, exists for the function at $x = 0$.

For the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) function $f(x) = |x|$, using the notation $f_s(x)$ for the symmetric derivative, we have at $x = 0$ that

$$\begin{aligned} f_s(0) &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(0 + h) - f(0 - h)}{2h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(h) - f(-h)}{2h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{|h| - |{-h}|}{2h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{|h| - |h|}{2h} \\ &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{0}{2h} = 0. \\ \end{aligned}$$

Hence the symmetric derivative of the absolute [value function](wikipedia://en/12617694) exists at $x = 0$ and is equal to zero, even though its [ordinary derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinary_derivative) does not exist at that point (due to a "sharp" turn in the curve at $x = 0$).

Note that in this example both the left and right derivatives at 0 exist, but they are unequal (one is −1, while the other is +1); their average is 0, as expected.

### The function *x*<sup>−2</sup> {#The_function_x−2}

*Image: Graphinversesqrt.png.* Graph of $y = 1/x^{2}$. Note the discontinuity at $x = 0$. The function hence possesses no ordinary derivative at $x = 0$. The symmetric derivative, however, exists for the function at $x = 0$.

For the function $f(x) = 1/x^2$, at $x = 0$ we have

$$\begin{aligned} f_s(0) &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(0 + h) - f(0 - h)}{2h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{f(h) - f(-h)}{2h} \\[1ex] &= \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{1/h^2 - 1/(-h)^2}{2h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{1/h^2 - 1/h^2}{2h} = \lim_{h \to 0}\frac{0}{2h} = 0. \end{aligned}$$

Again, for this function the symmetric derivative exists at $x = 0$, while its ordinary derivative does not exist at $x = 0$ due to discontinuity in the curve there. Furthermore, neither the left nor the right derivative is finite at 0, i.e. this is an [essential discontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_discontinuity).

### The Dirichlet function {#The_Dirichlet_function}

The [Dirichlet function](wikipedia://en/630005), defined as:

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 1, & \text{if }x\text{ is rational} \\ 0, & \text{if }x\text{ is irrational} \end{cases}$$

has a symmetric derivative at every $x \in \mathbb{Q}$, but is not symmetrically differentiable at any $x \in \mathbb{R} \setminus \mathbb{Q}$; i.e. the symmetric derivative exists at [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) but not at [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689).

:::

## Quasi-mean-value theorem {#Quasi-mean-value_theorem}

The symmetric derivative does not obey the usual [mean-value theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean-value_theorem) (of Lagrange). As a counterexample, the symmetric derivative of $f(x) = |x|$ has the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) $\{-1, 0, 1\}$, but secants for *f* can have a wider range of slopes; for instance, on the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) −1, 2, the mean-value theorem would mandate that there exist a point where the (symmetric) derivative takes the value $\frac{|2| - |-1|}{2 - (-1)} = \frac{1}{3}$.[^o13]

A theorem somewhat analogous to [Rolle's theorem](wikipedia://en/200305) but for the symmetric derivative was established in 1967 by C. E. Aull, who named it quasi-Rolle theorem. If $f$ is continuous on the [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) *a*, *b* and symmetrically differentiable on the [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) *a*, *b*, and $f(a) = f(b) = 0$, then there exist two points $x$, $y$ in *a*, *b* such that $f_{s}(x) \ge 0$, and $f_{s}(y) \le 0$. A lemma also established by Aull as a stepping stone to this theorem states that if $f$ is continuous on the closed interval *a*, *b* and symmetrically differentiable on the open interval *a*, *b*, and additionally $f(b) > f(a)$, then there exist a point $z$ in *a*, *b* where the symmetric derivative is non-negative, or with the notation used above, $f_{s}(z) \ge 0$. Analogously, if $f(b) < f(a)$, then there exists a point $z$ in *a*, *b* where $f_{s}(z) \le 0$.[^o13]

The **quasi-mean-value theorem** for a symmetrically differentiable function states that if $f$ is continuous on the closed interval *a*, *b* and symmetrically differentiable on the open interval *a*, *b*, then there exist $x$, $y$ in *a*, *b* such that[^o13][^o3]p. 7

$$f_s(x) \leq \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a} \leq f_s(y).$$

As an application, the quasi-mean-value theorem for $f(x) = |x|$ on an interval containing 0 predicts that the slope of any [secant](wikipedia://en/241886) of $f$ is between −1 and 1.

If the symmetric derivative of $f$ has the [Darboux property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darboux_property), then the (form of the) regular [mean-value theorem (of Lagrange)](wikipedia://en/19662) holds, i.e. there exists $z$ in *a*, *b* such that[^o13]

$$f_s(z) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}.$$

As a consequence, if a function is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) and its symmetric derivative is also continuous (thus has the [Darboux property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darboux_property)), then the function is differentiable in the usual sense.[^o13]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The notion generalizes to [higher-order symmetric derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher-order_derivative) and also to *n*-dimensional [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697).

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Second symmetric derivative"}

### The second symmetric derivative {#The_second_symmetric_derivative}

The second symmetric derivative is defined as[^o18][^o3]p. 1

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{f(x + h) - 2f(x) + f(x - h)}{h^2}.$$

If the (usual) [second derivative](wikipedia://en/3224219) exists, then the second symmetric derivative exists and is equal to it.[^o18] The second symmetric derivative may exist, however, even when the (ordinary) second derivative does not. As example, consider the [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449) $\operatorname{sgn}(x)$, which is defined by

$$\operatorname{sgn}(x) = \begin{cases} -1 & \text{if } x < 0, \\ 0 & \text{if } x = 0, \\ 1 & \text{if } x > 0. \end{cases}$$

The sign function is not continuous at zero, and therefore the second derivative for $x = 0$ does not exist. But the second symmetric derivative exists for $x = 0$:

$$\lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\operatorname{sgn}(0 + h) - 2\operatorname{sgn}(0) + \operatorname{sgn}(0 - h)}{h^2} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{\operatorname{sgn}(h) - 2\cdot 0 + (-\operatorname{sgn}(h))}{h^2} = \lim_{h \to 0} \frac{0}{h^2} = 0.$$
