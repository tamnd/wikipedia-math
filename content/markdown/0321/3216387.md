# Hausdorff moment problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hausdorff moment problem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hausdorff [moment problem](wikipedia://en/1244992)**, named after [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989), asks for necessary and sufficient conditions that a given [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(m_{0}, m_{1}, m_{2}, ...)$ be the sequence of [moments](wikipedia://en/368684)

:::

$$m_n = \int_0^1 x^n\,d\mu(x)$$

of some [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) $\mu$ [supported](wikipedia://en/6591796) on the closed [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) $[0, 1]$. In the case $m_{0} = 1$, this is equivalent to the existence of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ supported on $[0, 1]$, such that $E[Xn] = m_{n}$.

The essential difference between this and other well-known moment problems is that this is on a [bounded interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_interval), whereas in the [Stieltjes moment problem](wikipedia://en/3242790) one considers a half-line $[0, \infty)$, and in the [Hamburger moment problem](wikipedia://en/3216491) one considers the whole line $(-\infty, \infty)$. The Stieltjes moment problems and the Hamburger moment problems, if they are solvable, may have infinitely many solutions (indeterminate moment problem) whereas a Hausdorff moment problem always has a unique solution if it is solvable (determinate moment problem). In the indeterminate moment problem case, there are infinite measures corresponding to the same prescribed moments and they consist of a convex set. The set of polynomials may or may not be dense in the associated Hilbert spaces if the moment problem is indeterminate, and it depends on whether measure is extremal or not. But in the determinate moment problem case, the set of polynomials is dense in the associated Hilbert space.

## Completely monotonic sequences {#Completely_monotonic_sequences}

In 1921, Hausdorff showed that $(m_{0}, m_{1}, m_{2}, ...)$ is such a moment sequence if and only if the sequence is completely monotonic, that is, its difference sequences satisfy the equation

$$\Delta^k m_n \geq 0$$

for all $n, k \ge 0$. Here, $\Delta$ is the [difference operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_operator) given by

$$\Delta m_n = m_n - m_{n+1},$$

and in general for all $k\geq1$,

$$\Delta^k m_n = \sum_{i=0}^k \binom{k}{i} (-1)^i m_{n+i}.$$

The necessity of this condition is easily seen by observing that $\Delta^km_n$ can be expressed as the integral of a positive function and therefore must be positive:

$$0\leq \int_0^1 x^n(1-x)^k d\mu(x) =\Delta^km_n.$$

The equality in the identity above is justified by using the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) to expand the term $(1-x)^k = \sum_{i=0}^k \binom{k}{i} (-1)^ix^i$ within the integral and then [Fubini's theorem](wikipedia://en/255245) to switch the resulting sum and the integral:

$$\int_0^1 x^n (1-x)^k d\mu(x) =\sum_{i=0}^k\binom{k}{i}(-1)^i\int_0^1x^{n+i}d\mu(x)=\sum_{i=0}^k\binom{k}{i}(-1)^i m_{n+i} = \Delta^km_n.$$

For example, it is necessary to have

$$\Delta^4 m_6 = m_6 - 4m_7 + 6m_8 - 4m_9 + m_{10} = \int x^6 (1-x)^4 d\mu(x) \geq 0.$$
