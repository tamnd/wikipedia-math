# Quasi-identifier

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasi-identifier"}

**Quasi-identifiers** are pieces of information that are not of themselves [unique identifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_identifier), but are sufficiently well correlated with an entity that they can be combined with other quasi-identifiers to create a unique identifier.[^o2]

:::

Quasi-identifiers can thus, when combined, become [personally identifying information](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personally_identifying_information). This process is called [re-identification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Re-identification). As an example, [Latanya Sweeney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latanya_Sweeney) has shown that even though neither [gender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gender), [birth dates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birth_date) nor [postal codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postal_code) uniquely identify an individual, the combination of all three is sufficient to identify 87% of individuals in the United States.[^o3]

The term was introduced by Tore Dalenius in 1986.[^o4] Since then, quasi-identifiers have been the basis of several attacks on released data. For instance, Sweeney linked health records to publicly available information to locate the then-governor of Massachusetts' hospital records using uniquely identifying quasi-identifiers,[^o5][^o6] and Sweeney, Abu and Winn used public voter records to re-identify participants in the Personal Genome Project.[^o7] Additionally, Arvind Narayanan and [Vitaly Shmatikov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitaly_Shmatikov) discussed on quasi-identifiers to indicate statistical conditions for de-anonymizing data released by Netflix.[^o8]

Motwani and Ying warn about potential privacy breaches being enabled by publication of large volumes of government and business data containing quasi-identifiers.[^o9]
