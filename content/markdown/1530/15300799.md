# Geary's C

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geary's C"}

**Geary's *C*** is a measure of spatial [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) developed by [Roy C. Geary](wikipedia://en/15112696).[^o2] that attempts to determine if observations of the same variable are spatially autocorrelated globally (rather than at the neighborhood level). [Spatial autocorrelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_autocorrelation) is more complex than [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) because the correlation is multi-dimensional and bi-directional.

:::

## Global Geary's C {#Global_Geary's_C}

The generalised Geary coefficient[^o4] is defined as

$$C = \frac{(N-1) \sum_{i} \sum_{j} w_{ij} (x_i-x_j)^2}{2 S_0 \sum_{i}(x_i-\bar x)^2}$$

where $N$ is the number of spatial units indexed by $i$ and $j$; $x$ is the variable of interest; $\bar x$ is the mean of $x$; $w_{ij}$ is the $i^{th}$ row of the spatial weights matrix $W$ with zeroes on the diagonal (i.e., $w_{ii} = 0$); and $S_0$ is the sum of all weights in $W$.

*Image: Geary example.png.* Geary's C statistic computed for different spatial patterns. Using '[rook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rook_%28chess%29)' neighbors for each grid cell, setting $w_{ij}=1$ for neighbours $j$ of $i$ and then row normalizing the weight matrix. Top left shows gives $C>1$ indicating anti-correlation. Top right shows a spatial gradient giving $C<1$ indicating correlation. Bottom left shows random data giving a value of $C\sim1$ indicating no correlation. Bottom right shows a spreading pattern with positive autocorrelation.

The value of Geary's *C* lies between 0 and some unspecified value greater than 1. Values significantly lower than 1 demonstrate increasing positive spatial autocorrelation, whilst values significantly higher than 1 illustrate increasing negative spatial autocorrelation.

Geary's *C* is inversely related to [Moran's *I*](wikipedia://en/15308227), but it is not identical. While Moran's *I* and Geary's *C* are both measures of global spatial autocorrelation, they are slightly different. Geary's *C* uses the sum of squared distances whereas Moran's *I* uses standardized spatial covariance. By using squared distances Geary's *C* is less sensitive to linear associations and may pickup autocorrelation where Moran's *I* may not.[^o5]

Geary's *C* is also known as Geary's contiguity ratio or simply Geary's ratio.[^o6]

## Local Geary's C {#Local_Geary's_C}

Like [Moran's I](wikipedia://en/15308227), Geary's C can be decomposed into a sum of Local Indicators of Spatial Association (LISA) statistics. LISA statistics can be used to find local clusters through [significance testing](wikipedia://en/30284), though because a large number of tests must be performed (one per sampling area) this approach suffers from the [multiple comparisons problem](wikipedia://en/9444220). As noted by [Anselin](wikipedia://en/12921821),[^o8] this means the analysis of the local Geary statistic is aimed at identifying *interesting* points which should then be subject to further investigation. This is therefore a type of [exploratory data analysis](wikipedia://en/416589).

A local version of $C$ is given by[^o9]

$$c_i = \frac{1}{m_2}\sum_j w_{ij}(x_i-x_j)^2$$

where

$$m_2= \frac{\sum_i (x_i-\bar x)^2 }{N-1}$$

then,

$$C = \sum_i \frac{c_i}{2S_0}$$

Local Geary's C can be calculated in [GeoDa](wikipedia://en/9322583) and [PySAL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PySAL).[^o10]
