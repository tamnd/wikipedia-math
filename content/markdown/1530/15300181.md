# Category of rings

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Category of rings"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **category of rings**, denoted by **Ring**,[^o2] is the [category](wikipedia://en/53759) whose objects are [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) (with identity) and whose [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) are [ring homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/26411) (that preserve the identity). Like many categories in mathematics, the category of rings is [large](wikipedia://en/53759#Small_and_large_categories), meaning that the [class](wikipedia://en/20696) of all rings is [proper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_class).

:::

## As a concrete category {#As_a_concrete_category}

The category **Ring** is a [concrete category](wikipedia://en/167001) meaning that the objects are [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) with additional structure (addition and multiplication) and the morphisms are [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) that preserve this structure. There is a natural [forgetful functor](wikipedia://en/844783)

*U* : **Ring** → **Set**

for the category of rings to the [category of sets](wikipedia://en/26829) which sends each ring to its underlying set (thus "forgetting" the operations of addition and multiplication). This functor has a [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint)

*F* : **Set** → **Ring**

which assigns to each set *X* the [free ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_ring) generated by *X*.

One can also view the category of rings as a concrete category over **Ab** (the [category of abelian groups](wikipedia://en/454748)) or over **Mon** (the [category of monoids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_monoids)). Specifically, there are [forgetful functors](wikipedia://en/844783)

*A* : **Ring** → **Ab**

*M* : **Ring** → **Mon**

which "forget" multiplication and addition, respectively. Both of these functors have left adjoints. The left adjoint of *A* is the functor which assigns to every [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) *X* (thought of as a **Z**-[module](wikipedia://en/276410)) the [tensor ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_ring) *T*(*X*). The left adjoint of *M* is the functor which assigns to every [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) *X* the integral [monoid ring](wikipedia://en/20781) **Z**\[*X*\].

## Properties {#Properties}

### Limits and colimits {#Limits_and_colimits}

The category **Ring** is both [complete and cocomplete](wikipedia://en/62781), meaning that all small [limits and colimits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limits_and_colimits) exist in **Ring**. Like many other algebraic categories, the forgetful functor *U* : **Ring** → **Set** [creates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Creation_of_limits) (and preserves) limits and [filtered colimits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filtered_colimit), but does not preserve either [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889) or [coequalizers](wikipedia://en/431940). The forgetful functors to **Ab** and **Mon** also create and preserve limits.

Examples of limits and colimits in **Ring** include:

- The ring of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) **Z** is an [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object) in **Ring**.
- The [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) is a [terminal object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminal_object) in **Ring**.
- The [product](wikipedia://en/237868) in **Ring** is given by the [direct product of rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_product_of_rings). This is just the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of the underlying sets with addition and multiplication defined component-wise.
- The [coproduct of a family of rings](wikipedia://en/54138189) exists and is given by a construction analogous to the [free product](wikipedia://en/360036) of groups. The coproduct of nonzero rings can be the zero ring; in particular, this happens whenever the factors have [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime) [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) (since the characteristic of the coproduct of (*R*<sub>*i*</sub>)<sub>*i*∈*I*</sub> must divide the characteristics of each of the rings *R*<sub>*i*</sub>).
- The [equalizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equalizer_%28mathematics%29) in **Ring** is just the set-theoretic equalizer (the equalizer of two ring homomorphisms is always a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145)).
- The [coequalizer](wikipedia://en/431940) of two ring homomorphisms *f* and *g* from *R* to *S* is the [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635) of *S* by the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) generated by all elements of the form *f*(*r*) − *g*(*r*) for *r* ∈ *R*.
- Given a ring homomorphism *f* : *R* → *S* the [kernel pair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_pair) of *f* (this is just the [pullback](wikipedia://en/841689) of *f* with itself) is a [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167) on *R*. The ideal determined by this congruence relation is precisely the (ring-theoretic) [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28ring_theory%29) of *f*. Note that [category-theoretic kernels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category-theoretic_kernel) do not make sense in **Ring** since there are no [zero morphisms](wikipedia://en/320638) (see below).

### Morphisms {#Morphisms}

Unlike many categories studied in mathematics, there do not always exist morphisms between pairs of objects in **Ring**. This is a consequence of the fact that ring homomorphisms must preserve the identity. For example, there are no morphisms from the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) **0** to any nonzero ring. A necessary condition for there to be morphisms from *R* to *S* is that the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of *S* divide that of *R*.

Note that even though some of the hom-sets are empty, the category **Ring** is still [connected](wikipedia://en/1411533) since it has an initial object.

Some special classes of morphisms in **Ring** include:

- [Isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) in **Ring** are the [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) ring homomorphisms.
- [Monomorphisms](wikipedia://en/59538) in **Ring** are the [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) homomorphisms. Not every monomorphism is [regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_monomorphism) however.
- Every surjective homomorphism is an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539) in **Ring**, but the converse is not true. The inclusion **Z** → **Q** is a nonsurjective epimorphism. The natural ring homomorphism from any commutative ring *R* to any one of its [localizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Localization_of_a_ring) is an epimorphism which is not necessarily surjective.
- The surjective homomorphisms can be characterized as the [regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_epimorphism) or [extremal epimorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremal_epimorphism) in **Ring** (these two classes coinciding).
- [Bimorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bimorphism) in **Ring** are the injective epimorphisms. The inclusion **Z** → **Q** is an example of a bimorphism which is not an isomorphism.

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

- The only [injective object](wikipedia://en/473779) in **Ring** up to isomorphism is the [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) (i.e. the terminal object).
- Lacking [zero morphisms](wikipedia://en/320638), the category of rings cannot be a [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521). (However, every ring—considered as a category with a single object—is a preadditive category).
- The category of rings is a [symmetric monoidal category](wikipedia://en/3865249) with the [tensor product of rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_rings) ⊗<sub>**Z**</sub> as the monoidal product and the ring of integers **Z** as the unit object. It follows from the [Eckmann–Hilton theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eckmann–Hilton_theorem), that a [monoid](wikipedia://en/3955446) in **Ring** is a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346). The action of a monoid (= commutative ring) *R* on an object (= ring) *A* of **Ring** is an [*R*-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R-algebra).

:::

## Subcategories {#Subcategories}

The category of rings has a number of important [subcategories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subcategories). These include the [full subcategories](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Full_subcategories) of [commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_rings), [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462), [principal ideal domains](wikipedia://en/25039), and [fields](wikipedia://en/10603).

::: {#o11 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Category of commutative algebras"}

### Category of commutative rings {#Category_of_commutative_rings}

The **category of commutative rings**, denoted **CRing**, is the full subcategory of **Ring** whose objects are all [commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_rings). This category is one of the central objects of study in the subject of [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990).

Any ring can be made commutative by taking the [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635) by the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) generated by all elements of the form (*xy* − *yx*). This defines a functor **Ring** → **CRing** which is left adjoint to the inclusion functor, so that **CRing** is a [reflective subcategory](wikipedia://en/3967424) of **Ring**. The [free commutative ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_commutative_ring) on a set of generators *E* is the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) **Z**\[*E*\] whose variables are taken from *E*. This gives a left adjoint functor to the forgetful functor from **CRing** to **Set**.

**CRing** is limit-closed in **Ring**, which means that limits in **CRing** are the same as they are in **Ring**. Colimits, however, are generally different. They can be formed by taking the commutative quotient of colimits in **Ring**. The coproduct of two commutative rings is given by the [tensor product of rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_rings). Again, the coproduct of two nonzero commutative rings can be zero.

The [opposite category](wikipedia://en/372399) of **CRing** is [equivalent](wikipedia://en/379051) to the [category of affine schemes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Category_of_affine_schemes). The equivalence is given by the [contravariant functor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contravariant_functor) Spec which sends a commutative ring to its [spectrum](wikipedia://en/37291), an affine [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754).

:::

::: {#o13 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Category of fields"}

### Category of fields {#Category_of_fields}

The **category of fields**, denoted **Field**, is the full subcategory of **CRing** whose objects are [fields](wikipedia://en/10603). The category of fields is not nearly as well-behaved as other algebraic categories. In particular, free fields do not exist (i.e. there is no left adjoint to the forgetful functor **Field** → **Set**). It follows that **Field** is *not* a reflective subcategory of **CRing**.

The category of fields is neither [finitely complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_complete_category) nor finitely cocomplete. In particular, **Field** has neither products nor coproducts.

Another curious aspect of the category of fields is that every morphism is a [monomorphism](wikipedia://en/59538). This follows from the fact that the only ideals in a field *F* are the [zero ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) and *F* itself. One can then view morphisms in **Field** as [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634).

The category of fields is not [connected](wikipedia://en/1411533). There are no morphisms between fields of different [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621). The connected components of **Field** are the full subcategories of characteristic *p*, where *p* = 0 or is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666). Each such subcategory has an [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object): the [prime field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field) of characteristic *p* (which is **Q** if *p* = 0, otherwise the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) **F**<sub>*p*</sub>).

:::

## Related categories and functors {#Related_categories_and_functors}

### Category of groups {#Category_of_groups}

There is a natural functor from **Ring** to the [category of groups](wikipedia://en/800092), **Grp**, which sends each ring *R* to its [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) *U*(*R*) and each ring homomorphism to the restriction to *U*(*R*). This functor has a [left adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_adjoint) which sends each [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* to the [integral group ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_group_ring) **Z**\[*G*\].

Another functor between these categories sends each ring *R* to the group of units of the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) M<sub>2</sub>(*R*) which acts on the [projective line over a ring](wikipedia://en/1271901) P(*R*).

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Category of algebras"}

### *R*-algebras {#R-algebras}

Given a commutative ring *R* one can define the category ***R*-Alg** whose objects are all [*R*-algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_%28ring_theory%29) and whose morphisms are *R*-[algebra homomorphisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_homomorphism).

The category of rings can be considered a special case. Every ring can be considered a **Z**-algebra in a unique way. Ring homomorphisms are precisely the **Z**-algebra homomorphisms. The category of rings is, therefore, [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/489935) to the category **Z-Alg**.[^o18] Many statements about the category of rings can be generalized to statements about the category of *R*-algebras.

For each commutative ring *R* there is a functor ***R*-Alg** → **Ring** which forgets the *R*-module structure. This functor has a left adjoint which sends each ring *A* to the [tensor product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tensor_product_of_rings) *R*⊗<sub>**Z**</sub>*A*, thought of as an *R*-algebra by setting *r*·(*s*⊗*a*) = *rs*⊗*a*.

:::

::: {#o20 .statement cue=named-heading name="Rings without identity"}

### Rings without identity {#Rings_without_identity}

Many authors do not require rings to have a multiplicative identity element and, accordingly, do not require ring homomorphism to preserve the identity (should it exist). This leads to a rather different category. For distinction we call such algebraic structures *[rngs](wikipedia://en/3405004)* and their morphisms *rng homomorphisms*. The category of all rngs will be denoted by **Rng**.

The category of rings, **Ring**, is a *nonfull* [subcategory](wikipedia://en/310959) of **Rng**. It is nonfull because there are rng homomorphisms between rings which do not preserve the identity, and are therefore not morphisms in **Ring**. The inclusion functor **Ring** → **Rng** has a left adjoint which formally adjoins an identity to any rng. The inclusion functor **Ring** → **Rng** respects limits but not colimits.

The [zero ring](wikipedia://en/1872874) serves as both an initial and terminal object in **Rng** (that is, it is a [zero object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_object)). It follows that **Rng**, like **Grp** but unlike **Ring**, has [zero morphisms](wikipedia://en/320638). These are just the rng homomorphisms that map everything to 0. Despite the existence of zero morphisms, **Rng** is still not a [preadditive category](wikipedia://en/59521). The pointwise sum of two rng homomorphisms is generally not a rng homomorphism.

There is a fully faithful functor from the category of [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) to **Rng** sending an abelian group to the associated [rng of square zero](wikipedia://en/3405004#Rng_of_square_zero).

[Free constructions](wikipedia://en/333835) are less natural in **Rng** than they are in **Ring**. For example, the free rng generated by a set {*x*} is the ring of all integral polynomials over *x* with no constant term, while the free ring generated by {*x*} is just the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) **Z**\[*x*\].

:::
