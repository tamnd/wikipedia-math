# Goldman domain

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Goldman domain"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Goldman domain** or **G-domain** is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) *A* whose [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) is a finitely generated [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring) over *A*.[^o2] They are named after [Oscar Goldman](wikipedia://en/37060377).

:::

An [overring](wikipedia://en/24158853) (i.e., an intermediate ring lying between the ring and its field of fractions) of a Goldman domain is again a Goldman domain. There exists a Goldman domain where all [nonzero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) are [maximal](wikipedia://en/48164) although there are infinitely many prime ideals.[^o3]

An [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) *I* in a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *A* is called a **Goldman ideal** if the [quotient](wikipedia://en/184635) *A*/*I* is a Goldman domain. A Goldman ideal is thus prime, but not necessarily maximal. In fact, a commutative ring is a [Jacobson ring](wikipedia://en/4607512) if and only if every Goldman ideal in it is maximal.

The notion of a Goldman ideal can be used to give a slightly sharpened characterization of a [radical of an ideal](wikipedia://en/252009): the radical of an ideal *I* is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all Goldman ideals containing *I*.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Alternative definition"}

## Alternative definition {#Alternative_definition}

An [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) $D$ is a **G-domain** if and only if:

1. Its field of fractions is a [simple extension](wikipedia://en/4771726) of $D$
1. The intersection of its nonzero [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) (not to be confused with [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636)) is nonzero
1. There is a nonzero element $u$ such that for any nonzero ideal $I$, $u^n \in I$ for some $n$.[^o6]

A **G-ideal** is defined as an ideal $I \subset R$ such that $R/I$ is a G-domain. Since a [factor ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_ring) is an integral domain if and only if the ring is factored by a prime ideal, every G-ideal is also a prime ideal. G-ideals can be used as a refined collection of prime ideals in the following sense: the [radical of an ideal](wikipedia://en/252009) can be characterized as the intersection of all prime ideals containing the ideal, and in fact we still get the radical even if we take the intersection over the G-ideals.[^o7]

Every maximal ideal is a G-ideal, since quotient by maximal ideal is a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), and a field is trivially a G-domain. Therefore, maximal ideals are G-ideals, and G-ideals are prime ideals. G-ideals are the only maximal ideals in a [Jacobson ring](wikipedia://en/4607512), and in fact this is an equivalent characterization of Jacobson rings: a ring is a Jacobson ring when all G-ideals are maximal ideals. This leads to a simplified [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) of the [Nullstellensatz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullstellensatz).[^o8]

It is known that given $T \supset R$, a ring extension of a G-domain, $T$ is algebraic over $R$ if and only if every ring extension between $T$ and $R$ is a G-domain.[^o9]

A [Noetherian domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noetherian_domain) is a G-domain if and only if its [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) is at most one, and has only finitely many maximal ideals (or equivalently, prime ideals).[^o10]
