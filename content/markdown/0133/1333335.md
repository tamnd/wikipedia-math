# Open problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Open problem"}

In [science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **open problem** or an **open question** is a known problem which can be accurately stated, and which is assumed to have an objective and verifiable solution, but which has not yet been solved (i.e., no solution for it is known).

:::

In the [history of science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_science), some of these supposed open problems were "solved" by means of showing that they were not well-defined. In mathematics, many open problems are concerned with the question of whether a certain definition is or is not consistent.

Two notable examples in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) that have been solved and "closed" by researchers in the late twentieth century are [Fermat's Last Theorem](wikipedia://en/19021953)[^o2] and the [four-color theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-color_theorem).[^o3][^o4] An important open mathematics problem solved in the early [21st century](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/21st_century) was the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798).

Open problems exist in all scientific fields. For example, one of the most important open problems in biochemistry is the [protein structure prediction](wikipedia://en/306769) problem[^o5][^o6] – how to predict a [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein)'s structure from its sequence. In 2024, [David Baker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Baker_%28biochemist%29) and [Demis Hassabis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Demis_Hassabis) were awarded the [Nobel Prize in Chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Chemistry)[^o7] for their contributions to protein structure prediction.
