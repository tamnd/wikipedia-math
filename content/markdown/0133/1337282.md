# Topological property

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Topological property"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **topological property** or **topological invariant** is a property of a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) that is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660). Alternatively, a topological property is a [proper class](wikipedia://en/20696) of topological spaces which is closed under homeomorphisms. That is, a property of spaces is a topological property if whenever a space *X* possesses that property every space homeomorphic to *X* possesses that property. Informally, a topological property is a property of the space that can be expressed using [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358).

:::

A common problem in topology is to decide whether two topological spaces are [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) or not. To prove that two spaces are *not* homeomorphic, it is sufficient to find a topological property which is not shared by them.

## Properties of topological properties {#Properties_of_topological_properties}

A property $P$ is:

- **Hereditary**, if for every topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ and subset $S \subseteq X,$ the [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) $\left(S, \mathcal{T}|_S\right)$ has property $P.$
- **Weakly hereditary**, if for every topological space $(X, \mathcal{T})$ and closed subset $S \subseteq X,$ the subspace $\left(S, \mathcal{T}|_S\right)$ has property $P.$

## Common topological properties {#Common_topological_properties}

### Cardinal functions {#Cardinal_functions}

- The [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) $\vert X \vert$ of the space $X$.
- The cardinality $\vert \tau(X)\vert$ of the topology (the set of open subsets) of the space $X$.
- *Weight* $w(X)$, the least cardinality of a [basis of the topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28topology%29) of the space $X$.
- *Density* $d(X)$, the least cardinality of a subset of $X$ whose closure is $X$.

### Separation {#Separation}

Some of these terms are defined differently in older mathematical literature; see [history of the separation axioms](wikipedia://en/1481097).

- **T<sub>0</sub>** or **Kolmogorov**. A space is [Kolmogorov](wikipedia://en/68121) if for every pair of distinct points *x* and *y* in the space, there is at least either an open set containing *x* but not *y*, or an open set containing *y* but not *x*.
- **T<sub>1</sub>** or **Fréchet**. A space is [Fréchet](wikipedia://en/148721) if for every pair of distinct points *x* and *y* in the space, there is an open set containing *x* but not *y*. (Compare with T<sub>0</sub>; here, we are allowed to specify which point will be contained in the open set.) Equivalently, a space is T<sub>1</sub> if all its singletons are closed. T<sub>1</sub> spaces are always T<sub>0</sub>.
- **Sober**. A space is [sober](wikipedia://en/602490) if every irreducible closed set *C* has a unique generic point *p*. In other words, if *C* is not the (possibly nondisjoint) union of two smaller closed non-empty subsets, then there is a *p* such that the closure of {*p*} equals *C*, and *p* is the only point with this property.
- **T<sub>2</sub>** or **Hausdorff**. A space is [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) if every two distinct points have disjoint neighbourhoods. T<sub>2</sub> spaces are always T<sub>1</sub>.
- **T<sub>2½</sub>** or **Urysohn**. A space is [Urysohn](wikipedia://en/327166) if every two distinct points have disjoint *closed* neighbourhoods. T<sub>2½</sub> spaces are always T<sub>2</sub>.
- **Completely T<sub>2</sub>** or **completely Hausdorff**. A space is [completely T<sub>2</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_Hausdorff_space) if every two distinct points are [separated by a function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separated_by_a_function). Every completely Hausdorff space is Urysohn.
- **Regular**. A space is [regular](wikipedia://en/53058) if whenever *C* is a closed set and *p* is a point not in *C*, then *C* and *p* have disjoint neighbourhoods.
- **T<sub>3</sub>** or **Regular Hausdorff**. A space is [regular Hausdorff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_Hausdorff_space) if it is a regular T<sub>0</sub> space. (A regular space is Hausdorff if and only if it is T<sub>0</sub>, so the terminology is [consistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Consistent).)
- **Completely regular**. A space is [completely regular](wikipedia://en/30554) if whenever *C* is a closed set and *p* is a point not in *C*, then *C* and {*p*} are [separated by a function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separated_by_a_function).
- **T<sub>3½</sub>**, **Tychonoff**, **Completely regular Hausdorff** or **Completely T<sub>3</sub>**. A [Tychonoff space](wikipedia://en/30554) is a completely regular T<sub>0</sub> space. (A completely regular space is Hausdorff if and only if it is T<sub>0</sub>, so the terminology is consistent.) Tychonoff spaces are always regular Hausdorff.
- **Normal**. A space is [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) if any two disjoint closed sets have disjoint neighbourhoods. Normal spaces admit [partitions of unity](wikipedia://en/48636).
- **T<sub>4</sub>** or **Normal Hausdorff**. A normal space is Hausdorff if and only if it is T<sub>1</sub>. Normal Hausdorff spaces are always Tychonoff.
- **Completely normal**. A space is [completely normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Completely_normal) if any two [separated sets](wikipedia://en/148911) have disjoint neighbourhoods.
- **T<sub>5</sub>** or **Completely normal Hausdorff**. A completely normal space is Hausdorff if and only if it is T<sub>1</sub>. Completely normal Hausdorff spaces are always normal Hausdorff.
- **Perfectly normal**. A space is [perfectly normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfectly_normal_space) if any two disjoint closed sets are [precisely separated by a function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Precisely_separated_by_a_function). A perfectly normal space must also be completely normal.
- **T<sub>6</sub>** or **Perfectly normal Hausdorff**, or **perfectly T<sub>4</sub>**. A space is [perfectly normal Hausdorff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfectly_normal_Hausdorff_space), if it is both perfectly normal and T<sub>1</sub>. A perfectly normal Hausdorff space must also be completely normal Hausdorff.
- **Discrete space**. A space is [discrete](wikipedia://en/56061) if all of its points are completely isolated, i.e. if any subset is open.
- **Number of isolated points**. The number of [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557) of a topological space.

### Countability conditions {#Countability_conditions}

- **Separable**. A space is [separable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_%28topology%29) if it has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) dense subset.
- **First-countable**. A space is [first-countable](wikipedia://en/904940) if every point has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) local base.
- **Second-countable**. A space is [second-countable](wikipedia://en/901459) if it has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) base for its topology. Second-countable spaces are always separable, first-countable and Lindelöf.
- **Lindelöf**. A space is [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/904940) if every open cover has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) subcover.
- ***σ*-compact**. A space is [σ-compact](wikipedia://en/901459) if it is the union of [countably](wikipedia://en/6026) many [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [subspaces](wikipedia://en/910926).

### Connectedness {#Connectedness}

- **Connected**. A space is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) if it is not the union of a pair of disjoint non-empty open sets. Equivalently, a space is connected if the only [clopen sets](wikipedia://en/233398) are the empty set and itself.
- **Locally connected**. A space is [locally connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_connected) if every point has a local base consisting of connected sets.
- **Totally disconnected**. A space is [totally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_disconnected) if it has no connected subset with more than one point.
- **Extremally disconnected**. A space is [extremally disconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremally_disconnected) if every open set has an open closure.
- **Path-connected**. A space *X* is [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path-connected) if for every two points *x*, *y* in *X*, there is a path *p* from *x* to *y*, i.e., a continuous map *p*: \[0,1\] → *X* with *p*(0) = *x* and *p*(1) = *y*. Path-connected spaces are always connected.
- **Locally path-connected**. A space is [locally path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_path-connected) if every point has a local base consisting of path-connected sets. A locally path-connected space is connected if and only if it is path-connected.
- **Arc-connected**. A space *X* is [arc-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arc-connected) if for every two points *x*, *y* in *X*, there is an arc *f* from *x* to *y*, i.e., an [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) continuous map $f \colon [0,1]\to X$ with $p(0) = x$ and $p(1) = y$. Arc-connected spaces are path-connected.
- **Simply connected**. A space *X* is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) if it is path-connected and every continuous map $f \colon S^1 \to X$ is [homotopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopic) to a constant map.
- **Locally simply connected**. A space *X* is [locally simply connected](wikipedia://en/7541497) if every point *x* in *X* has a local base of neighborhoods *U* that is simply connected.
- **Semi-locally simply connected**. A space *X* is [semi-locally simply connected](wikipedia://en/518118) if every point has a local base of neighborhoods *U* such that *every* loop in *U* is contractible in *X*. Semi-local simple connectivity, a strictly weaker condition than local simple connectivity, is a necessary condition for the existence of a [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover).
- **Contractible**. A space *X* is [contractible](wikipedia://en/1752414) if the [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069) on *X* is homotopic to a constant map. Contractible spaces are always simply connected.
- **Hyperconnected**. A space is [hyperconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperconnected) if no two non-empty open sets are disjoint. Every hyperconnected space is connected.
- **Ultraconnected**. A space is [ultraconnected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ultraconnected) if no two non-empty closed sets are disjoint. Every ultraconnected space is path-connected.
- **Indiscrete** or **trivial**. A space is [indiscrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indiscrete_space) if the only open sets are the empty set and itself. Such a space is said to have the [trivial topology](wikipedia://en/382447).

### Compactness {#Compactness}

- **Compact**. A space is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) if every [open cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_cover) has a finite *subcover*. Some authors call these spaces **quasicompact** and reserve compact for [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) spaces where every open cover has finite subcover. Compact spaces are always Lindelöf and paracompact. Compact Hausdorff spaces are therefore normal.
- **Sequentially compact**. A space is [sequentially compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequentially_compact) if every sequence has a convergent subsequence.
- **Countably compact**. A space is [countably compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_compact) if every countable open cover has a finite subcover.
- **Pseudocompact**. A space is [pseudocompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudocompact) if every continuous real-valued function on the space is bounded.
- **σ-compact**. A space is [σ-compact](wikipedia://en/346681) if it is the union of countably many compact subsets.
- **Lindelöf**. A space is [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480) if every open cover has a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) subcover.
- **Paracompact**. A space is [paracompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paracompact) if every open cover has an open locally finite refinement. Paracompact Hausdorff spaces are normal.
- **Locally compact**. A space is [locally compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_compact) if every point has a local base consisting of compact neighbourhoods. Slightly different definitions are also used. Locally compact Hausdorff spaces are always Tychonoff.
- **Ultraconnected compact**. In an ultra-connected compact space *X* every open cover must contain *X* itself. Non-empty ultra-connected compact spaces have a largest proper open subset called a **monolith**.

### Metrizability {#Metrizability}

- **Metrizable**. A space is [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738) if it is homeomorphic to a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018). Metrizable spaces are always Hausdorff and paracompact (and hence normal and Tychonoff), and first-countable. Moreover, a topological space $(X,T)$ is said to be metrizable if there exists a metric for $X$ such that the metric topology $T(d)$ is identical with the topology $T.$
- **Polish**. A space is called [Polish](wikipedia://en/396622) if it is metrizable with a separable and complete metric.
- **Locally metrizable**. A space is locally metrizable if every point has a metrizable neighbourhood.

### Miscellaneous {#Miscellaneous}

- **Baire space**. A space *X* is a [Baire space](wikipedia://en/200550) if it is not [meagre](wikipedia://en/226975) in itself. Equivalently, *X* is a Baire space if the intersection of countably many dense open sets is dense.
- **Door space**. A topological space is a [door space](wikipedia://en/5075606) if every subset is open or closed (or both).
- **Topological Homogeneity**. A space *X* is (topologically) [homogeneous](wikipedia://en/363325) if for every *x* and *y* in *X* there is a homeomorphism $f \colon X \to X$ such that $f(x) = y.$ Intuitively speaking, this means that the space looks the same at every point. All [topological groups](wikipedia://en/42315) are homogeneous.
- **Finitely generated** or **Alexandrov**. A space *X* is [Alexandrov](wikipedia://en/542198) if arbitrary intersections of open sets in *X* are open, or equivalently if arbitrary unions of closed sets are closed. These are precisely the [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/38520847) members of the [category of topological spaces](wikipedia://en/435051) and continuous maps.
- **Zero-dimensional**. A space is [zero-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-dimensional) if it has a base of clopen sets. These are precisely the spaces with a small [inductive dimension](wikipedia://en/3173612) of *0*.
- **Almost discrete**. A space is [almost discrete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_discrete) if every open set is closed (hence clopen). The almost discrete spaces are precisely the finitely generated zero-dimensional spaces.
- **Boolean**. A space is [Boolean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_space) if it is zero-dimensional, compact and Hausdorff (equivalently, totally disconnected, compact and Hausdorff). These spaces correspond to [Boolean algebras](wikipedia://en/3959) via [Stone's representation theorem](wikipedia://en/604111).
- **[Reidemeister torsion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reidemeister_torsion)**
- **$\kappa$-resolvable**. A space is said to be κ-resolvable[^o11] (respectively: almost κ-resolvable) if it contains κ dense sets that are pairwise disjoint (respectively: almost disjoint over the ideal of nowhere dense subsets). If the space is not $\kappa$-resolvable then it is called $\kappa$-irresolvable.
- **Maximally resolvable**. Space $X$ is maximally resolvable if it is $\Delta(X)$-resolvable, where $\Delta(X) = \min\{|G| : G\neq \varnothing, G\mbox{ is open}\}.$ Number $\Delta(X)$ is called dispersion character of $X.$
- **Strongly discrete**. Set $D$ is strongly discrete subset of the space $X$ if the points in $D$ may be separated by pairwise disjoint neighborhoods. Space $X$ is said to be strongly discrete if every non-isolated point of $X$ is the [accumulation point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_point) of some strongly discrete set.

## Non-topological properties {#Non-topological_properties}

There are many examples of properties of [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018), etc, which are not topological properties. To show a property $P$ is not topological, it is sufficient to find two homeomorphic topological spaces $X \cong Y$ such that $X$ has $P$, but $Y$ does not have $P$.

For example, the metric space properties of [boundedness](wikipedia://en/20018#Bounded_and_totally_bounded_spaces) and [completeness](wikipedia://en/7832) are not topological properties. Let $X = \mathbb{R}$ and $Y = (-\tfrac{\pi}{2},\tfrac{\pi}{2})$ be metric spaces with the standard metric. Then, $X \cong Y$ via the homeomorphism $\operatorname{arctan}\colon X \to Y$. However, $X$ is complete but not bounded, while $Y$ is bounded but not complete.
