# Banach measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Banach measure"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) discipline of [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), a **Banach measure** is a certain way to assign a size (or area) to all subsets of the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), consistent with but extending the commonly used [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198). While there are certain [subsets of the plane which are not Lebesgue measurable](wikipedia://en/620134), all subsets of the plane have a Banach measure. On the other hand, the Lebesgue measure is [countably additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_additive) while a Banach measure is only finitely additive (and is therefore known as a "[content](wikipedia://en/9999200)").

:::

[Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) proved the existence of Banach measures in 1923.[^o2] This established in particular that paradoxical decompositions as provided by the [Banach-Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220) in Euclidean space **R**<sup>3</sup> cannot exist in the Euclidean plane **R**<sup>2</sup>.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A Banach measure[^o5] on **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is a function $\mu: {\mathcal P}(\mathbb{R}^n)\to [0,\infty]$ (assigning a non-negative [extended real number](wikipedia://en/51698) to each subset of **R**<sup>*n*</sup>) such that

- $\mu$ is finitely additive, i.e. $\mu(A \cup B) = \mu(A)+ \mu(B)$ for any two [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) sets $A,B\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$;
- $\mu$ extends the Lebesgue measure $\lambda$, i.e. $\mu(A)=\lambda(A)$ for every Lebesgue-measurable set $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$;
- $\mu$ is invariant under [isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometries) of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> , i.e. $\mu(A)=\mu(f(A))$ for every $A\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$ and every isometry $f : \mathbb{R}^n\to\mathbb{R}^n$.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The finite additivity of $\mu$ implies that $\mu(\varnothing) = 0$ and $\mu(A_1 \cup \cdots \cup A_k) = \sum_{i=1}^k\mu(A_i)$ for any pairwise disjoint sets $A_1,\ldots,A_k\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$. We also have $\mu(A)\leq\mu(B)$ whenever $A\subseteq B\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.

Since $\mu$ extends Lebesgue measure, we know that $\mu(A)=0$ whenever *A* is a finite or a countable set and that $\mu([a_1,b_1]\times \cdots \times [a_n,b_n]) =(b_1-a_1)\cdots(b_n-a_n)$ for any product of [intervals](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a_1,b_1]\times \cdots \times [a_n,b_n]\subseteq \mathbb{R}^n$.

Since $\mu$ is invariant under isometries, it is in particular invariant under rotations and translations.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement cue=heading name="Results"}

## Results {#Results}

Stefan Banach showed that Banach measures exist on **R**<sup>1</sup> and on **R**<sup>2</sup>. These results can be derived from the fact that the groups of isometries of **R**<sup>1</sup> and of **R**<sup>2</sup> are [solvable](wikipedia://en/105012).

The existence of these measures proves the impossibility of a [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220) in one or two dimensions: it is not possible to decompose a one- or two-dimensional set of finite Lebesgue measure into finitely many sets that can be reassembled into a set with a different Lebesgue measure, because this would violate the properties of the Banach measure that extends the Lebesgue measure.[^o10]

Conversely, the existence of the Banach-Tarski paradox in all dimensions *n ≥ 3* shows that no Banach measure can exist in these dimensions.

As [Vitali's paradox](wikipedia://en/424189) shows, Banach measures cannot be strengthened to countably additive ones: there exist subsets of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> that are not Lebesgue measurable, for all *n ≥ 1*.

Most of these results depend on some form of the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840). Using only the axioms of [Zermelo-Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) without the axiom of choice, it is not possible to derive the Banach-Tarski paradox, nor it is possible to prove the existence of sets that are not Lebesgue-measurable (the latter claim depends on a fairly weak and widely believed assumption, namely that the existence of [inaccessible cardinals](wikipedia://en/248076) is consistent). The existence of Banach measures on **R**<sup>1</sup> and on **R**<sup>2</sup> can also not be proven in the absence of the axiom of choice.[^o11] In particular, no concrete formula for these Banach measures can be given.
