# Peter Borwein

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Peter Borwein"}

**Peter Benjamin Borwein** (born [St. Andrews](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Andrews), [Scotland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scotland), May 10, 1953 – 23 August 2020) was a [Canadian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canadians) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and a professor at [Simon Fraser University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Fraser_University). He is known as a co-author of the paper which presented the [Bailey–Borwein–Plouffe algorithm](wikipedia://en/22684956) (discovered by [Simon Plouffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Plouffe)) for computing [π](wikipedia://en/23601).

:::

## First interest in mathematics {#First_interest_in_mathematics}

Borwein was born into a Jewish family. He became interested in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) and classical analysis during his second year of university. He had not previously been interested in math, although his father was the head of the [University of Western Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Western_Ontario)'s mathematics department and his mother is associate dean of medicine there. Borwein and his two siblings majored in mathematics.

## Academic career {#Academic_career}

After completing a Bachelor of Science in Honours Math at the University of Western Ontario in 1974, he went on to complete an [MSc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master's_degree) and [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) at the [University of British Columbia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_British_Columbia). He joined the Department of Mathematics at [Dalhousie University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dalhousie_University). While he was there, he, his brother [Jonathan Borwein](wikipedia://en/1145010) and [David H. Bailey](wikipedia://en/184462) of [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA) wrote the 1989 paper[^o4] that outlined and popularized a proof for computing one billion digits of π. The authors won the 1993 [Chauvenet Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chauvenet_Prize) and [Merten M. Hasse Prize](wikipedia://en/59046844) for this paper.

In 1993, he moved to Simon Fraser University, joining his brother Jonathan in establishing the [Centre for Experimental and Constructive Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_for_Experimental_and_Constructive_Mathematics) (CECM) where he developed the [Inverse Symbolic Calculator](wikipedia://en/9087019).

## Research {#Research}

In 1995, the Borweins collaborated with [Yasumasa Kanada](wikipedia://en/561940) of the [University of Tokyo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Tokyo) to compute [π](wikipedia://en/23601) to more than four billion digits.

Borwein has developed an algorithm that applies [Chebyshev polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev_polynomial) to the [Dirichlet eta function](wikipedia://en/447035) to produce a [very rapidly convergent series suitable for high precision numerical calculations](wikipedia://en/447035#Borwein's_method), which he published on the occasion of the awarding of an honorary doctorate to his brother, Jonathan.[^o6]

Peter Borwein also collaborated with [NASA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NASA)'s David Bailey and the [Université du Québec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Université_du_Québec)'s [Simon Plouffe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Plouffe) to calculate the individual [hexadecimal](wikipedia://en/13263) digits of π. This provided a way for mathematicians to determine the *n*th digit of π without calculating preceding digits. In 2007 with [Tamás Erdélyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamás_Erdélyi_%28mathematician%29), Ronald Ferguson, and Richard Lockhart he settled [Littlewood](wikipedia://en/158371)'s Problem 22.[^o7]

## Affiliations {#Affiliations}

A former professor at [Simon Fraser University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Fraser_University), Peter Borwein was affiliated with [Interdisciplinary Research in the Mathematical and Computational Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interdisciplinary_Research_in_the_Mathematical_and_Computational_Sciences) (IRMACS), Centre for Experimental and Constructive Mathematics (CECM), [Mathematics of Information Technology and Complex Systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mitacs) (MITACS), and [Pacific Institute for the Mathematical Sciences](wikipedia://en/34978739) (PIMS).

## Personal life and death {#Personal_life_and_death}

Borwein was diagnosed with [multiple sclerosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple_sclerosis) prior to 2000. He died on 23 August 2020 of [pneumonia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pneumonia) as a result of his MS.[^o10]

## Publications {#Publications}

As a co-author, Borwein has written *Pi: A Source Book* (with [Lennart Berggren](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lennart_Berggren) and Jonathan Borwein, 2000), *Polynomials and Polynomial Inequalities* (with [Tamas Erdelyi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamas_Erdelyi_%28mathematician%29), 1998), *Pi and the AGM* (1987; reprinted in 1998), *A Dictionary of Real Numbers* (with Jonathan Borwein), *Computational Excursions in Analysis and Number Theory* (2002), *The Riemann Hypothesis: A Resource for the Afficionado and Virtuoso Alike* (with Stephen Choi, Brendan Rooney, and Andrea Weirathmueller, 2007). He and his brother, Jonathan, co-edited the [Canadian Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/906055)/[Springer-Verlag](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Springer_Science+Business_Media) series of *Books in Mathematics*. In 2002 Peter Borwein, with Loki Jorgenson, won a [Lester R. Ford Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lester_R._Ford_Award) for their expository article *Visible Structures in Number Theory*.[^o12]
