# Category algebra

This page discusses the object called a **category algebra**; for categorical generalizations of algebra theory, see .

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Category algebra"}

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a field of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **category algebra** is an [associative algebra](wikipedia://en/2112), defined for any locally finite [category](wikipedia://en/53759) and [commutative ring with unity](wikipedia://en/61346). Category algebras generalize the notions of [group algebras](wikipedia://en/349223) and [incidence algebras](wikipedia://en/166980), just as [categories](wikipedia://en/53759) generalize the notions of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) and [partially ordered sets](wikipedia://en/23572).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

If the given category is finite (has finitely many [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29) and [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891)), then the following two definitions of the category algebra agree.

### Group algebra-style definition {#Group_algebra-style_definition}

Given a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) *G* and a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) *R*, one can construct *RG*, known as the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223); it is an *R*-[module](wikipedia://en/276410) equipped with a multiplication. A group is the same as a category with a single object in which all morphisms are [isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/14828) (where the elements of the group correspond to the morphisms of the category), so the following construction generalizes the definition of the group algebra from groups to arbitrary categories.

Let *C* be a category and *R* be a commutative ring with unity. Define *RC* (or *R*\[*C*\]) to be the [free *R*-module](wikipedia://en/326454) with the set $\operatorname{Hom}C$ of morphisms of *C* as its [basis](wikipedia://en/18420#Free_module). In other words, *RC* consists of formal [linear combinations](wikipedia://en/55632) (which are finite sums) of the form $\sum a_i f_i$, where *f<sub>i</sub>* are morphisms of *C*, and *a<sub>i</sub>* are elements of the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) *R*. Define a multiplication operation on *RC* as follows, using the composition operation in the category:

$$\sum a_i f_i \sum b_j g_j = \sum a_i b_j f_i g_j$$

where $f_i g_j=0$ if their composition is not defined. This defines a binary operation on *RC*, and moreover makes *RC* into an associative [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring) over the ring *R*. This algebra is called the **category algebra** of *C*.

From a different perspective, elements of the free module *RC* could also be considered as functions from the morphisms of *C* to *R* which are [finitely supported](wikipedia://en/381013#Formulation). Then the multiplication is described by a [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519): if $a, b \in RC$ (thought of as functionals on the morphisms of *C*), then their product is defined as:

$$(a * b)(h) := \sum_{fg=h} a(f)b(g).$$

The latter sum is finite because the functions are finitely supported, and therefore $a * b \in RC$.

### Incidence algebra-style definition {#Incidence_algebra-style_definition}

The definition used for incidence algebras assumes that the category *C* is locally finite (see below), is *dual* to the above definition, and defines a *different* object. This isn't a useful assumption for groups, as a group that is locally finite as a category is [finite](wikipedia://en/323707).

A **locally finite category** is one where every morphism can be written in only finitely many ways as the composition of two non-identity morphisms (not to be confused with the "has finite [Hom-sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hom-set)" meaning). The category algebra (in this sense) is defined as above, but allowing all coefficients to be non-zero.

In terms of formal sums, the elements are all formal sums

$$\sum_{f_i \in \operatorname{Hom}C} a_i f_i,$$

where there are no restrictions on the $a_i$ (they can all be non-zero).

In terms of functions, the elements are any functions from the morphisms of *C* to *R*, and multiplication is defined as convolution. The sum in the convolution is always finite because of the local finiteness assumption.

### Dual {#Dual}

The module dual of the category algebra (in the group algebra sense of the definition) is the space of all maps from the morphisms of *C* to *R*, denoted *F*(*C*), and has a natural [coalgebra](wikipedia://en/310886) structure. Thus for a locally finite category, the dual of a category algebra (in the group algebra sense) is the category algebra (in the incidence algebra sense), and has both an algebra and coalgebra structure.

## Examples {#Examples}

- If *C* is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) (thought of as a [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) with a single object), then *RC* is the [group algebra](wikipedia://en/349223).
- If *C* is a [monoid](wikipedia://en/19652) (thought of as a category with a single object), then *RC* is the [monoid ring](wikipedia://en/20781).
- If *C* is a [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572), then (using the appropriate definition), *RC* is the [incidence algebra](wikipedia://en/166980).
- While partial orders only allow for viewing upper or lower [triangular matrices](wikipedia://en/374222) as incidence algebras, the concept of category algebras also encompasses the [ring of matrices](wikipedia://en/487541) of *R*. Indeed, if *C* is the [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582) on *n* points where every point has a relation to every other (a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816)), then *RC* is the matrix ring $R^{n \times n}$.
- If *C* is a [discrete category](wikipedia://en/808519), then *RC* may be seen as the ring of functions $C \rightarrow R$ with pointwise addition and multiplication, or equivalently the [direct product](wikipedia://en/8887) of copies of *R* indexed over *C*. In the case of infinite *C*, one needs to distinguish the "group algebra-style" and the "incidence algebra-style", because in the former, one only allows for finitely many terms in the formal linear combination, resulting in *RC* being instead the [direct sum](wikipedia://en/2084687) of copies of *R*.
- The [path algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_algebra) of a [quiver](wikipedia://en/355100) Q is the category algebra of the [free category](wikipedia://en/36769284) on Q.
