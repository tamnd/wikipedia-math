# Socolar–Taylor tile

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Socolar–Taylor tile"}

*Image: Socolar-Taylor tiling.svg.* A patch of 25 monotiles, showing the triangular hierarchical structure

The **Socolar–Taylor tile** is a single non-connected [tile](wikipedia://en/321671) which is aperiodic on the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), meaning that it admits only [non-periodic tilings](wikipedia://en/868145) of the plane (due to the [Sierpinski's triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski's_triangle)-like tiling that occurs), with rotations and reflections of the tile allowed.[^o2] It is the first known example of a single aperiodic tile, or "[einstein](wikipedia://en/43542926)".[^o3] The basic version of the tile is a simple hexagon, with printed designs to enforce a local matching rule, regarding how the tiles may be placed.[^o4] One of their papers[^o3] shows a realization of the tile as a [connected set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_set). It is currently unknown whether this rule may be geometrically implemented in two dimensions while keeping the tile a [simply connected set](wikipedia://en/523879).[^o3][^o4]

:::

This is, however, confirmed to be possible in three dimensions, and, in their original paper, Socolar and Taylor suggest a three-dimensional analogue to the monotile.[^o2] Taylor and Socolar remark that the 3D monotile aperiodically tiles three-dimensional space. However the tile does allow tilings with a period, shifting one (non-periodic) two dimensional layer to the next, and so the tile is only "weakly aperiodic".

Physical copies of the three-dimensional tile could not be fitted together without allowing reflections, which would require access to four-dimensional space.[^o3][^o5]
