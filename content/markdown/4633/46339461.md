# Maximal lotteries

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Maximal lotteries"}

**Maximal lotteries** are a [probabilistic voting rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_social_choice) that use [ranked ballots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preferential_ballot) and returns a [lottery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lottery_%28decision_theory%29) over candidates that a majority of voters will prefer, in expectation, to any other. More formally, the rule has the property that, when averaging over a series of repeated head-to-head matchups, at least half of all voters will prefer the result of a maximal lottery to the result produced by any other [voting rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voting_rule).[^o2]

:::

Maximal lotteries satisfy a wide range of desirable properties: they elect the [Condorcet winner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condorcet_winner_criterion) with probability 1 if it exists[^o2] and never elect candidates outside the [Smith set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith_criterion).[^o2] Moreover, they satisfy [independence of clones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independence_of_clones).[^o6] The probabilistic voting rule that returns all maximal lotteries is the only rule satisfying reinforcement, Condorcet-consistency, and independence of clones.[^o6] The [social welfare function](wikipedia://en/217104) that top-ranks maximal lotteries has been uniquely characterized using Arrow's [independence of irrelevant alternatives](wikipedia://en/259105) and [Pareto efficiency](wikipedia://en/45468).[^o3]

Maximal lotteries do not satisfy the standard notion of strategyproofness, as [Allan Gibbard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allan_Gibbard) has shown that only [random dictatorships](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dictatorship_mechanism) can satisfy strategyproofness and ex post efficiency.[^o4] Maximal lotteries are also [nonmonotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonicity_criterion) in probabilities, meaning it is possible for the probability of an alternative to decrease if this alternative up.[^o2] However, they satisfy relative monotonicity, the probability of $x$ relative to that of $y$ does not decrease when $x$ is improved over $y$.[^o5]

The maximal lottery satisfies some generalizations of [reinforcement](wikipedia://en/1792861)[^o6] and [participation](wikipedia://en/1133956)[^o7] to the non-deterministic case: the [probability of superiority](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_of_superiority) exceeds 50%, meaning a voter will prefer the result they get by participating to the one they get by not participating more than half the time.[^o7] However, a maximal lottery can still exhibit a no-show paradox in the [expected utility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_utility) sense, so a voter feels the lottery they get by voting is worse (has lower expected utility) than the lottery they would receive if they had not turned out.

The [support](wikipedia://en/381013) of maximal lotteries, which is known as the *essential set* or the *bipartisan set*, has been studied in detail.[^o8][^o9][^o10][^o11]

## History {#History}

Maximal lotteries were first proposed by the French mathematician and social scientist Germain Kreweras in 1965[^o13] and popularized by [Peter Fishburn](wikipedia://en/1787186).[^o2] Since then, they have been rediscovered multiple times by economists,[^o9] mathematicians,[^o2][^o14] political scientists, philosophers,[^o15] and computer scientists.[^o16]

Several natural dynamics that converge to maximal lotteries have been observed in biology, physics, chemistry, and machine learning.[^o17][^o18][^o19]

## Collective preferences over lotteries {#Collective_preferences_over_lotteries}

The input to this voting system consists of the agents' ordinal preferences over outcomes (not lotteries over alternatives), but a relation on the set of lotteries can be constructed in the following way: if $p$ and $q$ are lotteries over alternatives, $p\succ q$ if the expected value of the margin of victory of an outcome selected with distribution $p$ in a head-to-head vote against an outcome selected with distribution $q$ is positive. In other words, $p\succ q$ if it is more likely that a randomly selected voter will prefer the alternatives sampled from $p$ to the alternative sampled from $q$ than vice versa.[^o3] While this relation is not necessarily transitive, it does always admit at least one maximal element.

It is possible that several such maximal lotteries exist, as a result of ties. However, the maximal lottery is unique whenever the number of voters is odd.[^o21] By the same argument, the bipartisan set is uniquely defined by taking the support of the unique maximal lottery that solves a tournament game.[^o9]

## Strategic interpretation {#Strategic_interpretation}

Maximal lotteries are equivalent to mixed [maximin strategies](wikipedia://en/19589) (or [Nash equilibria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibria)) of the symmetric [zero-sum game](wikipedia://en/34417) given by the pairwise majority margins. As such, they have a natural interpretation in terms of electoral competition between two political parties[^o23] and can be computed in polynomial time via linear programming.

## Example {#Example}

Suppose there are five voters who have the following preferences over three alternatives:

- 2 voters: $a\succ b\succ c$
- 2 voters: $b\succ c\succ a$
- 1 voter: $c\succ a\succ b$

The pairwise preferences of the voters can be represented in the following [skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055), where the entry for row $x$ and column $y$ denotes the number of voters who prefer $x$ to $y$ minus the number of voters who prefer $y$ to $x$.

$$\begin{matrix} \begin{matrix} & & a\quad & b\quad & c\quad \\ \end{matrix} \\ \begin{matrix} a\\ b\\ c\\ \end{matrix} \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1 & -1\\ -1 & 0 & 3\\ 1 & -3 & 0\\ \end{pmatrix} \end{matrix}$$

This matrix can be interpreted as a [zero-sum game](wikipedia://en/34417) and admits a unique [Nash equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45337) (or [minimax strategy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimax_strategy)) $p$ where $p(a)=3/5$, $p(b)=1/5$, $p(c)=1/5$. By definition, this is also the unique maximal lottery of the preference profile above. The example was carefully chosen not to have a [Condorcet winner](wikipedia://en/1133911). Many preference profiles admit a Condorcet winner, in which case the unique maximal lottery will assign probability 1 to the Condorcet winner. If the last voter in the example above swaps alternatives $a$ and $c$ in his preference relation, $a$ becomes the Condorcet winner and will be selected with probability 1.

:::
