# Prym variety

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Prym variety"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Prym variety** construction (named for [Friedrich Prym](wikipedia://en/34925052)) is a method in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) of making an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) from a [morphism](wikipedia://en/24457573) of [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260). In its original form, it was applied to an unramified [double covering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_cover_%28topology%29) of a [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181), and was used by [F. Schottky](wikipedia://en/5872505) and [H. W. E. Jung](wikipedia://en/37050659) in relation with the [Schottky problem](wikipedia://en/4125909), as it is now called, of characterising [Jacobian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_varieties) among abelian varieties. It is said to have appeared first in the late work of [Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980), and was extensively studied by [Wirtinger](wikipedia://en/2044921) in 1895, including degenerate cases.

:::

Given a non-constant morphism

φ: *C*<sub>1</sub> → *C*<sub>2</sub>

of algebraic curves, write *J*<sub>*i*</sub> for the Jacobian variety of *C*<sub>*i*</sub>. Then from φ construct the corresponding morphism

ψ: *J*<sub>1</sub> → *J*<sub>2</sub>,

which can be defined on a divisor class *D* of degree zero by applying φ to each point of the divisor. This is a well-defined morphism, often called the *norm homomorphism*. Then the Prym variety of φ is the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of ψ. To qualify that somewhat, to get an abelian *variety*, the [connected component of the identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_of_the_identity) of the [reduced scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_scheme) underlying the [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28scheme_theory%29) may be intended. Or in other words take the largest abelian subvariety of *J*<sub>1</sub> on which ψ is trivial.

The theory of Prym varieties was dormant for a long time, until revived by [David Mumford](wikipedia://en/394594) around 1970. It now plays a substantial role in some contemporary theories, for example of the [Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation](wikipedia://en/4211219). One advantage of the method is that it allows one to apply the theory of curves to the study of a wider class of abelian varieties than Jacobians. For example, [principally polarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principally_polarized) abelian varieties (p.p.a.v.'s) of dimension > 3 are not generally Jacobians, but all p.p.a.v.'s of dimension 5 or less are Prym varieties. It is for this reason that p.p.a.v.'s are fairly well understood up to dimension 5.
