# Countably compact space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Countably compact space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is called **countably compact** if every countable open cover has a finite subcover.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Equivalent definitions"}

## Equivalent definitions {#Equivalent_definitions}

A topological space *X* is called **countably compact** if it satisfies any of the following equivalent conditions: [^o4][^o5]

(1) Every countable open cover of *X* has a finite subcover.

(2) Every infinite *set* *A* in *X* has an [ω-accumulation point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ω-accumulation_point) in *X*.

(3) Every *sequence* in *X* has an [accumulation point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accumulation_point_%28sequence%29) in *X*.

(4) Every countable family of closed subsets of *X* with an empty intersection has a finite subfamily with an empty intersection.

::: {#o6 .proof cue=collapse name="Proof of equivalence"}

**(1) $\Rightarrow$ (2):** Suppose (1) holds and *A* is an infinite subset of *X* without $\omega$-accumulation point. By taking a subset of *A* if necessary, we can assume that *A* is countable. Every $x\in X$ has an open neighbourhood $O_x$ such that $O_x\cap A$ is finite (possibly empty), since *x* is *not* an ω-accumulation point. For every finite subset *F* of *A* define $O_F = \cup\{O_x: O_x\cap A=F\}$. Every $O_x$ is a subset of one of the $O_F$, so the $O_F$ cover *X*. Since there are countably many of them, the $O_F$ form a countable open cover of *X*. But every $O_F$ intersect *A* in a finite subset (namely *F*), so finitely many of them cannot cover *A*, let alone *X*. This contradiction proves (2).

**(2) $\Rightarrow$ (3):** Suppose (2) holds, and let $(x_n)_n$ be a sequence in *X*. If the sequence has a value *x* that occurs infinitely many times, that value is an [accumulation point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accumulation_point_%28sequence%29) of the sequence. Otherwise, every value in the sequence occurs only finitely many times and the set $A=\{x_n: n\in\mathbb N\}$ is infinite and so has an [ω-accumulation point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ω-accumulation_point) *x*. That *x* is then an accumulation point of the sequence, as is easily checked.

**(3) $\Rightarrow$ (1):** Suppose (3) holds and $\{O_n: n\in\mathbb N\}$ is a countable open cover without a finite subcover. Then for each $n$ we can choose a point $x_n\in X$ that is *not* in $\cup_{i=1}^n O_i$. The sequence $(x_n)_n$ has an accumulation point *x* and that *x* is in some $O_k$. But then $O_k$ is a neighborhood of *x* that does not contain any of the $x_n$ with $n>k$, so *x* is not an accumulation point of the sequence after all. This contradiction proves (1).

**(4) $\Leftrightarrow$ (1):** Conditions (1) and (4) are easily seen to be equivalent by taking complements.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [first uncountable ordinal](wikipedia://en/20860540) (with the [order topology](wikipedia://en/213650)) is an example of a countably compact space that is not compact.[^n1]

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- Every [compact space](wikipedia://en/6042) is countably compact.
- A countably compact space is compact if and only if it is [Lindelöf](wikipedia://en/310480).
- Every countably compact space is [limit point compact](wikipedia://en/9861462).
- For [T1 spaces](wikipedia://en/148721), countable compactness and limit point compactness are equivalent.
- Every [sequentially compact space](wikipedia://en/8293820) is countably compact.[^o12] The converse does not hold. For example, the product of [continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901)-many closed intervals $[0,1]$ with the product topology is compact and hence countably compact; but it is not sequentially compact.[^o13]
- For [first-countable spaces](wikipedia://en/904940), countable compactness and sequential compactness are equivalent.[^o14] More generally, the same holds for [sequential spaces](wikipedia://en/4668395).[^o15]
- For [metrizable spaces](wikipedia://en/19738), countable compactness, sequential compactness, limit point compactness and compactness are all equivalent. The same holds for [second countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countable) [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) spaces.
- The example of the set of all real numbers with the [standard topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_topology) shows that neither [local compactness](wikipedia://en/48632) nor [σ-compactness](wikipedia://en/346681) nor [paracompactness](wikipedia://en/48631) imply countable compactness.
- Closed subspaces of a countably compact space are countably compact.[^o16]
- The continuous image of a countably compact space is countably compact.[^o17]
- Every countably compact space is [pseudocompact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudocompact).
- In a countably compact space, every locally finite family of nonempty subsets is finite.[^n2][^o20]
- Every countably compact [paracompact space](wikipedia://en/48631) is compact.[^n3][^o20] More generally, every countably compact [metacompact space](wikipedia://en/5074872) is compact.[^n4]
- Every countably compact [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [first-countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-countable) space is [regular](wikipedia://en/53058).[^o22][^o23]
- Every normal countably compact space is [collectionwise normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collectionwise_normal).
- The product of a compact space and a countably compact space is countably compact.[^o24][^o25]
- The product of two countably compact spaces need not be countably compact.[^o26]
