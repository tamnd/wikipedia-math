# Bhāskara I

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bhāskara I"}

**Bhāskara I** (c. 600) was a 7th-century Indian mathematician and [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) of the classical *Siddhānta* school of *Jyotiṣa* (astronomy and applied mathematics—one of the six *Vedāngas* or auxillary disciplines of Vedic scholarship). He was the first to write [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690) in the [Hindu–Arabic decimal system](wikipedia://en/3393371)[^o3] with a circle for the [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29), and who gave a unique and remarkable rational [approximation](wikipedia://en/336271) of the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) function (*jyā*) in his commentary on [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204)'s work.[^o4] This commentary, *Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya*, written in 629, is among the oldest known prose works in [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) on [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) to be completely extant today. He also wrote two astronomical works in the line of Aryabhata's school: the *Mahābhāskarīya* ("Great Book of Bhāskara") and the *Laghubhāskarīya* ("Small Book of Bhāskara").[^o4][^o5]

:::

On 7 June 1979, the [Indian Space Research Organisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISRO) launched the [Bhāskara I satellite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhaskara_%28satellite%29), named in honour of the mathematician.[^o6]

## Biography {#Biography}

Little is known about Bhāskara's life, except for what can be deduced from his writings. He was born in India in the 7th century, and was probably an [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer).[^o8] Bhāskara I received his [astronomical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy) education from his father.

There are references to places in India in Bhāskara's writings, such as [Vallabhi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vallabhi) (the capital of the [Maitraka dynasty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maitraka_dynasty) in the 7th century) and Sivarajapura, both of which are in the [Saurastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saurashtra_%28region%29) region of the present-day state of [Gujarat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gujarat) in India. Also mentioned are [Bharuch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bharuch) in southern Gujarat, and [Thanesar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thanesar) in the eastern Punjab, which was ruled by [Harsha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harsha). Therefore, a reasonable guess would be that Bhāskara was born in [Saurastra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saurashtra_%28region%29) and later moved to [Aśmaka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aśmaka).[^o9][^o10]

Bhāskara I is considered the most important scholar of [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204)'s astronomical school. He and [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) are two of the most renowned Indian mathematicians; both made considerable contributions to the study of fractions.

## Representation of numbers {#Representation_of_numbers}

The most important mathematical contribution of Bhāskara I concerns the representation of numbers in a [positional numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positional_numeral_system). Before Bhāskara I, astronomers and mathematicians of India used the *[Bhūtasaṅkhyā](wikipedia://en/26551950)* numerical system of concrete word-numerals for representing numbers in Sanskrit literature.[^o12] In this system, specific objects or philosophical concepts were associated with specific numerals. For example, *śaśin* meaning moon (represented the number 1), *netra* (eyes), or *aśvin* (twins), denoted the number 2, while *bhūta* (as in *pancha-bhūta,* 5 elements) or *indirya* (5 senses), represented number 5. According to classical Indian mathematical rule *aṅkānāṁ vāmato gatiḥ* (numerals proceed from right to left), digits are mentioned in the increasing order of tens, evidencing the [decimal place value system](wikipedia://en/8214).[^o13]

In his *Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya*, Bhāskara I made combined use of poetic and technical arithmetics frequently in denoting numeral figures, noting *aṅkairapi* (in figure also it is written). He recorded these numbers from left to right using nine [Brahmi numerals](wikipedia://en/1903587). He used a small circle (*śūnya-bindu*) to represent zero.

## Further contributions {#Further_contributions}

### Mathematics {#Mathematics}

Bhāskara I wrote three astronomical contributions. In 629, he annotated the *[Āryabhaṭīya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Āryabhaṭīya)*, an astronomical treatise by [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204) written in verses. Bhāskara's comments referred exactly to the 33 verses dealing with mathematics, in which he considered variable equations and trigonometric formulae. In general, he emphasized proving mathematical rules instead of simply relying on tradition or expediency.[^o4]

His work *Mahābhāskarīya* is divided into eight chapters about mathematical astronomy. In chapter 7, he gives [a remarkable approximation formula for sin x](wikipedia://en/27060913):

$$\sin x \approx \frac{16x (\pi - x)}{5 \pi^2 - 4x (\pi - x)}, \qquad (0 \leq x \leq \pi )$$

which he assigns to Aryabhata. It reveals a relative error of less than 1.9% (the greatest deviation $\frac{16}{5\pi} - 1 \approx 1.859\%$ at $x=0$). Additionally, he gives relations between sine and cosine, as well as relations between the sine of an angle less than 90° and the sines of angles 90°–180°, 180°–270°, and greater than 270°.

#### Indeterminate equations (Kuṭṭaka) and mathematical proof {#Indeterminate_equations_(Kuṭṭaka)_and_mathematical_proof}

Bhāskara's commentary on *Gaṇitapāda* is the first known work of *[Kuṭṭaka](wikipedia://en/49669987)* (the pulverizer), which was—an algorithmic method—proposed by Āryabhata as a systemic approach for solving linear indeterminate equations in first degree, like $ax+c=by$ (where $a, b$ and $c$ are integers).[^o17] It can be effectively identified with the [Euclidean algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) division and [continued fractions](wikipedia://en/739199). This was utilized in Indian astronomy to calculate planetary revolutions and conjunctions in cosmic cycles (*yugas*).[^o18]

In his remarks, Bhāskara emphasizes the role of mathematical reasoning and demonstration (*upapatti*), affirming that rules must not be accepted solely based on traditions. They must be accepted only if there is evidence or verification (*hetu*). To demonstrate the algorithm's practical use, he provided several applied problems involving fractions of days (*adhimāsa*) and celestial revolutions.[^o19]

In indeterminate analysis, Bhāskara I focussed on linear indeterminate equations ($ax+c=by$), by providing commentary and solutions using the *[Kuṭṭaka](wikipedia://en/49669987)* algorithm on Āryabhata's verses. Quadratic indeterminate equations of the form $Nx^2+1=y^2$, (*Vargaprakṛti*), historically misattributed in Western literature as [Pell's equations](wikipedia://en/24877),[^o20] were not handled by Bhāskara I. They were treated by his contemporary [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) and later expanded by [Bhāskara II](wikipedia://en/480509).[^o17]

Bhāskara clearly believed that [*$\pi$*](wikipedia://en/23601) was irrational. In support of [Aryabhata](wikipedia://en/297204)'s approximation of $\pi$, he criticized its approximation to $\sqrt{10}$, a practice common among [Jain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jainism) mathematicians.[^o4][^o10]

He was the first mathematician to openly discuss [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278) with four unequal, nonparallel sides.[^o21]

### Astronomy {#Astronomy}

The *Mahābhāskarīya* consists of eight chapters dealing with mathematical astronomy. The book deals with topics such as the longitudes of the planets, the [conjunctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjunction_%28astronomy%29) among the planets and stars, the phases of the moon, solar and lunar [eclipses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eclipse), and the rising and setting of the planets.[^o4]

Parts of *Mahābhāskarīya* were later translated into [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic).
