# Theta divisor

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Theta divisor"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **theta divisor** Θ is the [divisor](wikipedia://en/1693055) in the sense of [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) defined on an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) *A* over the complex numbers (and [principally polarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principally_polarized)) by the zero locus of the associated [Riemann theta-function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_theta-function). It is therefore an [algebraic subvariety](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_subvariety) of *A* of dimension dim *A* − 1.

:::

## Classical theory {#Classical_theory}

Classical results of [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) describe Θ in another way, in the case that *A* is the [Jacobian variety](wikipedia://en/489734) *J* of an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) ([compact Riemann surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Riemann_surface)) *C*. There is, for a choice of base point *P* on *C*, a standard mapping of *C* to *J*, by means of the interpretation of *J* as the [linear equivalence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_equivalence) classes of divisors on *C* of degree 0. That is, *Q* on *C* maps to the class of *Q* − *P*. Then since *J* is an [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924), *C* may be added to itself *k* times on *J*, giving rise to subvarieties *W*<sub>*k*</sub>.

If *g* is the [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) of *C*, Riemann proved that Θ is a translate on *J* of *W*<sub>*g* − 1</sub>. He also described which points on *W*<sub>*g* − 1</sub> are [non-singular](wikipedia://en/253260#Singularities): they correspond to the effective divisors *D* of degree *g* − 1 with no associated meromorphic functions other than constants. In more classical language, these *D* do not move in a [linear system of divisors](wikipedia://en/1186886) on *C*, in the sense that they do not dominate the polar divisor of a non constant function.

Riemann further proved the **Riemann singularity theorem**, identifying the [multiplicity of a point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicity_of_a_point) *p* = class(*D*) on *W*<sub>*g* − 1</sub> as the number of linearly independent meromorphic functions with pole divisor dominated by *D*, or equivalently as *h*<sup>0</sup>(O(*D*)), the number of linearly independent [global sections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Global_section) of the [holomorphic line bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holomorphic_line_bundle) associated to *D* as [Cartier divisor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartier_divisor) on *C*.

## Later work {#Later_work}

The Riemann singularity theorem was extended by [George Kempf](wikipedia://en/34816743) in 1973,[^o4] building on work of [David Mumford](wikipedia://en/394594) and Andreotti - Mayer, to a description of the singularities of points *p* = class(*D*) on *W*<sub>*k*</sub> for 1 ≤ *k* ≤ *g* − 1. In particular he computed their multiplicities also in terms of the number of independent meromorphic functions associated to *D* (**Riemann-Kempf singularity theorem**).[^o5]

More precisely, Kempf mapped *J* locally near *p* to a family of matrices coming from an [exact sequence](wikipedia://en/138215) which computes *h*<sup>0</sup>(O(*D*)), in such a way that *W*<sub>*k*</sub> corresponds to the locus of matrices of less than maximal rank. The multiplicity then agrees with that of the point on the corresponding rank locus. Explicitly, if

*h*<sup>0</sup>(O(*D*)) = *r* + 1,

the multiplicity of *W*<sub>*k*</sub> at class(*D*) is the binomial coefficient

$${g-k+r \choose r}.$$

When *k* = *g* − 1, this is *r* + 1, Riemann's formula.
