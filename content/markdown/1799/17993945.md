# Gδ space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gδ space"}

In mathematics, particularly [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a **G<sub>δ</sub> space** is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) in which [closed sets](wikipedia://en/47279) are in a way ‘separated’ from their complements using only countably many [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358). A G<sub>δ</sub> space may thus be regarded as a space satisfying a different kind of [separation axiom](wikipedia://en/40117302). In fact [normal](wikipedia://en/48629) G<sub>δ</sub> spaces are referred to as [perfectly normal spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfectly_normal_space), and satisfy the strongest of [separation axioms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separation_axioms).

:::

G<sub>δ</sub> spaces are also called **perfect spaces**.[^o2] The term *perfect* is also used, incompatibly, to refer to a space with no [isolated points](wikipedia://en/390557); see [Perfect set](wikipedia://en/5075551).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A countable [intersection](wikipedia://en/41780044) of open sets in a topological space is called a [G<sub>δ</sub> set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G-delta_set). Trivially, every open set is a G<sub>δ</sub> set. Dually, a countable union of closed sets is called an [F<sub>σ</sub> set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-sigma_set). Trivially, every closed set is an F<sub>σ</sub> set.

A topological space *X* is called a **G<sub>δ</sub> space**[^o5] if every closed subset of *X* is a G<sub>δ</sub> set. Dually and equivalently, a *G<sub>δ</sub> space* is a space in which every open set is an F<sub>σ</sub> set.

:::

## Properties and examples {#Properties_and_examples}

- Every subspace of a G<sub>δ</sub> space is a G<sub>δ</sub> space.
- Every [metrizable space](wikipedia://en/19738) is a G<sub>δ</sub> space. The same holds for [pseudometrizable spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudometrizable_space).
- Every [second countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_countable) [regular](wikipedia://en/53058) space is a G<sub>δ</sub> space. This follows from the [Urysohn's metrization theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urysohn's_metrization_theorem) in the Hausdorff case, but can easily be shown directly.[^o7]
- Every countable regular space is a G<sub>δ</sub> space.
- Every [hereditarily Lindelöf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hereditarily_Lindelöf) regular space is a G<sub>δ</sub> space.[^o8] Such spaces are in fact [perfectly normal](wikipedia://en/48629). This generalizes the previous two items about second countable and countable regular spaces.
- A G<sub>δ</sub> space need not be normal, as **R** endowed with the [K-topology](wikipedia://en/18360606) shows. That example is not a regular space. Examples of [Tychonoff](wikipedia://en/30554) G<sub>δ</sub> spaces that are not normal are the [Sorgenfrey plane](wikipedia://en/682853)[^o9] and the [Niemytzki plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niemytzki_plane).[^o10]
- In a [first countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_countable) [T<sub>1</sub> space](wikipedia://en/148721), every [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) is a G<sub>δ</sub> set. That is not enough for the space to be a G<sub>δ</sub> space, as shown for example by the [lexicographic order topology on the unit square](wikipedia://en/29044559).[^o11]
- The [Sorgenfrey line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sorgenfrey_line) is an example of a perfectly normal (i.e. normal G<sub>δ</sub>) space that is not metrizable.
- The [topological sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_sum) $X={\coprod}_i X_i$ of a family of disjoint topological spaces is a G<sub>δ</sub> space if and only if each $X_i$ is a G<sub>δ</sub> space.
