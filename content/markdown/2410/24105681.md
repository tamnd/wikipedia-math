# Carleson's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Carleson's theorem"}

**Carleson's theorem** is a fundamental result in [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) establishing the ([Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/18198)) [pointwise almost everywhere convergence](wikipedia://en/340198#Almost_everywhere_convergence) of [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) of [$L^{2}$ functions](wikipedia://en/45194), proved by [Lennart Carleson](wikipedia://en/1669541).[^o2] The name is also often used to refer to the extension of the result by [Richard Hunt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Hunt_%28mathematician%29) to $L^{p}$ functions for $p \in (1, \infty]$ (also known as the *Carleson–Hunt theorem*) and the analogous results for pointwise almost everywhere convergence of [Fourier integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_integral), which can be shown to be equivalent by transference methods.[^o3]

## History {#History}

A fundamental question about Fourier series, asked by [Fourier](wikipedia://en/25767401) himself at the beginning of the 19th century, is whether the Fourier series of a [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) function [converges pointwise](wikipedia://en/340198) to the function.

By strengthening the continuity assumption slightly one can easily show that the Fourier series converges everywhere. For example, if a function has [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673) then its Fourier series converges everywhere to the local average of the function. In particular, if a function is [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable) then its Fourier series converges to it everywhere. This was proven by [Dirichlet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirichlet), who expressed his belief that he would soon be able to extend his result to cover all continuous functions. Another way to obtain convergence everywhere is to change the summation method. For example, [Fejér's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fejér's_theorem) shows that if one replaces ordinary summation by [Cesàro summation](wikipedia://en/705600) then the Fourier series of any continuous function [converges uniformly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Converges_uniformly) to the function. Further, it is easy to show that the Fourier series of any $L^{2}$ function converges to it in $L^{2}$ norm.

After Dirichlet's result, several experts, including Dirichlet, [Riemann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann), [Weierstrass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass) and [Dedekind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dedekind), stated their belief that the Fourier series of any continuous function would converge everywhere. This was disproved by [Paul du Bois-Reymond](wikipedia://en/7457861), who showed in 1876 that there is [a continuous function whose Fourier series diverges at one point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_boundedness_theorem#Example:_pointwise_convergence_of_Fourier_series).

The almost-everywhere convergence of Fourier series for $L^{2}$ functions was postulated by [N. N. Luzin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolai_Nikolaevich_Luzin),[^o7] and the problem was known as *Luzin's conjecture* up until its proof by Carleson.[^o2] (Kolmogorov 1923) showed that the analogue of Luzin's conjecture for $L^{1}$ is false by finding such a function whose Fourier series diverges almost everywhere (improved slightly in 1926 to diverging everywhere).[^o9] Before Carleson's result, the best known estimate for the [partial sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sums) $s_{n}$ of the Fourier series of a function in $L^{p}$ was

$$s_n(x) = o(\log (n)^{1/p})\text{ almost everywhere}.$$

In other words, the function $s_{n}(x)$ can still grow to infinity at any given point *x* as one takes more and more terms of the Fourier series into account, though the growth must be quite slow (slower than the [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of $n$ to a small power). This result was proved by Kolmogorov–Seliverstov–Plessner for $p = 2$, by [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) for $p = 1$, and by Littlewood–Paley for $p > 1$.[^o10] This result had not been improved for several decades, leading some experts to suspect that it was the best possible and that Luzin's conjecture was false. Kolmogorov's [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) in $L^{1}$ was unbounded in any [interval](wikipedia://en/49172), but it was thought to be only a matter of time before a continuous counterexample was found. Carleson said in a 2007 interview that he started by trying to find a continuous counterexample and at one point thought he had a method that would construct one, but realized eventually that his approach could not work. He then tried instead to prove Luzin's conjecture since the failure of his counterexample convinced him that it was probably true.[^o11]

Carleson's original proof is exceptionally hard to read, and although several authors have simplified the argument there are still no easy proofs of his theorem. Expositions of the original paper include (Kahane 1995),[^o13] (Mozzochi 1971),[^o15] (Jørsboe Mejlbro 1982),[^o17] and (Arias de Reyna 2002).[^o19] [Charles Fefferman](wikipedia://en/502831) published a new proof of Hunt's extension which proceeded by bounding a [maximal operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_operator).[^o20] This, in turn, inspired a much simplified proof of the *L*<sup>2</sup> result by (txt),[^o22] explained in more detail in (Lacey 2004).[^o24] The books (Fremlin 2003) and (Grafakos 2014) also give proofs of Carleson's theorem.[^o27][^o28] The theorem was formalized in [Lean 4](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lean_4) in 2025.

(Katznelson 1966) showed that for any set of [measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero) there is a continuous periodic function whose Fourier series diverges at all points of the set (and possibly elsewhere).[^o30] When combined with Carleson's theorem this shows that there is a continuous function whose Fourier series diverges at all points of a given set of [reals](wikipedia://en/20646438) if and only if the set has measure zero.

The extension of Carleson's theorem to $L^{p}$ for $p > 1$ was stated to be a "rather obvious" extension of the case $p = 2$ in Carleson's paper, and was proved by Hunt.[^o3] Carleson's result was improved further by (Sjölin 1971) to the space $L\,\log_{+}(L)\log_{+}\log_{+}(L)$[^o32] and by (Antonov 1996) to the space $L\,\log_{+}(L)\log_{+}\log_{+}\log_{+}(L)$.[^o34] (Here $\log_{+}(L)$ is $\log(L)$ if $L > 1$ and $0$ otherwise, and if $\varphi$ is a function then $\varphi(L)$ stands for the space of functions $f$ such that $\varphi(\lvert f(x)\rvert)$ is [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function).)

(Konyagin 2000) improved Kolmogorov's counterexample by finding functions with everywhere-divergent Fourier series in a space slightly larger than $L\,\log_{+}(L)^{1/2}$.[^o36] One can ask if there is in some sense a largest natural space of functions whose Fourier series converge almost everywhere. The simplest candidate for such a space that is consistent with the results of Antonov and Konyagin is $L\,\log_{+}(L)$.

The extension of Carleson's theorem to Fourier series and integrals in several variables is made more complicated as there are many different ways in which one can sum the coefficients; for example, one can sum over increasing balls, or increasing rectangles. Convergence of rectangular partial sums (and indeed general polygonal partial sums) follows from the one-dimensional case, but the spherical summation problem is still [open](wikipedia://en/1333335) for $L^{2}$.

## The Carleson operator {#The_Carleson_operator}

The Carleson operator $C$ is the non-linear operator defined by

$$Cf(x) = \sup_N\left|\int_{-N}^N \hat f(y)e^{2\pi i xy} \, dy\right|$$

It is relatively easy to show that the Carleson–Hunt theorem follows from the [boundedness](wikipedia://en/455961) of the Carleson operator from $L^{p}(\mathbf{R})$ to itself for $1 < p < \infty$. However, proving that it is bounded is difficult, and this was actually what Carleson proved.
