# Bonferroni correction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bonferroni correction"}

**Bonferroni correction** is a method to counteract the [multiple comparisons problem](wikipedia://en/9444220) in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685). It is named after the mathematician [Carlo Emilio Bonferroni](wikipedia://en/605123).

:::

## Background {#Background}

[Statistical hypothesis testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing) is based on rejecting the [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) when the likelihood of the observed data would be low if the null hypothesis were true. When multiple hypotheses are tested, the probability of observing a rare event increases, so the likelihood of incorrectly rejecting a null hypothesis (i.e., making a [Type I error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_error)) increases if multiple null hypotheses are true.[^o3]

The Bonferroni correction compensates for that increase by testing each individual hypothesis at a significance level of $\alpha/m$, where $\alpha$ is the desired overall alpha level and $m$ is the number of hypotheses.[^o4] For example, if a trial is testing $m = 20$ hypotheses with a desired overall $\alpha = 0.05$, the Bonferroni-corrected alpha level would be $\alpha = 0.05/20 = 0.0025$. The method is named for its use of the [Bonferroni inequalities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bonferroni_inequalities).[^o5]

The Bonferroni correction can also be applied as a p-value adjustment: Using that approach, instead of adjusting the alpha level, each p-value is multiplied by the number of tests (with adjusted p-values that exceed 1 then being reduced to 1), and the alpha level is left unchanged. The significance decisions using this approach will be the same as when using the alpha-level adjustment approach.

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $H_1,\ldots,H_m$ be a family of null hypotheses and let $p_1,\ldots,p_m$ be their corresponding [p-values](wikipedia://en/554994). Let $m$ be the total number of null hypotheses, and let $m_0$ be the number of true null hypotheses (which is presumably unknown to the researcher). The [family-wise error rate](wikipedia://en/4621448) (FWER) is the probability of rejecting at least one true $H_{i}$, that is, of making at least one [type I error](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_I_error). The Bonferroni correction rejects the null hypothesis for each $p_i\leq\frac \alpha m$, thereby controlling the [FWER](wikipedia://en/4621448) at $\leq \alpha$. Proof of this control follows from [Boole's inequality](wikipedia://en/344887), as follows:

$$\text{FWER} = P\left\{ \bigcup_{i=1}^{m_0}\left(p_i\leq\frac \alpha m \right) \right\} \leq\sum_{i=1}^{m_0}\left\{P\left(p_i\leq\frac \alpha m\right)\right\} \leq m_0 \frac \alpha m \leq \alpha.$$

This control does not require any assumptions about dependence among the p-values or about how many of the null hypotheses are true.[^o8]

:::

## Extensions {#Extensions}

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

### Generalization {#Generalization}

Rather than testing each hypothesis at the $\alpha/m$ level, the hypotheses may be tested at any other combination of levels that add up to $\alpha$, provided that the level of each test is decided before looking at the data.[^o13] For example, for two hypothesis tests, an overall $\alpha$ of 0.05 could be maintained by conducting one test at 0.04 and the other at 0.01.

:::

### Confidence intervals {#Confidence_intervals}

The procedure proposed by Dunn[^o15] can be used to adjust [confidence intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Confidence_intervals). If one establishes $m$ confidence intervals, and wishes to have an overall confidence level of $1-\alpha$, each individual confidence interval can be adjusted to the level of $1-\frac{\alpha}{m}$.[^o15]

### Continuous problems {#Continuous_problems}

When searching for a signal in a continuous parameter space there can also be a problem of multiple comparisons, or look-elsewhere effect. For example, a physicist might be looking to discover a particle of unknown mass by considering a large range of masses; this was the case during the Nobel Prize winning detection of the [Higgs boson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higgs_boson). In such cases, one can apply a continuous generalization of the Bonferroni correction by employing [Bayesian](wikipedia://en/404412) logic to relate the effective number of trials, $m$, to the prior-to-posterior volume ratio.[^o17]

:::

## Alternatives {#Alternatives}

There are alternative ways to control the [family-wise error rate](wikipedia://en/4621448). For example, the [Holm–Bonferroni method](wikipedia://en/10726541) and the [Šidák correction](wikipedia://en/38510169) are universally more powerful procedures than the Bonferroni correction, meaning that they are always at least as powerful. But unlike the Bonferroni procedure, these methods do not control the [expected number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expected_number) of Type I errors per family (the per-family Type I error rate).[^o19]

## Criticism {#Criticism}

With respect to [Family-wise error rate (FWER)](wikipedia://en/4621448) control, the Bonferroni correction can be conservative if there are a large number of tests and/or the test statistics are positively correlated, due to looseness of the union bound for overlapping sets.[^o21]

Multiple-testing corrections, including the Bonferroni procedure, increase the probability of [Type II errors](wikipedia://en/5657877#Type_II_error) when null hypotheses are false, i.e., they reduce [statistical power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_power).[^o22][^o21]
