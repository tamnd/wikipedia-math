# Roth's theorem on arithmetic progressions

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Roth's theorem on arithmetic progressions"}

**Roth's theorem on arithmetic progressions** is a result in [additive combinatorics](wikipedia://en/18243392) concerning the existence of [arithmetic progressions](wikipedia://en/168389) in subsets of the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). It was first [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) by [Klaus Roth](wikipedia://en/787827) in 1953.[^o2] Roth's theorem is a special case of [Szemerédi's theorem](wikipedia://en/591703) for the case $k = 3$.

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

A subset *A* of the natural numbers is said to have positive upper density if

$$\limsup_{n\to\infty}\frac{|A\cap \{1, 2, 3, \dotsc, n\}|}{n} > 0.$$

> **Roth's theorem on arithmetic progressions (infinite version)**: A subset of the natural numbers with positive upper density contains a 3-term arithmetic progression.

An alternate, more qualitative, formulation of the theorem is concerned with the maximum size of a [Salem–Spencer set](wikipedia://en/59850911) which is a subset of $[N] = \{1, \dots, N\}$. Let $r_3([N])$ be the size of the largest subset of $[N]$ which contains no 3-term arithmetic progression.

> **Roth's theorem on arithmetic progressions (finitary version)**: $r_3([N]) = o(N).$

Improving [upper and lower bounds](wikipedia://en/42693) on $r_3([N])$ is still an open research problem.

:::

## History {#History}

The first result in this direction was [Van der Waerden's theorem](wikipedia://en/157178) in 1927, which states that for sufficiently large N, coloring the integers $1, \dots, n$ with $r$ colors will result in a $k$ term arithmetic progression.[^o6]

Later on in 1936 Erdős and Turán [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) a much stronger result that any subset of the integers with positive density contains arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. In 1942, [Raphaël Salem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphaël_Salem) and [Donald C. Spencer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_C._Spencer) provided a construction of a 3-AP-free set (i.e. a set with no 3-term arithmetic progressions) of size $\frac{N}{e^{O(\log N/\log \log N)}}$,[^o7] disproving an additional conjecture of Erdős and Turán that $r_3([N]) = N^{1 - \delta}$ for some $\delta > 0$.[^o8]

In 1953, Roth partially resolved the initial conjecture by proving they must contain an arithmetic progression of length 3 using Fourier analytic methods. Eventually, in 1975, Szemerédi proved [Szemerédi's theorem](wikipedia://en/591703) using combinatorial techniques, resolving the original conjecture in full.

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof techniques"}

## Proof techniques {#Proof_techniques}

The original proof given by Roth used Fourier analytic methods. Later on another proof was given using [Szemerédi's regularity lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Szemerédi's_regularity_lemma).

::: {#o12 .proof cue=heading name="Proof sketch via Fourier analysis"}

### Proof sketch via Fourier analysis {#Proof_sketch_via_Fourier_analysis}

In 1953, Roth used [Fourier analysis](wikipedia://en/11659) to prove an upper bound of $r_3([N]) = O\left(\frac{N}{\log \log N}\right)$. Below is a sketch of this proof.

Define the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) of a function $f : \mathbb{Z} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ to be the function $\widehat{f} : [0, 1) \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ satisfying

$$\widehat{f}(\theta) = \sum_{x \in \mathbb{Z}}f(x)e(-x\theta),$$

where $e(t) = e^{2\pi i t}$.

Let $A$ be a 3-AP-free subset of $\{1, \dots, N\}$. The proof proceeds in 3 steps.

1. Show that a $A$ admits a large Fourier coefficient.
1. Deduce that there exists a sub-progression of $\{1, \dots, N\}$ such that $A$ has a density increment when restricted to this subprogression.
1. Iterate Step 2 to obtain an upper bound on $|A|$.

#### Step 1 {#Step_1}

For functions, $f, g, h : \mathbb{Z} \rightarrow \mathbb{C},$ define

$$\Lambda(f, g, h) = \sum_{x, y \in \mathbb{Z}} f(x)g(x + y)h(x + 2y)$$

> **Counting Lemma** Let $f, g : \mathbb{Z} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ satisfy $\sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}|f(n)|^2, \sum_{n \in \mathbb{Z}}|g(n)|^2 \le M$. Define $\Lambda_3(f) = \Lambda(f,f,f)$. Then $|\Lambda_3(f) - \Lambda_3(g)| \le 3M\|\widehat{f - g}\|_\infty$.

The counting lemma tells us that if the Fourier Transforms of $f$ and $g$ are "close", then the number of 3-term arithmetic progressions between the two should also be "close." Let $\alpha = |A|/N$ be the density of $A$. Define the functions $f = \mathbf{1}_{A}$ (i.e the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $A$), and $g = \alpha \cdot \mathbf{1}_{[N]}$. Step 1 can then be deduced by applying the Counting Lemma to $f$ and $g$, which tells us that there exists some $\theta$ such that

$$\left|\sum_{n=1}^N (1_A - \alpha)(n)e(\theta n) \right| \ge \frac{\alpha^2}{10}N.$$

#### Step 2 {#Step_2}

Given the $\theta$ from step 1, we first show that it's possible to split up $[N]$ into relatively large subprogressions such that the character $x \mapsto e(x\theta)$ is roughly constant on each subprogression.

> **Lemma 1:** Let $0 < \eta < 1, \theta \in \mathbb{R}$. Assume that $N > C\eta^{-6}$ for a universal constant $C$. Then it is possible to partition $[N]$ into arithmetic progressions $P_i$ with length $N^{1/3} \le |P_i| \le 2N^{1/3}$ such that $\sup_{x, y \in P_i}|e(x\theta) - e(y\theta)| < \eta$ for all $i$.

Next, we apply Lemma 1 to obtain a partition into subprogressions. We then use the fact that $\theta$ produced a large coefficient in step 1 to show that one of these subprogressions must have a density increment:

> **Lemma 2:** Let $A$ be a 3-AP-free subset of $[N]$, with $|A| = \alpha N$ and $N > C\alpha^{-12}$. Then, there exists a sub progression $P \subset [N]$ such that $|P| \ge N^{1/3}$ and $|A \cap P| \ge (\alpha + \alpha^2/40)|P|$.

#### Step 3 {#Step_3}

We now iterate step 2. Let $a_t$ be the density of $A$ after the $t$th iteration. We have that $\alpha_0 = \alpha,$ and $\alpha_{t + 1} \ge \alpha + \alpha^2/40.$ First, see that $\alpha$ doubles (i.e. reach $T$ such that $\alpha_T \ge 2\alpha_0$) after at most $40/\alpha + 1$ steps. We double $\alpha$ again (i.e reach $\alpha_T \ge 4\alpha_0$) after at most $20/\alpha + 1$ steps. Since $\alpha \le 1$, this process must terminate after at most $O(1/\alpha)$ steps.

Let $N_t$ be the size of our current progression after $t$ iterations. By Lemma 2, we can always continue the process whenever $N_t \ge C\alpha_t^{-12},$ and thus when the process terminates we have that $N_t \le C\alpha_t^{-12} \le C\alpha^{-12}.$ Also, note that when we pass to a subprogression, the size of our set decreases by a [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506). Therefore

$$N \le N_t^{3^t} \le (C\alpha^{-12})^{3^{O(1/\alpha)}} = e^{e^{O(1/\alpha)}}.$$

Therefore $\alpha = O(1/\log \log N),$ so $|A| = O \left(\frac{N}{\log \log N}\right),$ as desired. $\blacksquare$

Unfortunately, this technique does not generalize directly to larger arithmetic progressions to prove Szemerédi's theorem. An extension of this proof eluded mathematicians for decades until 1998, when [Timothy Gowers](wikipedia://en/365715) developed the field of higher-order Fourier analysis specifically to generalize the above proof to prove Szemerédi's theorem.[^o16]

:::

### Proof sketch via graph regularity {#Proof_sketch_via_graph_regularity}

From the [Szemerédi regularity lemma](wikipedia://en/1962417) and [counting lemma](wikipedia://en/62471938) one obtains the [graph removal lemma](wikipedia://en/59842040), and as a corollary the following:

**Diamond-free Lemma.** Any graph $G$ on $n$ vertices in which every edge lies in precisely one unique triangle has $o(n^2)$ edges.

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Application to Roth’s theorem"}

#### Application to Roth’s theorem {#Application_to_Roth’s_theorem}

First note that a set $A\subseteq\{1,\dots,N\}$ has a 3-term arithmetic progression iff its reduction modulo $M:=2N+1$ does.

Fix $A\subseteq \{1,\dots,N\}$ with no 3-term AP. Construct a tripartite graph $G$ with parts $X,Y,Z$, each a copy of $\mathbb{Z}/M\mathbb{Z}$. Add edges as follows:

- $x\in X$ to $y\in Y$ if $y-x\in A$;
- $y\in Y$ to $z\in Z$ if $z-y\in A$;
- $z\in Z$ to $x\in X$ if $(x-z)/2\in A$ (since 2 is invertible mod $M$).

If $x,y,z$ form a triangle, then $y-x,\ \frac{x-z}{2},\ z-y\in A$. These three numbers form an arithmetic progression in that order with step $(x+z-2y)/2$. Because $A$ has no nontrivial 3-term AP, they are equal, so $(x+z-2y)/2=0$. Therefore, if we fix any two vertices of $G$ connected by an edge, every triangle extending this edge must satisfy $x+z-2y=0$, yielding a unique choice of the third vertex. Furthermore, such a choice guarantees the existence of the other two edges in that triangle. Thus, the diamond-free lemma applies, and $e(G)=o((3M)^2)=o(N^2)$.

Notice now, that each element $a \in A$ contributes exactly one edge for each vertex in a part: for instance, for every $x \in X$, there is exactly one $y \in Y$ such that $y - x \equiv a \pmod{M}$, so $a$ gives exactly $M$ edges between $X$ and $Y$. The same holds for the other two pairs, hence $e(G)=3|A|M$. Consequently,

$$|A| = \frac{e(G)}{3M} = \frac{o(N^2)}{3(2N+1)} = o(N)$$

proving Roth’s theorem.

:::

## Extensions and generalizations {#Extensions_and_generalizations}

Szemerédi's theorem resolved the original conjecture and generalized Roth's theorem to arithmetic progressions of arbitrary length. Since then it has been extended in multiple fashions to create new and interesting results.

[Furstenberg](wikipedia://en/6450152) and [Katznelson](wikipedia://en/5643045)[^o22] used [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) to prove a multidimensional version and Leibman and [Bergelson](wikipedia://en/24810745)[^o23] extended it to polynomial progressions as well. Most recently, [Green](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ben_J._Green) and [Tao](wikipedia://en/1118498) proved the [Green–Tao theorem](wikipedia://en/7006166) which says that the [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions. Since the prime numbers are a subset of density 0, they introduced a "relative" Szemerédi theorem which applies to subsets with density 0 that satisfy certain [pseudorandomness](wikipedia://en/23210) conditions. Later on [Conlon](wikipedia://en/36837348), [Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacob_Fox), and Zhao[^o24][^o25] strengthened this theorem by weakening the necessary pseudorandomness condition. In 2020, Bloom and Sisask[^o26] proved that any set $A$ such that $\sum_{n \in A} \frac{1}{n}$ diverges must contain arithmetic progressions of length 3; this is the first non-trivial case of another [conjecture](wikipedia://en/4312009) of [Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) postulating that any such set must in fact contain arbitrarily long arithmetic progressions.

### Improving bounds {#Improving_bounds}

There has also been work done on improving the bound in Roth's theorem. The bound from the original proof of Roth's theorem showed that

$$r_3([N]) \leq c\cdot\frac{N}{\log\log N}$$

for some constant $c$. Over the years this bound has been continually lowered by Szemerédi,[^o28] [Heath-Brown](wikipedia://en/2984550),[^o29] [Bourgain](wikipedia://en/1024131),[^o30][^o31] and [Sanders](wikipedia://en/36766438).[^o32][^o33] The current (July 2020) best bound is due to Bloom and Sisask[^o26] who have shown the existence of an absolute constant c>0 such that

$$r_3([N]) \leq \frac{N}{(\log N)^{1+c}}.$$

In February 2023 a preprint[^o34][^o35] (later published[^o36]) by Kelley and Meka gave a new bound of:

$$r_3([N]) \leq 2^{-\Omega((\log N)^{1/12})} \cdot N.$$

Four days later, Bloom and Sisask published a preprint giving an exposition of the result[^o37] (later published[^o38]), simplifying the argument and yielding some additional applications. Several months later, Bloom and Sisask obtained a further improvement to $r_3([N]) \leq \exp(-c(\log N)^{1/9})N$, and stated (without proof) that their techniques can be used to show $r_3([N]) \leq \exp(-c(\log N)^{5/41})N$.[^o39]

In a 2026 preprint,[^o40] Raghavan reported further improved bounds, proving:

$$|A| \leq \exp(-c\log(N)^{1/6}\log\log(N)^{-1/6})N.$$

There has also been work done on the other end, constructing the largest set with no 3-term arithmetic progressions. The best construction has barely been improved since 1946 when [Behrend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Felix_Behrend)[^o41] improved on the initial construction by Salem and Spencer and proved

$$r_3([N]) \geq N\exp(-c\sqrt{\log N}).$$

Due to no improvements in over 70 years, it is conjectured that Behrend's set is asymptotically very close in size to the largest possible set with no 3-term progressions.[^o26] If correct, the Kelley-Meka bound will prove this conjecture.

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Roth's theorem in finite fields"}

### Roth's theorem in finite fields {#Roth's_theorem_in_finite_fields}

As a variation, we can consider the analogous problem over [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615). Consider the finite field $\mathbb{F}_3^n$, and let $r_3(\mathbb{F}_3^n)$ be the size of the largest subset of $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ which contains no 3-term arithmetic progression. This problem is actually equivalent to the [cap set](wikipedia://en/51223601) problem, which asks for the largest subset of $\mathbb{F}_3^n$ such that no 3 points lie on a line. The cap set problem can be seen as a generalization of the card game [Set](wikipedia://en/29538).

In 1982, Brown and Buhler[^o44] were the first to show that $r_3(\mathbb{F}_3^n) = o(3^n).$ In 1995, Roy Mesuhlam[^o45] used a similar technique to the Fourier-analytic proof of Roth's theorem to show that $r_3(\mathbb{F}_3^n) = O\left(\frac{3^n}{n}\right).$ This bound was improved to $O(3^n/n^{1 + \epsilon})$ in 2012 by Bateman and Katz.[^o46]

In 2016, [Ernie Croot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernie_Croot), Vsevolod Lev, Péter Pál Pach, [Jordan Ellenberg](wikipedia://en/19765796) and Dion Gijswijt developed a new technique based on the polynomial method to prove that $r_3(\mathbb{F}_3^n) = O(2.756^n)$.[^o47][^o48][^o49]

The best known lower bound is $2.2202^{n}$, discovered in December 2023 by [Google DeepMind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google_DeepMind) researchers using a [large language model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Large_language_model) (LLM).[^o50]

:::

::: {#o52 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Roth's theorem with popular differences"}

### Roth's theorem with popular differences {#Roth's_theorem_with_popular_differences}

Another generalization of Roth's theorem shows that for positive density subsets, there not only exists a 3-term arithmetic progression, but that there exist many 3-APs all with the same common difference.

> **Roth's theorem with popular differences:** For all $\epsilon > 0$, there exists some $n_0 = n_0(\epsilon)$ such that for every $n > n_0$ and $A \subset \mathbb{F}_3^n$ with $|A| = \alpha3^n,$ there exists some $y \neq 0$ such that $|\{x : x, x + y, x + 2y \in A\}| \ge (\alpha^3 - \epsilon)3^n.$

If $A$ is chosen randomly from $\mathbb{F}_3^n,$ then we would expect there to be $\alpha^33^n$ progressions for each value of $y$. The popular differences theorem thus states that for each $|A|$ with positive density, there is some $y$ such that the number of 3-APs with common difference $y$ is close to what we would expect.

This theorem was first proven by Green in 2005,[^o53] who gave a bound of $n_0 = \text{tow}((1/\epsilon)^{O(1)}),$ where $\text{tow}$ is the tower function. In 2019, Fox and Pham recently improved the bound to $n_0 = \text{tow}(O(\log\frac{1}{\epsilon})).$[^o54]

A corresponding statement is also true in $\mathbb{Z}$ for both 3-APs and 4-APs.[^o55] However, the claim has been shown to be false for 5-APs.[^o56]
