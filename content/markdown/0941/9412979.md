# Pushforward measure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pushforward measure"}

In [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory), a **pushforward measure** (also known as **push forward**, **push-forward** or **image measure**) is obtained by transferring ("pushing forward") a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) from one [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) to another using a [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Given [measurable spaces](wikipedia://en/43333) $(X_1,\Sigma_1)$ and $(X_2,\Sigma_2)$, a [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) $f\colon X_1\to X_2$ and a measure $\mu\colon\Sigma_1\to[0,+\infty]$, the **pushforward** of $\mu$ by $f$ is defined to be the measure $f_{*}(\mu)\colon\Sigma_2\to[0,+\infty]$ given by

$f_{*} (\mu) (B) = \mu \left( f^{-1} (B) \right)$ for $B \in \Sigma_{2}.$

This definition applies *[mutatis mutandis](wikipedia://en/742665)* for a [signed](wikipedia://en/2985811) or [complex measure](wikipedia://en/3057532). The pushforward measure is also denoted as $\mu \circ f^{-1}$, $f_\sharp \mu$, $f \sharp \mu$, or $f \# \mu$.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

::: {#o7 .statement cue=named-heading name="Change of variable formula"}

### Change of variable formula {#Change_of_variable_formula}

Theorem:[^o8] A measurable function *g* on *X*<sub>2</sub> is integrable with respect to the pushforward measure *f*<sub>∗</sub>(*μ*) if and only if the composition $g \circ f$ is integrable with respect to the measure *μ*. In that case, the integrals coincide, i.e.,

$$\int_{X_2} g \, d(f_* \mu) = \int_{X_1} g \circ f \, d\mu.$$

Note that in the previous formula $X_1=f^{-1}(X_2)$.

:::

### Functoriality {#Functoriality}

Pushforwards of measures allow to induce, from a function between [measurable spaces](wikipedia://en/43333) $f:X\to Y$, a function between the spaces of measures $M(X)\to M(Y)$. As with many induced mappings, this construction has the structure of a [functor](wikipedia://en/10987), on the [category of measurable spaces](wikipedia://en/75966255).

For the special case of [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437), this property amounts to functoriality of the [Giry monad](wikipedia://en/75998118).

:::

## Examples and applications {#Examples_and_applications}

- If $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ is a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325), $(E, \mathcal{E})$ is a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333), and $X : \Omega \to E$ is a $(E, \mathcal{E})$-valued random variable, then the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of $X$ is the pushforward measure of $P$ by $X$ onto $(E, \mathcal{E})$.
- A natural "[Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)" on the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) **S**<sup>1</sup> (here thought of as a subset of the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) **C**) may be defined using a push-forward construction and Lebesgue measure *λ* on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) **R**. Let *λ* also denote the restriction of Lebesgue measure to the interval \[0, 2*π*) and let *f* : \[0, 2*π*) → **S**<sup>1</sup> be the natural bijection defined by *f*(*t*) = exp(*i* *t*). The natural "Lebesgue measure" on **S**<sup>1</sup> is then the push-forward measure *f*<sub>∗</sub>(*λ*). The measure *f*<sub>∗</sub>(*λ*) might also be called "[arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) measure" or "angle measure", since the *f*<sub>∗</sub>(*λ*)-measure of an arc in **S**<sup>1</sup> is precisely its arc length (or, equivalently, the angle that it subtends at the centre of the circle.)
- The previous example extends nicely to give a natural "Lebesgue measure" on the *n*-dimensional [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) **T**<sup>*n*</sup>. The previous example is a special case, since **S**<sup>1</sup> = **T**<sup>1</sup>. This Lebesgue measure on **T**<sup>*n*</sup> is, up to normalization, the [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) for the [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) **T**<sup>*n*</sup>.
- [Gaussian measures](wikipedia://en/6572909) on infinite-dimensional vector spaces are defined using the push-forward and the standard Gaussian measure on the real line: a [Borel measure](wikipedia://en/4322) *γ* on a [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) *X* is called **Gaussian** if the push-forward of *γ* by any non-zero [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) in the [continuous dual space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dual_space) to *X* is a Gaussian measure on **R**.
- Consider a measurable function *f* : *X* → *X* and the [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of *f* with itself *n* times:

$$f^{(n)} = \underbrace{f \circ f \circ \dots \circ f}_{n \mathrm{\, times}} : X \to X.$$

This [iterated function](wikipedia://en/1448702) forms a [dynamical system](wikipedia://en/9087). It is often of interest in the study of such systems to find a measure *μ* on *X* that the map *f* leaves unchanged, a so-called [invariant measure](wikipedia://en/7635201), i.e one for which *f*<sub>∗</sub>(*μ*) = *μ*.

- One can also consider [quasi-invariant measures](wikipedia://en/2749048) for such a dynamical system: a measure *$\mu$* on *$(X,\Sigma)$* is called **quasi-invariant under** $f$ if the push-forward of *$\mu$* by $f$ is merely [equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equivalence_of_measures) to the original measure *μ*, not necessarily equal to it. A pair of measures $\mu, \nu$ on the same space are equivalent if and only if $\forall A\in \Sigma: \ \mu(A) = 0 \iff \nu(A) = 0$, so $\mu$ is quasi-invariant under $f$ if $\forall A \in \Sigma: \ \mu(A) = 0 \iff f_* \mu(A) = \mu\big(f^{-1}(A)\big) = 0$
- Many natural probability distributions, such as the [chi distribution](wikipedia://en/1901933), can be obtained via this construction.

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="A generalization"}

## A generalization {#A_generalization}

In general, any [measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) can be pushed forward. The push-forward then becomes a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator), known as the [transfer operator](wikipedia://en/1346096) or [Frobenius&ndash;Perron operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius&ndash;Perron_operator). In finite spaces this operator typically satisfies the requirements of the [Frobenius&ndash;Perron theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frobenius&ndash;Perron_theorem), and the maximal eigenvalue of the operator corresponds to the invariant measure.

The adjoint to the push-forward is the [pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508); as an operator on spaces of functions on measurable spaces, it is the [composition operator](wikipedia://en/2702039) or [Koopman operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koopman_operator).
