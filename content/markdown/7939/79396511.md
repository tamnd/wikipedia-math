# Jerome V. Moloney

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Jerome V. Moloney"}

**Jerome V. Moloney** is an [Irish-American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Americans) professor of [optical sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_sciences) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) at the [University of Arizona](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Arizona) and serves as the director of the Arizona Center for Mathematical Sciences.[^o3]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Moloney earned his B.Sc. degree from [University College Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College_Cork), [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland), in 1970, and completed his Ph.D. at the [University of Western Ontario](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Western_Ontario), [Canada](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canada), in 1976. From 1977 to 1979, he served as a [research associate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_associate) in physics at [Universität Bielefeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bielefeld_University) in [Bielefeld](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bielefeld), [Germany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany).[^o5][^o6]

In 1979, he joined the Optical Sciences Center, University of Arizona, as a research associate.[^o5] From 1981 until 1984 he was a [research assistant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Research_assistant) and associate professor in optical sciences at the University of Arizona. In 1984, he became a reader in physics at [Heriot-Watt University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heriot-Watt_University),[^o5] till 1990. In 1990, he was appointed a full professor at the University of Arizona and became the director of the Arizona Center for Mathematical Sciences the same year.[^o3][^o6]

## Main research topics {#Main_research_topics}

Moloney's research interests cover multiple topics in large-scale [physics simulations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_simulation), [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969), [photonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photonics), and nonlinear optics fields.[^o8] He, with colleagues at Arizona, suggested that the reason for the non-spreading propagation of X waves found by Di Trapani's was due to the interplay of Kerr nonlinearity and chromatic dispersion,[^o9] and later, that optical carrier shock waves strongly influence long wavelength laser pulses propagating in the atmosphere.[^o9]

Moloney proposed the “[Airy beam](wikipedia://en/23974214)” to create a new method for guiding laser light. Combining an Airy beam with a high-intensity pulse beam, Moloney's team created curved plasma channels. By using the lens, these channels can be bent or straightened.[^o10] The phenomenon propagated to ultra-intense self-bending Airy beams in transparent dielectric media.[^o11]

Moloney in 1999–2000, with colleagues at the University of Arizona as described in [Optics Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optics_Letters) and [Physical Review Letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_Review_Letters), proposed and described a mechanism whereby multiple light filaments created by high-power lasers in the atmosphere could be sustained over long distances, a mechanism critical to the generation of laser lightning rods. The latter concept was further developed with colleagues from the [University of Central Florida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Central_Florida), described in [Nature Photonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature_Photonics),[^o12] and was recently implemented in a field experiment to divert lightning strikes away from buildings and populated areas.

Moloney, with the collaboration of a team of US and German researchers, established a rigorous theoretical foundation to describe how semiconductor lasers operate, transitioning this knowledge to the development of commercial semiconductor epitaxy design software for lasers and sensors. The team designed a series of continuous, room-temperature, vertical-external-cavity semiconductor laser sources ([VECSEL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertical-external-cavity_surface-emitting-laser)) that deliver narrowband, milliwatt beams at terahertz-gap frequencies and a yellow laser wavelength for guidestar applications. Their devices incorporate a nonlinear optics element into the external-cavity laser. Moloney and his coworkers generated a narrowband emission at the uppermost frequencies of the terahertz gap and at the laser guidestar (yellow) wavelengths by mixing two IR beams. He proposed visible light VECSELs[^o13] as practical compact Guidestar laser sources and recognized the potential of room temperature, tunable terahertz external-cavity surface-emitting lasers (TECSELs) as a practical technology for astronomical observations.[^o14]
