# Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation"}

*Image: Ile de ré.JPG.* Crossing [swells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swell_%28ocean%29), consisting of near-cnoidal wave trains. Photo taken from Phares des Baleines (Whale Lighthouse) at the western point of [Île de Ré](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Île_de_Ré) (Isle of Rhé), France, in the [Atlantic Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atlantic_Ocean). The interaction of such near-[solitons](wikipedia://en/51654) in shallow water may be modeled through the Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), the **Kadomtsev–Petviashvili equation** (often abbreviated as **KP equation**) is a [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564) to describe [nonlinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear) [wave motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_motion). Named after [Boris Borisovich Kadomtsev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Kadomtsev) and [Vladimir Iosifovich Petviashvili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Iosifovich_Petviashvili), the KP equation is usually written as

$$\displaystyle \partial_x(\partial_t u+u \partial_x u+\epsilon^2\partial_{xxx}u)+\lambda\partial_{yy}u=0$$

where $\lambda=\pm 1$. The above form shows that the KP equation is a generalization to two [spatial dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_dimension), *x* and *y*, of the one-dimensional [Korteweg–de Vries (KdV) equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korteweg–de_Vries_equation). To be physically meaningful, the wave propagation direction has to be not-too-far from the *x* direction, i.e. with only slow variations of solutions in the *y* direction. The transverse stability and dynamics of a single planar soliton with respect to finite-amplitude and wavelength perturbations are described by the [Shrira-Pesenson equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrira-Pesenson_equation).[^o2]

:::

Like the KdV equation, the KP equation is completely integrable.[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6] It can also be solved using the [inverse scattering transform](wikipedia://en/4916481) much like the [nonlinear Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/1422748).[^o7]

The [Lax pair](wikipedia://en/5527029) representation for the KP equation, which underlies its integrability by the inverse scattering transform, was first obtained by Valery Dryuma in 1974,[^o8][^o9] shortly before the inverse scattering method for two-dimensional equations was developed by [Zakharov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_E._Zakharov) and [Shabat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexei_Borisovich_Shabat) the same year.[^o10]

In 2002, the regularized version of the KP equation, naturally referred to as the [Benjamin](wikipedia://en/16733041)–[Bona](wikipedia://en/21528082)–[Mahony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Joseph_Mahony)–[Kadomtsev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boris_Kadomtsev)–[Petviashvili](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Iosifovich_Petviashvili) equation (or simply the **BBM-KP equation**), was introduced as an alternative model for small amplitude long waves in shallow water moving mainly in the *x* direction in 2+1 space.[^o11]

$$\displaystyle \partial_x(\partial_t u+u \partial_x u+\epsilon^2\partial_{xxt}u)+\lambda\partial_{yy}u=0$$

where $\lambda=\pm 1$. The BBM-KP equation provides an alternative to the usual KP equation, in a similar way that the [Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation](wikipedia://en/16703476) is related to the classical [Korteweg–de Vries equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Korteweg–de_Vries_equation), as the linearized dispersion relation of the BBM-KP is a good approximation to that of the KP but does not exhibit the unwanted limiting behavior as the [Fourier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fourier_Analysis) variable dual to *x* approaches $\pm \infty$. The BBM-KP equation can be viewed as a weak transverse perturbation of the [Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation](wikipedia://en/16703476). As a result, the solutions of their corresponding Cauchy problems share an intriguing and complex mathematical relationship. Aguilar et al. proved that the solution of the Cauchy problem for the BBM-KP model equation converges to the solution of the Cauchy problem associated to the [Benjamin–Bona–Mahony equation](wikipedia://en/16703476) in the $L^2$ -based [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) $H^{k}_{x}(\mathbb{R})$ for all $k \ge 1$, provided their corresponding initial data are close in $H^{k}_{x}(\mathbb{R})$ as the transverse variable $y \rightarrow \pm \infty$.[^o12]

## History {#History}

*Image: RIAN archive 151311 Russian physicist Boris Kadomtsev.jpg.* Boris Kadomtsev.

The KP equation was first written in 1970 by Soviet physicists Boris B. Kadomtsev (1928–1998) and Vladimir I. Petviashvili (1936–1993); it came as a natural generalization of the KdV equation (derived by Korteweg and De Vries in 1895). Whereas in the KdV equation waves are strictly one-dimensional, in the KP equation this restriction is relaxed. Still, both in the KdV and the KP equation, waves have to travel in the positive *x*-direction.

## Connections to physics {#Connections_to_physics}

The KP equation can be used to model [water waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_wave) of long [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) with weakly non-linear restoring forces and [frequency dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650). If [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) is weak compared to [gravitational forces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_gravity), $\lambda=+1$ is used; if surface tension is strong, then $\lambda=-1$. Because of the asymmetry in the way *x*- and *y*-terms enter the equation, the waves described by the KP equation behave differently in the direction of propagation (*x*-direction) and transverse (*y*) direction; oscillations in the *y*-direction tend to be smoother (be of small-deviation).

The KP equation can also be used to model waves in [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetism) media,[^o15] as well as two-dimensional matter–wave pulses in [Bose–Einstein condensates](wikipedia://en/4474).

## Limiting behavior {#Limiting_behavior}

For $\epsilon\ll 1$, typical *x*-dependent oscillations have a wavelength of $O(1/\epsilon)$ giving a singular limiting regime as $\epsilon\rightarrow 0$. The limit $\epsilon\rightarrow 0$ is called the [dispersionless](wikipedia://en/16901031) limit.[^o17][^o18][^o19]

If we also assume that the solutions are independent of *y* as $\epsilon\rightarrow 0$, then they also satisfy the inviscid [Burgers' equation](wikipedia://en/1023353):

$$\displaystyle \partial_t u+u\partial_x u=0.$$

Suppose the amplitude of oscillations of a solution is asymptotically small — $O(\epsilon)$ — in the dispersionless limit. Then the amplitude satisfies a mean-field equation of [Davey–Stewartson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Davey–Stewartson_equations) type.
