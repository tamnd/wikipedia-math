# W-curve

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="W-curve"}

In geometry, a **W-curve** is a curve in [projective *n*-space](wikipedia://en/242135) that is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under a [1-parameter group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1-parameter_group) of [projective transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformations). W-curves were first investigated by [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) and [Sophus Lie](wikipedia://en/54010) in 1871, who also named them. W-curves in the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325) can be constructed with [straightedge](wikipedia://en/147854) alone. Many well-known curves are W-curves, among them [conics](wikipedia://en/19008673), [logarithmic spirals](wikipedia://en/42373), powers (like *y* = *x*<sup>3</sup>), [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860) and the [helix](wikipedia://en/179924), but not e.g. the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine). W-curves occur widely in the realm of plants.

:::

*Image: PlaneWcurve.svg.* A typical plane W-curve with source O and sink Y

## Name {#Name}

The 'W' stands for the German 'Wurf' – a *throw* – which in this context refers to a series of four points on a line. A 1-dimensional W-curve (read: the motion of a point on a projective line) is determined by such a series.

The German "W-Kurve" sounds almost exactly like "Weg-Kurve" and the last can be translated by "path curve". That is why in the English literature one often finds "path curve" or "pathcurve".
