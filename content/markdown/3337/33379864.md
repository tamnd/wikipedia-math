# Henry Perigal

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Henry Perigal"}

**Henry Perigal, Jr.** [FRAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Astronomical_Society) [MRI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution) (1 April 1801 – 6 June 1898) was a British stockbroker and [amateur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur) mathematician, known for his [dissection](wikipedia://en/2915839)-based proof of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) and for his unorthodox belief that the moon does not rotate.[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6][^o7][^o8]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Perigal descended from a [Huguenot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Huguenot) family who emigrated to England in the late 17th century,[^o6][^o7][^o8] and was the oldest of six siblings.[^o8] After working as a clerk for the [Privy Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Privy_Council_of_the_United_Kingdom), he became a bookkeeper in a London stockbrokerage in the 1840s.[^o7][^o8] He remained a lifelong bachelor.[^o3]

Perigal was a member of the [London Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/591097) from 1868 to 1897,[^o4] and was treasurer of the [Royal Meteorological Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Meteorological_Society) for 45 years, from 1853 until his death in 1898.[^o5] He was elected as a [fellow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow) of the [Royal Astronomical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Astronomical_Society) in 1850.[^o8] He attended the [Royal Institution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Royal_Institution) regularly as a visitor for many years, and finally became a member in 1895, at age 94.[^o3][^o8] Friends with [Washington Teasdale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington_Teasdale) and [James Glaisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Glaisher).[^o10] He was an original member of the British Astronomical Association in 1890; he would be oldest member of the BAA if all the members were gathered together. Although Perigal was long-lived, his father lived even longer, becoming a [centenarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centenarian).[^o7]

## Mathematics {#Mathematics}

*Image: Perigal 1891 p1.jpg.* Page 1 of *Geometric Dissections and Transpositions*, showing Perigal's dissection-based proof of the Pythagorean theorem

In his booklet *Geometric Dissections and Transpositions* (London: Bell & Sons, 1891) Perigal provided a proof of the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) based on the idea of [dissecting](wikipedia://en/2915839) two smaller squares into a larger square. The five-piece dissection that he found may be generated by overlaying a regular square tiling whose [prototile](wikipedia://en/39372) is the larger square with a [Pythagorean tiling](wikipedia://en/33371056) generated by the two smaller squares.[^o3][^o12] Perigal had the same dissection printed on his business cards, and it also appears on his tombstone,[^o3][^o7] which is located within the grounds of [St Mary and St Peter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Mary_and_St_Peter's_Church,_Wennington), [Wennington](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wennington,_London), [Essex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essex), now the [London Borough of Havering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Borough_of_Havering).[^o13]

In the same book, Perigal expressed the hope that dissection based methods would also solve the 1925 Tarski's problem of [circle-squaring by dissection](wikipedia://en/216811). That problem had been shown to be impossible to solve in a constructive way in 1963.[^o14] Nevertheless, a non-constructive solution has been proposed by [Miklós Laczkovich](wikipedia://en/6021299) in 1990.[^o15]

Perigal also proposed the first 6-pieces solution to the [square trisection](wikipedia://en/31982221) problem.

As well as being interested in mathematics, Perigal was an accomplished [lathe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lathe) worker, and made models of mathematical curves for [Augustus De Morgan](wikipedia://en/18949289). He falsely believed that the moon does not rotate with respect to the fixed stars, and used his knowledge of curvilinear motion in an attempt to demonstrate this belief to others.[^o3][^o7]
