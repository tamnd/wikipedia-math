# Daniel Shanks

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Daniel Shanks"}

**Daniel Charles Shanks** (January 17, 1917 – September 6, 1996) was an [American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Americans) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who worked primarily in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). He was the first person [to compute π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_approximations_of_π) to 100,000 decimal places.

:::

## Life and education {#Life_and_education}

Shanks was born on January 17, 1917, in [Chicago, Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chicago,_Illinois). He is not related to the English mathematician [William Shanks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Shanks), who was also known for his computation of π. He earned his [Bachelor of Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Science) degree in physics from the [University of Chicago](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Chicago) in 1937, and a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D.) in Mathematics from the [University of Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Maryland,_College_Park) in 1954. Prior to obtaining his PhD, Shanks worked at the [Aberdeen Proving Ground](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen_Proving_Ground) and the [Naval Ordnance Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Ordnance_Laboratory), first as a physicist and then as a mathematician. During this period he wrote his PhD thesis, which he completed in 1949, despite having never taken any graduate math courses.[^o4]

After earning his PhD in mathematics, Shanks continued working at the [Naval Ordnance Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Ordnance_Laboratory) and the Naval Ship Research and Development Center at [David Taylor Model Basin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Taylor_Model_Basin), where he stayed until 1976. He spent one year at the [National Bureau of Standards](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Bureau_of_Standards) before moving to the [University of Maryland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Maryland,_College_Park) as an adjunct professor. He remained in Maryland for the rest of his life.[^o4] Shanks died on September 6, 1996.[^o4]

## Works {#Works}

Shanks worked primarily in [numerical analysis](wikipedia://en/21506) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527); however, he had many interests and also worked on [black body](wikipedia://en/44364) radiation, [ballistics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ballistics), [mathematical identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_identities), and [Epstein zeta functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epstein_zeta_function).[^o4]

### Numerical analysis {#Numerical_analysis}

Shanks's most prominent work in numerical analysis was a collaboration with [John Wrench](wikipedia://en/22533779) and others to [compute the number π](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_approximations_of_π) to 100,000 decimal digits on a computer.[^o7] This was done in 1961 on an [IBM 7090](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_7090), and it was a major advancement over previous work.[^o4]

Shanks was an editor of the *[Mathematics of Computation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematics_of_Computation)* from 1959 until his death.[^o8] He was noted for his very thorough reviews of papers, and for doing whatever was necessary to get the journal out.[^o4]

### Number theory {#Number_theory}

Shanks wrote the book *Solved and Unsolved Problems in Number Theory*,[^o10] which mostly depended on [quadratic residues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_residues) and [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877). The third edition of the book contains a long essay on judging conjectures,[^o10] in which Shanks contended that unless there is a lot of evidence to suggest that something is true, it should not be classified as a conjecture, but rather as an open question. His essay provided many examples of bad thinking that were derived from premature conjecturing. Writing about the possible non-existence of odd [perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_numbers), which had been checked to 10<sup>50</sup>, he famously remarked that "10<sup>50</sup> is a long way from infinity."[^o10]

Most of Shanks's number theory work was in [computational number theory](wikipedia://en/511466). He developed a number of fast computer factorization methods based on [quadratic forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_forms) and the [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_%28number_theory%29).[^o4] His [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms) include: [Baby-step giant-step](wikipedia://en/563928) algorithm for computing the [discrete logarithm](wikipedia://en/181334), which is useful in [public-key cryptography](wikipedia://en/24222); [Shanks's square forms factorization](wikipedia://en/3387328), [integer factorization](wikipedia://en/15491) method that generalizes [Fermat's factorization method](wikipedia://en/2132433); and the [Tonelli–Shanks algorithm](wikipedia://en/3667375) that finds square roots modulo a prime, which is useful for the [quadratic sieve](wikipedia://en/582340) method of [integer factorization](wikipedia://en/15491).

In 1974, Shanks and [John Wrench](wikipedia://en/22533779) did some of the first computer work on estimating the value of [Brun's constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brun's_constant), the sum of the reciprocals of the [twin primes](wikipedia://en/41997), calculating it over the twin primes among the first two million primes.[^o11]
