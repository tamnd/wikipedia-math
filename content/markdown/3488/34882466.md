# Turing pattern

*Image: TuringPattern.PNG.* Three examples of Turing patterns

*Image: Six States - Turing Patterns.jpg.* Six stable states from Turing equations, the last one forms Turing patterns

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Turing pattern"}

The **Turing pattern** is a concept introduced by English mathematician [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208) in a 1952 paper titled "[The Chemical Basis of Morphogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Chemical_Basis_of_Morphogenesis)", which describes how [patterns in nature](wikipedia://en/35659147), such as stripes and spots, can arise naturally and autonomously from a homogeneous, uniform state.[^o2][^o3] The pattern arises due to [Turing instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_instability), which in turn arises due to the interplay between differential [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) of chemical species and chemical reaction.[^o4] The instability mechanism is surprising because a pure diffusion, such as [molecular diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_diffusion), would be expected to have a stabilizing influence on the system (i.e., complete mixing).

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

In his paper,[^o2] Turing examined the behaviour of a system in which two diffusible substances interact with each other, and found that such a system is able to generate a spatially periodic pattern even from a random or almost uniform initial condition.[^o6] Prior to the discovery of this instability mechanism arising due to unequal diffusion coefficients of the two substances, diffusional effects were always presumed to have stabilizing influences on the system.

Turing hypothesized that the resulting wavelike patterns are the chemical basis of [morphogenesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morphogenesis).[^o6] Turing patterning is often found in combination with other patterns: vertebrate [limb development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limb_development) is one of the many [phenotypes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenotypes) exhibiting Turing patterning overlapped with a complementary pattern (in this case a [French flag model](wikipedia://en/14578596)).[^o7]

Before Turing, [Yakov Zeldovich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yakov_Zeldovich) in 1944 discovered this instability mechanism in connection with the cellular structures observed in lean [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen) flames.[^o8] Zeldovich explained the cellular structure as a consequence of hydrogen's diffusion coefficient being larger than the [thermal diffusion coefficient](wikipedia://en/58282). In [combustion](wikipedia://en/5638) literature, Turing instability is referred to as [diffusive–thermal instability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusive–thermal_instability).

## Concept {#Concept}

*Image: Turing bifurcation 3.gif.* A Turing [bifurcation](wikipedia://en/2326042) pattern

*Image: Giant Pufferfish skin pattern detail.jpg.* An example of a natural Turing pattern on a [giant pufferfish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giant_pufferfish)

The original theory, a [reaction–diffusion](wikipedia://en/8635379) theory of morphogenesis, has served as an important model in [theoretical biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_biology).[^o10] Reaction–diffusion systems have attracted much interest as a prototype model for [pattern formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern_formation). Patterns such as [fronts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Front_%28physics%29), [hexagons](wikipedia://en/59733), [spirals](wikipedia://en/61563), [stripes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stripe_%28pattern%29) and [dissipative solitons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissipative_soliton) are found as solutions of Turing-like [reaction–diffusion equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reaction–diffusion_equation).[^o11]

Turing proposed a model wherein two homogeneously distributed substances (P and S) interact to produce stable patterns during morphogenesis. These patterns represent regional differences in the concentrations of the two substances. Their interactions would produce an ordered structure out of random chaos.[^o12]

In Turing's model, substance P promotes the production of more substance P as well as substance S. However, substance S inhibits the production of substance P; if S diffuses more readily than P, sharp waves of concentration differences will be generated for substance P. An important feature of Turing's model is that particular wavelengths in the substances' distribution will be amplified while other wavelengths will be suppressed.[^o12]

The parameters depend on the physical system under consideration. In the context of fish skin pigmentation, the associated equation is a three field reaction–diffusion one in which the linear parameters are associated with pigmentation cell concentration and the diffusion parameters are not the same for all fields.[^o13] In dye-doped [liquid crystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid_crystals), a photoisomerization process in the liquid crystal matrix is described as a reaction–diffusion equation of two fields (liquid crystal order parameter and concentration of cis-isomer of the azo-dye).[^o14] The systems have very different physical mechanisms on the chemical reactions and diffusive process, but on a phenomenological level, both have the same ingredients.

Turing-like patterns have also been demonstrated to arise in developing organisms without the classical requirement of diffusible morphogens. Studies in chick and mouse embryonic development suggest that the patterns of feather and hair-follicle precursors can be formed without a morphogen pre-pattern, and instead are generated through self-aggregation of mesenchymal cells underlying the skin.[^o15][^o16] In these cases, a uniform population of cells can form regularly patterned aggregates that depend on the mechanical properties of the cells themselves and the rigidity of the surrounding extra-cellular environment. Regular patterns of cell aggregates of this sort were originally proposed in a theoretical model formulated by George Oster, which postulated that alterations in cellular motility and stiffness could give rise to different self-emergent patterns from a uniform field of cells.[^o17] This mode of pattern formation may act in tandem with classical reaction-diffusion systems, or independently to generate patterns in biological development.

Turing patterns may also be responsible for the formation of human [fingerprints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fingerprint).[^o18]

As well as in biological organisms, Turing patterns occur in other natural systems – for example, the wind patterns formed in sand, the atomic-scale repetitive ripples that can form during growth of bismuth crystals, and the uneven distribution of matter in [galactic disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galactic_disc).[^o19][^o20] Although Turing's ideas on morphogenesis and Turing patterns remained dormant for many years, they are now inspirational for much research in [mathematical biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_biology).[^o21] It is a major theory in [developmental biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_biology); the importance of the Turing model is obvious, as it provides an answer to the fundamental question of morphogenesis: "how is spatial information generated in organisms?".[^o6]

Turing patterns can also be created in nonlinear optics as demonstrated by the [Lugiato–Lefever equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lugiato–Lefever_equation). Reaction-diffusion models can be used to forecast the exact location of the tooth cusps in mice and voles based on differences in gene expression patterns.[^o12] The model can be used to explain the differences in gene expression between mice and vole teeth, the [signaling center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Developmental_signaling_center) of the tooth, enamel knot, secrets BMPs, FGFs and Shh. Shh and FGF inhibits BMP production, while BMP stimulates both the production of more BMPs and the synthesis of their own inhibitors. BMPs also induce epithelial differentiation, while FGFs induce epithelial growth.[^o22] The result is a pattern of gene activity that changes as the shape of the tooth changes, and vice versa. Under this model, the large differences between mouse and vole molars can be generated by small changes in the binding constants and diffusion rates of the BMP and Shh proteins. A small increase in the diffusion rate of BMP4 and a stronger binding constant of its inhibitor is sufficient to change the vole pattern of tooth growth into that of the mouse.[^o22][^o23]

Experiments with the sprouting of [chia seeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chia_seed) planted in trays have confirmed Turing's mathematical model.[^o24]

## Biological application {#Biological_application}

*Image: Turin1.png.* Simulations of effect of limb bud distal expansion[^o26]

A mechanism that has gained increasing attention as a generator of spot- and stripe-like patterns in developmental systems is related to the chemical reaction-diffusion process described by Turing in 1952. This has been schematized in a biological "local autoactivation-lateral inhibition" (LALI) framework by Meinhardt and Gierer.[^o27] LALI systems, while formally similar to reaction-diffusion systems, are more suitable to biological applications, since they include cases where the activator and inhibitor terms are mediated by cellular "reactors" rather than simple chemical reactions,[^o28] and spatial transport can be mediated by mechanisms in addition to simple diffusion.[^o29] These models can be applied to limb formation and teeth development among other examples.
