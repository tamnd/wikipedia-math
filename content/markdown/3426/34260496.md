# Hadamard product (matrices)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hadamard product"}

*Image: Hadamard product qtl1.svg.* The Hadamard product operates on identically shaped matrices and produces a third matrix of the same dimensions.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Hadamard product** (also known as the **element-wise product**, **entrywise product**[^o16] or **Schur product**)[^o2] is a [binary operation](wikipedia://en/3948) that takes in two [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) of the same dimensions and returns a matrix of the multiplied corresponding elements. This operation can be thought as a "naive matrix multiplication" and is different from the [matrix product](wikipedia://en/125280). It is attributed to, and named after, either French mathematician [Jacques Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675) or Russian mathematician [Issai Schur](wikipedia://en/617522).

:::

The Hadamard product is [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative) and [distributive](wikipedia://en/103118). Unlike the matrix product, it is also [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative).[^o3]

## History {#History}

Entrywise multiplication first appeared as a relevant concept in Hadamard's 1899 work on [power series](wikipedia://en/59958),[^o5] where he proved the [Hadamard multiplication theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hadamard_multiplication_theorem). [Paul Halmos](wikipedia://en/318598) is credited for the earliest use of the term "Hadamard product" in the context of matrices, and helped popularize it.[^o6] Halmos later reported that the name was suggested to him by [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) and it is conjectured that it was because of the analogy with the well-known theorem of Hadamard.[^o7]

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

For two matrices $A$ and $B$ of the same dimension $m \times n$, the Hadamard product $A \odot B$ (machine learning convention) or $A \circ B$ (matrix analysis convention)[^o10][^o11][^o12] is a matrix of the same dimension as the operands, with elements given by[^o3]

$$(A \odot B)_{ij} = (A)_{ij} (B)_{ij}.$$

For matrices of different dimensions ($m \times n$ and $p \times q$, where $m \ne p$ or $n \ne q$), the Hadamard product is undefined.

An example of the Hadamard product for two arbitrary 2 × 3 matrices:

$$\begin{bmatrix} 2 & 3 & 1 \\ 0 & 8 & -2 \end{bmatrix} \odot \begin{bmatrix} 3 & 1 & 4 \\ 7 & 9 & 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 \times 3 & 3 \times 1 & 1 \times 4 \\ 0 \times 7 & 8 \times 9 & -2 \times 5 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 6 & 3 & 4 \\ 0 & 72 & -10 \end{bmatrix}.$$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

- The Hadamard product is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) (when working with a commutative ring), [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), and [distributive](wikipedia://en/103118) over addition. That is, if *A*, *B*, and *C* are matrices of the same size, and *k* is a scalar: $$\begin{aligned} A \odot B &= B \odot A, \\ A \odot (B \odot C) &= (A \odot B) \odot C, \\ A \odot (B + C) &= A \odot B + A \odot C, \\ (kA) \odot B &= A \odot (kB) = k(A \odot B), \\ A \odot 0 &= 0 \odot A = 0. \end{aligned}$$
- The identity matrix under Hadamard multiplication of two $m \times n$ matrices is an [$m \times n$ matrix where all elements are equal to 1](wikipedia://en/690246). This is different from the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) under regular matrix multiplication, where only the elements of the main diagonal are equal to 1. Furthermore, a matrix has an inverse under Hadamard multiplication if and only if all of the elements are invertible, or equivalently over a field, if and only if none of the elements are equal to zero.[^o15]
- For vectors $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ and corresponding diagonal matrices $D_{\mathbf{x}}$ and $D_{\mathbf{y}}$ with these vectors as their main diagonals, the following identity holds:[^o16] $$\mathbf{x}^* (A \odot B)\mathbf{y} = \operatorname{tr}\left({D}_\mathbf{x}^* A {D}_\mathbf{y} {B}^\mathsf{T}\right),$$ where $\mathbf{x}^{*}$ denotes the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of $\mathbf{x}$. In particular, using vectors of ones, this shows that the sum of all elements in the Hadamard product is the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of $AB^{T}$ where superscript T denotes the [matrix transpose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_transpose), that is, $\operatorname{tr}\left(AB^{\mathsf T}\right) = \mathbf{1}^\mathsf{T}\left(A\odot B\right)\mathbf{1}$. A related result for square $A$ and $B$, is that the row-sums of their Hadamard product are the diagonal elements of $AB^{T}$:[^o26] $$\sum_i (A \odot B)_{ij} = \left(B^\mathsf{T} A\right)_{jj} = \left(AB^\mathsf{T}\right)_{ii}.$$ Similarly, $$(\mathbf{y}\mathbf{x}^*) \odot A = D_\mathbf{y} A D_\mathbf{x}^*.$$ Furthermore, a Hadamard matrix–vector product can be expressed as $$(A \odot B) \mathbf{y} = \operatorname{diag}(A D_\mathbf{y} B^\mathsf{T}),$$ where $\operatorname{diag}(M)$ is the vector formed from the diagonals of matrix $M$. Taking $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{1}$, this implies that $$(A \odot B) \mathbf{1} = \operatorname{diag}(A B^\mathsf{T})$$
- The Hadamard product is a principal [submatrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submatrix) of the [Kronecker product](wikipedia://en/712430).[^o17][^o18][^o19]
- The Hadamard product satisfies the rank inequality $$\operatorname{rank}(A \odot B) \leq \operatorname{rank}(A) \operatorname{rank}(B).$$
- If $A$ and $B$ are [positive-definite matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix), then the following inequality involving the Hadamard product holds:[^o20] $$\prod_{i=k}^n \lambda_i(A \odot B) \ge \prod_{i=k}^n \lambda_i(A B),\quad k = 1, \ldots, n,$$ where $\lambda_{i}(A)$ is the $i$th largest [eigenvalue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of $A$.
- If $D$ and $E$ are [diagonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diagonal_matrices), then[^o21] $$\begin{aligned} D (A \odot B) E &= (D A E) \odot B = (D A) \odot (B E) \\ &= (AE) \odot (D B) = A \odot (D B E). \end{aligned}$$
- The Hadamard product of two vectors $\mathbf a$ and $\mathbf b$ is the same as matrix multiplication of the corresponding [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) of one vector by the other vector: $$\mathbf a \odot \mathbf b = D_\mathbf{a} \mathbf b = D_\mathbf{b} \mathbf a.$$
- The [$\operatorname{diag}$ operator](wikipedia://en/174080#Vector-to-matrix_diag_operator) transforming a vector to a diagonal matrix may be expressed using the Hadamard product as $$\operatorname{diag}(\mathbf{a}) = (\mathbf{a} \mathbf{1}^T) \odot I,$$ where $\mathbf{1}$ is a constant vector with elements $1$, and $I$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

:::

## The mixed-product property {#The_mixed-product_property}

The Hadamard product obeys certain relationships with other matrix product operators.

- If $\otimes$ is the [Kronecker product](wikipedia://en/712430), assuming $A$ has the same dimensions as $C$ and $B$ as $D$, then $$(A \otimes B) \odot (C \otimes D) = (A \odot C) \otimes (B \odot D) .$$
- If $\bullet$ is the [face-splitting product](wikipedia://en/64530953#Face-splitting_product), then[^o23] $$(A \bullet B) \odot (C \bullet D) = (A \odot C) \bullet (B \odot D).$$
- If $\ast$ is the column-wise [Khatri–Rao product](wikipedia://en/64530953), then $$(A \bullet B)(C \ast D) = (A C) \odot (B D).$$

::: {#o25 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Schur product theorem"}

## Schur product theorem {#Schur_product_theorem}

The Hadamard product of two [positive-semidefinite matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix) is positive-semidefinite.[^o3][^o26] This is known as the Schur product theorem,[^o15] after Russian mathematician [Issai Schur](wikipedia://en/617522). For two positive-semidefinite matrices $A$ and $B$, it is also known that the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of their Hadamard product is greater than or equal to the product of their respective determinants:[^o26]

$$\det({A} \odot {B}) \ge \det({A}) \det({B}).$$

:::

## Analogous operations {#Analogous_operations}

Other Hadamard operations are also seen in the mathematical literature,[^o28] namely the *Hadamard root* and *Hadamard power* (which are in effect the same thing because of fractional indices), defined for a matrix such that:

For

$$\begin{aligned} {B} &= {A}^{\circ 2} \\ B_{ij} &= {A_{ij}}^2 \end{aligned}$$

and for

$$\begin{aligned} {B} &= {A}^{\circ \frac12} \\ B_{ij} &= {A_{ij}}^\frac12 \end{aligned}$$

The *Hadamard inverse* reads:[^o28]

$$\begin{aligned} {B} &= {A}^{\circ -1} \\ B_{ij} &= {A_{ij}}^{-1} \end{aligned}$$

A *Hadamard division* is defined as:[^o29][^o30]

$$\begin{aligned} {C} &= {A} \oslash {B} \\ C_{ij} &= \frac{A_{ij}}{B_{ij}} \end{aligned}$$

## In programming languages {#In_programming_languages}

Most scientific or numerical [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_languages) include the Hadamard product, under various names.

In [MATLAB](wikipedia://en/20412), the Hadamard product is expressed as "dot multiply": `a .* b`, or the function call: `times(a, b)`.[^o32] It also has analogous dot operators which include, for example, the operators `a .^ b` and `a ./ b`.[^o33] Because of this mechanism, it is possible to reserve `*` and `^` for matrix multiplication and matrix exponentials, respectively.

The programming language [Julia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julia_%28programming_language%29) has similar syntax as MATLAB, where Hadamard multiplication is called *broadcast multiplication* and also denoted with `a .* b`, and other operators are analogously defined element-wise, for example Hadamard powers use `a .^ b`.[^o34] But unlike MATLAB, in Julia this "dot" syntax is generalized with a generic *broadcasting operator `.`* which can apply any function element-wise. This includes both binary operators (such as the aforementioned multiplication and exponentiation, as well as any other binary operator such as the Kronecker product), and also unary operators such as `!` and `√`. Thus, any function in [prefix notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prefix_notation) `f` can be applied as `f.(x)`.[^o35]

[Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_%28programming_language%29) does not have built-in array support, leading to inconsistent/conflicting notations. The [NumPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NumPy) numerical library interprets `a*b` or `a.multiply(b)` as the Hadamard product, and uses `a@b` or `a.matmul(b)` for the matrix product. With the [SymPy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SymPy) symbolic library, multiplication of array objects as either `a*b` or `a@b` will produce the matrix product. The Hadamard product can be obtained with the method call `a.multiply_elementwise(b)`.[^o36] Some Python packages include support for Hadamard powers using methods like `np.power(a, b)`, or the [Pandas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandas_%28software%29) method `a.pow(b)`.

In C++, the [Eigen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigen_%28C++_library%29) library provides a `cwiseProduct` member function for the Matrix class (`a.cwiseProduct(b)`), while the [Armadillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armadillo_%28C++_library%29) library uses the operator `%` to make compact expressions (`a % b`; `a * b` is a matrix product).

In [GAUSS](wikipedia://en/5516230), and [HP Prime](wikipedia://en/40988986), the operation is known as *array multiplication.*

In [Fortran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fortran), [R](wikipedia://en/376707), [APL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APL_%28programming_language%29), [J](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J_%28programming_language%29), and [Wolfram Language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wolfram_Language) ([Mathematica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematica)), the multiplication operator `*` or `×` apply the Hadamard product, whereas the matrix product is written using `matmul`, `%*%`, `+.×`, `+/ .*` and `.`, respectively.

In the [Maple programming language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maple_%28software%29), the multiplication operator `*` is not defined for Matrices and Vectors. The matrix product is written using `.`, while the Hadamard product may be obtained using `*~`. (The `~` is a 'broadcast operator' similar to `.` in Julia.)

The R package [matrixcalc](https://cran.r-project.org/web/packages/matrixcalc/matrixcalc.pdf) introduces the function `hadamard.prod()` for Hadamard Product of numeric matrices or vectors.[^o37]

The computer algebra system [Maxima](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxima_%28software%29) uses `.` for matrix multiplication and `*` for the Hadamard Product.

::: {#o39 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The Hadamard product appears in [lossy compression](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lossy_compression) algorithms such as [JPEG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JPEG). The decoding step involves an entry-for-entry product, in other words the Hadamard product.

In [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing), the Hadamard operator can be used for enhancing, suppressing or masking image regions. One matrix represents the original image, the other acts as weight or masking matrix.

It is used in the [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) literature, for example, to describe the architecture of recurrent neural networks as [GRUs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gated_recurrent_unit) or [LSTMs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Long_short-term_memory).[^o40]

It is also used to study the statistical properties of random vectors and matrices.[^o41][^o42]

:::

## The penetrating face product {#The_penetrating_face_product}

*Image: Penetrating face product.jpg.* The penetrating face product of matrices

According to the definition of [V. Slyusar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vadym_Slyusar) the penetrating face product of the *p*×*g* matrix ${A}$ and *n*-dimensional matrix ${B}$ (*n* > 1) with *p*×*g* blocks (${B} = [B_n]$) is a matrix of size ${B}$ of the form:[^o44]

$${A} [\circ] {B} = \left[\begin{array} { c | c | c | c } {A} \circ {B}_1 & {A} \circ {B}_2 & \cdots & {A} \circ {B}_n \end{array}\right].$$

::: {#o46 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

If

$${A} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 2 & 3 \\ 4 & 5 & 6 \\ 7 & 8 & 9 \end{bmatrix},\quad {B} = \left[\begin{array} { c | c | c } {B}_1 & {B}_2 & {B}_3 \end{array}\right] = \left[\begin{array} { c c c | c c c | c c c } 1 & 4 & 7 & 2 & 8 & 14 & 3 & 12 & 21 \\ 8 & 20 & 5 & 10 & 25 & 40 & 12 & 30 & 6 \\ 2 & 8 & 3 & 2 & 4 & 2 & 7 & 3 & 9 \end{array}\right]$$

then

$${A} [\circ] {B} = \left[\begin{array} { c c c | c c c | c c c } 1 & 8 & 21 & 2 & 16 & 42 & 3 & 24 & 63 \\ 32 & 100 & 30 & 40 & 125 & 240 & 48 & 150 & 36 \\ 14 & 64 & 27 & 14 & 32 & 18 & 49 & 24 & 81 \end{array}\right].$$

:::

### Main properties {#Main_properties}

${A} [\circ] {B} = {B} [\circ] {A};$[^o44]

$${M} \bullet {M} = {M} [\circ] \left( {M} \otimes \mathbf {1}^\textsf{T}\right),$$

where $\bullet$ denotes the [face-splitting product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face-splitting_product) of matrices,

$\mathbf{c} \bullet {M} = \mathbf {c} [\circ] {M},$ where $\mathbf {c}$ is a vector.

::: {#o49 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

### Applications {#Applications}

The penetrating face product is used in the [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965)-matrix theory of [digital antenna arrays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_antenna_array).[^o44] This operation can also be used in [artificial neural network](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artificial_neural_network) models, specifically convolutional layers.[^o50]

:::
