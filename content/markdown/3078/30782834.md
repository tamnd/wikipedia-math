# Matched Z-transform method

*Image: Chebyshev s plane.svg.* The *s*-plane poles and zeros of a 5th-order [Chebyshev type II lowpass filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev_filter#Type_II_Chebyshev_filters) to be approximated as a discrete-time filter

*Image: Chebyshev mapped z plane.svg.* The *z*-plane poles and zeros of the discrete-time Chebyshev filter, as mapped into the z-plane using the matched Z-transform method with *T* = 1/10 second. The labeled frequency points and band-edge dotted lines have also been mapped through the function *z=e<sup>iωT</sup>*, to show how frequencies along the *iω* axis in the *s*-plane map onto the unit circle in the *z*-plane.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Matched Z-transform method"}

The **matched Z-transform method**, also called the **pole–zero mapping**[^o2][^o3] or **pole–zero matching method**,[^o4] and abbreviated **MPZ** or **MZT**,[^o5] is a technique for converting a [continuous-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous-time) filter design to a [discrete-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time) filter ([digital filter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_filter)) design.

:::

The method works by mapping all poles and zeros of the [*s*-plane](wikipedia://en/18610) design to [*z*-plane](wikipedia://en/171589) locations $z=e^{sT}$, for a sample interval $T=1 / f_\mathrm{s}$.[^o6] So an analog filter with transfer function:

$$H(s) = k_{\mathrm a} \frac{\prod_{i=1}^M (s-\xi_i) }{\prod_{i=1}^N (s-p_i) }$$

is transformed into the digital transfer function

$$H(z) = k_{\mathrm d} \frac{ \prod_{i=1}^M (1 - e^{\xi_iT}z^{-1})}{ \prod_{i=1}^N (1 - e^{p_iT}z^{-1})}$$

The gain $k_{\mathrm d}$ must be adjusted to normalize the desired gain, typically set to match the analog filter's gain at DC by [setting $s=0$ and $z=1$](wikipedia://en/19884227) and solving for $k_{\mathrm d}$.[^o4][^o7]

Since the mapping wraps the *s*-plane's $j\omega$ axis around the *z*-plane's unit circle repeatedly, any zeros (or poles) greater than the Nyquist frequency will be mapped to an aliased location.[^o8]

In the (common) case that the analog transfer function has more poles than zeros, the zeros at $s=\infty$ may optionally be shifted down to the Nyquist frequency by putting them at $z=-1$, causing the transfer function to drop off as $z \rightarrow -1$ in much the same manner as with the [bilinear transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bilinear_transform) (BLT).[^o2][^o4][^o7][^o8]

While this transform preserves [stability](wikipedia://en/1145733) and [minimum phase](wikipedia://en/548131), it preserves neither time- nor frequency-domain response and so is not widely used.[^o9][^o8] More common methods include the BLT and [impulse invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impulse_invariance) methods.[^o5] MZT does provide less high frequency response error than the BLT, however, making it easier to correct by adding additional zeros, which is called the MZTi (for "improved").[^o10]

A specific application of the *matched Z-transform method* in the digital control field is with the [Ackermann's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ackermann's_formula), which changes the poles of the [controllable](wikipedia://en/100638) system; in general from an unstable (or nearby) location to a stable location.

*Image: Chebyshev responses.svg.* Responses of the filter (dashed), and its discrete-time approximation (solid), for nominal cutoff frequency of 1 Hz, sample rate 1/T = 10 Hz. The discrete-time filter does not reproduce the Chebyshev equiripple property in the stopband due to the interference from cyclic copies of the response.
