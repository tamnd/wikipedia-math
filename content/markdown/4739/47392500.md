# Hamiltonian field theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hamiltonian field theory"}

In [theoretical physics](wikipedia://en/19594028), **Hamiltonian field theory** is the field-theoretic analogue to classical [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319). It is a formalism in [classical field theory](wikipedia://en/1293340) alongside [Lagrangian field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_field_theory). It also has applications in [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319) for a system of discrete particles is a function of their [generalized coordinates](wikipedia://en/473514) and conjugate momenta, and possibly, time. For continua and fields, Hamiltonian mechanics is unsuitable but can be extended by considering a large number of point masses, and taking the continuous limit, that is, infinitely many particles forming a continuum or field. Since each point mass has one or more [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29), the field formulation has infinitely many degrees of freedom.

### One scalar field {#One_scalar_field}

The Hamiltonian density is the continuous analogue for fields; it is a function of the fields, the conjugate "momentum" fields, and possibly the space and time coordinates themselves. For one [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879) $\varphi(\mathbf{x}, t)$, the Hamiltonian density is defined from the [Lagrangian density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_density) by[^o5]

$$\mathcal{H}(\phi, \pi, \mathbf{x},t) = \dot{\phi}\pi - \mathcal{L}(\phi, \nabla\phi, \partial \phi/\partial t , \mathbf{x},t)\,.$$

with $\nabla$ the ["del" or "nabla" operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradient_operator), $\mathbf{x}$ is the [position vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_vector) of some point in space, and $t$ is [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time). The Lagrangian density is a function of the fields in the system, their space and time derivatives, and possibly the space and time coordinates themselves. It is the field analogue to the Lagrangian function for a system of discrete particles described by generalized coordinates.

As in Hamiltonian mechanics where every generalized coordinate has a corresponding generalized momentum, the field $\varphi(\mathbf{x}, t)$ has a **conjugate momentum field** $\pi(\mathbf{x}, t)$, defined as the partial derivative of the Lagrangian density with respect to the time derivative of the field,

$$\pi = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\phi}} \,,\quad \dot{\phi}\equiv\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t}\,,$$

in which the overdot[^o6] denotes a [*partial*](wikipedia://en/52565) time derivative $\partial/\partial t$, not a [total](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative) time derivative $d/dt$.

### Many scalar fields {#Many_scalar_fields}

For many fields $\varphi_{i}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ and their conjugates $\pi_{i}(\mathbf{x}, t)$ the Hamiltonian density is a function of them all:

$$\mathcal{H}(\phi_1, \phi_2, \ldots, \pi_1, \pi_2, \ldots, \mathbf{x},t)= \sum_i\dot{\phi_i}\pi_i - \mathcal{L}(\phi_1,\phi_2,\ldots \nabla\phi_1,\nabla\phi_2,\ldots, \partial \phi_1/\partial t ,\partial \phi_2/\partial t ,\ldots, \mathbf{x},t)\,.$$

where each conjugate field is defined with respect to its field,

$$\pi_i(\mathbf{x},t) = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial\dot{\phi}_i} \,.$$

In general, for any number of fields, the [volume integral](wikipedia://en/1247265) of the Hamiltonian density gives the Hamiltonian, in three spatial dimensions:

$$H = \int \mathcal{H} \ d^3 x \,.$$

The Hamiltonian density is the Hamiltonian per unit spatial volume. The corresponding [dimension](wikipedia://en/8267) is \[energy\]\[length\]<sup>−3</sup>, in [SI units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_units) Joules per metre cubed, J m<sup>−3</sup>.

### Tensor and spinor fields {#Tensor_and_spinor_fields}

The above equations and definitions can be extended to [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641) and more generally [tensor fields](wikipedia://en/256738) and [spinor fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spinor_field). In physics, tensor fields describe [bosons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boson) and spinor fields describe [fermions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermion).

## Phase space {#Phase_space}

The fields $\varphi_{i}$ and conjugates $\pi_{i}$ form an infinite dimensional [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), because fields have an infinite number of degrees of freedom.

## Poisson bracket {#Poisson_bracket}

For two functions which depend on the fields $\varphi_{i}$ and $\pi_{i}$, their spatial derivatives, and the space and time coordinates,

$$A = \int d^3 x \mathcal{A}\left(\phi_1,\phi_2,\ldots,\pi_1,\pi_2,\ldots,\nabla\phi_1,\nabla\phi_2,\ldots,\nabla\pi_1,\nabla\pi_2,\ldots,\mathbf{x},t\right)\,,$$

$$B = \int d^3 x \mathcal{B}\left(\phi_1,\phi_2,\ldots,\pi_1,\pi_2,\ldots,\nabla\phi_1,\nabla\phi_2,\ldots,\nabla\pi_1,\nabla\pi_2,\ldots,\mathbf{x},t\right)\,,$$

and the fields are zero on the boundary of the volume the integrals are taken over, the field theoretic [Poisson bracket](wikipedia://en/294340) is defined as (not to be confused with the [anticommutator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anticommutator) from quantum mechanics).[^o13]

$$\{A,B\}_{\phi,\pi} = \int d^3 x \sum_i \left(\frac{\delta \mathcal{A}}{\delta \phi_i}\frac{\delta \mathcal{B}}{\delta \pi_i}-\frac{\delta \mathcal{B}}{\delta \phi_i}\frac{\delta \mathcal{A}}{\delta \pi_i}\right)\,,$$

where $\delta \mathcal{F}/\delta f$ is the [variational derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variational_derivative)

$$\frac{\delta \mathcal{F}}{\delta f} = \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial f} - \sum_i \nabla_i \frac{\partial \mathcal{F}}{\partial (\nabla_i f)} \,.$$

Under the same conditions of vanishing fields on the surface, the following result holds for the time evolution of $A$ (similarly for $B$):

$$\frac{dA}{dt} = \{A,H\} + \frac{\partial A}{\partial t}$$

which can be found from the total time derivative of $A$, [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252), and using the above Poisson bracket.

## Explicit time-independence {#Explicit_time-independence}

The following results are true if the Lagrangian and Hamiltonian densities are explicitly time-independent (they can still have implicit time-dependence via the fields and their derivatives),

### Kinetic and potential energy densities {#Kinetic_and_potential_energy_densities}

The Hamiltonian density is the total energy density, the sum of the kinetic energy density ($\mathcal{T}$) and the potential energy density ($\mathcal{V}$),

$$\mathcal{H} = \mathcal{T}+\mathcal{V}\,.$$

### Continuity equation {#Continuity_equation}

Taking the partial time derivative of the definition of the Hamiltonian density above, and using the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) for [implicit differentiation](wikipedia://en/470397) and the definition of the conjugate momentum field, gives the [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793):

$$\frac{\partial\mathcal{H}}{\partial t} + \nabla\cdot \mathbf{S}=0$$

in which the Hamiltonian density can be interpreted as the energy density, and

$$\mathbf{S} = \frac{\partial\mathcal{L}}{\partial(\nabla\phi)}\frac{\partial \phi}{\partial t}$$

the energy flux, or flow of energy per unit time per unit surface area.

## Relativistic field theory {#Relativistic_field_theory}

**Covariant Hamiltonian field theory** is the [relativistic](wikipedia://en/30001) formulation of Hamiltonian field theory.

Hamiltonian field theory usually means the symplectic [Hamiltonian formalism](wikipedia://en/1197531) when applied to [classical field theory](wikipedia://en/1293340), that takes the form of the instantaneous Hamiltonian formalism on an infinite-dimensional [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), and where [canonical coordinates](wikipedia://en/706311) are field functions at some instant of time.[^o19] This Hamiltonian formalism is applied to [quantization of fields](wikipedia://en/25267), e.g., in quantum [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/23912155). In Covariant Hamiltonian field theory, [canonical momenta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canonical_momenta) *p<sup>μ</sup><sub>i</sub>* corresponds to derivatives of fields with respect to all world coordinates *x<sup>μ</sup>*.[^o20] Covariant Hamilton equations are equivalent to the [Euler–Lagrange equations](wikipedia://en/24585634) in the case of hyperregular [Lagrangians](wikipedia://en/24585634). Covariant Hamiltonian field theory is developed in the Hamilton–De Donder,[^o21] polysymplectic,[^o22] multisymplectic[^o23] and *k*-symplectic[^o24] variants. A phase space of covariant Hamiltonian field theory is a finite-dimensional [polysymplectic](wikipedia://en/28356) or [multisymplectic](wikipedia://en/28356) manifold.

[Hamiltonian non-autonomous mechanics](wikipedia://en/23635894) is formulated as covariant Hamiltonian field theory on [fiber bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fiber_bundles) over the time axis, i.e. the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$.
