# Clebsch representation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Clebsch representation"}

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Clebsch representation** of an arbitrary [three-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional) [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) $\boldsymbol{v}(\boldsymbol{x})$ is:[^o2][^o3]

:::

$$\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{\nabla} \varphi + \psi\, \boldsymbol{\nabla} \chi,$$

where the [scalar fields](wikipedia://en/161879) $\varphi(\boldsymbol{x})$$, \psi(\boldsymbol{x})$ and $\chi(\boldsymbol{x})$ are known as **Clebsch potentials**[^o4] or **Monge potentials**,[^o5] named after [Alfred Clebsch](wikipedia://en/1369957) (1833–1872) and [Gaspard Monge](wikipedia://en/77840) (1746–1818), and $\boldsymbol{\nabla}$ is the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) operator.

## Background {#Background}

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034) and [plasma physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_physics), the Clebsch representation provides a means to overcome the difficulties to describe an [inviscid flow](wikipedia://en/5877457) with non-zero [vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010) – in the [Eulerian reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eulerian_reference_frame) – using [Lagrangian mechanics](wikipedia://en/23371726) and [Hamiltonian mechanics](wikipedia://en/198319).[^o7][^o8][^o9] At the [critical point](wikipedia://en/1951424) of such [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948) the result is the [Euler equations](wikipedia://en/396022), a set of equations describing the fluid flow. Note that the mentioned difficulties do not arise when describing the flow through a [variational principle](wikipedia://en/491097) in the [Lagrangian reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_reference_frame). In case of [surface gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_gravity_wave), the Clebsch representation leads to a rotational-flow form of [Luke's variational principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luke's_variational_principle).[^o10]

For the Clebsch representation to be possible, the vector field $\boldsymbol{v}$ has (locally) to be [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509), [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) and sufficiently [smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000). For global applicability $\boldsymbol{v}$ has to decay fast enough towards [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824).[^o11] The Clebsch decomposition is not unique, and (two) additional [constraints](wikipedia://en/7418540) are necessary to uniquely define the Clebsch potentials.[^o2] Since $\psi\boldsymbol{\nabla}\chi$ is in general not [solenoidal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solenoidal), the Clebsch representation does not in general satisfy the [Helmholtz decomposition](wikipedia://en/820253).[^o12]

## Vorticity {#Vorticity}

The vorticity $\boldsymbol{\omega}(\boldsymbol{x})$ is equal to[^o3]

$$\boldsymbol{\omega} = \boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{v} = \boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\left( \boldsymbol{\nabla} \varphi + \psi\, \boldsymbol{\nabla} \chi\right) = \boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi \times \boldsymbol{\nabla}\chi,$$

with the last step due to the [vector calculus identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_calculus_identity) $\boldsymbol{\nabla} \times (\psi \boldsymbol{A})=\psi(\boldsymbol{\nabla}\times\boldsymbol{A})+\boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi\times\boldsymbol{A}.$ So the vorticity $\boldsymbol{\omega}$ is perpendicular to both $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\psi$ and $\boldsymbol{\nabla}\chi,$ while further the vorticity does not depend on $\varphi.$
