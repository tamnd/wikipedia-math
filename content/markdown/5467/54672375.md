# Marshall–Olkin exponential distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Marshall–Olkin exponential distribution"}

In applied statistics, the **Marshall–Olkin exponential distribution** is any member of a certain family of continuous multivariate probability distributions with positive-valued components. It was introduced by Albert W. Marshall and [Ingram Olkin](wikipedia://en/26702356).[^o2] One of its main uses is in reliability theory, where the Marshall–Olkin copula models the dependence between random variables subjected to external shocks. [^o3] [^o4] [^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\{E_B : \varnothing \ne B\subset \{1,2,\ldots,b\}\}$ be a set of independent, [exponentially distributed](wikipedia://en/45906) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685), where $E_B$ has mean $1/\lambda_B$. Let

$$T_j=\min\{E_B:j\in B\},\ \ j=1,\ldots,b.$$

The joint distribution of $T=(T_1,\ldots,T_b)$ is called the Marshall–Olkin exponential distribution with parameters $\{\lambda _B,B\subset \{1,2,\ldots,b\}\}.$

### Concrete example {#Concrete_example}

Suppose *b* = 3. Then there are seven nonempty subsets of { 1, ..., *b* } = { 1, 2, 3 }; hence seven different exponential random variables:

$$E_{\{1\}}, E_{\{2\}}, E_{\{3\}}, E_{\{1,2\}}, E_{\{1,3\}}, E_{\{2,3\}}, E_{\{1,2,3\}}$$

Then we have:

$$\begin{aligned} T_1 & = \min\{ E_{\{1\}}, E_{\{1,2\}}, E_{\{1,3\}}, E_{\{1,2,3\}} \} \\ T_2 & = \min\{ E_{\{2\}}, E_{\{1,2\}}, E_{\{2,3\}}, E_{\{1,2,3\}} \} \\ T_3 & = \min\{ E_{\{3\}}, E_{\{1,3\}}, E_{\{2,3\}}, E_{\{1,2,3\}} \} \\ \end{aligned}$$
