# Complex random variable

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Complex random variable"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), **complex random variables** are a generalization of real-valued [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables) to [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), i.e. the possible values a complex random variable may take are complex numbers.[^o2] Complex random variables can always be considered as pairs of real random variables: their real and imaginary parts. Therefore, the distribution of one complex random variable may be interpreted as the [joint distribution](wikipedia://en/879637) of two real random variables.

:::

Some concepts of real random variables have a straightforward generalization to complex random variables—e.g., the definition of the mean of a complex random variable. Other concepts are unique to complex random variables.

Applications of complex random variables are found in [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing),[^o3] [quadrature amplitude modulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadrature_amplitude_modulation) and [information theory](wikipedia://en/14773).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A complex random variable $Z$ on the [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325) $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $Z \colon \Omega \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ such that both its real part $\Re{(Z)}$ and its imaginary part $\Im{(Z)}$ are real [random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_variables) on $(\Omega,\mathcal{F},P)$.

:::

## Probability density function {#Probability_density_function}

The probability density function of a complex random variable is defined as $f_Z(z)=f_{\Re{(Z)},\Im{(Z)}}(\Re{(z)},\Im{(z)})$, i.e. the value of the density function at a point $z \in \mathbb{C}$ is defined to be equal to the value of the joint density of the real and imaginary parts of the random variable evaluated at the point $(\Re{(z)},\Im{(z)})$.

An equivalent definition is given by $f_Z(z)=\frac{\partial^2 }{\partial x \partial y} P(\Re{(Z)} \leq x , \Im{(Z)} \leq y)$ where $x=\Re{(z)}$ and $y=\Im{(z)}$.

As in the real case the density function may not exist.

## Expectation {#Expectation}

The expectation of a complex random variable is defined based on the definition of the expectation of a real random variable:[^o18]

Note that the expectation of a complex random variable does not exist if $\operatorname{E}[\Re{(Z)}]$ or $\operatorname{E}[\Im{(Z)}]$ does not exist.

If the complex random variable $Z$ has a probability density function $f_Z(z)$, then the expectation is given by $\operatorname{E}[Z] = \iint_{\mathbb{C}}z \cdot f_Z(z) \, dx \, dy$.

If the complex random variable $Z$ has a [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725) $p_Z$ supported on $D \subset \mathbb{C}$, then the expectation is given by $\operatorname{E}[Z] = \sum_{z \in D}z \cdot p_Z(z)$.

**Properties**

Whenever the expectation of a complex random variable exists, taking the expectation and [complex conjugation](wikipedia://en/173918) commute:

$$\overline{\operatorname{E}[Z]} = \operatorname{E}[\overline Z] .$$

The expected value operator $\operatorname{E}[\cdot]$ is [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) in the sense that

$$\operatorname{E}[aZ + bW] = a\operatorname{E}[Z] + b\operatorname{E}[W]$$

for any complex coefficients $a, b$ even if $Z$ and $W$ are not [independent](wikipedia://en/27593).

**Properties**

The variance is always a nonnegative [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438). It is equal to the sum of the variances of the real and imaginary part of the complex random variable:

$$\operatorname{Var}[Z]= \operatorname{Var}[\Re{(Z)}]+\operatorname{Var}[\Im{(Z)}] .$$

The variance of a linear combination of complex random variables may be calculated using the following formula:

$$\operatorname{Var}\left[\sum_{k=1}^N a_{k}Z_k \right] = \sum_{i=1}^N \sum_{j=1}^N a_{i}\overline{a_{j}}\operatorname{Cov}[Z_i,Z_j].$$

## Covariance matrix of real and imaginary parts {#Covariance_matrix_of_real_and_imaginary_parts}

For a general complex random variable, the pair $(\Re{(Z)},\Im{(Z)})$ has a [covariance matrix](wikipedia://en/191752) of the form:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \operatorname{Var}[\Re{(Z)}] & \operatorname{Cov}[\Im{(Z)},\Re{(Z)}] \\ \operatorname{Cov}[\Re{(Z)},\Im{(Z)}] & \operatorname{Var}[\Im{(Z)}] \end{bmatrix}$$

The matrix is symmetric, so $\operatorname{Cov}[\Re{(Z)},\Im{(Z)}] = \operatorname{Cov}[\Im{(Z)},\Re{(Z)}]$

Its elements equal:

$$\begin{aligned} & \operatorname{Var}[\Re{(Z)}] = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}(\operatorname{K}_{ZZ} + \operatorname{J}_{ZZ}) \\ & \operatorname{Var}[\Im{(Z)}] = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Re}(\operatorname{K}_{ZZ} - \operatorname{J}_{ZZ}) \\ & \operatorname{Cov}[\Re{(Z)},\Im{(Z)}] = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Im}(\operatorname{J}_{ZZ}) \\ \end{aligned}$$

Conversely:

$$\begin{aligned} & \operatorname{K}_{ZZ} = \operatorname{Var}[\Re{(Z)}] + \operatorname{Var}[\Im{(Z)}] \\ & \operatorname{J}_{ZZ} = \operatorname{Var}[\Re{(Z)}] - \operatorname{Var}[\Im{(Z)}] + i2 \operatorname{Cov}[\Re{(Z)},\Im{(Z)}] \end{aligned}$$

- $\operatorname{Cov}[\alpha Z,W]=\alpha\operatorname{Cov}[Z,W]$ (Sesquilinearity)

- Uncorrelatedness: two complex random variables $Z$ and $W$ are called **uncorrelated** if $\operatorname{K}_{ZW}=\operatorname{J}_{ZW}=0$ (see also: [uncorrelatedness (probability theory)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncorrelatedness_%28probability_theory%29)).
- Orthogonality: two complex random variables $Z$ and $W$ are called **orthogonal** if $\operatorname{E}[Z\overline{W}] = 0$.

## Circular symmetry {#Circular_symmetry}

Circular symmetry of complex random variables is a common assumption used in the field of wireless communication. A typical example of a circular symmetric complex random variable is the [complex Gaussian random variable](wikipedia://en/24666969) with zero mean and zero pseudo-covariance matrix.

A complex random variable $Z$ is circularly symmetric if, for any deterministic $\phi \in [-\pi,\pi]$, the distribution of $e^{\mathrm i \phi}Z$ equals the distribution of $Z$.

**Properties**

By definition, a circularly symmetric complex random variable has

$$\operatorname{E}[Z] = \operatorname{E}[e^{\mathrm i \phi} Z] = e^{\mathrm i \phi}\operatorname{E}[Z]$$

for any $\phi$.

Thus the expectation of a circularly symmetric complex random variable can only be either zero or undefined.

Additionally,

$$\operatorname{E}[ZZ] = \operatorname{E}[e^{\mathrm i \phi} Z e^{\mathrm i \phi}Z] = e^{\mathrm 2i \phi} \operatorname{E}[ZZ]$$

for any $\phi$.

Thus the pseudo-variance of a circularly symmetric complex random variable can only be zero.

If $Z$ and $e^{\mathrm i\phi}Z$ have the same distribution, the phase of $Z$ must be uniformly distributed over $[-\pi,\pi]$ and independent of the amplitude of $Z$.[^o34]

## Proper complex random variables {#Proper_complex_random_variables}

The concept of proper random variables is unique to complex random variables, and has no correspondent concept with real random variables.

A complex random variable $Z$ is called proper if the following three conditions are all satisfied:

- $$\operatorname{E}[Z] = 0$$
- $$\operatorname{Var}[Z] < \infty$$
- $$\operatorname{E}[Z^2] = 0$$

This definition is equivalent to the following conditions. This means that a complex random variable is proper if, and only if:

- $$\operatorname{E}[Z] = 0$$
- $$\operatorname{E}[\Re{(Z)}^2]=\operatorname{E}[\Im{(Z)}^2]\neq\infty$$
- $$\operatorname{E}[\Re{(Z)}\Im{(Z)}]=0$$

::: {#o36 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**

Every circularly symmetric complex random variable with finite variance is proper.

:::

For a proper complex random variable, the covariance matrix of the pair $(\Re{(Z)},\Im{(Z)})$ has the following simple form:

$$\begin{bmatrix} \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{Var}[Z] & 0 \\ 0 & \frac{1}{2} \operatorname{Var}[Z] \end{bmatrix}.$$

I.e.:

$$\begin{aligned} & \operatorname{Var}[\Re{(Z)}] = \operatorname{Var}[\Im{(Z)}] = \tfrac{1}{2}\operatorname{Var}[Z] \\ & \operatorname{Cov}[\Re{(Z)},\Im{(Z)}] = 0 \\ \end{aligned}$$

## Cauchy–Schwarz inequality {#Cauchy–Schwarz_inequality}

The [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128) for complex random variables, which can be derived using the [Triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) and [Hölder's inequality](wikipedia://en/191538#Probability_measure), is

$$\left| \operatorname{E} \left[ Z\overline{W} \right] \right|^2 \leq \left| \operatorname{E} \left[ \left| Z\overline{W} \right| \right] \right|^2 \leq \operatorname{E} \left[ |Z|^2 \right] \operatorname{E}\left[ |W|^2 \right].$$

:::

## Characteristic function {#Characteristic_function}

The [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/3229401) of a complex random variable is a function $\mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}$ defined by

$$\varphi_Z(\omega) = \operatorname{E} \left [ e^{i\Re{(\overline{\omega} Z)}} \right ] = \operatorname{E} \left [ e^{i( \Re{(\omega)}\Re{(Z)} + \Im{(\omega)}\Im{(Z)} )} \right ].$$
