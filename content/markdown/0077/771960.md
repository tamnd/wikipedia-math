# Volker Strassen

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Volker Strassen"}

**Volker Strassen** (born April 29, 1936) is a German [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), a professor emeritus in the department of mathematics and statistics at the [University of Konstanz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Konstanz).[^o3]

:::

For important contributions to the [analysis of algorithms](wikipedia://en/2230) he has received many awards, including the [Cantor medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor_medal),[^o5] the [Konrad Zuse Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse_Medal),[^o12] the [Paris Kanellakis Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Kanellakis_Award) for work on [randomized primality testing](wikipedia://en/1013089),[^o7] the [Knuth Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knuth_Prize) for "seminal and influential contributions to the design and analysis of efficient algorithms."[^o11]

## Biography {#Biography}

*Image: VStrassen 19791026 340.jpg.* Strassen in 1979

Strassen was born on April 29, 1936, in [Düsseldorf-Gerresheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Düsseldorf-Gerresheim).[^o5] After studying music, philosophy, physics, and mathematics at several German universities,[^o5] he received his Ph.D. in mathematics in 1962 from the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen) under the supervision of Konrad Jacobs. He then took a position at the department of mathematics and later at the department of statistics at the [University of California, Berkeley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_California,_Berkeley), where he eventually became an associate professor.

In 1968, Strassen moved to [Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zurich) as the director of the [Seminar (later Institute) for Applied Mathematics](wikipedia://en/42563941). He remained at the [University of Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zurich) for twenty years before moving to the [University of Konstanz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Konstanz) in 1988.[^o5] He retired in 1998.[^o7]

## Research (selected papers) {#Research_(selected_papers)}

Strassen began his research as a [probability theorist](wikipedia://en/23542) with his 1964 paper *An Invariance Principle for the Law of the Iterated Logarithm*. Strassen's law of the iterated logarithm has been widely cited and led to a 1966 presentation at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in Moscow.

In 1965, Strassen briefly worked under the guidance of [Kolmogorov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrei_Kolmogorov).[^o9] That year, he published *The Existence of Probability Measures with Given Marginals*, where he derived necessary and sufficient conditions for a sequence of [probability measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability_measures) to be the sequence of distributions of a [martingale](wikipedia://en/274536), a sub-martingale, or of partial sums of independent [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685).

In his *[Habilitationsschrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitationsschrift)* *Almost Sure Behavior of Sums of Independent Random Variables and Martingales*, Strassen refined and extended his previous work.

In 1969, Strassen began his work on the invention of [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) and [lower complexity bounds](wikipedia://en/7543) with the paper *Gaussian Elimination is Not Optimal*. In this paper, he introduced what is now known as [Strassen's algorithm](wikipedia://en/771965), an algorithm for performing the [multiplication of large matrices](wikipedia://en/125280) much faster than the naive O(n³) time bound. In the same paper, he also presented an asymptotically fast algorithm for [matrix inversion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_inversion), based on the fast matrix multiplication method. This result was a significant theoretical breakthrough, spurring further research into fast matrix multiplication. Despite later theoretical improvements, Strassen’s algorithm remains a practical method for multiplying dense matrices of moderate to large sizes.

In 1971, Strassen co-authored a paper with [Arnold Schönhage](wikipedia://en/1354452) on the fast multiplication of large integers. The [Schönhage–Strassen algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schönhage–Strassen_algorithm) remained the fastest such algorithm for multiple decades, and is still used for many practical applications.

In 1973, [Huber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_J._Huber) and Strassen collaborated on the paper *Minimax Tests and the Neyman–Pearson Lemma for Capacities*, which, in Huber’s subsequent work, became foundational to the field of [robust statistics](wikipedia://en/2885691).

At the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in Vancouver in 1974, Strassen presented the paper *Some Results in Algebraic Complexity Theory*, where he introduced nonlinear lower bounds for the algebraic complexity of several important computational problems. He continued this line of work in the 1976 paper *Computational Complexity over Finite Fields*.

In 1977, Strassen collaborated with [Robert M. Solovay](wikipedia://en/3160110) on the [Solovay–Strassen primality test](wikipedia://en/1013089), the first method to demonstrate that [primality testing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Primality_testing) can be done in [randomized polynomial time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Randomized_polynomial_time).

In the 1980 paper *Some Polynomials That Are Hard to Compute*, Strassen and [Joachim von zur Gathen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joachim_von_zur_Gathen)—using results of [Joos Heintz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joos_Heintz) and [Malte Sieveking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Malte_Sieveking)—showed that certain quite interesting univariate polynomials with rational coefficients are computationally hard to evaluate.

In 1983, [Walter Baur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Baur) and Strassen proved an inequality that essentially bounds the complexity of a single [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210) in an arbitrary number of indeterminates from below by the complexity of the function together with all of its first-order derivatives. This extended the lower bounds of Strassen's 1974 paper to individual rational functions. Among the many applications, there is also a proof that computing the inverse of a matrix is not much harder than computing the determinant of that matrix.

In 1990, Strassen wrote a survey on *Algebraic Complexity Theory*.

At the first [European Congress of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/13505846) in Paris (1992), Strassen presented his work on the asymptotic spectrum of sets of bilinear maps, documented in the paper *Algebra and Complexity*.

Strassen's pioneering work contributed to [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), [mathematical statistics](wikipedia://en/888711), [algorithm design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithm_design), and [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543).

## Awards and honors {#Awards_and_honors}

In 1999 Strassen was awarded the [Cantor medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cantor_medal),[^o5] and in 2003 he was co-recipient of the [Paris Kanellakis Award](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paris_Kanellakis_Award) with [Robert Solovay](wikipedia://en/3160110), [Gary Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gary_Miller_%28computer_scientist%29), and [Michael Rabin](wikipedia://en/298404) for their work on randomized primality testing.[^o7] In 2008 he was awarded the [Knuth Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knuth_Prize) for "seminal and influential contributions to the design and analysis of efficient algorithms."[^o11] In 2011 he won the [Konrad Zuse Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Konrad_Zuse_Medal) of the [Gesellschaft für Informatik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gesellschaft_für_Informatik).[^o12][^o13] Strassen also became a member of several leading german scientific academies, as well as a fellow of the American Mathematical Society.
