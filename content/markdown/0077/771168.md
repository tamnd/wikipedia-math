# Polynomial remainder theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Polynomial remainder theorem"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the **polynomial remainder theorem** or **little Bézout's theorem** (named after [Étienne Bézout](wikipedia://en/96017))[^o2] is an application of [Euclidean division of polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_division_of_polynomials). It states that, for every number $r$, any [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) $f(x)$ is the [sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_%28mathematics%29) of $f(r)$ and the [product](wikipedia://en/24977) of $x-r$ and a polynomial in $x$ of a [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) one less than the degree of $f$. In particular, $f(r)$ is the remainder of the Euclidean division of $f(x)$ by $x-r$, and $x-r$ is a [divisor](wikipedia://en/49492) of $f(x)$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $f(r)=0$,[^o3] a property known as the [factor theorem](wikipedia://en/1881195).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Example 1 {#Example_1}

Let $f(x) = x^3 - 12x^2 - 42$. Polynomial division of $f(x)$ by $(x-3)$ gives the quotient $x^2 - 9x - 27$ and the remainder $-123$. By the polynomial remainder theorem, $f(3)=-123$.

### Example 2 {#Example_2}

Proof that the polynomial remainder theorem holds for an arbitrary second degree polynomial $f(x) = ax^2 + bx + c$ by using algebraic manipulation:

$$\begin{aligned} f(x)-f(r) &= ax^2+bx+c-(ar^2+br+c)\\ &= a(x^2-r^2)+ b(x-r)\\ &= a(x-r)(x+r)+b(x-r)\\ &= (x-r)(ax +ar+ b) \end{aligned}$$

So,

$$f(x) = (x - r)(ax + ar + b) + f(r),$$

which is exactly the formula of Euclidean division.

## Proofs {#Proofs}

### Using Euclidean division {#Using_Euclidean_division}

The polynomial remainder theorem follows from the theorem of [Euclidean division](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_division_of_polynomials), which, given two polynomials $f(x)$ (the dividend) and $g(x)$ (the divisor), asserts the existence (and the uniqueness) of a quotient $Q(x)$ and a remainder $R(x)$ such that

$$f(x)=Q(x)g(x) + R(x)\quad \text{and}\quad R(x) = 0 \ \text{ or } \deg(R)<\deg(g).$$

If the divisor is $g(x) = x-r,$ where r is a constant, then either $R(x) = 0$ or its degree is zero; in both cases, $R(x)$ is a constant that is independent of $x$; that is

$$f(x)=Q(x)(x-r) + R.$$

Setting $x=r$ in this formula, we obtain:

$$f(r)=R.$$

### Direct proof {#Direct_proof}

A [constructive proof](wikipedia://en/360113)—that does not involve the existence theorem of Euclidean division—uses the identity

$$x^k-r^k=(x-r)(x^{k-1}+x^{k-2}r+\dots+xr^{k-2}+r^{k-1}).$$

If $S_{k}$ denotes the large factor in the right-hand side of this identity, and

$$f(x)=a_nx^n+a_{n-1}x^{n-1} + \cdots + a_1x +a_0,$$

one has

$$f(x)-f(r)=(x-r)(a_n S_n +\cdots + a_2S_2 +a_1),$$

(since $S_1=1$).

Adding $f(r)$ to both sides of this equation, one gets simultaneously the polynomial remainder theorem and the existence part of the theorem of Euclidean division for this specific case.

## Applications {#Applications}

The polynomial remainder theorem may be used to evaluate $f(r)$ by calculating the remainder, $R$. Although [polynomial long division](wikipedia://en/314205) is more difficult than evaluating the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) itself, [synthetic division](wikipedia://en/314709) is computationally easier. Thus, the function may be more "cheaply" evaluated using synthetic division and the polynomial remainder theorem.

The [factor theorem](wikipedia://en/1881195) is another application of the remainder theorem: if the remainder is zero, then the linear divisor is a factor. Repeated application of the factor theorem may be used to factorize the polynomial.[^o14]
