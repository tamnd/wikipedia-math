# Degenerate bilinear form

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Degenerate bilinear form"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), a **degenerate bilinear form** $f(x,y)$ on a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $V$ is a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) such that the map from $V$ to $V^*$ (the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of $V$) given by $v\mapsto(x\mapsto f(x,v))$ has a non-trivial [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullspace), i.e. there exist some non-zero $x$ in $V$ such that $f(x,y)=0$ for all $y\in V$.

:::

An equivalent definition when $V$ is [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) is that the previous map is not an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828).

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Nondegenerate form"}

## Nondegenerate forms {#Nondegenerate_forms}

A **nondegenerate** or **nonsingular** form is a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) that is not degenerate, meaning that $v \mapsto (x \mapsto f(x,v))$ is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828), or equivalently in finite dimensions, [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922)[^o4]

$f(x,y)=0$ for all $y \in V$ implies that $x = 0$.

:::

## Using the determinant {#Using_the_determinant}

If *V* is finite-dimensional then, relative to some [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) for *V*, a bilinear form is degenerate if and only if the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the associated [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is zero – if and only if the matrix is *[singular](wikipedia://en/245528)*, and accordingly degenerate forms are also called **singular forms**. Likewise, a nondegenerate form is one for which the associated matrix is [non-singular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-singular_matrix), and accordingly nondegenerate forms are also referred to as **non-singular forms**. These statements are independent of the chosen basis.

## Related notions {#Related_notions}

If for a [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) *Q* there is a non-zero vector *v* ∈ *V* such that *Q*(*v*) = 0, then *Q* is an [isotropic quadratic form](wikipedia://en/10917170). If *Q* has the same sign for all non-zero vectors, it is a [definite quadratic form](wikipedia://en/20437320) or an **anisotropic quadratic form**.

There is the closely related notion of a [unimodular form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unimodular_form) and a [perfect pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_pairing); these agree over [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) but not over general [rings](wikipedia://en/48404).

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The study of real, [quadratic algebras](wikipedia://en/23565253) shows the distinction between types of quadratic forms. The product *zz*\* is a quadratic form for each of the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), [split-complex numbers](wikipedia://en/893559), and [dual numbers](wikipedia://en/42169). For *z* = *x* + ε *y*, the dual number form is *x*<sup>2</sup> which is a **degenerate quadratic form**. The split-complex case is an isotropic form, and the complex case is a definite form.

The most important examples of nondegenerate forms are [inner products](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) and [symplectic forms](wikipedia://en/292852). [Symmetric](wikipedia://en/1803375) nondegenerate forms are important generalizations of inner products, in that often all that is required is that the map $V \to V^*$ be an isomorphism, not positivity. For example, a [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) with an inner product structure on its [tangent spaces](wikipedia://en/30807) is a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652), while relaxing this to a symmetric nondegenerate form yields a [pseudo-Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/287555).

:::

## Infinite dimensions {#Infinite_dimensions}

Which dual space? Note that in an infinite-dimensional space, we can have a bilinear form ƒ for which $v \mapsto (x \mapsto f(x,v))$ is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) but not [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) onto $V^*$. For example, on the space of [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) on a closed bounded [interval](wikipedia://en/49172), the form given by

$$f(\phi,\psi) = \int\psi(x)\phi(x) \,dx$$

is not surjective: for instance, the [Dirac delta functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dirac_delta_functional) is in the dual space but not of the required form. On the other hand, this bilinear form satisfies

$f(\phi,\psi)=0$ for all $\phi$ implies that $\psi=0\,$,

and hence it is non-degenerate. In such a case where ƒ is injective but not (necessarily) surjective, ƒ can be said to be *weakly nondegenerate* for emphasis.

::: {#o11 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

If *f* vanishes identically on all vectors it is said to be **totally degenerate**. Given any bilinear form *f* on *V* the set of vectors

$$\{x\in V \mid f(x,y) = 0 \mbox{ for all } y \in V\}$$

forms a totally degenerate [subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of *V*. The map *f* is nondegenerate if and only if this subspace is trivial.

Geometrically, an [isotropic line](wikipedia://en/8088814) of the quadratic form corresponds to a point of the associated [quadric hypersurface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadric_surface) in [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135). Such a line is additionally isotropic for the bilinear form if and only if the corresponding point is a [singularity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singular_variety). Hence, over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018), [Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215) guarantees that the quadratic form always has isotropic lines, while the bilinear form has them if and only if the surface is singular.
