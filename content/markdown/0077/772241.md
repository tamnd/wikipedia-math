# Hurwitz quaternion

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hurwitz quaternion"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Hurwitz quaternion** (or **Hurwitz integer**) is a [quaternion](wikipedia://en/51440) whose components are *either* all [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) *or* all [half-integers](wikipedia://en/1568608) (halves of [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) integers; a mixture of integers and half-integers is excluded). The set of all Hurwitz quaternions is

:::

$$H = \left\{a+bi+cj+dk \in \mathbb{H} \mid a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{Z} \;\mbox{ or }\, a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{Z} + \tfrac{1}{2}\right\}.$$

That is, either *a*, *b*, *c*, *d* are all integers, or they are all halves of odd integers. *H* is closed under quaternion multiplication and addition, which makes it a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of all quaternions **H**. Hurwitz quaternions were introduced by (txt).

A **Lipschitz quaternion** (or **Lipschitz integer**; named after [Rudolf Lipschitz](wikipedia://en/339965)) is a quaternion whose components are all integers. The set of all Lipschitz quaternions

$$L = \left\{a+bi+cj+dk \in \mathbb{H} \mid a,b,c,d \in \mathbb{Z}\right\}$$

forms a subring of the Hurwitz quaternions *H*. Hurwitz integers have the advantage over Lipschitz integers that it is possible to perform [Euclidean division](wikipedia://en/679987) on them, obtaining a small remainder.

Both the Hurwitz and Lipschitz quaternions are examples of [noncommutative](wikipedia://en/24062516) [domains](wikipedia://en/487627) which are not [division rings](wikipedia://en/9067).

## Structure of the ring of Hurwitz quaternions {#Structure_of_the_ring_of_Hurwitz_quaternions}

*Image: Binary tetrahedral group elements.png.* 24 quaternion elements of the [binary tetrahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997586), seen in projection: 1 order-1: 1; 1 order-2: −1; 6 order-4: ±*i*, ±*j*, ±*k*; 8 order-6: (+1±*i*±*j*±*k*)/2; 8 order-3: (−1±*i*±*j*±*k*)/2

As an additive [group](wikipedia://en/19447), *H* is [free abelian](wikipedia://en/247296) with generators {(1 + *i* + *j* + *k*) / 2, *i*, *j*, *k*}. It therefore forms a [lattice](wikipedia://en/469246) in **R**<sup>4</sup>. This lattice is known as the [*F*<sub>4</sub> lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F4_lattice) since it is the [root lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_lattice) of the [semisimple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1337587) [*F*<sub>4</sub>](wikipedia://en/292877). The Lipschitz quaternions *L* form an index 2 sublattice of *H*.

The [group of units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_of_units) in *L* is the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_of_a_group) 8 [quaternion group](wikipedia://en/153130) *Q* = {±1, ±*i*, ±*j*, ±*k*}. The group of units in *H* is a [nonabelian group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonabelian_group) of order 24 known as the [binary tetrahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997586). The elements of this group include the 8 elements of *Q* along with the 16 quaternions {(±1 ± *i* ± *j* ± *k*) / 2}, where signs may be taken in any combination. The quaternion group is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of the binary tetrahedral group U(*H*). The elements of U(*H*), which all have norm 1, form the vertices of the [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485) inscribed in the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792).

The Hurwitz quaternions form an [order](wikipedia://en/1114171) (in the sense of [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424)) in the [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) of quaternions with [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) components. It is in fact a [maximal order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_order); this accounts for its importance. The Lipschitz quaternions, which are the more obvious candidate for the idea of an *integral quaternion*, also form an order. However, this latter order is not a maximal one, and therefore (as it turns out) less suitable for developing a theory of [left ideals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Left_ideal) comparable to that of [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705). What [Adolf Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690) realised, therefore, was that this definition of Hurwitz integral quaternion is the better one to operate with. For a [non-commutative ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_ring) such as **H**, maximal orders need not be unique, so one needs to fix a maximal order, in carrying over the concept of an [algebraic integer](wikipedia://en/98981).

## The lattice of Hurwitz quaternions {#The_lattice_of_Hurwitz_quaternions}

The [(arithmetic, or field) norm](wikipedia://en/450555) of a Hurwitz quaternion *a* + *bi* + *cj* + *dk*, given by *a*${}^{2}$ + *b*${}^{2}$ + *c*${}^{2}$ + *d*${}^{2}$, is always an integer. By a [theorem of Lagrange](wikipedia://en/326395) every nonnegative integer can be written as a sum of at most four [squares](wikipedia://en/659942). Thus, every nonnegative integer is the norm of some Lipschitz (or Hurwitz) quaternion. More precisely, the number *c*(*n*) of Hurwitz quaternions of given positive norm *n* is 24 times the sum of the odd [divisors](wikipedia://en/49492) of *n*. The generating function of the numbers *c*(*n*) is given by the level 2 weight 2 [modular form](wikipedia://en/286000)

$$2E_2(2\tau)-E_2(\tau) = \sum_nc(n)q^n = 1 + 24q + 24q^2 + 96q^3 + 24q^4 + 144q^5 + \dots$$

where

$$q=e^{2\pi i \tau}$$

and

$$E_2(\tau) = 1-24\sum_n\sigma_1(n)q^n$$

is the weight 2 level 1 [Eisenstein series](wikipedia://en/1270458) (which is a [quasimodular form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasimodular_form)) and *σ*<sub>1</sub>(*n*) is the sum of the divisors of *n*.

## Factorization into irreducible elements {#Factorization_into_irreducible_elements}

A Hurwitz integer is called irreducible if it is not 0 or a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) and is not a product of non-units. A Hurwitz integer is irreducible [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its norm is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666). The irreducible quaternions are sometimes called prime quaternions, but this can be misleading as they are not [primes](wikipedia://en/60692) in the usual sense of [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990): it is possible for an irreducible quaternion to divide a product *ab* without dividing either *a* or *b*. Every Hurwitz quaternion can be factored as a product of irreducible quaternions. This factorization is not in general unique, even up to units and order, because a positive odd prime *p* can be written in 24(*p*+1) ways as a product of two irreducible Hurwitz quaternions of norm *p*, and for large *p* these cannot all be equivalent under left and right multiplication by units as there are only 24 units. However, if one excludes this case then there is a version of unique factorization. More precisely, every Hurwitz quaternion can be written uniquely as the product of a positive integer and a primitive quaternion (a Hurwitz quaternion not divisible by any integer greater than 1). The factorization of a primitive quaternion into irreducibles is unique up to order and units in the following sense: if

*p*<sub>0</sub>*p*<sub>1</sub>...*p*<sub>*n*</sub>

and

*q*<sub>0</sub>*q*<sub>1</sub>...*q*<sub>*n*</sub>

are two factorizations of some primitive Hurwitz quaternion into irreducible quaternions where *p*<sub>*k*</sub> has the same norm as *q*<sub>*k*</sub> for all *k*, then

$$\begin{aligned} q_0 & = p_0 u_1 \\ q_1 & = u_1^{-1} p_1 u_2 \\ & \,\,\,\vdots \\ q_n & = u_n^{-1} p_n \end{aligned}$$

for some units *u*<sub>*k*</sub>.

## Division with remainder {#Division_with_remainder}

The ordinary integers and the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) allow a division with remainder or [Euclidean division](wikipedia://en/679987).

For positive integers *M* and *D*, there is always a quotient *Q* and a nonnegative remainder *R* such that

- *M* = *QD* + *R* where *R* \< *D*.

For complex or Gaussian integers *M* = *a* + i*b* and *D* = *c* + i*d*, with the norm N(*D*) > 0, there always exist *Q* = *p* + i*q* and *R* = *r* + i*s* such that

- *M* = *QD* + *R*, where N(*R*) \< N(*D*).

However, for Lipschitz integers *M* = (*a*, *b*, *c*, *d*) and *D* = (*e*, *f*, *g*, *h*) it can happen that N(*R*) = N(*D*). This motivated a switch to Hurwitz integers, for which the condition N(*R*) \< N(*D*) is guaranteed.[^o7]

Many algorithms depend on division with remainder, for example, [Euclid's algorithm](wikipedia://en/10377) for the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354).
