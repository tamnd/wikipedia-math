# Generalized additive model for location, scale and shape

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Generalized additive model for location, scale and shape"}

The **generalized additive model for location, scale and shape** (**GAMLSS**) is a distributional [regression model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regression_model) in which a parametric [statistical distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_distribution) is assumed for the response (target) variable but the parameters of this distribution can vary according to explanatory variables. Therefore the shape of this distribution for the target variable can change with explanatory variables.

:::

GAMLSS is an input output model, i.e. $X \rightarrow Y$ but differs from the classical model in that the input X affects the distribution of the target variable as a whole not just the mean, i.e. $X \rightarrow D(Y|X)$.

GAMLSS allows flexible regression by using [smoothing](wikipedia://en/4155662) or [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488) techniques to model the parameters of the target variable (response). GAMLSS assumes the response variable could follows any theoretical parametric distribution, which might be heavy or light-tailed, and positively or negatively skewed. In addition, all the parameters of the distribution which often are **location** (e.g., mean), **scale** (e.g., variance) and **shape** ([skewness](wikipedia://en/28212) and [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848)) – can be modelled as linear, nonlinear or using algorithm modelling functions of the explanatory variables. The distributional assumption for the target variables can be checked through diagnostic plots like [Q–Q plot](wikipedia://en/4031859) or worm plot. GAMLSS is a [supervised machine learning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supervised_machine_learning) model since the target value (the output) is always present.

## Overview of the model {#Overview_of_the_model}

The generalized additive model for location, scale and shape (GAMLSS) is a [statistical model](wikipedia://en/27576) introduced by Rigby and Stasinopoulos (2005)[^o3] to overcome some of the limitations associated with the popular [generalized linear models](wikipedia://en/747122) (GLMs) of Nelder and Wedderburn (1972)[^o4] and [generalized additive models](wikipedia://en/3608284) (GAMs) of Hastie and Tibshirani.[^o5] Most of the limitations arise from the limited choice of distributions for the response. Both GLM and GAM assumed that the response comes from the [exponential family](wikipedia://en/339174) a family rich enough to allow continuous and discrete responses (and very good for modelling the mean of the distribution as a function of the explanatory variables) but not very flexible enough to model other characteristics of the distribution i.e tails.

In GAMLSS the [exponential family](wikipedia://en/339174) [distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) assumption for the [response variable](wikipedia://en/437701#response_variable), ($y$), (essential in [GLMs](wikipedia://en/747122) and [GAMs](wikipedia://en/3608284)), is relaxed and replaced by a general distribution family, including highly [skew](wikipedia://en/28212) and/or [kurtotic](wikipedia://en/16848) [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_distribution) and [discrete distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_distribution).

The systematic part of the model is expanded to allow modelling not only of the [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) (or [location](wikipedia://en/17516)) but possibly other parameters of the distribution of response as linear and/or nonlinear, parametric and/or additive [non-parametric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-parametric) functions of [explanatory variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explanatory_variable) and/or [random effects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_effect).

GAMLSS, for continuous responses, is especially suited for modelling a [leptokurtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leptokurtic) or [platykurtic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platykurtic) and/or positively or negatively skewed variables therefor modelling the tails of the distribution. For [count type response variable data](wikipedia://en/15690807) it deals with [over-dispersion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Over-dispersion) and **zero-inflation** by using proper over-dispersed and zero inflated distributions. Heterogeneity also is dealt with by modeling the [scale](wikipedia://en/769503) or [shape parameters](wikipedia://en/9210865) using explanatory variables. GAMLSS allow also **mixed** distributions, that is, distributions which have discrete and continuous parts, i.e the zero inflated beta is one or them. Note that while the [beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074) allow value in $(0,1)$ the beta inflated allows values in $[0,1]$.

There are several packages written in [R](wikipedia://en/376707) related to GAMLSS models,[^o6] and tutorials for using and interpreting GAMLSS.[^o7]

A GAMLSS model assumes independent observations $y_i$ for $i = 1, 2, \dots , n$ with probability (density) function $D (y_i | \boldsymbol{\theta}_i )$ conditional on $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i$. The parameter $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i$ often is a vector of four distribution parameters, $\boldsymbol{\theta}_i= (\mu_i, \sigma_i, \nu_i, \tau_i)$ each of which can be a function of the explanatory variables, for example, $\sigma=g(x)$ The first two distribution parameters $\mu_i$ and $\sigma_i$ are usually characterised as **location** and **scale** parameters, while the remaining parameter(s), if any, are characterised as shape parameters, e.g. [skewness](wikipedia://en/28212) and [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848) parameters. The model may be applied more generally to the parameters of any population distribution. The most general formulation of a GAMLSS model is

$$\begin{aligned} g_1 (\boldsymbol{\theta}_1) &= \boldsymbol{\eta}_1= ML(\textbf{x}_{j1}) \\ g_2(\boldsymbol{\theta}_2) &= \boldsymbol{\eta}_2= ML(\textbf{x}_{j2}) \\ \ldots &= \ldots =\ldots\\ g_4(\boldsymbol{\theta}_k)&=\boldsymbol{\eta}_k= ML(\textbf{x}_{j4}) \end{aligned}$$

where $ML()$ is any machine learning (mathematical or algorithmic) model and $k$ is the number of parameters in the distribution for the response. The original formulation of GAMLSS in the 2005 RSS paper had only four parameters and it was written as;

$$\begin{aligned} g_1 (\boldsymbol{\mu}) = \boldsymbol{\eta}_1= \textbf{X}_1 \boldsymbol{\beta}_1 + \sum_{j=1}^{J_1} {h}_{j1}(\bf{x}_{j1}) \\ g_2(\boldsymbol{\sigma}) = \boldsymbol{\eta}_2= \textbf{X}_2 \boldsymbol{\beta}_2 + \sum_{j=1}^{J_2}{h}_{j2}(\bf{x}_{j2}) \\ g_3(\boldsymbol{\nu} = \boldsymbol{\eta}_3 = \textbf{X}_3 \boldsymbol{\beta}_3 + \sum_{j=1}^{J_3}{h}_{j3}(\bf{x}_{j3}) \\ g_4(\boldsymbol{\tau})=\boldsymbol{\eta}_4=\textbf{X}_4 \boldsymbol{\beta}_4 + \sum_{j=1}^{J_4}{h}_{j4}(\bf{x}_{j4}) \end{aligned}$$

where $\boldsymbol{\mu}, \boldsymbol{\sigma}, \boldsymbol{\nu}$, $\boldsymbol{\tau}$ and $\boldsymbol{\eta}_k$ are vectors of length $n$, $\boldsymbol{\beta}^{T}_k = (\beta_{1k},\beta_{2k},\ldots,\beta_{J'_{k} k})$ is a parameter vector of length $J'_k$, $\bf~{X}_k$ is a fixed known [design matrix](wikipedia://en/949189) of order $n \times J'_k$ and $h_{jk}$ is a smooth non-parametric function of explanatory variable $\bf{x}_{jk}$, for $j=1,2,\ldots, J_{k}$ and $k=1,2,3,4$. $g_k$ for $k=1,2,3,4$ are [link functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Link_function) to ensure that the parameter are in the correct range of values.

## Applications of the model {#Applications_of_the_model}

For centile estimation, the [WHO Multicentre Growth Reference Study Group](https://www.who.int/childgrowth/en) have recommended GAMLSS and the Box–Cox power exponential (BCPE) distributions[^o9] for the construction of the WHO Child Growth Standards.[^o10][^o11] Recent studies have used GAMLSS to predict the probability of cyanobacterial toxins exceeding critical health thresholds in lakes,[^o12] as well as in applications related to [remote sensing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Remote_sensing),[^o13] biogeochemical modeling[^o14] and medicine.[^o15]

## What distributions can be used {#What_distributions_can_be_used}

The form of the distribution assumed for the response variable y, is very general. For example, an implementation of GAMLSS in [R](wikipedia://en/376707)[^o17] has around 100 different distributions available. Such implementations also allow use of truncated distributions and censored (or interval) response variables.[^o17]
