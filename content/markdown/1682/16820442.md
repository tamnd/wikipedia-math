# Director string

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Director string"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), in the area of [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203) and [computation](wikipedia://en/5926), **directors** or **director strings** are a mechanism for keeping track of the [free variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variable) in a [term](wikipedia://en/609125). Loosely speaking, they can be understood as a kind of [memoization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Memoization) for free variables; that is, as an [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Program_optimization) technique for rapidly locating the free variables in a [term algebra](wikipedia://en/2216678) or in a lambda expression. Director strings were introduced by Kennaway and Sleep in 1982 and further developed by Sinot, Fernández and Mackie[^o2] as a mechanism for understanding and controlling the [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/2230) cost of [beta reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta_reduction).

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

In beta reduction, one defines the value of the expression on the left to be that on the right:

$(\lambda x.E)y \equiv E[x:= y]\,$ or $(\lambda x.E)y \equiv E[y/x]$ (Replace all *x* in *E*(body) by *y*)

While this is a conceptually simple operation, the [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/2230) of the step can be non-trivial: a naive algorithm would scan the expression *E* for all occurrences of the free variable *x*. Such an algorithm is clearly *O*(*n*) in the length of the expression *E*. Thus, one is motivated to somehow track the occurrences of the free variables in the expression. One may attempt to track the position of *every* free variable, wherever it may occur in the expression, but this can clearly become very costly in terms of storage; furthermore, it provides a level of detail that is not really needed. Director strings suggest that the correct model is to track free variables in a hierarchical fashion, by tracking their use in component terms.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Consider, for simplicity, a [term algebra](wikipedia://en/2216678), that is, a collection of free variables, constants, and operators which may be freely combined. Assume that a term *t* takes the form

$$t ::= f(t_1,t_2,\dots,t_n)$$

where *f* is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427), of [arity](wikipedia://en/42301) *n*, with no [free variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_variable), and the $t_i$ are terms that may or may not contain free variables. Let *V* denote the set of all free variables that may occur in the set of all terms. The director is then the map

$$\sigma_t: V\to P(\lbrace 1,2,\dots,n\rbrace)$$

from the free variables to the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) $P(X)$ of the set $X=\lbrace 1,2,\dots,n\rbrace$. The values taken by $\sigma_t$ are simply a list of the indices of the $t_i$ in which a given free variable occurs. Thus, for example, if a free variable $x\in V$ occurs in $t_3$ and $t_5$ but in no other terms, then one has $\sigma_t(x) = \lbrace 3,5\rbrace$.

Thus, for every term $t\in T$ in the set of all terms *T*, one maintains a function $\sigma_t$, and instead of working only with terms *t*, one works with pairs $(t,\sigma_t)$. Thus, the time complexity of finding the free variables in *t* is traded for the space complexity of maintaining a list of the terms in which a variable occurs.

:::

## General case {#General_case}

Although the above definition is formulated in terms of a [term algebra](wikipedia://en/2216678), the general concept applies more generally, and can be defined both for [combinatory algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatory_algebra) and for [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203) proper, specifically, within the framework of [explicit substitution](wikipedia://en/16874985).
