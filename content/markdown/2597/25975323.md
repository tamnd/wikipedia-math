# Resolvent cubic

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Resolvent cubic"}

*Image: Resolvente.png.* Graph of the polynomial function $x^{4} + x^{3} - x^{2} - 7x/4 - 1/2$ (in green) together with the graph of its resolvent cubic $R_{4}(y)$ (in red). The roots of both polynomials are visible too.

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), a **resolvent cubic** is one of several distinct, although related, [cubic polynomials](wikipedia://en/243709) defined from a [monic](wikipedia://en/314730) [polynomial of degree four](wikipedia://en/308152):

:::

$$P(x)=x^4+a_3x^3+a_2x^2+a_1x+a_0.$$

In each case:

- The coefficients of the resolvent cubic can be obtained from the coefficients of $P(x)$ using only sums, subtractions and multiplications.
- Knowing the roots of the resolvent cubic of $P(x)$ is useful for finding the roots of $P(x)$ itself. Hence the name “resolvent cubic”.
- The polynomial $P(x)$ has a [multiple root](wikipedia://en/19211790#Multiplicity_of_a_root_of_a_polynomial) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) its resolvent cubic has a multiple root.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Suppose that the coefficients of $P(x)$ belong to a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $k$ whose [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621#Case_of_fields) is different from $2$. Whenever roots of $P(x)$ are mentioned, they belong to some [extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $K$ of $k$ such that $P(x)$ factors into linear factors in $K[x]$. If $k$ is the field $\mathbf{Q}$ of rational numbers, then $K$ can be the field $\mathbf{C}$ of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) or the field $\overline{\mathbf{Q}}$ of [algebraic numbers](wikipedia://en/1158).

In some cases, the concept of resolvent cubic is defined only when $P(x)$ is a quartic in depressed form—that is, when $a_{3} = 0$.

Note that the [fourth](wikipedia://en/25975323#Fourth_definition) and [fifth](wikipedia://en/25975323#Fifth_definition) definitions below also make sense and that the relationship between these resolvent cubics and $P(x)$ are still valid if the characteristic of $k$ is equal to $2$.

### First definition {#First_definition}

Suppose that $P(x)$ is a depressed quartic—that is, that $a_{3} = 0$. A possible definition of the resolvent cubic of $P(x)$ is:[^o5]

$$R_1(y)=8y^3+8a_2y^2+(2{a_2}^2-8a_0)y-{a_1}^2.$$

The origin of this definition lies in applying [Ferrari's method](wikipedia://en/308152#Ferrari's_solution) to find the roots of $P(x)$. To be more precise:

$$\begin{aligned}P(x)=0&\Longleftrightarrow x^4+a_2x^2=-a_1x-a_0\\ &\Longleftrightarrow \left(x^2+\frac{a_2}2\right)^2=-a_1x-a_0+\frac{{a_2}^2}4.\end{aligned}$$

Add a new unknown, $y$, to $x^{2} + a_{2}/2$. Now you have:

$$\begin{aligned}\left(x^2+\frac{a_2}2+y\right)^2&=-a_1x-a_0+\frac{{a_2}^2}4+2x^2y+a_2y+y^2\\ &=2yx^2-a_1x-a_0+\frac{{a_2}^2}4+a_2y+y^2.\end{aligned}$$

If this expression is a square, it can only be the square of

$$\sqrt{2y}\,x-\frac{a_1}{2\sqrt{2y}}.$$

But the equality

$$\left(\sqrt{2y}\,x-\frac{a_1}{2\sqrt{2y}}\right)^2=2yx^2-a_1x-a_0+\frac{{a_2}^2}4+a_2y+y^2$$

is equivalent to

$$\frac{{a_1}^2}{8y}=-a_0+\frac{{a_2}^2}4+a_2y+y^2\text{,}$$

and this is the same thing as the assertion that $R_{1}(y)$ = 0.

If $y_{0}$ is a root of $R_{1}(y)$, then it is a consequence of the computations made above that the roots of $P(x)$ are the roots of the polynomial

$$x^2-\sqrt{2y_0}\,x+\frac{a_2}2+y_0+\frac{a_1}{2\sqrt{2y_0}}$$

together with the roots of the polynomial

$$x^2+\sqrt{2y_0}\,x+\frac{a_2}2+y_0-\frac{a_1}{2\sqrt{2y_0}}.$$

Of course, this makes no sense if $y_{0} = 0$, but since the [constant term](wikipedia://en/79861) of $R_{1}(y)$ is $-a_{1}^{2}$, $0$ is a root of $R_{1}(y)$ if and only if $a_{1} = 0$, and in this case the roots of $P(x)$ can be found using the [quadratic formula](wikipedia://en/59217).

### Second definition {#Second_definition}

Another possible definition[^o5] (still supposing that $P(x)$ is a depressed quartic) is

$$R_2(y)=8y^3-4a_2y^2-8a_0y+4a_2a_0-{a_1}^2$$

The origin of this definition is similar to the previous one. This time, we start by doing:

$$\begin{aligned}P(x)=0&\Longleftrightarrow x^4=-a_2x^2-a_1x-a_0\\ &\Longleftrightarrow(x^2+y)^2=-a_2x^2-a_1x-a_0+2yx^2+y^2\end{aligned}$$

and a computation similar to the previous one shows that this last expression is a square if and only if

$$8y^3-4a_2y^2-8a_0y+4a_2a_0-{a_1}^2=0\text{.}$$

A simple computation shows that

$$R_2\left(y+\frac{a_2}2\right)=R_1(y).$$

### Third definition {#Third_definition}

Another possible definition[^o8][^o9] (again, supposing that $P(x)$ is a depressed quartic) is

$$R_3(y)=y^3+2a_2y^2+({a_2}^2-4a_0)y-{a_1}^2\text{.}$$

The origin of this definition lies in another method of solving quartic equations, namely [Descartes' method](wikipedia://en/308152#Descartes'_solution). If you try to find the roots of $P(x)$ by expressing it as a product of two monic quadratic polynomials $x^{2} + \alpha x + \beta$ and $x^{2} - \alpha x + \gamma$, then

$$P(x)=(x^2+\alpha x+\beta)(x^2-\alpha x+\gamma)\Longleftrightarrow\left\{\begin{array}{l}\beta+\gamma-\alpha^2=a_2\\ \alpha(-\beta+\gamma)=a_1\\ \beta\gamma=a_0.\end{array}\right.$$

If there is a solution of this system with $\alpha \ne 0$ (note that if $a_{1} \ne 0$, then this is automatically true for any solution), the previous system is equivalent to

$$\left\{\begin{array}{l}\beta+\gamma=a_2+\alpha^2\\-\beta+\gamma=\frac{a_1}{\alpha}\\ \beta\gamma=a_0.\end{array}\right.$$

It is a consequence of the first two equations that then

$$\beta=\frac12\left(a_2+\alpha^2-\frac{a_1}{\alpha}\right)$$

and

$$\gamma=\frac12\left(a_2+\alpha^2+\frac{a_1}{\alpha}\right).$$

After replacing, in the third equation, $\beta$ and $\gamma$ by these values one gets that

$$\left(a_2+\alpha^2\right)^2-\frac{{a_1}^2}{\alpha^2}=4a_0\text{,}$$

and this is equivalent to the assertion that $\alpha^{2}$ is a root of $R_{3}(y)$. So, again, knowing the roots of $R_{3}(y)$ helps to determine the roots of $P(x)$.

Note that

$$R_3(y)=R_1\left(\frac y2\right)\text{.}$$

### Fourth definition {#Fourth_definition}

Still another possible definition is[^o11]

$$R_4(y)=y^3-a_2y^2+(a_1a_3-4a_0)y+4a_0a_2-{a_1}^2-a_0{a_3}^2.$$

In fact, if the roots of $P(x)$ are $\alpha_{1}, \alpha_{2}, \alpha_{3}$, and $\alpha_{4}$, then

$$R_4(y)=\bigl(y-(\alpha_1\alpha_2+\alpha_3\alpha_4)\bigr)\bigl(y-(\alpha_1\alpha_3+\alpha_2\alpha_4)\bigr)\bigl(y-(\alpha_1\alpha_4+\alpha_2\alpha_3)\bigr)\text{,}$$

a fact the follows from [Vieta's formulas](wikipedia://en/714050). In other words, *R*<sub>4</sub>(*y*) is the monic polynomial whose roots are $\alpha_{1}\alpha_{2} + \alpha_{3}\alpha_{4}$, $\alpha_{1}\alpha_{3} + \alpha_{2}\alpha_{4}$, and $\alpha_{1}\alpha_{4} + \alpha_{2}\alpha_{3}$.

It is easy to see that

$$\alpha_1\alpha_2+\alpha_3\alpha_4-(\alpha_1\alpha_3+\alpha_2\alpha_4)=(\alpha_1-\alpha_4)(\alpha_2-\alpha_3)\text{,}$$

$$\alpha_1\alpha_3+\alpha_2\alpha_4-(\alpha_1\alpha_4+\alpha_2\alpha_3)=(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)(\alpha_3-\alpha_4)\text{,}$$

$$\alpha_1\alpha_2+\alpha_3\alpha_4-(\alpha_1\alpha_4+\alpha_2\alpha_3)=(\alpha_1-\alpha_3)(\alpha_2-\alpha_4)\text{.}$$

Therefore, $P(x)$ has a [multiple root](wikipedia://en/19211790#Multiplicity_of_a_root_of_a_polynomial) if and only if $R_{4}(y)$ has a multiple root. More precisely, $P(x)$ and $R_{4}(y)$ have the same [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607).

If $P(x)$ is a depressed polynomial, then

$$\begin{aligned}R_4(y)&=y^3-a_2y^2-4a_0y+4a_0a_2-{a_1}^2\\ &=R_2\left(\frac y2\right)\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

### Fifth definition {#Fifth_definition}

Yet another definition is[^o13][^o14]

$$R_5(y)=y^3-2a_2y^2+({a_2}^2+a_3a_1-4a_0)y+{a_1}^2-a_3a_2a_1+{a_3}^2a_0\text{.}$$

If, as above, the roots of $P(x)$ are $\alpha_{1}, \alpha_{2}, \alpha_{3}$, and $\alpha_{4}$, then

$$R_5(y)=\bigl(y-(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)(\alpha_3+\alpha_4)\bigr)\bigl(y-(\alpha_1+\alpha_3)(\alpha_2+\alpha_4)\bigr)\bigl(y-(\alpha_1+\alpha_4)(\alpha_2+\alpha_3)\bigr)\text{,}$$

again as a consequence of [Vieta's formulas](wikipedia://en/714050). In other words, $R_{5}(y)$ is the monic polynomial whose roots are $(\alpha_{1} + \alpha_{2})(\alpha_{3} + \alpha_{4})$, $(\alpha_{1} + \alpha_{3})(\alpha_{2} + \alpha_{4})$, and $(\alpha_{1} + \alpha_{4})(\alpha_{2} + \alpha_{3})$.

It is easy to see that

$$(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)(\alpha_3+\alpha_4)-(\alpha_1+\alpha_3)(\alpha_2+\alpha_4)=-(\alpha_1-\alpha_4)(\alpha_2-\alpha_3)\text{,}$$

$$(\alpha_1+\alpha_2)(\alpha_3+\alpha_4)-(\alpha_1+\alpha_4)(\alpha_2+\alpha_3)=-(\alpha_1-\alpha_3)(\alpha_2-\alpha_4)\text{,}$$

$$(\alpha_1+\alpha_3)(\alpha_2+\alpha_4)-(\alpha_1+\alpha_4)(\alpha_2+\alpha_3)=-(\alpha_1-\alpha_2)(\alpha_3-\alpha_4)\text{.}$$

Therefore, as it happens with $R_{4}(y)$, $P(x)$ has a multiple root if and only if $R_{5}(y)$ has a multiple root. More precisely, $P(x)$ and $R_{5}(y)$ have the same discriminant. This is also a consequence of the fact that $R_{5}(y + a_{2})$ = $-R_{4}(-y)$.

Note that if $P(x)$ is a depressed polynomial, then

$$\begin{aligned}R_5(y)&=y^3-2a_2y^2+({a_2}^2-4a_0)y+{a_1}^2\\ &=-R_3(-y)\\ &=-R_1\left(-\frac y2\right)\text{.}\end{aligned}$$

## Applications {#Applications}

### Solving quartic equations {#Solving_quartic_equations}

It was explained above how $R_{1}(y)$, $R_{2}(y)$, and $R_{3}(y)$ can be used to find the roots of $P(x)$ if this polynomial is depressed. In the general case, one simply has to find the roots of the depressed polynomial $P(x - a_{3}/4)$. For each root $x_{0}$ of this polynomial, $x_{0} - a_{3}/4$ is a root of $P(x)$.

### Factoring quartic polynomials {#Factoring_quartic_polynomials}

If a quartic polynomial $P(x)$ is [reducible](wikipedia://en/188725) in $k[x]$, then it is the product of two quadratic polynomials or the product of a linear polynomial by a cubic polynomial. This second possibility occurs if and only if $P(x)$ has a root in $k$. In order to determine whether or not $P(x)$ can be expressed as the product of two quadratic polynomials, let us assume, for simplicity, that $P(x)$ is a depressed polynomial. Then it was seen above that if the resolvent cubic $R_{3}(y)$ has a non-null root of the form $\alpha^{2}$, for some $\alpha \in k$, then such a decomposition exists.

This can be used to prove that, in $\mathbf{R}[x]$, every quartic polynomial without real roots can be expressed as the product of two quadratic polynomials. Let $P(x)$ be such a polynomial. We can assume [without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000) that $P(x)$ is monic. We can also assume without loss of generality that it is a reduced polynomial, because $P(x)$ can be expressed as the product of two quadratic polynomials if and only if $P(x - a_{3}/4)$ can and this polynomial is a reduced one. Then $R_{3}(y)$ = $y^{3} + 2a_{2}y^{2} + (a_{2}^{2} - 4a_{0})y - a_{1}^{2}$. There are two cases:

- If $a_{1} \ne 0$ then $R_{3}(0)$ = $-a_{1}^{2} < 0$. Since $R_{3}(y) > 0$ if $y$ is large enough, then, by the [intermediate value theorem](wikipedia://en/14884), $R_{3}(y)$ has a root $y_{0}$ with $y_{0} > 0$. So, we can take $\alpha$ = $\sqrt{y_{0}}$.
- If $a_{1}$ = $0$, then $R_{3}(y)$ = $y^{3} + 2a_{2}y^{2} + (a_{2}^{2} - 4a_{0})y$. The roots of this polynomial are $0$ and the roots of the quadratic polynomial $y^{2} + 2a_{2}y + a_{2}^{2} - 4a_{0}$. If $a_{2}^{2} - 4a_{0} < 0$, then the product of the two roots of this polynomial is smaller than $0$ and therefore it has a root greater than $0$ (which happens to be $-a_{2} + 2\sqrt{a_{0}}$) and we can take $\alpha$ as the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of that root. Otherwise, $a_{2}^{2} - 4a_{0} \ge 0$ and then,

$$P(x)=\left(x^2+\frac{a_2+\sqrt{{a_2}^2-4a_0}}2\right)\left(x^2+\frac{a_2-\sqrt{{a_2}^2-4a_0}}2\right)\text{.}$$

More generally, if $k$ is a [real closed field](wikipedia://en/1066093), then every quartic polynomial without roots in $k$ can be expressed as the product of two quadratic polynomials in $k[x]$. Indeed, this statement can be expressed in [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983) and any such statement that holds for $\mathbf{R}$ also holds for any real closed field.

A similar approach can be used to get an algorithm[^o8] to determine whether or not a quartic polynomial $P(x) \in \mathbf{Q}[x]$ is reducible and, if it is, how to express it as a product of polynomials of smaller degree. Again, we will suppose that $P(x)$ is monic and depressed. Then $P(x)$ is reducible if and only if at least one of the following conditions holds:

- The polynomial $P(x)$ has a rational root (this can be determined using the [rational root theorem](wikipedia://en/26426)).
- The resolvent cubic $R_{3}(y)$ has a root of the form $\alpha^{2}$, for some non-null rational number $\alpha$ (again, this can be determined using the [rational root theorem](wikipedia://en/26426)).
- The number $a_{2}^{2} - 4a_{0}$ is the square of a [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) and $a_{1}$ = $0$.

Indeed:

- If $P(x)$ has a rational root $r$, then $P(x)$ is the product of $x - r$ by a cubic polynomial in $\mathbf{Q}[x]$, which can be determined by [polynomial long division](wikipedia://en/314205) or by [Ruffini's rule](wikipedia://en/766014).
- If there is a rational number $\alpha \ne 0$ such that $\alpha^{2}$ is a root of $R_{3}(y)$, it was shown above how to express $P(x)$ as the product of two quadratic polynomials in $\mathbf{Q}[x]$.
- Finally, if the third condition holds and if $\delta \in \mathbf{Q}$ is such that $\delta^{2}$=$a_{2}^{2} - 4a_{0}$, then $P(x)$ = $(x^{2} + (a_{2} + \delta)/2)(x^{2} + (a_{2} - \delta)/2)$.

### Galois groups of irreducible quartic polynomials {#Galois_groups_of_irreducible_quartic_polynomials}

The resolvent cubic of an [irreducible quartic polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) $P(x)$ can be used to determine its [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) $G$; that is, the Galois group of the [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of $P(x)$. Let $m$ be the [degree](wikipedia://en/4382697) over $k$ of the splitting field of the resolvent cubic (it can be either $R_{4}(y)$ or $R_{5}(y)$; they have the same splitting field). Then the group $G$ is a subgroup of the [symmetric group](wikipedia://en/28901) $S_{4}$. More precisely:[^o11]

- If $m = 1$ (that is, if the resolvent cubic factors into linear factors in $k$), then $G$ is the group $\{e, (12)(34), (13)(24), (14)(23)$}.
- If $m = 2$ (that is, if the resolvent cubic has one and, [up to multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790#Multiplicity_of_a_root_of_a_polynomial), only one root in $k$), then, in order to determine $G$, one can determine whether or not $P(x)$ is still irreducible after adjoining to the field $k$ the roots of the resolvent cubic. If not, then $G$ is a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of [order](wikipedia://en/493527) 4; more precisely, it is one of the three cyclic subgroups of $S_{4}$ generated by any of its six $4$-cycles. If it is still irreducible, then $G$ is one of the three subgroups of $S_{4}$ of order $8$, each of which is isomorphic to the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) of order $8$.
- If $m = 3$, then $G$ is the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) $A_{4}$.
- If $m = 6$, then $G$ is the whole group $S_{4}$.
