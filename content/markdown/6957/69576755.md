# Zone theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zone theorem"}

*Image: Zone theorem.svg.* The zone of a line (red) in an arrangement of lines, consisting of all faces that touch the given line

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **zone theorem** is a result that establishes the complexity of the zone of a line in an [arrangement of lines](wikipedia://en/864149).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A *line arrangement*, denoted as $A(L)$, is a subdivision of the plane, induced by a set of lines $L$, into cells ($2$-dimensional faces), edges ($1$-dimensional faces) and vertices ($0$-dimensional faces). Given a set of $n$ lines $L$, the line arrangement $A(L)$, and a line $l$ (not belonging to $L$), the *zone* of $l$ is the set of faces intersected by $l$. The *complexity* of a zone is the total number of edges in its boundary, expressed as a function of $n$. The zone theorem states that said complexity is [$O(n)$](wikipedia://en/44578).

:::

## History {#History}

This result was published for the first time in 1985;[^o5] Chazelle et al. gave the [upper bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_Bound) of $10n+2$ for the complexity of the zone of a line in an arrangement. In 1991,[^o6] this bound was improved to $\lfloor9.5n\rfloor -1$, and it was also shown that this is the best possible upper bound up to a small additive factor. Then, in 2011,[^o7] Rom Pinchasi proved that the complexity of the zone of a line in an arrangement is at most $\lfloor9.5n\rfloor -3$, and this is a [tight bound](wikipedia://en/42693).

Some paradigms used in the different proofs of the theorem are [induction](wikipedia://en/18881),[^o5] [sweep](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_sweep) technique,[^o6][^o8] [tree](wikipedia://en/48560) construction,[^o9] and [Davenport-Schinzel sequences](wikipedia://en/20232529).[^o10][^o11]

::: {#o13 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Although the most popular version is for arrangements of lines in the plane, there exist some generalizations of the zone theorem. For instance, in dimension $d$, considering [arrangements of hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/864438), the complexity of the zone of a hyperplane $h$ is the number of facets ($d-1$ - dimensional faces) bounding the set of cells ($d$-dimensional faces) intersected by $h$. Analogously, the $d$-dimensional zone theorem states that the complexity of the zone of a hyperplane is $O(n^{d-1})$.[^o11] There are considerably fewer proofs for the theorem for dimension $d\geq3$. For the $3$-dimensional case, there are proofs based on sweep techniques and for higher dimensions is used Euler's relation:[^o14]

$$\sum_{i=0}^{d} (-1)^i F_i \geq 0.$$

Another generalization is considering arrangements of [pseudolines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudoline) (and [pseudohyperplanes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudohyperplanes) in dimension $d$) instead of lines (and hyperplanes). Some proofs for the theorem work well in this case since they do not use the straightness of the lines substantially through their arguments.[^o11]

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

The primary motivation to study the zone complexity in arrangements arises from looking for efficient [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775) to construct arrangements. A classical algorithm is the incremental construction, which can be roughly described as adding the lines one after the other and storing all faces generated by each in an appropriate [data structure](wikipedia://en/8519) (the usual structure for arrangements is the [doubly connected edge list (DCEL)](wikipedia://en/12015290)). Here, the consequence of the zone theorem is that the entire construction of any arrangement of $n$ lines can be done in time $O(n^2)$, since the insertion of each line takes time $O(n)$.
