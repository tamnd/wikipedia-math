# Pythagorean quadruple

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pythagorean quadruple"}

*Image: Pythagorean\_quadruples\_examples.svg.* All four primitive Pythagorean quadruples with only single-digit values

A **Pythagorean quadruple** is a [tuple](wikipedia://en/132729) of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $a$, $b$, $c$, and $d$, such that $a^{2} + b^{2} + c^{2} = d^{2}$. They are solutions of a [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109) and often only positive integer values are considered.[^o2] However, to provide a more complete geometric interpretation, the integer values can be allowed to be negative and zero (thus allowing [Pythagorean triples](wikipedia://en/24172) to be included) with the only condition being that $d > 0$. In this setting, a Pythagorean quadruple $(a, b, c, d)$ defines a [cuboid](wikipedia://en/156802) with integer side lengths $\lvert a\rvert$, $\lvert b\rvert$, and $\lvert c\rvert$, whose [space diagonal](wikipedia://en/1337505) has integer length $d$; with this interpretation, Pythagorean quadruples are thus also called *Pythagorean boxes*.[^o3] In this article we will assume, unless otherwise stated, that the values of a Pythagorean quadruple are all positive integers.

:::

## Parametrization of primitive quadruples {#Parametrization_of_primitive_quadruples}

A Pythagorean quadruple is called **primitive** if the [greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/12354) of its entries is 1. Every Pythagorean quadruple is an integer multiple of a primitive quadruple. The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of primitive Pythagorean quadruples for which $a$ is odd can be generated by the formulas

$$\begin{aligned} a &= m^2+n^2-p^2-q^2, \\ b &= 2(mq+np), \\ c &= 2(nq-mp), \\ d &= m^2+n^2+p^2+q^2, \end{aligned}$$

where $m$, $n$, $p$, $q$ are non-negative integers with greatest common divisor 1 such that $m + n + p + q$ is odd.[^o5][^o6][^o2] Thus, all primitive Pythagorean quadruples are characterized by the identity

$$(m^2 + n^2 + p^2 + q^2)^2 = (2mq + 2np)^2 + (2nq - 2mp)^2 + (m^2 + n^2 - p^2 - q^2)^2.$$

## Alternate parametrization {#Alternate_parametrization}

All Pythagorean quadruples (including non-primitives, and with repetition, though $a$, $b$, and $c$ do not appear in all possible orders) can be generated from two positive integers $a$ and $b$ as follows:

If $a$ and $b$ have different [parity](wikipedia://en/143135), let $p$ be any factor of $a^{2} + b^{2}$ such that $p^{2} < a^{2} + b^{2}$. Then $c = \tfrac{a^{2} + b^{2} - p^{2}}{2p}$ and $d = \tfrac{a^{2} + b^{2} + p^{2}}{2p}$. Note that $p = d - c$.

A similar method exists[^o8] for generating all Pythagorean quadruples for which $a$ and $b$ are both even. Let $l = \tfrac{a}{2}$ and $m = \tfrac{b}{2}$ and let $n$ be a factor of $l^{2} + m^{2}$ such that $n^{2} < l^{2} + m^{2}$. Then $c = \tfrac{l^{2} + m^{2} - n^{2}}{n}$ and $d = \tfrac{l^{2} + m^{2} + n^{2}}{n}$. This method generates all Pythagorean quadruples exactly once each when $l$ and $m$ run through all pairs of natural numbers and $n$ runs through all permissible values for each pair.

No such method exists if both $a$ and $b$ are odd, in which case no solutions exist as can be seen by the parametrization in the previous section.

## Properties {#Properties}

The largest number that always divides the product $abcd$ is 12.[^o11] The quadruple with the minimal product is (1, 2, 2, 3).

Given a Pythagorean quadruple $(a,b,c,d)$ where $d^2=a^2+b^2+c^2$ then $d$ can be defined as the norm of the quadruple in that $d = \sqrt{a^2+b^2+c^2}$ and is analogous to the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) of a Pythagorean triple.

Of the 3 terms within a primitive Pythagorean quadruple that are not the norm, exactly one will always be odd and the other two will be even. Consequently, the norm has to be odd.[^o12] [^o13]

Every odd positive number other than 1 and 5 can be the norm of a primitive Pythagorean quadruple $d^2=a^2+b^2+c^2$ such that $a, b, c$ are greater than zero and are coprime.[^o14] All primitive Pythagorean quadruples with the odd numbers as norms up to 29 except 1 and 5 are given in the table below.

Similar to a Pythagorean triple which generates a distinct right triangle, a Pythagorean quadruple will generate a distinct [Heronian triangle](wikipedia://en/731959).[^o15] If $a, b, c, d$ is a Pythagorean quadruple with $a^2 + b^2 + c^2 = d^2$ it will generate a Heronian triangle with sides $x, y, z$ as follows:

$$\begin{aligned} x &= d^2 - a^2 \\ y &= d^2 - b^2 \\ z &= d^2 - c^2 \end{aligned}$$

It will have a semiperimeter $s = d^2$, an area $A = abcd$ and an inradius $r = abc/d$.

The exradii will be:

$$\begin{aligned} r_x &= bcd/a, \\ r_y &= acd/b, \\ r_z &= abd/c. \end{aligned}$$

The [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) will be:

$$R = \frac{(d^2 - a^2)(d^2 - b^2)(d^2 - c^2)}{4abcd} = \frac{abcd(1/a^2 + 1/b^2 + 1/c^2 -1/d^2)}{4}$$

## Relationship with quaternions and rational orthogonal matrices {#Relationship_with_quaternions_and_rational_orthogonal_matrices}

A primitive Pythagorean quadruple $(a, b, c, d)$ [parametrized](wikipedia://en/453198) by $(m, n, p, q)$ corresponds to the first [column](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector) of the [matrix representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation#Matrices) $E(\alpha)$ of [conjugation](wikipedia://en/51440#Conjugation,_the_norm,_and_reciprocal) $\alpha(\cdot)\overline{\alpha}$ by the [Hurwitz quaternion](wikipedia://en/772241) $\alpha = m + ni + pj + qk$ [restricted](wikipedia://en/185427#Restriction_and_extension) to the subspace of [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) spanned by $i$, $j$, $k$, which is given by

$$E(\alpha) = \begin{pmatrix} m^2+n^2-p^2-q^2&2np-2mq &2mp+2nq \\ 2mq+2np &m^2-n^2+p^2-q^2&2pq-2mn \\ 2nq-2mp &2mn+2pq &m^2-n^2-p^2+q^2\\ \end{pmatrix},$$

where the columns are pairwise [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/102221) and each has [norm](wikipedia://en/990534#Euclidean_norm) $d$. Furthermore, we have that $\tfrac{1}{d}E(\alpha)$ belongs to the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $SO(3,\mathbb{Q})$, and, in fact, *all* 3 × 3 orthogonal matrices with [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024) coefficients arise in this manner.[^o17]
