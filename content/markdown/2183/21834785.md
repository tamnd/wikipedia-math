# Quadratic integer

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quadratic integer"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **quadratic integers** are a generalization of the usual [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) to [quadratic fields](wikipedia://en/454781). A complex number is called a quadratic integer if it is a [root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of some [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) (a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) whose [leading coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leading_coefficient) is 1) of degree two whose coefficients are integers, i.e. quadratic integers are [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) of degree two. Thus quadratic integers are those complex numbers that are solutions of equations of the form

:::

$$x^{2} + bx + c = 0$$

with $b$ and $c$ (usual) integers. When algebraic integers are considered, the usual integers are often called *rational integers*.

Common examples of quadratic integers are the [square roots](wikipedia://en/29208) of rational integers, such as $\sqrt{2}$, and the [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $i=\sqrt{-1}$, which generates the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628). Another common example is the non-[real](wikipedia://en/20646438) cubic [root of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) $\frac{-1+\sqrt{-3}}{2}$, which generates the [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035).

Quadratic integers occur in the solutions of many [Diophantine equations](wikipedia://en/9109), such as [Pell's equations](wikipedia://en/24877), and other questions related to integral [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478). The study of **rings of quadratic integers** is basic for many questions of [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705).

## History {#History}

Medieval [Indian mathematicians](wikipedia://en/1848052) had already discovered a multiplication of quadratic integers of the same [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) $D$, which allowed them to solve some cases of [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877).

The characterization given in of the quadratic integers was first given by [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106) in 1871.[^n1][^n2]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **quadratic integer** is an [algebraic integer](wikipedia://en/98981) of degree two. More explicitly, it is a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $x = (-b\pm\sqrt{b^2-4c})/2$, which solves an equation of the form $x^{2} + bx + c = 0$, with $b$ and $c$ [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). Each quadratic integer that is not an integer is not [rational](wikipedia://en/19727024)—namely, it is a real [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) if $b^{2} - 4c > 0$ and non-real if $b^{2} - 4c < 0$—and lies in a uniquely determined [quadratic field](wikipedia://en/454781) $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$, the extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ generated by the square root of the unique [square-free integer](wikipedia://en/29525) $D$ that satisfies $b^{2} - 4c = De^{2}$ for some integer $e$. If $D$ is positive, the quadratic integer is real. If $D < 0$, it is *imaginary* (that is, complex and non-real).

The quadratic integers (including the ordinary integers) that belong to a quadratic field $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$ form an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) called the *ring of integers of* $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,).$

Although the quadratic integers belonging to a given quadratic field form a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404), the set of *all* quadratic integers is not a ring because it is not closed under addition or multiplication. For example, $1+\sqrt{2}$ and $\sqrt{3}$ are quadratic integers, but $1+\sqrt{2}+\sqrt{3}$ and $(1+\sqrt{2})\cdot\sqrt{3}$ are not, as their [minimal polynomials](wikipedia://en/9667106) have [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) four.

:::

## Explicit representation {#Explicit_representation}

Here and in the following, the quadratic integers that are considered belong to a [quadratic field](wikipedia://en/454781) $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,),$ where $D$ is a square-free integer. This does not restrict the generality, as the equality $\sqrt{a^2D} = a\sqrt{D}$(for any positive integer $a$) implies $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,) = \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{a^2D}\,).$

An element $x$ of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$ is a quadratic integer if and only if there are two integers $a$ and $b$ such that either

$$x = a+b\sqrt D,$$

or, if $D -\,1$ is a multiple of $4$

$x = \frac{a}{2}+\frac{b}{2}\sqrt{D},$ with $a$ and $b$ both [odd](wikipedia://en/143135).

In other words, every quadratic integer may be written $a + \omega b\,$, where $a$ and $b$ are integers, and where $\omega$ is defined by

$$\omega = \begin{cases} \sqrt{D} & \mbox{if }D \equiv 2, 3 \pmod{4} \\ {{1 + \sqrt{D}} \over 2} & \mbox{if }D \equiv 1 \pmod{4} \end{cases}$$

(as $D$ has been supposed square-free the case $D \equiv 0\pmod{4}$ is impossible, since it would imply that $D$ is divisible by the [square](wikipedia://en/213919) 4).[^o8]

## Norm and conjugation {#Norm_and_conjugation}

A quadratic integer in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$ may be written

$$a+b\sqrt{D},$$

where $a$ and $b$ are either both integers, or, only if $D \equiv 1\pmod{4}$, both [halves of odd integers](wikipedia://en/1568608). The **norm** of such a quadratic integer is

$$N(a+b\sqrt{D})=a^2-Db^2.$$

The norm of a quadratic integer is always an integer. If $D < 0$, the norm of a quadratic integer is the square of its [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) as a complex number (this is false if $D > 0$). The norm is a [completely multiplicative function](wikipedia://en/1055940), which means that the norm of a product of quadratic integers is always the product of their norms.

Every quadratic integer $a+b\sqrt{D}$ has a **conjugate**

$$\overline{a+b\sqrt{D}} = a-b\sqrt{D}.$$

A quadratic integer has the same norm as its conjugate, and this norm is the product of the quadratic integer and its conjugate. The conjugate of a sum or a product of quadratic integers is the sum or the product (respectively) of the conjugates. This means that the conjugation is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of the ring of the integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$ – see , below.

## Quadratic integer rings {#Quadratic_integer_rings}

Every [square-free integer](wikipedia://en/29525) (different from 0 and 1) $D$ defines a **quadratic integer ring**, which is the [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) consisting of the [algebraic integers](wikipedia://en/98981) contained in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,).$ It is the set $\mathbb{Z}[\omega] = \{ a + \omega b : a,b \in \mathbb{Z} \}$ where $\omega = \tfrac{1+\sqrt D}{2}$ if $D = 4k + 1$, and $\omega = \sqrt{D}$ otherwise. It is often denoted $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)}$, because it is the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$, which is the [integral closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure) of $\mathbb{Z}$ in $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,).$ The ring $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$ consists of all [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of all equations $x^{2} + Bx + C = 0$ whose [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) $B^{2} - 4C$ is the product of $D$ by the square of an integer. In particular $\sqrt{D}$ belongs to $\mathbb{Z}[\omega]$, being a root of the equation $x^{2} - D = 0$, which has $4D$ as its discriminant.

The square root of any integer is a quadratic integer, as every integer can be written $n = m^{2}D$, where $D$ is a square-free integer, and its square root is a root of $x^{2} - m^{2}D = 0$.

The [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](wikipedia://en/11556) is not true in many rings of quadratic integers. However, there is a unique factorization for [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977), which is expressed by the fact that every ring of algebraic integers is a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132). Being the simplest examples of algebraic integers, quadratic integers are commonly the starting examples of most studies of [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705).[^n3]

The quadratic integer rings divide in two classes depending on the sign of $D$. If $D > 0$, all elements of $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)}$ are real, and the ring is a *real quadratic integer ring*. If $D < 0$, the only real elements of $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)}$ are the ordinary integers, and the ring is a *complex quadratic integer ring*.

For real quadratic integer rings, the [class number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_number_%28number_theory%29) – which measures the failure of unique factorization – is given in [OEIS A003649](https://oeis.org/A003649); for the imaginary case, they are given in [OEIS A000924](https://oeis.org/A000924).

### Units {#Units}

A quadratic integer is a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666) in the ring of the integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$ if and only if its norm is $1$ or $-1$. In the first case its [multiplicative inverse](wikipedia://en/229940) is its conjugate. It is the [negation](wikipedia://en/228312) of its conjugate in the second case.

If $D < 0$, the ring of the integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$ has at most six units. In the case of the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) ($D = -1$), the four units are $1, -1, \sqrt{-1}, -\sqrt{-1}$. In the case of the [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035) ($D = -3$), the six units are $\pm 1, \frac{\pm 1 \pm \sqrt{-3}}{2}$. For all other negative $D$, there are only two units, which are $1$ and $-1$.

If $D > 0$, the ring of the integers of $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)$ has infinitely many units that are equal to $\pm\,u^{i}$, where $i$ is an arbitrary integer, and $u$ is a particular unit called a *[fundamental unit](wikipedia://en/3995927)*. Given a fundamental unit $u$, there are three other fundamental units, its conjugate $\overline{u},$ and also $-u$ and $-\overline{u}.$ Commonly, one calls "*the* fundamental unit" the unique one which has an absolute value greater than 1 (as a real number). It is the unique fundamental unit that may be written as $a + b\sqrt{D}$, with $a$ and $b$ positive (integers or halves of integers).

The fundamental units for the 10 smallest positive square-free $D$ are $1+\sqrt{2}$ (the [silver ratio](wikipedia://en/920526#Properties)), $2+\sqrt{3}$, $\frac{1+\sqrt{5}}{2}$ (the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386#Mathematics)), $5+2\sqrt{6}$, $8+3\sqrt{7}$, $3+\sqrt{10}$, $10+3\sqrt{11}$, $\frac{3+\sqrt{13}}{2}$, $15+4\sqrt{14}$, $4+\sqrt{15}$. For larger $D$, the [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) of the fundamental unit may be very large. For example, for $D = 19, 31, 43$, the fundamental units are respectively $170+39\sqrt{19}$, $1520+273\sqrt{31}$ and $3482+531\sqrt{43}$.

### Examples of complex quadratic integer rings {#Examples_of_complex_quadratic_integer_rings}

*Image: Punktraster.svg.* Gaussian integers in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628)

*Image: EisensteinPrimes-01.svg.* Eisenstein primes in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628)

For $D$ \< 0, $\omega$ is a complex ([imaginary](wikipedia://en/14554) or otherwise non-real) number. Therefore, it is natural to treat a quadratic integer ring as a set of algebraic [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826).

- A classic example is $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-1}\,]$, the [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628), which was introduced by [Carl Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Gauss) around 1800 to state his biquadratic reciprocity law.[^n4]
- The elements in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-3}\,)} = \mathbf{Z}\left[\tfrac12\bigl(1 + \sqrt{-3}~\!\bigr)\right]$ are called [Eisenstein integers](wikipedia://en/2353035).
- The elements in $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-7}\,)} = \mathbf{Z}\left[\tfrac12\bigl(1 + \sqrt{-7}~\!\bigr)\right]$ are called [Kleinian integers](wikipedia://en/42826100)

The first two rings mentioned above are rings of integers of [cyclotomic fields](wikipedia://en/30872462) **Q**(*ζ*<sub>4</sub>) and **Q**(*ζ*<sub>3</sub>) correspondingly. In contrast, $\mathbf{Z}\bigl[\sqrt{-3}~\!\bigr]$ is not even a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132).

All the above examples are [principal ideal rings](wikipedia://en/6961771) and also [Euclidean domains](wikipedia://en/10376) for the norm. This is not the case for

$$\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-5}\,)} = \mathbf{Z}\left[\sqrt{-5}\,\right],$$

which is not even a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546). This can be shown as follows.

In $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{-5}\,)},$ we have

$$9 = 3\cdot3 = (2+\sqrt{-5})(2-\sqrt{-5}).$$

The factors 3, $2+\sqrt{-5}$ and $2-\sqrt{-5}$ are [irreducible](wikipedia://en/60693), as they have all a norm of 9, and if they were not irreducible, they would have a factor of norm 3, which is impossible, the norm of an element different of $\pm1$ being at least 4. Thus the factorization of 9 into irreducible factors is not unique.

The [ideals](wikipedia://en/25977) $\langle 3, 1+\sqrt{-5}\,\rangle$ and $\langle 3, 1-\sqrt{-5}\,\rangle$ are not [principal](wikipedia://en/145126), as a simple computation shows that their product is the ideal generated by 3, and, if they were principal, this would imply that 3 would not be irreducible.

### Examples of real quadratic integer rings {#Examples_of_real_quadratic_integer_rings}

*Image: GoldenPrimes.svg.* [Golden primes](wikipedia://en/80262208#Golden_integers) $\pi = a + b\omega$ in the real plane, using map $x, y = (\overline{\pi}, \pi)$ and leaving out multiples $\pm\omega^{n}\pi$ with $n \ne 0.$

For $D > 0$, $\omega$ is a positive irrational [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438), and the corresponding quadratic integer ring is a set of algebraic real numbers. The solutions of the [Pell's equation](wikipedia://en/24877) $X^{\,2} - DY^{\,2} = 1$, a [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109) that has been widely studied, are the [units](wikipedia://en/289666) of these rings, for $D \equiv 2,\,3\pmod{4}$.

- For $D = 5$, $\omega = \tfrac{1}{2}(1+\sqrt{5})$ is the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386). This [golden integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Golden_integer) ring was studied by [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002). Its units have the form $\pm\omega^{n}$, where $n$ is an arbitrary integer. This ring also arises from studying 5-fold [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) on [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410), for example, [Penrose tilings](wikipedia://en/26611936).[^n5]
- Indian mathematician [Brahmagupta](wikipedia://en/297206) treated the Pell's equation $X^{2} - 61Y^{2} = 1$, corresponding to the ring $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{61}]$. Some results were presented to the European community by [Pierre Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Fermat) in 1657.

### Principal rings of quadratic integers {#Principal_rings_of_quadratic_integers}

The [unique factorization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unique_factorization) property is not always verified for rings of quadratic integers, as seen above for the case of $\mathbf{Z}[\sqrt{-5}]$. However, as for every [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132), a ring of quadratic integers is a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546) if and only if it is a [principal ideal domain](wikipedia://en/25039). This occurs if and only if the [class number](wikipedia://en/150907) of the corresponding [quadratic field](wikipedia://en/454781) is one.

The imaginary rings of quadratic integers that are principal ideal rings have been completely determined. These are $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)}$ for

$$D = -1, -2, -3, -7, -11, -19, -43, -67, -163.$$

This result was first [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) by [Gauss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss) and [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) by [Kurt Heegner](wikipedia://en/621799), although Heegner's proof was not believed until [Harold Stark](wikipedia://en/788807) gave a later proof in 1967 (see *[Stark–Heegner theorem](wikipedia://en/391251)*). This is a special case of the famous [class number problem](wikipedia://en/596282).

There are many known positive integers $D > 0$ for which the ring of quadratic integers is a principal ideal ring. However, the complete list is not known; it is not even known if the number of these principal ideal rings is finite or not.

### Euclidean rings of quadratic integers {#Euclidean_rings_of_quadratic_integers}

When a ring of quadratic integers is a principal ideal domain, it is interesting to know whether it is a [Euclidean domain](wikipedia://en/10376). This problem has been completely solved as follows.

Equipped with the norm $N(a + b\sqrt{D}\,) = |a^2 - Db^2|$ as a [Euclidean function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_function), $\mathcal{O}_{\mathbf{Q}(\sqrt{D}\,)}$ is a Euclidean domain for negative $D$ when

$D = -1,\,-2,\,-3,\,-7,\,-11$,[^n6]

and, for positive $D$, when

$D = 2, 3, 5, 6, 7, 11, 13, 17, 19, 21, 29, 33, 37, 41, 57, 73$ .

There is no other ring of quadratic integers that is Euclidean with the norm as a Euclidean function.[^n7] For negative $D$, a ring of quadratic integers is Euclidean if and only if the norm is a [Euclidean function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_function) for it. It follows that, for

$$D = -19, -43, -67, -163,$$

the four corresponding rings of quadratic integers are among the rare known examples of principal ideal domains that are not Euclidean domains.

On the other hand, the [generalized Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/152567) implies that a ring of *real* quadratic integers that is a principal ideal domain is also a Euclidean domain for some Euclidean function, which can indeed differ from the usual norm.[^o22] The values *D* = 14, 69 were the first for which the ring of quadratic integers was proven to be Euclidean, but not norm-Euclidean.[^n8][^n9]
