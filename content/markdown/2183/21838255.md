# Multiple-prism dispersion theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Multiple-prism dispersion theory"}

The first description of multiple-prism arrays, and multiple-prism dispersion, was given by [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627) in his book *[Opticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opticks),* also introducing prisms as beam expanders.[^o2][^o3][^o4] Prism pair expanders were introduced by [David Brewster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Brewster) in 1813.[^o5] A modern mathematical description of the single-prism dispersion was given by [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born) and [Emil Wolf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Wolf) in 1959.[^o6] The generalized multiple-prism dispersion theory was introduced by [F. J. Duarte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._J._Duarte) and Piper[^o7][^o8] in 1982.

## Generalized multiple-prism dispersion equations {#Generalized_multiple-prism_dispersion_equations}

The generalized mathematical description of multiple-prism dispersion, as a function of the angle of incidence, prism geometry, prism [refractive index](wikipedia://en/25880), and number of prisms, was introduced as a design tool for [multiple-prism grating laser oscillators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiple-prism_grating_laser_oscillator) by [Duarte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F._J._Duarte) and Piper,[^o7][^o8] and is given by

$$\frac{\partial\phi_{2,m}}{\partial\lambda} = H_{2,m} \frac{\partial n_m}{\partial\lambda} + (k_{1,m}k_{2,m})^{-1}\bigg(H_{1,m} \frac{\partial n_m}{\partial\lambda} \pm\ \frac{\partial\phi_{2,(m-1)}}{\partial\lambda}\bigg)$$

which can also be written as

$$\nabla_{\lambda}\phi_{2,m} = H_{2,m}\nabla_{\lambda}n_m + (k_{1,m}k_{2,m})^{-1}\bigg(H_{1,m}\nabla_{\lambda}n_m \pm \nabla_{\lambda}\phi_{2,(m-1)}\bigg)$$

using

$$\nabla_\lambda = \frac{\partial}{\partial \lambda}$$

Also,

$$\begin{aligned} k_{1,m} = \frac{\cos\psi_{1,m}}{\cos\phi_{1,m}} &\qquad k_{2,m} = \frac{\cos\phi_{2,m}}{\cos\psi_{2,m}} \\[8pt] H_{1,m} = \frac{\tan\phi_{1,m}}{n_m} &\qquad H_{2,m} = \frac{\tan\phi_{2,m}}{n_m} \end{aligned}$$

Here, $\phi_{1,m}$ is the angle of incidence, at the $m$th prism, and $\psi_{1,m}$ its corresponding angle of refraction. Similarly, $\phi_{2,m}$ is the exit angle and $\psi_{2,m}$ its corresponding angle of refraction. The two main equations give the first order dispersion for an array of $m$ prisms at the exit surface of the $m$th prism. The plus sign in the second term in parentheses refers to a positive dispersive configuration while the minus sign refers to a compensating configuration.[^o7][^o8] The $k$ factors are the corresponding beam expansions, and the $H$ factors are additional geometrical quantities. It can also be seen that the dispersion of the $m$th prism depends on the dispersion of the previous prism $(m - 1)$.

These equations can also be used to quantify the angular dispersion in prism arrays, as described in [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s book *[Opticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opticks)*, and as deployed in dispersive instrumentation such as multiple-prism spectrometers. A comprehensive review on practical multiple-prism [beam expanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_expander) and multiple-prism angular dispersion theory, including explicit and ready to apply equations (engineering style), is given by Duarte.[^o15]

More recently, the generalized multiple-prism dispersion theory has been extended to include positive and [negative refraction](wikipedia://en/2219887).[^o11] Also, higher order phase derivatives have been derived using a Newtonian iterative approach.[^o12] This extension of the theory enables the evaluation of the Nth higher derivative via an elegant mathematical framework. Applications include further refinements in the design of [prism pulse compressors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_compressor) and [nonlinear optics](wikipedia://en/21723).

### Single-prism dispersion {#Single-prism_dispersion}

For a single generalized prism ($m = 1$), the generalized multiple-prism dispersion equation simplifies to[^o6][^o14]

$$\frac{\partial\phi_{2,1}}{\partial\lambda} = \frac{\sin\psi_{2,1}}{\cos\phi_{2,1}} \frac{\partial n_1}{\partial\lambda} + \frac{\cos\psi_{2,1}}{\cos\phi_{2,1}} \tan\psi_{1,1} \frac{\partial n_1}{\partial\lambda}$$

If the single prism is a right-angled prism with the beam exiting normal to the output face, that is $\phi_{2,m}$ equal to zero, this equation reduces to[^o15]

$$\frac{\partial\phi_{2,1}}{\partial\lambda} = \tan\psi_{1,1} \frac{\partial n_1}{\partial\lambda}$$

## Intracavity dispersion and laser linewidth {#Intracavity_dispersion_and_laser_linewidth}

The first application of this theory was to evaluate the [laser linewidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_linewidth) in multiple-prism grating laser oscillators.[^o7] The total intracavity angular dispersion plays an important role in the [linewidth narrowing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_linewidth) of pulsed tunable lasers through the equation[^o7][^o15]

$$\Delta\lambda \approx \Delta \theta \left({\partial\Theta\over\partial\lambda}\right)^{-1}$$

where $\Delta \theta$ is the [beam divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_divergence) and the *overall intracavity angular dispersion* is the quantity in parentheses (elevated to –1). Although originally classical in origin, in 1992 it was shown that this [laser cavity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_cavity) linewidth equation can also be derived from [interferometric quantum principles](wikipedia://en/25474577).[^o17]

For the special case of zero dispersion from the multiple-prism beam expander, the single-pass [laser linewidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_linewidth) is given by[^o15][^o14]

$$\Delta\lambda \approx \Delta \theta \left(M {\partial\theta\over\partial\lambda}\right)^{-1}$$

where *M* is the beam magnification provided by the beam expander that multiplies the angular dispersion provided by the diffraction grating. In practice, *M* can be as high as 100-200.[^o15][^o14]

When the dispersion of the multiple-prism expander is not equal to zero, then the single-pass linewidth is given by[^o7][^o15]

$$\Delta\lambda \approx \Delta \theta \left(M {\partial\theta\over\partial\lambda} + {\partial\phi_{2,m}\over\partial\lambda} \right)^{-1}$$

where the first differential refers to the angular dispersion from the grating and the second differential refers to the overall dispersion from the multiple-prism beam expander (given in the section above).[^o15][^o14]

## Further applications {#Further_applications}

In 1987 the multiple-prism angular dispersion theory was extended to provide explicit second order equations directly applicable to the design of [prismatic pulse compressors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_compressor).[^o19] The generalized multiple-prism dispersion theory is applicable to:

- [Amici prisms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amici_prism)[^o20][^o21]
- laser [microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscopy),[^o22][^o23]
- narrow-linewidth [tunable laser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tunable_laser) design,[^o24]
- [prismatic beam expanders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beam_expander)[^o7][^o8]
- [prism compressors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prism_compressor) for [femtosecond pulse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Femtosecond_pulse) lasers.[^o25][^o26][^o27]
