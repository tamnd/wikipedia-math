# Biharmonic map

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Biharmonic map"}

In the mathematical field of [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), a **biharmonic map** is a map between [Riemannian](wikipedia://en/144652) or [pseudo-Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/287555) which satisfies a certain fourth-order [partial differential equation](wikipedia://en/52564). A **biharmonic submanifold** refers to an embedding or immersion into a Riemannian or pseudo-Riemannian manifold which is a biharmonic map when the domain is equipped with its induced metric. The problem of understanding biharmonic maps was posed by [James Eells](wikipedia://en/37329912) and Luc Lemaire in 1983. The study of [harmonic maps](wikipedia://en/4577484), of which the study of biharmonic maps is an outgrowth (any harmonic map is also a biharmonic map), had been (and remains) an active field of study for the previous twenty years. A simple case of biharmonic maps is given by [biharmonic functions](wikipedia://en/3010562).

:::

## Definition {#Definition}

Given Riemannian or pseudo-Riemannian manifolds $(M, g)$ and $(N, h)$, a map $f$ from $M$ to $N$ which is differentiable at least four times is called a *biharmonic map* if

$$\Delta\Delta f+\sum_{i=1}^m R^h\big(\Delta f,df(e_i),df(e_i)\big)=0;$$

given any point $p$ of $M$, each side of this equation is an element of the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) to $N$ at $f(p)$. In other words, the above equation is an equality of sections of the [vector bundle](wikipedia://en/276524) $f^{*}TN \to M$. In the equation, $e_{1}, ..., e_{m}$ is an arbitrary $g$-orthonormal basis of the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) to $M$ and $R^{h}$ is the [Riemann curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/130526), following the convention $R(u, v, w) = \nabla_{u}\nabla_{v}w - \nabla_{v}\nabla_{u}w - \nabla_{[u, v]}w$. The quantity $\text{∆}f$ is the "tension field" or "Laplacian" of $f$, as was introduced by Eells and Sampson in the study of harmonic maps.

In terms of the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270), [interior product](wikipedia://en/2154963), and [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) operations, the biharmonic map equation can be written as

$$\Delta\Delta f+\operatorname{tr}_g\Big(f^\ast\big(\iota_{\Delta f}R^h\big)\Big)=0.$$

In terms of local coordinates $x^{i}$ for $M$ and local coordinates $y^{\alpha}$ for $N$, the biharmonic map equation is written as

$$g^{ij} \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x^i} \left(\frac{\partial(\Delta f)^\alpha}{\partial x^j}+\frac{\partial f^\beta}{\partial x^j}\Gamma_{\beta\gamma}^\alpha(\Delta f)^\gamma\right) - \Gamma_{ij}^k \left(\frac{\partial(\Delta f)^\alpha}{\partial x^k}+\frac{\partial f^\beta}{\partial x^k}\Gamma_{\beta\gamma}^\alpha(\Delta f)^\gamma\right) + \frac{\partial f^\delta}{\partial x^i} \Gamma_{\delta\epsilon}^\alpha\left(\frac{\partial(\Delta f)^\epsilon}{\partial x^j}+\frac{\partial f^\beta}{\partial x^j}\Gamma_{\beta\gamma}^\epsilon(\Delta f)^\gamma\right) \right) + g^{ij} R_{\beta\gamma\delta}^\alpha (\Delta f)^\beta\frac{\partial f^\gamma}{\partial x^i}\frac{\partial f^\delta}{\partial x^j} = 0,$$

in which the [Einstein summation convention](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Einstein_summation_convention) is used with the following definitions of the [Christoffel symbols](wikipedia://en/1401020), [Riemann curvature tensor](wikipedia://en/130526), and [tension field](wikipedia://en/4577484):

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma_{ij}^k &= \frac{1}{2}g^{kl}\left(\frac{\partial g_{jl}}{\partial x^i}+\frac{\partial g_{il}}{\partial x^j}-\frac{\partial g_{ij}}{\partial x^l}\right) \\ \Gamma_{\beta\gamma}^\alpha &= \frac{1}{2}h^{\alpha\delta} \left(\frac{\partial h_{\gamma\delta}}{\partial y^\beta}+\frac{\partial h_{\beta\delta}}{\partial y^\gamma} - \frac{\partial h_{\beta\gamma}}{\partial y^\delta}\right) \\ R_{\beta\gamma\delta}^\alpha &= \frac{\partial\Gamma_{\gamma\delta}^\alpha}{\partial y^\beta}-\frac{\partial\Gamma_{\beta\delta}^\alpha}{\partial y^\gamma}+\Gamma_{\beta\rho}^\alpha\Gamma_{\gamma\delta}^\rho-\Gamma_{\gamma\rho}^\alpha\Gamma_{\beta\delta}^\rho\\ \left(\Delta f\right)^\alpha &= g^{ij} \left(\frac{\partial^2f^\alpha}{\partial x^i\partial x^j}-\Gamma_{ij}^k\frac{\partial f^\alpha}{\partial x^k}+\frac{\partial f^\beta}{\partial x^i}\Gamma_{\beta\gamma}^\alpha\frac{\partial f^\gamma}{\partial x^j}\right). \end{aligned}$$

It is clear from any of these presentations of the equation that any harmonic map is automatically biharmonic. For this reason, a *proper biharmonic map* refers to a biharmonic map which is not harmonic.

In the special setting where $f$ is a (pseudo-)Riemannian immersion, meaning that it is an [immersion](wikipedia://en/6993953) and that $g$ is equal to the [induced metric](wikipedia://en/1253303) $f^{*}h$, one says that one has a *biharmonic submanifold* instead of a biharmonic map. Since the [mean curvature vector](wikipedia://en/684210) of $f$ is equal to the laplacian of $f : (M, f^{*}h) \to (N, h)$, one knows that an immersion is [minimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_submanifold) if and only if it is harmonic. In particular, any minimal immersion is automatically a biharmonic [submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095). A *proper biharmonic submanifold* refers to a biharmonic submanifold which is not minimal.

The motivation for the biharmonic map equation is from the *bienergy functional*

$$E_2(f) = \frac{1}{2} \, \int_M \left|\Delta f\right|_h^2 \, dv_g,$$

in the setting where $M$ is [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) and $g$ and $h$ are both Riemannian; $dv_{g}$ denotes the volume [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on $M$ induced by $g$. Eells & Lemaire, in 1983, suggested the study of [critical points](wikipedia://en/1951424) of this functional. Guo Ying Jiang, in 1986, calculated its first variation formula, thereby finding the above biharmonic map equation as the corresponding [Euler-Lagrange equation](wikipedia://en/294995). Harmonic maps correspond to critical points for which the bioenergy functional takes on its minimal possible value of zero.

## Examples and classification {#Examples_and_classification}

A number of examples of biharmonic maps, such as inverses of [stereographic projections](wikipedia://en/143431) in the special case of four dimensions, and inversions of punctured [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), are known. There are many examples of biharmonic submanifolds, such as (for any $k$) the generalized [Clifford torus](wikipedia://en/7950934)

$$\left\{x\in\mathbb{R}^{n+2}:x_1^2+\cdots+x_{k+1}^2=x_{k+2}^2+\cdots+x_{n+2}^2 = \frac{1}{2}\right\},$$

as a submanifold of the $(n + 1)$-sphere. It is minimal if and only if $n$ is even and equal to $2k$.

The biharmonic curves in three-dimensional [space forms](wikipedia://en/1087009) can be studied via the [Frenet equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frenet_equations). It follows easily that every constant-speed biharmonic curve in a three-dimensional space form of nonpositive curvature must be geodesic. Any constant-speed biharmonic curves in the round three-dimensional sphere $S^{3}$ can be viewed as the solution of a certain [constant-coefficient fourth-order linear ordinary differential equation](wikipedia://en/379868#Homogeneous_equation_with_constant_coefficients) for a $\mathbf{R}^{4}$-valued function. As such the situation can be completely analyzed, with the result that any such curve is, up to an isometry of the sphere:

- a constant-speed parametrization of the intersection of $S^{3} \subset \mathbf{R}^{4}$ with the two-dimensional linear subspace $\mathbf{R} \times \mathbf{R} \times \{0\} \times \{0\}$
- a constant-speed parametrization of the intersection of $S^{3} \subset \mathbf{R}^{4}$ with the two-dimensional affine subspace $\mathbf{R} \times \mathbf{R} \times \{d_{1}\} \times \{d_{2}\}$, for any choice of $(d_{1}, d_{2})$ which is on the circle of radius $2^{-1/2}$ around the origin in $\mathbf{R}^{2}$
- a constant-speed reparametrization of $$t\mapsto \Big(\frac{\cos at}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{\sin at}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{\cos bt}{\sqrt{2}},\frac{\sin bt}{\sqrt{2}}\Big)$$ for any $(a, b)$ on the circle of radius $2^{1/2}$ around the origin in $\mathbf{R}^{2}$.

In particular, every constant-speed biharmonic curve in $S^{3}$ has constant [geodesic curvature](wikipedia://en/483526).

As a consequence of the purely local study of the [Gauss-Codazzi equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss-Codazzi_equations) and the biharmonic map equation, any connected biharmonic surface in $S^{3}$ must have constant mean curvature. If it is nonzero (so that the surface is not minimal) then the [second fundamental form](wikipedia://en/652078) must have constant length equal to $2^{1/2}$, as follows from the biharmonic map equation. Surfaces with such strong geometric conditions can be completely classified, with the result that any connected biharmonic surface in $S^{3}$ must be either locally (up to isometry) part of the hypersphere

$$\left\{\Big((w,x,y,\frac{1}{\sqrt{2}}\Big):w^2+x^2+y^2=\frac{1}{2}\right\},$$

or minimal. In a similar way, any biharmonic hypersurface of [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) which has constant mean curvature must be minimal.

Guo Ying Jiang showed that if $g$ and $h$ are Riemannian, and if $M$ is closed and $h$ has nonpositive [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618), then a map from $(M, g)$ to $(N, h)$ is biharmonic if and only if it is harmonic. The proof is to show that, due to the sectional curvature assumption, the Laplacian of $|\text{∆}f|^{2}$ is nonnegative, at which point the [maximum principle](wikipedia://en/1436104) applies. This result and proof can be compared to Eells & Sampson's vanishing theorem, which says that if additionally the [Ricci curvature](wikipedia://en/276582) of $g$ is nonnegative, then a map from $(M, g)$ to $(N, h)$ is harmonic if and only if it is [totally geodesic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_geodesic). As a special case of Jiang's result, a closed submanifold of a Riemannian manifold of nonpositive sectional curvature is biharmonic if and only if it is minimal. Partly based on these results, it was conjectured by R. Caddeo, [S. Montaldo](wikipedia://en/52949144) and C. Oniciuc that *every* biharmonic submanifold of a Riemannian manifold of nonpositive sectional curvature must be minimal. This, however, is now known to be false. The special case of submanifolds of Euclidean space is an older conjecture of [Bang-Yen Chen](wikipedia://en/60155531). Chen's conjecture has been proven in a number of geometrically special cases.
