# Weaire–Phelan structure

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weaire–Phelan structure"}

*Image: 12-14-hedral honeycomb.png.* Weaire–Phelan structure (polyhedral cells)

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Weaire–Phelan structure** is a three-dimensional structure representing an idealised [foam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foam) of equal-sized bubbles, with two different shapes. In 1993, [Denis Weaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Weaire) and Robert Phelan found that this structure was a better solution of the **Kelvin problem** of tiling space by equal volume cells of minimum surface area than the previous best-known solution, the Kelvin structure.

:::

::: {#o3 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Kelvin conjecture"}

## History and the Kelvin problem {#History_and_the_Kelvin_problem}

In two dimensions, the subdivision of the plane into cells of equal area with minimum average perimeter is given by the [hexagonal tiling](wikipedia://en/2863719), but although the first record of this [honeycomb conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honeycomb_conjecture) goes back to the ancient Roman scholar [Marcus Terentius Varro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcus_Terentius_Varro) (116-27 BCE), it was not proven until the work of [Thomas C. Hales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_C._Hales) in 1999. In 1887, [Lord Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_Kelvin) asked the corresponding question for three-dimensional space: how can space be partitioned into cells of equal volume with the least area of surface between them? Or, in short, what was the most efficient [soap bubble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soap_bubble) foam? This problem has since been referred to as the **Kelvin problem.**

*Image: Truncated octahedra.png.* The [bitruncated cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3736299), a convex honeycomb whose [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654) cells are deformed slightly to form the Kelvin structure

Kelvin proposed a foam called the **Kelvin structure**. His foam is based on the [bitruncated cubic honeycomb](wikipedia://en/3736299), a [convex uniform honeycomb](wikipedia://en/2961) formed by the [truncated octahedron](wikipedia://en/279654), a space-filling [convex polyhedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron) with 6 square faces and 8 hexagonal faces. However, this honeycomb does not satisfy [Plateau's laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plateau's_laws), formulated by Joseph Plateau in the 19th century, according to which minimal foam surfaces meet at $120^\circ$ angles at their edges, with these edges meeting each other in sets of four with angles of $\arccos(-\tfrac{1}{3})\approx 109.47^\circ$. The angles of the polyhedral structure are different; for instance, its edges meet at angles of $90^\circ$ on square faces, or $120^\circ$ on hexagonal faces. Therefore, Kelvin's proposed structure uses curvilinear edges and slightly warped [minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734) for its faces, obeying Plateau's laws and reducing the area of the structure by 0.2% compared with the corresponding polyhedral structure.

Although Kelvin did not state it explicitly as a conjecture,[^o4] the idea that the foam of the bitruncated cubic honeycomb is the most efficient foam, and solves Kelvin's problem, became known as the **Kelvin conjecture**. It was widely believed, and no counterexample was known for more than 100 years. Finally, in 1993, [Trinity College Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College_Dublin) physicist [Denis Weaire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Weaire) and his student Robert Phelan discovered the Weaire–Phelan structure through computer simulations of foam, and showed that it was more efficient, disproving the Kelvin conjecture.

Since the discovery of the Weaire–Phelan structure, other counterexamples to the Kelvin conjecture have been found, but the Weaire–Phelan structure continues to have the smallest known surface area per cell of these counterexamples. Although numerical experiments suggest that the Weaire–Phelan structure is optimal, this remains unproven. In general, it has been very difficult to prove the optimality of structures involving [minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734). The minimality of the sphere as a surface enclosing a single volume was not proven until the 19th century, and the next simplest such problem, the [double bubble conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_bubble_conjecture) on enclosing two volumes, remained open for over 100 years until being proven in 2002.

## Description {#Description}

The Weaire–Phelan structure differs from Kelvin's in that it uses two kinds of cells, although they have equal volume. Like the cells in Kelvin's structure, these cells are combinatorially equivalent to [convex polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedron). One is a [pyritohedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyritohedron), an irregular [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407) with pentagonal faces, possessing [tetrahedral symmetry](wikipedia://en/2904472#Pyritohedral_symmetry) (*T<sub>h</sub>*). The second is a form of [truncated hexagonal trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/3521966), a species of [tetrakaidecahedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrakaidecahedron) with two hexagonal and twelve pentagonal faces, in this case only possessing two mirror planes and a [rotoreflection](wikipedia://en/44362809#glide_reflection_symmetry) symmetry. Like the hexagons in the Kelvin structure, the pentagons in both types of cells are slightly curved. The surface area of the Weaire–Phelan structure is 0.3% less than that of the Kelvin structure.

*Image: Tetrastix.gif.* [Tetrastix](wikipedia://en/64254647), modeling the face-to-face chains of tetra­kai­deca­hedron cells in the Weaire–Phelan structure

The tetrakaidecahedron cells, linked up in face-to-face chains of cells along their hexagonal faces, form chains in three perpendicular directions. A combinatorially equivalent structure to the Weaire–Phelan structure can be made as a tiling of space by unit cubes, lined up face-to-face into infinite square prisms in the same way to form a structure of interlocking prisms called [tetrastix](wikipedia://en/64254647). These prisms surround cubical voids which form one fourth of the cells of the cubical tiling; the remaining three fourths of the cells fill the prisms, offset by half a unit from the integer grid aligned with the prism walls. Similarly, in the Weaire–Phelan structure itself, which has the same symmetries as the tetrastix structure, 1/4 of the cells are dodecahedra and 3/4 are tetrakaidecahedra.

The polyhedral [honeycomb](wikipedia://en/6008654) associated with the Weaire–Phelan structure (obtained by flattening the faces and straightening the edges) is also referred to loosely as the Weaire–Phelan structure. It was known well before the Weaire–Phelan structure was discovered, but the application to the Kelvin problem was overlooked.

## Applications {#Applications}

### In physical systems {#In_physical_systems}

*Image: Patterned mold for ordered foams.jpg.* A close-up of the mold used for the growth of ordered liquid foams.

Experiments have shown that, with favorable [boundary conditions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_conditions), equal-volume bubbles spontaneously [self-assemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-assembly) into the Weaire–Phelan structure.

The associated polyhedral honeycomb is found in two related geometries of [crystal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_structure) in [chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemistry). Where the components of the crystal lie at the centres of the polyhedra it forms one of the [Frank–Kasper phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Kasper_phases), the [A15 phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A15_phase).

Where the components of the crystal lie at the corners of the polyhedra, it is known as the "Type I [clathrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clathrate) structure". [Gas hydrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas_hydrate) formed by methane, propane and carbon dioxide at low temperatures have a structure in which [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) molecules lie at the nodes of the Weaire–Phelan structure and are [hydrogen bonded](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen_bond) together, and the larger gas molecules are trapped in the polyhedral cages. Some [alkali metal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metal) [hydrides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrides) [silicides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicides) and [germanides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanium) also form this structure, with silicon or germanium at nodes, and alkali metals in cages. [Melanophlogite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melanophlogite), a [silicate mineral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silicate_mineral), also crystallises into this structure.

### In architecture {#In_architecture}

*Image: 北京 水立方 - panoramio.jpg.* [Beijing National Aquatics Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing_National_Aquatics_Centre)

The Weaire–Phelan structure is the inspiration for the design by [Tristram Carfrae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tristram_Carfrae) of the [Beijing National Aquatics Centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beijing_National_Aquatics_Centre), the 'Water Cube', for the [2008 Summer Olympics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2008_Summer_Olympics).
