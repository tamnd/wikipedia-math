# Brun's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Brun's theorem"}

*Image: Bruns-constant.svg.* The convergence to Brun's constant (*B*<sub>2</sub>). Each dot represents the effect of an additional pair of twin primes. While the exact value of *B*<sub>2</sub> is unknown, it is thought to be around \1.9 (Red Line). Calculations have shown it to be greater than \1.83 (Blue line).

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), **Brun's theorem** states that the sum of the [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of the [twin primes](wikipedia://en/41997) (pairs of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) which differ by 2) [converges](wikipedia://en/1743431) to a finite value known as **Brun's constant**, usually denoted by *B*<sub>2</sub> . Brun's theorem was [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) by [Viggo Brun](wikipedia://en/40647) in 1919, and it has historical importance in the introduction of [sieve methods](wikipedia://en/1035915).

:::

## Asymptotic bounds on twin primes {#Asymptotic_bounds_on_twin_primes}

The convergence of the sum of reciprocals of twin primes follows from bounds on the [density](wikipedia://en/343156) of the sequence of twin primes. Let $\pi_2(x)$ denote the number of [primes](wikipedia://en/23666) *p* ≤ *x* for which *p* + 2 is also prime (i.e. $\pi_2(x)$ is the number of twin primes with the smaller at most *x*). Then, we have

$$\pi_2(x) = O\!\left(\frac {x(\log\log x)^2}{(\log x)^2} \right)\!.$$

That is, twin primes are less frequent than prime numbers by nearly a logarithmic factor. This bound gives the intuition that the sum of the reciprocals of the twin primes converges, or stated in other words, the twin primes form a [small set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_set_%28combinatorics%29). In explicit terms, the sum

$$\sum\limits_{ p \, : \, p + 2 \in \mathbb{P} } {\left( {\frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{{p + 2}}} \right)} = \left( {\frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5}} \right) + \left( {\frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{7}} \right) + \left( {\frac{1}{{11}} + \frac{1}{{13}}} \right) + \cdots$$

either has finitely many terms or has infinitely many terms but is convergent: its value is known as Brun's constant.

If it were the case that the sum diverged, then that fact would imply that there are infinitely many twin primes. Because the sum of the reciprocals of the twin primes instead converges, it is not possible to conclude from this result that there are finitely many or infinitely many twin primes. Brun's constant could be an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) only if there are infinitely many twin primes.

## Numerical estimates {#Numerical_estimates}

The series converges extremely slowly. Thomas Nicely remarks that after summing the first billion (10<sup>9</sup>) terms, the relative error is still more than 5%.[^o4]

By calculating the twin primes up to 10<sup>14</sup> (and discovering the [Pentium FDIV bug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentium_FDIV_bug) along the way), Nicely heuristically estimated Brun's constant to be 1.902160578.[^o4] Nicely has extended his computation to 1.6 as of 18 January 2010 but this is not the largest computation of its type.

In 2002, [Pascal Sebah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_Sebah_%28mathematician%29) and [Patrick Demichel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Demichel) used all twin primes up to 10<sup>16</sup> to give the estimate[^o5] that *B*<sub>2</sub> ≈ 1.902160583104. Hence,

::: {#o6 .table cue=table}

| Year | *B*<sub>2</sub> | set of twin primes below # | by |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1976 | \1.902160540 | 1 × 10<sup>11</sup> | Brent |
| 1996 | \1.902160578 | 1 × 10<sup>14</sup> | Nicely |
| 2002 | \1.902160583104 | 1 × 10<sup>16</sup> | Sebah and Demichel |

:::

The last is based on extrapolation from the sum 1.830484424658... for the twin primes below 10<sup>16</sup>. Dominic Klyve showed conditionally (in an unpublished thesis) that *B*<sub>2</sub> \< 2.1754, assuming the [extended Riemann hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extended_Riemann_hypothesis). Then in 2025, Lachlan Dunn showed *B*<sub>2</sub> \< 2.1609, assuming the generalised Riemann hypothesis.[^o7] [Richard Crandall](wikipedia://en/9210048) and [Carl Pomerance](wikipedia://en/2134159) have shown unconditionally that *B*<sub>2</sub> \< 2.347.[^o8]

There is also a **Brun's constant for prime quadruplets**. A [prime quadruplet](wikipedia://en/812561) is a pair of two twin prime pairs, separated by a distance of 4 (the smallest possible distance). The first prime quadruplets are (5, 7, 11, 13), (11, 13, 17, 19), (101, 103, 107, 109). Brun's constant for prime quadruplets, denoted by *B*<sub>4</sub>, is the sum of the reciprocals of all prime quadruplets:

$$B_4 = \left(\frac{1}{5} + \frac{1}{7} + \frac{1}{11} + \frac{1}{13}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{11} + \frac{1}{13} + \frac{1}{17} + \frac{1}{19}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{101} + \frac{1}{103} + \frac{1}{107} + \frac{1}{109}\right) + \cdots$$

with value:

*B*<sub>4</sub> = \0.8705883800 ± \0.0000000005, the error range having a 99% confidence level according to Nicely.[^o4]

This constant should not be confused with the **Brun's constant for [cousin primes](wikipedia://en/343085)**, as prime pairs of the form (*p*, *p* + 4), which is also written as *B*<sub>4</sub>. Wolf derived an estimate for the Brun-type sums *B<sub>n</sub>* of 4/*n*.

## Further results {#Further_results}

Let $C_2=0.6601\ldots$ be the [twin prime constant](wikipedia://en/41997#First_Hardy.E2.80.93Littlewood_conjecture). Then it is [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that

$$\pi_2(x) \sim 2C_2\frac{x}{(\log x)^2}.$$

In particular,

$$\pi_2(x) < (2C_2+\varepsilon)\frac{x}{(\log x)^2}$$

for every $\varepsilon>0$ and all sufficiently large *x*.

Many special cases of the above have been proved. Jie Wu proved that for sufficiently large *x*,

$$\pi_2(x) \le 3.3996\cdot2C_2\,\frac{x}{(\log x)^2} < 4.5\,\frac{x}{(\log x)^2}.$$

### In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

The digits of Brun's constant were used in a bid of \$1,902,160,540 in the [Nortel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nortel) patent auction. The bid was posted by [Google](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Google) and was one of three Google bids based on [mathematical constants](wikipedia://en/48779551).[^o11] Furthermore, academic research on the constant ultimately resulted in the [Pentium FDIV bug](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentium_FDIV_bug) becoming a notable [public relations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_relations) fiasco for [Intel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intel).[^o12][^o13]
