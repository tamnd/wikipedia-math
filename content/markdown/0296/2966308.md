# Solvable Lie algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Solvable Lie algebra"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{g}$ is **solvable** if its derived series terminates in the zero subalgebra. The *derived Lie algebra* of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is the subalgebra of $\mathfrak{g}$, denoted

:::

$$[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]$$

that consists of all linear combinations of [Lie brackets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_bracket) of pairs of elements of $\mathfrak{g}$. The *derived series* is the sequence of subalgebras

$$\mathfrak{g} \supseteq [\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}] \supseteq [[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}],[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]] \supseteq [[[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}],[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]],[[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}],[\mathfrak{g},\mathfrak{g}]]] \supseteq ...$$

If the derived series eventually arrives at the zero subalgebra, then the Lie algebra is called solvable.[^o2] The derived series for Lie algebras is analogous to the [derived series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_series) for [commutator subgroups](wikipedia://en/8847) in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), and solvable Lie algebras are analogs of [solvable groups](wikipedia://en/105012).

Any [nilpotent Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/3358867) is [a fortiori](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_fortiori) solvable but the converse is not true. The solvable Lie algebras and the [semisimple Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/1337587) form two large and generally complementary classes, as is shown by the [Levi decomposition](wikipedia://en/2774198). The solvable Lie algebras are precisely those that can be obtained from [semidirect products](wikipedia://en/45603435), starting from 0 and adding one dimension at a time.[^o13]

A maximal solvable subalgebra is called a [Borel subalgebra](wikipedia://en/62074925). The largest solvable [ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_%28Lie_algebra%29) of a Lie algebra is called the [radical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radical_of_Lie_algebra).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterizations"}

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be a finite-dimensional Lie algebra over a field of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) $0$. The following are equivalent.

- (i) $\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable.
- (ii) ${\rm ad}(\mathfrak{g})$, the [adjoint representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_representation_of_a_Lie_algebra) of $\mathfrak{g}$, is solvable.
- (iii) There is a finite sequence of ideals $\mathfrak{a}_i$ of $\mathfrak{g}$:

$$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{a}_0 \supset \mathfrak{a}_1 \supset ... \mathfrak{a}_r = 0, \quad [\mathfrak{a}_i, \mathfrak{a}_i] \subset \mathfrak{a}_{i+1} \,\, \forall i .$$

- (iv) $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$ is nilpotent.[^o5]
- (v) For $\mathfrak{g}$ $n$-dimensional, there is a finite sequence of subalgebras $\mathfrak{a}_i$ of $\mathfrak{g}$:

$$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{a}_0 \supset \mathfrak{a}_1 \supset ... \mathfrak{a}_n = 0, \quad \operatorname{dim} \mathfrak{a}_{i}/\mathfrak{a}_{i + 1} = 1 \,\, \forall i,$$

with each $\mathfrak{a}_{i+1}$ an ideal in $\mathfrak{a}_i$.[^o6] A sequence of this type is called an **elementary sequence**.

- (vi) There is a finite sequence of subalgebras $\mathfrak{g}_i$ of $\mathfrak{g}$,

$$\mathfrak{g} = \mathfrak{g}_0 \supset \mathfrak{g}_1 \supset ... \mathfrak{g}_r = 0,$$

such that $\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}$ is an ideal in $\mathfrak{g}_i$ and $\mathfrak{g}_i/\mathfrak{g}_{i+1}$ is abelian.[^o7]

- (vii) The [Killing form](wikipedia://en/982386) $B$ of $\mathfrak{g}$ satisfies $B(X,Y)=0$ for all $X$ in $\mathfrak{g}$ and $Y$ in $[\mathfrak{g}, \mathfrak{g}]$.[^o8] This is [Cartan's criterion for solvability](wikipedia://en/3052668#Cartan's_criterion_for_solvability).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

[Lie's Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie's_Theorem) states that if $V$ is a finite-dimensional vector space over an algebraically closed field of [characteristic zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_%28field_theory%29), and $\mathfrak{g}$ is a solvable Lie algebra, and if $\pi$ is a [representation](wikipedia://en/318712) of $\mathfrak{g}$ over $V$, then there exists a simultaneous [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) $v \in V$ of the endomorphisms $\pi(X)$ for all elements $X \in \mathfrak{g}$.[^o11]

- Every Lie subalgebra and quotient of a solvable Lie algebra are solvable.[^o12]
- Given a Lie algebra $\mathfrak g$ and an ideal $\mathfrak h$ in it,

$\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable if and only if both $\mathfrak h$ and $\mathfrak{g}/\mathfrak h$ are solvable.[^o12][^o13]

The analogous statement is true for nilpotent Lie algebras provided $\mathfrak h$ is contained in the center. Thus, an extension of a solvable algebra by a solvable algebra is solvable, while a *central* extension of a nilpotent algebra by a nilpotent algebra is nilpotent.

- A solvable nonzero Lie algebra has a nonzero abelian ideal, the last nonzero term in the derived series.[^o13]
- If $\mathfrak{a}, \mathfrak{b} \subset \mathfrak{g}$ are solvable ideals, then so is $\mathfrak{a} + \mathfrak{b}$.[^o2] Consequently, if $\mathfrak{g}$ is finite-dimensional, then there is a unique solvable ideal $\mathfrak{r} \subset \mathfrak{g}$ containing all solvable ideals in $\mathfrak{g}$. This ideal is the **[radical](wikipedia://en/11484689)** of $\mathfrak{g}$.[^o13]
- A solvable Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ has a unique largest nilpotent ideal $\mathfrak{n}$, called the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/22468313), the set of all $X \in \mathfrak{g}$ such that ${\rm ad}_X$ is nilpotent. If $D$ is any derivation of $\mathfrak{g}$, then $D(\mathfrak{g}) \subset \mathfrak{n}$.[^o14]

:::

## Completely solvable Lie algebras {#Completely_solvable_Lie_algebras}

A Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is called **completely solvable** or **split solvable** if it has an elementary sequence of ideals in $\mathfrak{g}$ from $0$ to $\mathfrak{g}$. A finite-dimensional nilpotent Lie algebra is completely solvable, and a completely solvable Lie algebra is solvable. Over an algebraically closed field a solvable Lie algebra is completely solvable, but the $3$-dimensional real Lie algebra of the group of Euclidean isometries of the plane is solvable but not completely solvable.

A solvable Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is split solvable if and only if the eigenvalues of ${\rm ad}_X$ are in $k$ for all $X$ in $\mathfrak{g}$.[^o13]

## Examples {#Examples}

### Abelian Lie algebras {#Abelian_Lie_algebras}

Every [abelian Lie algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_Lie_algebra) $\mathfrak{a}$ is solvable by definition, since its commutator $[\mathfrak{a},\mathfrak{a}] = 0$. This includes the Lie algebra of diagonal matrices in $\mathfrak{gl}(n)$, which are of the form

> $$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} * & 0 & 0 \\ 0 & * & 0 \\ 0 & 0 & * \end{bmatrix} \right\}$$

for $n = 3$. The Lie algebra structure on a vector space $V$ given by the trivial bracket $[m,n] = 0$ for any two matrices $m,n \in \text{End}(V)$ gives another example.

### Nilpotent Lie algebras {#Nilpotent_Lie_algebras}

Another class of examples comes from [nilpotent Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/3358867) since the adjoint representation is solvable. Some examples include the upper-diagonal matrices, such as the class of matrices of the form

> $$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * \\ 0 & 0 & 0 \end{bmatrix} \right\}$$

called the Lie algebra of **strictly upper triangular matrices**. In addition, the Lie algebra of **upper diagonal matrices** in $\mathfrak{gl}(n)$ form a solvable Lie algebra. This includes matrices of the form

> $$\left\{ \begin{bmatrix} * & * & * \\ 0 & * & * \\ 0 & 0 & * \end{bmatrix} \right\}$$

and is denoted $\mathfrak{b}_k$.

### Solvable but not split-solvable {#Solvable_but_not_split-solvable}

Let $\mathfrak{g}$ be the set of matrices on the form

> $$X = \left(\begin{matrix}0 & \theta & x\\ -\theta & 0 & y\\ 0 & 0 & 0\end{matrix}\right), \quad \theta, x, y \in \mathbb{R}.$$

Then $\mathfrak{g}$ is solvable, but not split solvable.[^o13] It is isomorphic with the Lie algebra of the group of translations and rotations in the plane.

::: {#o22 .example kind=example/counterexample cue=heading name="Non-example"}

### Non-example {#Non-example}

A [semisimple Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/1337587) $\mathfrak{l}$ is never solvable since its [radical](wikipedia://en/11484689) $\text{Rad}(\mathfrak{l})$, which is the largest solvable ideal in $\mathfrak{l}$, is trivial.[^o2] <sup>page 11</sup>

:::

:::

## Solvable Lie groups {#Solvable_Lie_groups}

Because the term "solvable" is also used for [solvable groups](wikipedia://en/105012) in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), there are several possible definitions of **solvable Lie group**. For a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $G$, there is

- termination of the usual [derived series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derived_series) of the group $G$ (as an abstract group);
- termination of the closures of the derived series;
- having a solvable Lie algebra
