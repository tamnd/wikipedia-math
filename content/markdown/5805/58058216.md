# Dimension of a scheme

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dimension of a scheme"}

In algebraic geometry, the **dimension of a scheme** is a generalization of the [dimension of an algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/253251). [Scheme theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_theory) emphasizes the [relative point of view](wikipedia://en/1816636) and, accordingly, the **relative dimension** of a [morphism of schemes](wikipedia://en/7482797) is also important.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

By definition, the dimension of a scheme *X* is the dimension of the underlying [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450): the supremum of the lengths *ℓ* of chains of [irreducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreducible_space) closed subsets:

$\emptyset \ne V_0 \subsetneq V_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq V_\ell \subset X.$[^o4]

In particular, if $X = \operatorname{Spec} A$ is an [affine scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_scheme), then such chains correspond to chains of [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) (inclusion reversed), so the dimension of *X* is precisely the [Krull dimension](wikipedia://en/56079) of *A*.

If *Y* is an irreducible closed subset of a scheme *X*, then the codimension of *Y* in *X* is the supremum of the lengths *ℓ* of chains of irreducible closed subsets:

$Y = V_0 \subsetneq V_1 \subsetneq \cdots \subsetneq V_\ell \subset X.$[^o5]

An irreducible subset of *X* is an [irreducible component](wikipedia://en/2652476) of *X* if and only if its codimension in *X* is zero. If $X = \operatorname{Spec} A$ is affine, then the codimension of *Y* in *X* is precisely the height of the prime ideal defining *Y* in *X*.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- If a finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) *V* over a field is viewed as a scheme over the field,[^o8] then the dimension of the scheme *V* is the same as the vector-space dimension of *V*.
- Let $X = \operatorname{Spec} k[x, y, z]/(xy, xz)$, *k* a field. Then it has dimension 2 (since it contains the [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) $H = \{ x = 0 \} \subset \mathbb{A}^3$ as an irreducible component). If *x* is a [closed point](wikipedia://en/33086408) of *X*, then $\operatorname{codim}(x, X)$ is 2 if *x* lies in *H* and is 1 if it is in $X - H$. Thus, $\operatorname{codim}(x, X)$ for closed points *x* can vary.
- Let $X$ be an algebraic pre-variety; i.e., an integral scheme [of finite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scheme_of_finite_type) over a field $k$. Then the dimension of $X$ is the [transcendence degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_degree) of the [function field](wikipedia://en/381301) $k(X)$ of $X$ over $k$.[^o9] Also, if $U$ is a nonempty open subset of $X$, then $\dim U = \dim X$.[^o10]
- Let *R* be a [discrete valuation ring](wikipedia://en/848633) and $X = \mathbb{A}^1_R = \operatorname{Spec}(R[t])$ the affine line over it. Let $\pi: X \to \operatorname{Spec}R$ be the projection. $\operatorname{Spec}(R) = \{ s, \eta \}$ consists of 2 points, $s$ corresponding to the [maximal ideal](wikipedia://en/48164) and closed and $\eta$ the zero ideal and open. Then the fibers $\pi^{-1}(s), \pi^{-1}(\eta)$ are closed and open, respectively. We note that $\pi^{-1}(\eta)$ has dimension one,[^o11] while $X$ has dimension $2 = 1 + \dim R$ and $\pi^{-1}(\eta)$ is dense in $X$. Thus, the dimension of the closure of an open subset can be strictly bigger than that of the open set.
- Continuing the same example, let $\mathfrak{m}_R$ be the maximal ideal of *R* and $\omega_R$ a generator. We note that $R[t]$ has height-two and height-one maximal ideals; namely, $\mathfrak{p}_1 = (\omega_R t - 1)$ and $\mathfrak{p}_2 =$ the kernel of $R[t] \to R/\mathfrak{m}_R, f \mapsto f(0) \bmod\mathfrak{m}_R$. The first ideal $\mathfrak{p}_1$ is maximal since $R[t]/(\omega_R t - 1) = R[\omega_R^{-1}] =$ the [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) of *R*. Also, $\mathfrak{p}_1$ has height one by [Krull's principal ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/542421) and $\mathfrak{p}_2$ has height two since $\mathfrak{m}_R[t] \subsetneq \mathfrak{p}_2$. Consequently,

$$\operatorname{codim}(\mathfrak{p}_1, X) = 1, \, \operatorname{codim}(\mathfrak{p}_2, X) = 2,$$

while *X* is irreducible.

:::

## Equidimensional scheme {#Equidimensional_scheme}

An **equidimensional scheme** (or, **pure dimensional scheme**) is a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) whose [irreducible components](wikipedia://en/2652476) are of the same [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28scheme%29) (implicitly assuming the dimensions are all well-defined).

::: {#o14 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

All irreducible schemes are equidimensional.[^o15]

In an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), the union of a line and a point not on the line is *not* equidimensional. Generally, if two closed subschemes of some scheme, neither containing the other, have unequal dimensions, then their union is not equidimensional.

If a scheme is [smooth](wikipedia://en/35033819) (for instance, [étale](wikipedia://en/3022771)) over Spec *k* for some field *k*, then every [*connected* component](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) (which is then, in fact, an irreducible component) is equidimensional.

:::

::: {#o17 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Relative dimension (scheme theory)"}

## Relative dimension {#Relative_dimension}

Let $f: X\rightarrow Y$ be a [morphism](wikipedia://en/27716891) locally of [finite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_type_scheme) between two [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) $X$ and $Y$. The relative dimension of $f$ at a point $y \in Y$ is the [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) of the [fiber](wikipedia://en/6603892) $f^{-1} (y)$. If all the nonempty fibers are purely of the same dimension $n$, then one says that $f$ is of relative dimension $n$.[^o18]

:::
