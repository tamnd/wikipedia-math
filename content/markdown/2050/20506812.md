# FriCAS

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="FriCAS"}

**FriCAS** is a general purpose [computer algebra system](wikipedia://en/54427) with a strong focus on mathematical research and development of new [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775). It comprises an [interpreter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interpreter_%28computing%29), a [compiler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compiler) and a still-growing [library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Library_%28computing%29)[^o3] of more than 1,000 domains and categories.

:::

FriCAS provides a [strongly typed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strong_and_weak_typing) [high-level programming language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High-level_programming_language) called SPAD and a similar interactive language that uses [type-inferencing](wikipedia://en/279690) for convenience. [Aldor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aldor) was intentionally developed being the next generation compile preparation for the Axiom CAS and its [forks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fork_%28software_development%29). FriCAS optionally allows running Aldor programs (aldor is not required for compiling or running fricas). Both languages share a similar syntax and a sophisticated ([dependent](wikipedia://en/1949487)) [type system](wikipedia://en/199701).[^o4][^o5][^o6]

FriCAS is comprehensively documented and available as [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code) and as a binary [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_distribution) for the most common platforms. Compiling the Axiom lisp resources requires besides other prerequisites a [Common Lisp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_Lisp) environment, freely available as [open source](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open-source_software). A list of (incompatible) CL implementations are supported by fricas lisp code.

FriCAS runs on many [POSIX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/POSIX) platforms such as [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux), [macOS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MacOS), [Unix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix), [BSD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berkeley_Software_Distribution) as well as under [Cygwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cygwin) and [Microsoft Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows) ([WSL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_Subsystem_for_Linux)).

## History {#History}

Two computer algebra systems named **Scratchpad** were developed by [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM). The first one was started in 1965 by James Griesmer[^o8] at the request of [Ralph Gomory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_E._Gomory).[^o9] The development of this software was stopped before any public release. The second Scratchpad, originally named **Scratchpad II**, was developed from 1977 on, at [Thomas J. Watson Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_J._Watson_Research_Center), under the direction of Richard Dimick Jenks.[^o10]

The design is principally due to Richard D. Jenks (IBM Research)[^o11] , [James H. Davenport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_H._Davenport) (University of Bath), Barry M. Trager (IBM Research), David Y.Y. Yun (Southern Methodist University) and [Victor S. Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_S._Miller) (IBM Research). Early consultants on the project were David Barton (University of California, Berkeley) and [James W. Thatcher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_W._Thatcher) (IBM Research). Implementation included Robert Sutor (IBM Research), Scott C. Morrison (University of California, Berkeley), Christine J. Sundaresan (IBM Research), Timothy Daly (IBM Research), [Patrizia Gianni](wikipedia://en/70319019) (University of Pisa), Albrecht Fortenbacher (Universitaet Karlsruhe), [Stephen M. Watt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stephen_M._Watt) (IBM Research and University of Waterloo), Josh Cohen (Yale University), Michael Rothstein (Kent State University), Manuel Bronstein (IBM Research), Michael Monagan (Simon Fraser University), Jonathan Steinbach (IBM Research), William Burge (IBM Research), Jim Wen (IBM Research), William Sit (City College of New York), and Clifton Williamson (IBM Research).[^o12]

Scratchpad II was renamed **Axiom** when IBM decided, circa 1990, to make it a commercial product. A few years later, it was sold to [NAG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_Algorithms_Group). In 2001, it was withdrawn from the market and re-released to Tim Daly under the [Modified BSD License](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modified_BSD_License). In 2007, Axiom was [forked](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fork_%28software_development%29) as **FriCAS** by Waldek Hebisch following encouragement from Tim Daly[^o13] to resolve disagreements about project goals.[^o14]

## Examples {#Examples}

FriCAS has a relatively complete implementation of the [Risch–Bronstein–Trager algorithm](wikipedia://en/289450).[^o17]

Another useful feature is [stream](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_%28computer_science%29):

```
)set stream calculate 5
exp_series := series(exp x, x=0)
```

$$\ \ \ \ 1+x+{{\frac{1}{2}} \ {{x}^{2}}}+{{\frac{1}{6}} \ {{x}^{3}}}+{{\frac{1}{24}} \ {{x}^{4}}}+{{\frac{1}{120}} \ {{x}^{5}}}+{O \left( {{{x}^{6}}} \right)}$$

```
Type: UnivariatePuiseuxSeries(Expression(Integer),x,0)
```

So any coefficient may be retrieved, for instance $n=40$:

```
coefficient(exp_series,40)
```

$$\ \ \ \ \frac{1}{81591528324789773434 56112695961158942720 00000000}$$

```
Type: Expression(Integer)
```
