# Descartes number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Descartes number"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **Descartes number** is an [odd number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) which would have been an [odd perfect number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_perfect_number) if one of its [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) [factors](wikipedia://en/49492) were [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). They are named after [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), who observed that the number $D = 3^{2} \cdot 7^{2} \cdot 11^{2} \cdot 13^{2} \cdot 22021 = (3\cdot1001)^{2}\,\cdot\,(22\cdot1001 - 1) = 198585576189$ would be an odd perfect number if only $22021$ were a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), since in that case the [sum-of-divisors function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum-of-divisors_function) for $D$ would satisfy

$$\begin{aligned} \sigma(D) &= (3^2+3+1)\cdot(7^2+7+1)\cdot(11^2+11+1)\cdot(13^2+13+1)\cdot(22021+1) \\ &= (13)\cdot(3\cdot19)\cdot(7\cdot19)\cdot(3\cdot61)\cdot(22\cdot1001) \\ &= 3^2\cdot7\cdot13\cdot19^2\cdot61\cdot(22\cdot7\cdot11\cdot13) \\ &= 2 \cdot (3^2\cdot7^2\cdot11^2\cdot13^2) \cdot (19^2\cdot61) \\ &= 2 \cdot (3^2\cdot7^2\cdot11^2\cdot13^2) \cdot 22021 = 2D. \end{aligned}$$

In reality, 22021 is composite ($22021 = 19^{2}\,\cdot\,61$), and $\sigma(D) = 426027470778 = \frac{23622}{11011} D$.

:::

A Descartes number is defined as an odd number $n = m\,\cdot\,p$ where $m$ and $p$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) and $2n = \sigma(m)\,\cdot\,(p + 1)$, whence $p$ is taken as a 'spoof' prime. The example given is the only one currently known.

If $m$ is an odd [almost perfect number](wikipedia://en/322008),[^o2] that is, $\sigma(m) = 2m - 1$ and $2m - 1$ is taken as a 'spoof' prime, then $n = m\,\cdot\,(2m - 1)$ is a Descartes number, since $\sigma(n) = \sigma(m\,\cdot\,(2m - 1)) = \sigma(m)\,\cdot\,2m = (2m - 1)\,\cdot\,2m = 2n$. If $2m - 1$ were prime, $n$ would be an odd perfect number.

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

If $n$ is a [cube-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube-free) Descartes number not [divisible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divisible) by $3$, then $n$ has over one million distinct prime divisors.[^o5] If $D=pq$ is a Descartes number other than Descartes' example, with spoof-prime factor $p$, then $q > 10^{12}$.[^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

[John Voight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Voight_%28mathematician%29) generalized Descartes numbers to allow negative bases. He found the example $3^4 7^2 11^2 19^2 (-127)^1$.[^o9] Subsequent work by a group at [Brigham Young University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young_University) found more examples similar to Voight's example,[^o9] and also allowed a new class of spoofs where one is allowed to also not notice that a prime is the same as another prime in the factorization.[^o10] A generalization of Descartes numbers to [multiperfect numbers](wikipedia://en/321801) has also been constructed. ((Tóth 2025)).

:::
