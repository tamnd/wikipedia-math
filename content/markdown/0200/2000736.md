# Gauge fixing

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gauge fixing"}

In the [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) of [gauge theories](wikipedia://en/23912155), **gauge fixing** (also called **choosing a gauge**) denotes a mathematical procedure for coping with redundant [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29) in [field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_%28physics%29) variables. By definition, a [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/23912155) represents each physically distinct configuration of the system as an [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of detailed local field configurations. Any two detailed configurations in the same equivalence class are related by a certain transformation, equivalent to a [shear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_transformation) along unphysical axes in configuration space. Most of the quantitative physical predictions of a gauge theory can only be obtained under a coherent prescription for suppressing or ignoring these unphysical degrees of freedom.

:::

Although the unphysical axes in the space of detailed configurations are a fundamental property of the physical model, there is no special set of directions "perpendicular" to them. Hence there is an enormous amount of freedom involved in taking a "cross section" representing each physical configuration by a *particular* detailed configuration (or even a weighted distribution of them). Judicious gauge fixing can simplify calculations immensely, but becomes progressively harder as the physical model becomes more realistic; its application to [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) is fraught with complications related to [renormalization](wikipedia://en/291453), especially when the computation is continued to higher [orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perturbative_expansion). Historically, the search for [logically consistent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logically_consistent) and computationally tractable gauge fixing procedures, and efforts to demonstrate their equivalence in the face of a bewildering variety of technical difficulties, has been a major driver of [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416) from the late nineteenth century to the present.

## Gauge freedom {#Gauge_freedom}

The archetypical gauge theory is the [Heaviside](wikipedia://en/22831)–[Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332) formulation of continuum [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics) in terms of an [electromagnetic four-potential](wikipedia://en/731884), which is presented here in space/time asymmetric Heaviside notation. The [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) **E** and [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) **B** of [Maxwell's equations](wikipedia://en/19737) contain only "physical" degrees of freedom, in the sense that every *mathematical* degree of freedom in an electromagnetic field configuration has a separately measurable effect on the motions of test charges in the vicinity. These "field strength" variables can be expressed in terms of the [electric scalar potential](wikipedia://en/59615) $\varphi$ and the [magnetic vector potential](wikipedia://en/1191067) **A** through the relations:

$${\mathbf E} = -\nabla\varphi - \frac{\partial{\mathbf A}}{\partial t}\,, \quad {\mathbf B} = \nabla\times{\mathbf A}.$$

If the transformation

::: {#o3 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\mathbf{A} \rightarrow \mathbf{A}+\nabla\psi \tag{1}$$

:::

is made, then **B** remains unchanged, since (with the identity $\nabla \times \nabla \psi = 0$)

$${\mathbf B} = \nabla\times ({\mathbf A}+ \nabla \psi) = \nabla\times{\mathbf A}.$$

However, this transformation changes **E** according to

$$\mathbf E = -\nabla\varphi - \frac{\partial{\mathbf A}}{\partial t} - \nabla \frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial t} = -\nabla \left( \varphi + \frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial t}\right) - \frac{\partial{\mathbf A}}{\partial t}.$$

If another change

::: {#o4 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\varphi\rightarrow\varphi - \frac{\partial{\psi}}{\partial t} \tag{2}$$

:::

is made then **E** also remains the same. Hence, the **E** and **B** fields are unchanged if one takes any function $\psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ and simultaneously transforms **A** and *φ* via the transformations ((1)) and ((2)).

A particular choice of the scalar and vector potentials is a **gauge** (more precisely, **gauge potential**) and a scalar function *ψ* used to change the gauge is called a **gauge function**. The existence of arbitrary numbers of gauge functions $\psi(\mathbf{r}, t)$ corresponds to the [U(1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%281%29) **gauge freedom** of this theory. Gauge fixing can be done in many ways, some of which we exhibit below.

Although classical electromagnetism is now often spoken of as a gauge theory, it was not originally conceived in these terms. The motion of a classical point charge is affected only by the electric and magnetic field strengths at that point, and the potentials can be treated as a mere mathematical device for simplifying some proofs and calculations. Not until the advent of quantum field theory could it be said that the potentials themselves are part of the physical configuration of a system. The earliest consequence to be accurately predicted and experimentally verified was the [Aharonov–Bohm effect](wikipedia://en/277664), which has no classical counterpart. Nevertheless, gauge freedom is still true in these theories. For example, the Aharonov–Bohm effect depends on a [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) of **A** around a closed loop, and this integral is not changed by

$$\mathbf{A} \rightarrow \mathbf{A} + \nabla \psi\,.$$

Gauge fixing in [non-abelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-abelian_gauge_theory) gauge theories, such as [Yang–Mills theory](wikipedia://en/672202) and [general relativity](wikipedia://en/12024), is a rather more complicated topic; for details see [Gribov ambiguity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gribov_ambiguity), [Faddeev–Popov ghost](wikipedia://en/671768), and [frame bundle](wikipedia://en/366550).

### An illustration {#An_illustration}

*Image: gauge.png.* Gauge fixing of a *twisted* cylinder. (Note: the line is on the *surface* of the cylinder, not inside it.)

As an illustration of gauge fixing, one may look at a cylindrical rod and attempt to tell whether it is twisted. If the rod is perfectly cylindrical, then the circular symmetry of the cross section makes it impossible to tell whether or not it is twisted. However, if there were a straight line drawn along the length of the rod, then one could easily say whether or not there is a twist by looking at the state of the line. Drawing a line is **gauge fixing**. Drawing the line spoils the gauge symmetry, i.e., the circular symmetry [U(1)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/U%281%29) of the cross section at each point of the rod. The line is the equivalent of a **gauge function**; it need not be straight. Almost any line is a valid gauge fixing, i.e., there is a large **gauge freedom**. In summary, to tell whether the rod is twisted, the gauge must be known. Physical quantities, such as the energy of the torsion, do not depend on the gauge, i.e., they are **gauge invariant**.

## Coulomb gauge {#Coulomb_gauge}

The **Coulomb gauge** (also known as the **transverse gauge**[^o10] or the **radiation gauge**[^o8]) is used in [quantum chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_chemistry) and [condensed matter physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics) and is defined by the gauge condition (more precisely, gauge fixing condition)

$$\nabla\cdot{\mathbf A}(\mathbf{r},t)=0\,.$$

It is particularly useful for "semi-classical" calculations in quantum mechanics, in which the vector potential is [quantized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantization_%28physics%29) but the Coulomb interaction is not.

## Lorenz gauge {#Lorenz_gauge}

The Lorenz gauge is given, in [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) units, by:

$$\nabla\cdot{\mathbf A} + \frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}=0$$

and in [Gaussian units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian_units) by:

$$\nabla\cdot{\mathbf A} + \frac{1}{c}\frac{\partial\varphi}{\partial t}=0.$$

This may be rewritten as:

$$\partial_{\mu} A^{\mu} = 0.$$

where $A^\mu = \left[\,\tfrac{1}{c}\varphi,\,\mathbf{A}\,\right]$ is the [electromagnetic four-potential](wikipedia://en/731884), $\partial_{\mu}$ the [4-gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-gradient) \[using the [metric signature](wikipedia://en/463225) (+, −, −, −)\].

It is unique among the constraint gauges in retaining manifest [Lorentz invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariance). Note, however, that this gauge was originally named after the Danish physicist [Ludvig Lorenz](wikipedia://en/3422173) and not after [Hendrik Lorentz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lorentz); it is often misspelled "Lorentz gauge". (Neither was the first to use it in calculations; it was introduced in 1888 by [George Francis FitzGerald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Francis_FitzGerald).)

The Lorenz gauge leads to the following inhomogeneous wave equations for the potentials:

$$\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\varphi}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2{\varphi} = \frac{\rho}{\varepsilon_0}$$

$$\frac{1}{c^2}\frac{\partial^2\mathbf A}{\partial t^2} - \nabla^2{\mathbf A} = \mu_0 \mathbf{J}$$

It can be seen from these equations that, in the absence of current and charge, the solutions are potentials which propagate at the speed of light.

The Lorenz gauge is *incomplete* in some sense: there remains a subspace of gauge transformations which can also preserve the constraint. These remaining degrees of freedom correspond to gauge functions which satisfy the [wave equation](wikipedia://en/33691)

$$\frac{ \partial^2 \psi }{ \partial t^2 } = c^2 \nabla^2\psi$$

These remaining gauge degrees of freedom propagate at the speed of light. To obtain a fully fixed gauge, one must add boundary conditions along the [light cone](wikipedia://en/310474) of the experimental region.

Maxwell's equations in the Lorenz gauge simplify to

$$\partial_\mu \partial^\mu A^\nu = \mu_0 j^\nu$$

where $j^\nu = \left[\,c\,\rho,\,\mathbf{j}\,\right]$ is the [four-current](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Four-current).

Two solutions of these equations for the same current configuration differ by a solution of the vacuum wave equation

$$\partial_\mu \partial^\mu A^\nu = 0.$$

In this form it is clear that the components of the potential separately satisfy the [Klein–Gordon equation](wikipedia://en/209627), and hence that the Lorenz gauge condition allows transversely, longitudinally, and "time-like" [polarized](wikipedia://en/41564) waves in the four-potential. The transverse polarizations correspond to classical radiation, i.e., transversely polarized waves in the field strength. To suppress the "unphysical" longitudinal and time-like polarization states, which are not observed in experiments at classical distance scales, one must also employ auxiliary constraints known as [Ward identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ward_identities). Classically, these identities are equivalent to the [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793)

$$\partial_\mu j^\mu = 0.$$

Many of the differences between classical and [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) can be accounted for by the role that the longitudinal and time-like polarizations play in interactions between charged particles at microscopic distances.

## *R*<sub>*ξ*</sub> gauges {#Rξ_gauges}

section The ***R*<sub>*ξ*</sub> gauges** are a generalization of the Lorenz gauge applicable to theories expressed in terms of an [action principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Action_principle) with [Lagrangian density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lagrangian_density) $\mathcal{L}$. Instead of fixing the gauge by constraining the [gauge field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauge_field) *a priori*, via an auxiliary equation, one adds a gauge *breaking* term to the "physical" (gauge invariant) Lagrangian

$$\delta \mathcal{L} = -\frac{\left(\partial_{\mu} A^{\mu}\right)^2}{2 \xi}$$

The choice of the parameter *ξ* determines the choice of gauge. The ***R*<sub>ξ</sub> Landau gauge** is classically equivalent to Lorenz gauge: it is obtained in the limit *ξ* → 0 but postpones taking that limit until after the theory has been quantized. It improves the rigor of certain existence and equivalence proofs. Most [quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/25267) computations are simplest in the **Feynman–'t Hooft gauge**, in which $\xi = 1$; a few are more tractable in other *R*<sub>ξ</sub> gauges, such as the **Yennie gauge** $\xi = 3$ (named after [Donald R. Yennie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_R._Yennie)).

An equivalent formulation of *R*<sub>ξ</sub> gauge uses an [auxiliary field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Auxiliary_field), a scalar field *B* with no independent dynamics:

$$\delta \mathcal{L} = B\,\partial_{\mu} A^{\mu} + \frac{\xi}{2} B^2$$

The auxiliary field, sometimes called a [Nakanishi–Lautrup field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nakanishi–Lautrup_field), can be eliminated by "completing the square" to obtain the previous form. From a mathematical perspective the auxiliary field is a variety of [Goldstone boson](wikipedia://en/628183), and its use has advantages when identifying the [asymptotic states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_state) of the theory, and especially when generalizing beyond QED.

Historically, the use of *R*<sub>ξ</sub> gauges was a significant technical advance in extending [quantum electrodynamics](wikipedia://en/25268) computations beyond [one-loop order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-loop_order). In addition to retaining manifest [Lorentz invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariance), the *R<sub>ξ</sub>* prescription breaks the symmetry under local gauge *transformations* while preserving the ratio of [functional measures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_measure) of any two physically distinct gauge *configurations*. This permits a [change of variables](wikipedia://en/2146848) in which infinitesimal perturbations along "physical" directions in configuration space are entirely uncoupled from those along "unphysical" directions, allowing the latter to be absorbed into the physically meaningless [normalization](wikipedia://en/398189) of the [functional integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_integral). When ξ is finite, each physical configuration (orbit of the group of gauge transformations) is represented not by a single solution of a constraint equation but by a Gaussian distribution centered on the [extremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extremum) of the gauge breaking term. In terms of the [Feynman rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feynman_rules) of the gauge-fixed theory, this appears as a contribution to the [photon propagator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Photon_propagator) for internal lines from [virtual photons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virtual_photon) of unphysical [polarization](wikipedia://en/41564).

The photon propagator, which is the multiplicative factor corresponding to an internal photon in the [Feynman diagram](wikipedia://en/11617) expansion of a QED calculation, contains a factor *g*<sub>μν</sub> corresponding to the [Minkowski metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_metric). An expansion of this factor as a sum over photon polarizations involves terms containing all four possible polarizations. Transversely polarized radiation can be expressed mathematically as a sum over either a [linearly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_polarization) or [circularly polarized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circularly_polarized) basis. Similarly, one can combine the longitudinal and time-like gauge polarizations to obtain "forward" and "backward" polarizations; these are a form of [light-cone coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light-cone_coordinates) in which the metric is off-diagonal. An expansion of the *g*<sub>μν</sub> factor in terms of circularly polarized (spin ±1) and light-cone coordinates is called a [spin sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_sum). Spin sums can be very helpful both in simplifying expressions and in obtaining a physical understanding of the experimental effects associated with different terms in a theoretical calculation.

[Richard Feynman](wikipedia://en/25523) used arguments along approximately these lines largely to justify calculation procedures that produced consistent, finite, high precision results for important observable parameters such as the [anomalous magnetic moment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anomalous_magnetic_moment) of the electron. Although his arguments sometimes lacked mathematical rigor even by physicists' standards and glossed over details such as the derivation of [Ward–Takahashi identities](wikipedia://en/30863437) of the quantum theory, his calculations worked, and [Freeman Dyson](wikipedia://en/11397) soon demonstrated that his method was substantially equivalent to those of [Julian Schwinger](wikipedia://en/269980) and [Sin-Itiro Tomonaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sin-Itiro_Tomonaga), with whom Feynman shared the 1965 [Nobel Prize in Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physics).

Forward and backward polarized radiation can be omitted in the [asymptotic states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_states) of a quantum field theory (see [Ward–Takahashi identity](wikipedia://en/30863437)). For this reason, and because their appearance in spin sums can be seen as a mere mathematical device in QED (much like the electromagnetic four-potential in classical electrodynamics), they are often spoken of as "unphysical". But unlike the constraint-based gauge fixing procedures above, the *R<sub>ξ</sub>* gauge generalizes well to [non-abelian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-abelian_gauge_theory) gauge groups such as the [SU(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%283%29) of [QCD](wikipedia://en/25264). The couplings between physical and unphysical perturbation axes do not entirely disappear under the corresponding change of variables; to obtain correct results, one must account for the non-trivial [Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351) of the embedding of gauge freedom axes within the space of detailed configurations. This leads to the explicit appearance of forward and backward polarized gauge bosons in Feynman diagrams, along with [Faddeev–Popov ghosts](wikipedia://en/671768), which are even more "unphysical" in that they violate the [spin–statistics theorem](wikipedia://en/352827). The relationship between these entities, and the reasons why they do not appear as particles in the quantum mechanical sense, becomes more evident in the [BRST formalism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/BRST_formalism) of quantization.

## Maximal abelian gauge {#Maximal_abelian_gauge}

In any non-[abelian gauge theory](wikipedia://en/23912155), any **maximal abelian gauge** is an *incomplete* gauge which fixes the gauge freedom outside of the [maximal abelian subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximal_abelian_subgroup). Examples are

- For [SU(2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%282%29) gauge theory in D dimensions, the maximal abelian subgroup is a U(1) subgroup. If this is chosen to be the one generated by the [Pauli matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pauli_matrix) *σ*<sub>3</sub>, then the maximal abelian gauge is that which maximizes the function $$\int d^Dx \left[\left(A_\mu^1\right)^2+\left(A_\mu^2\right)^2\right]\,,$$ where $${\mathbf A}_\mu = A_\mu^a \sigma_a\,.$$
- For [SU(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%283%29) gauge theory in D dimensions, the maximal abelian subgroup is a U(1)×U(1) subgroup. If this is chosen to be the one generated by the [Gell-Mann matrices](wikipedia://en/563694) *λ*<sub>3</sub> and *λ*<sub>8</sub>, then the maximal abelian gauge is that which maximizes the function $$\int d^Dx \left[\left(A_\mu^1\right)^2 + \left(A_\mu^2\right)^2 + \left(A_\mu^4\right)^2 + \left(A_\mu^5\right)^2 + \left(A_\mu^6\right)^2 + \left(A_\mu^7\right)^2\right]\,,$$ where $${\mathbf A}_\mu = A_\mu^a \lambda_a$$

This applies regularly in higher algebras (of groups in the algebras), for example the Clifford Algebra and as it is regularly.

## Less commonly used gauges {#Less_commonly_used_gauges}

Various other gauges, which can be beneficial in specific situations have appeared in the literature.[^o10]

::: {#o20 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Temporal gauge"}

### Weyl gauge {#Weyl_gauge}

The **Weyl gauge** (also known as the **Hamiltonian** or **temporal gauge**) is an *incomplete* gauge obtained by the choice

$$\varphi=0$$

It is named after [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544). It eliminates the negative-norm [ghost](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ghost_%28physics%29), lacks manifest [Lorentz invariance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_invariance), and requires longitudinal photons and a constraint on states.[^o21]

:::

::: {#o23 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Poincaré gauge"}

### Multipolar gauge {#Multipolar_gauge}

The gauge condition of the **multipolar gauge** (also known as the **line gauge**, **point gauge** or **Poincaré gauge** (named after [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740))) is:

$$\mathbf{r}\cdot\mathbf{A} = 0.$$

This is another gauge in which the potentials can be expressed in a simple way in terms of the instantaneous fields

$$\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{r},t) = -\mathbf{r} \times\int_0^1 \mathbf{B}(u \mathbf{r},t) u \, du$$

$$\varphi(\mathbf{r},t) = -\mathbf{r} \cdot \int_0^1 \mathbf{E}(u \mathbf{r},t) du.$$

:::

::: {#o25 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Fock-Schwinger gauge"}

### Fock–Schwinger gauge {#Fock–Schwinger_gauge}

The gauge condition of the **Fock–Schwinger gauge** (named after [Vladimir Fock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vladimir_Fock) and [Julian Schwinger](wikipedia://en/269980); sometimes also called the **relativistic Poincaré gauge**) is:

$$x^{\mu}A_{\mu}=0$$

where *x*<sup>*μ*</sup> is the [position four-vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_four-vector).

:::

::: {#o27 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Dirac gauge"}

### Dirac gauge {#Dirac_gauge}

The nonlinear Dirac gauge condition (named after [Paul Dirac](wikipedia://en/24742)) is:

$$A_{\mu} A^{\mu} = k^2$$

:::
