# Regulus (geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regulus"}

*Image: Ruled hyperboloid.jpg.* A string model of a portion of a regulus and its opposite to show the rules on a hyperboloid of one sheet

In three-dimensional space, a **regulus** *R* is a set of [skew lines](wikipedia://en/1975821), every point of which is on a [transversal](wikipedia://en/897733) which intersects an element of *R* only once, and such that every point on a transversal lies on a line of *R*.

:::

The set of transversals of *R* forms an **opposite regulus** *S*. In $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ the union *R* ∪ *S* is the [ruled surface](wikipedia://en/705158) of a [hyperboloid of one sheet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperboloid_of_one_sheet).

Any 3 skew lines generate a pair of reguli:[^o2]

1. The set of lines that intersect all 3 of them sweeps out a quadratic surface. This ruling of this quadratic surface is the regulus.
1. The set of lines that intersect all lines in the regulus is the *complementary regulus* or *associated regulus*, by [Gallucci's theorem](wikipedia://en/1975821#Gallucci's_theorem).

Any 3 lines in a regulus generate the complementary regulus, and vice versa. The regulus surface is the unique quadratic surface that contains these 3 lines. The pair of reguli sweep out the same surface, showing that it is a [doubly ruled surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doubly_ruled_surface).

According to [Charlotte Scott](wikipedia://en/8610998), "The regulus supplies extremely simple proofs of the properties of a conic...the theorems of Chasles, [Brianchon](wikipedia://en/1554065), and [Pascal](wikipedia://en/699966) ..."[^o3]

In a [finite geometry](wikipedia://en/267061) PG(3, *q*), a regulus has *q* + 1 lines.[^o4] For example, in 1954 [William Edge](wikipedia://en/3996306) described a pair of reguli of four lines each in PG(3,3).[^o5]

[Robert J. T. Bell](wikipedia://en/29914241) described how the regulus is generated by a moving straight line. First, the hyperboloid $\frac{x^2}{a^2} + \frac{y^2}{b^2} - \frac{z^2}{c^2} \ = \ 1$ is factored as

$$\left(\frac{x}{a} + \frac{z}{c}\right) \left(\frac{x}{a} - \frac{z}{c}\right) \ =\ \left(1 + \frac{y}{b}\right) \left(1 - \frac{y}{b}\right) .$$

Then the following two systems of lines, parametrized by λ and μ satisfy that equation:

$\frac{x}{a} + \frac{z}{c} \ =\ \lambda \left(1 + \frac{y}{b}\right), \quad \frac{x}{a} - \frac{z}{c} \ =\ \frac{1}{\lambda} \left(1 - \frac{y}{b}\right)$ and

$$\frac{x}{a} - \frac{z}{c} \ =\ \mu \left(1 + \frac{y}{b}\right), \quad \frac{x}{a} + \frac{z}{c} \ =\ \frac{1}{\mu} \left(1 - \frac{y}{b}\right) .$$

No member of the first set of lines is a member of the second. As λ or μ varies, the hyperboloid is generated. The two sets represent a regulus and its opposite. Using [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202), Bell proves that no two generators in a set intersect, and that any two generators in opposite reguli do intersect and form the plane tangent to the hyperboloid at that point. (page 155).[^o6]
