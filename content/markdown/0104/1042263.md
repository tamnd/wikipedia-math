# Poynting's theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Poynting's theorem"}

In [electrodynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrodynamics), **Poynting's theorem** is a statement of [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088) for [electromagnetic fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_field) that was developed by British [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist) [John Henry Poynting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_Poynting).[^o2] It states that in a given volume, the stored energy changes at a rate given by the [work](wikipedia://en/149861) done on the [charges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_distribution) within the volume, minus the rate at which energy leaves the volume. It is only strictly true in media that is not [dispersive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersive_medium), but can be extended for the dispersive case.[^o3] The theorem is analogous to the [work-energy theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Work-energy_theorem) in [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), and mathematically similar to the [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Poynting's theorem states that the rate of energy transfer per unit volume from a region of space equals the rate of [work](wikipedia://en/149861) done on the charge distribution in the region, plus the [energy flux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy_flux) leaving that region.

Mathematically:

where:

- $-\frac{\partial u}{\partial t}$ is the rate of change of the energy density in the volume.
- ∇ ⋅ **S** is the energy flow out of the volume, given by the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of the [Poynting vector](wikipedia://en/49718) **S**.
- **J** ⋅ **E** is the power density of the field doing work on charges (**J** is the [current density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Current_density) corresponding to the motion of charge, **E** is the [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092), and ⋅ is the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093)).

where

- **S** is the energy flow, given by the Poynting Vector
- *u* is the energy density
- $\partial V \!$ is the boundary of the volume. The shape of the volume is arbitrary but fixed.

### Continuity equation analog {#Continuity_equation_analog}

In an [electrical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_engineering) context the theorem is sometimes written with the energy density term *u* expanded as shown. This form resembles the [continuity equation](wikipedia://en/410793):

$$\nabla\cdot\mathbf{S} + \varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E}\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t} + \frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E} = 0 ,$$

where

- *ε*<sub>0</sub> is the [vacuum permittivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permittivity) and *μ*<sub>0</sub> is the [vacuum permeability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_permeability).
- $\varepsilon_0 \mathbf{E}\cdot\frac{\partial \mathbf{E}}{\partial t}$ is the density of [reactive power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power) driving the build-up of electric field,
- $\frac{\mathbf{B}}{\mu_0}\cdot\frac{\partial\mathbf{B}}{\partial t}$ is the density of [reactive power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power) driving the build-up of magnetic field, and
- $\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$ is the density of [electric power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_power) dissipated by the [Lorentz force](wikipedia://en/18631) acting on charge carriers.

## Derivation {#Derivation}

The rate of work done by the electromagnetic field on the infinitesimal charge

$$dq=\rho d^3x$$

is given by the [Lorentz Force Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentz_Force_Law) as:

$$dP=d\mathbf{F}\cdot\mathbf{v}= (\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})dq\cdot\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{E}\cdot \rho\mathbf{v}d^3x+0=\mathbf{E}\cdot\mathbf{J}d^3x$$

(the dot product $(\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B})\cdot \mathbf{v}=0$ because from the definition of [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) the cross product of **v** and **B** is perpendicular to **v**). Where *ρ* is the volume charge density and **J** = *ρ***v** is the current density at the point and time where **v** is the velocity of the charge dq. The rate of work done on the whole charges in the volume *V* will be the volume integral

$$P=\int_V dP = \int_V \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} ~\mathrm d^{3}x$$

By [Ampère's circuital law](wikipedia://en/199304):

$$\mathbf{J} = \nabla \times \mathbf{H} - \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}$$

(Note that the **H** and **D** forms of the magnetic and electric fields are used here. The **B** and **E** forms could also be used in an equivalent derivation.)[^o11]

Substituting this into the expression for rate of work gives:

$$\int_V \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} ~\mathrm d^{3}x = \int_V \left [ \mathbf{E} \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{H}) - \mathbf{E} \cdot \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\right ] ~ \mathrm d^{3}x$$

Using the [vector identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_identity) $\nabla \cdot (\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}) =\ (\nabla {\times} \mathbf{E}) \cdot \mathbf{H} \,-\, \mathbf{E} \cdot (\nabla {\times} \mathbf{H})$:

$$\int_V \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} ~ \mathrm d^{3}x = - \int_V \left [ \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}) - \mathbf{H} \cdot (\nabla \times \mathbf{E}) + \mathbf{E} \cdot \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t}\right ] ~ \mathrm d^{3}x$$

By [Faraday's Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell–Faraday_equation):

$$\nabla \times \mathbf{E} = -\frac{\partial \mathbf{B}} {\partial t}$$

giving:

$$\int_V \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} ~ \mathrm d^{3}x = - \int_V \left [ \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}) + \mathbf{E} \cdot \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{H} \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}} {\partial t}\right ] ~ \mathrm d^{3}x$$

Continuing the derivation requires the following assumptions:[^o3]

- the charges are moving in a medium that is not [dispersive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dispersive_medium).
- the total electromagnetic energy density, even for time-varying fields, is given by $$u = \frac{1}{2} (\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{D} + \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathbf{H})$$

It can be shown[^o12] that:

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{D}) = 2 \mathbf{E} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathbf{D}$$

and

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t}(\mathbf{H} \cdot \mathbf{B}) = 2 \mathbf{H} \cdot \frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathbf{B}$$

and so:

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \mathbf{E} \cdot \frac{\partial\mathbf{D}}{\partial t} + \mathbf{H} \cdot \frac{\partial \mathbf{B}} {\partial t}$$

Returning to the equation for rate of work,

$$\int_V \mathbf{J} \cdot \mathbf{E} ~ \mathrm d^{3}x = - \int_V \left [ \frac{\partial u}{\partial t} + \nabla \cdot (\mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H})\right ] ~ \mathrm d^{3}x$$

Since the volume is arbitrary, this can be cast in differential form as:

$$-\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \nabla\cdot\mathbf{S}+\mathbf{J}\cdot\mathbf{E}$$

where $\mathbf{S} = \mathbf{E} \times \mathbf{H}$ is the Poynting vector.

## Poynting vector in macroscopic media {#Poynting_vector_in_macroscopic_media}

In a macroscopic medium, electromagnetic effects are described by spatially averaged (macroscopic) fields. The Poynting vector in a macroscopic medium can be defined self-consistently with microscopic theory, in such a way that the spatially averaged microscopic Poynting vector is exactly predicted by a macroscopic formalism. This result is strictly valid in the limit of low-loss and allows for the unambiguous identification of the Poynting vector form in macroscopic electrodynamics.[^o14][^o15]

## Alternative forms {#Alternative_forms}

It is possible to derive alternative versions of Poynting's theorem.[^o17] Instead of the flux vector **E** × **H** as above, it is possible to follow the same style of derivation, but instead choose **E** × **B**, the [Minkowski](wikipedia://en/1190648) form **D** × **B**, or perhaps **D** × **H**. Each choice represents the response of the propagation medium in its own way: the **E** × **B** form above has the property that the response happens only due to electric currents, while the **D** × **H** form uses only (fictitious) [magnetic monopole](wikipedia://en/175470) currents. The other two forms (Abraham and Minkowski) use complementary combinations of electric and magnetic currents to represent the polarization and magnetization responses of the medium.[^o17]

## Modification {#Modification}

The derivation of the statement is dependent on the assumption that the materials the equation models can be described by a set of [susceptibility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_susceptibility) properties that are [linear](wikipedia://en/91591), [isotropic](wikipedia://en/14865), homogenous and independent of [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779).[^o19] The assumption that the materials have no absorption must also be made. A modification to Poynting's theorem to account for variations includes a term for the rate of [non-Ohmic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-ohmic_resistance) [absorption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorption_%28electromagnetic_radiation%29) in a material, which can be calculated by a simplified approximation based on the [Drude model](wikipedia://en/542575).[^o19]

$$\frac{\partial}{\partial t} \mathcal{U} + \nabla \cdot \mathbf{S} + \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathbf{J}_\text{free} + \mathcal{R}_{\dashv\int} = 0$$

## Complex Poynting vector theorem {#Complex_Poynting_vector_theorem}

This form of the theorem is useful in Antenna theory, where one has often to consider harmonic fields propagating in the space. In this case, using phasor notation, $E(t) = E e^{j\omega t}$ and $H(t) = H e^{j\omega t}$. Then the following mathematical identity holds:

$${1\over 2} \int_{\partial \Omega} E\times H^* \cdot d{\mathbf a} = {j\omega \over 2}\int_\Omega (\varepsilon E E^* - \mu H H^*) dv - {1\over 2} \int_\Omega EJ^* dv,$$

where $J$ is the current density.

Note that in free space, $\varepsilon$ and $\mu$ are real, thus, taking the real part of the above formula, it expresses the fact that the [averaged radiated power](wikipedia://en/49718#Time-averaged_Poynting_vector) flowing through $\partial \Omega$ is equal to the work on the charges.
