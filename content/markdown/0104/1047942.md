# Muirhead's inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Muirhead's inequality"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Muirhead's inequality**, named after [Robert Franklin Muirhead](wikipedia://en/31785962), also known as the "bunching" method, generalizes the [inequality of arithmetic and geometric means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_of_arithmetic_and_geometric_means).

:::

## Preliminary definitions {#Preliminary_definitions}

### *a*-mean {#a-mean}

For any [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [vector](wikipedia://en/32370)

$$a=(a_1,\dots,a_n)$$

define the "*a*-mean" \[*a*\] of positive real numbers *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub> by

$$[a]=\frac{1}{n!}\sum_\sigma x_{\sigma_1}^{a_1}\cdots x_{\sigma_n}^{a_n},$$

where the sum extends over all [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027) σ of { 1, ..., *n* }.

When the elements of *a* are nonnegative integers, the *a*-mean can be equivalently defined via the [monomial symmetric polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomial_symmetric_polynomial) $m_a(x_1,\dots,x_n)$ as

$$[a] = \frac{k_1!\cdots k_l!}{n!} m_a(x_1,\dots,x_n),$$

where ℓ is the number of distinct elements in *a*, and *k*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *k*<sub>ℓ</sub> are their multiplicities.

Notice that the *a*-mean as defined above only has the usual properties of a [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) (e.g., if the mean of equal numbers is equal to them) if $a_1+\cdots+a_n=1$. In the general case, one can consider instead $[a]^{1/(a_1+\cdots+a_n)}$, which is called a [Muirhead mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muirhead_mean).[^o4]

**Examples**

- For *a* = (1, 0, ..., 0), the *a*-mean is just the ordinary [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) of *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>.
- For *a* = (1/*n*, ..., 1/*n*), the *a*-mean is the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of *x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>.
- For *a* = (*x*, 1 − *x*), the *a*-mean is the [Heinz mean](wikipedia://en/6967420).
- The [Muirhead mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muirhead_mean) for *a* = (−1, 0, ..., 0) is the [harmonic mean](wikipedia://en/14463).

### Doubly stochastic matrices {#Doubly_stochastic_matrices}

An *n* × *n* matrix *P* is *[doubly stochastic](wikipedia://en/2906180)* precisely if both *P* and its transpose *P*<sup>T</sup> are [stochastic matrices](wikipedia://en/217313). A *stochastic matrix* is a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) of nonnegative real entries in which the sum of the entries in each column is 1. Thus, a doubly stochastic matrix is a square matrix of nonnegative real entries in which the sum of the entries in each row and the sum of the entries in each column is 1.

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Muirhead's inequality states that \[*a*\] ≤ \[*b*\] for all *x* such that *x*<sub>*i*</sub> > 0 for every *i* ∈ { 1, ..., *n* } if and only if there is some doubly stochastic matrix *P* for which *a* = *Pb*.

Furthermore, in that case we have \[*a*\] = \[*b*\] if and only if *a* = *b* or all *x*<sub>*i*</sub> are equal.

The latter condition can be expressed in several equivalent ways; one of them is given below.

The proof makes use of the fact that every doubly stochastic matrix is a weighted average of [permutation matrices](wikipedia://en/238849) ([Birkhoff-von Neumann theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkhoff-von_Neumann_theorem)).

### Another equivalent condition {#Another_equivalent_condition}

Because of the symmetry of the sum, no generality is lost by sorting the exponents into decreasing order:

$$a_1 \geq a_2 \geq \cdots \geq a_n$$

$$b_1 \geq b_2 \geq \cdots \geq b_n.$$

Then the existence of a doubly stochastic matrix *P* such that *a* = *Pb* is equivalent to the following system of inequalities:

$$\begin{aligned} a_1 & \leq b_1 \\ a_1+a_2 & \leq b_1+b_2 \\ a_1+a_2+a_3 & \leq b_1+b_2+b_3 \\ & \,\,\, \vdots \\ a_1+\cdots +a_{n-1} & \leq b_1+\cdots+b_{n-1} \\ a_1+\cdots +a_n & = b_1+\cdots+b_n. \end{aligned}$$

(The *last* one is an equality; the others are weak inequalities.)

The sequence $b_1, \ldots, b_n$ is said to [**majorize**](wikipedia://en/2864495) the sequence $a_1, \ldots, a_n$.

:::

## Symmetric sum notation {#Symmetric_sum_notation}

It is convenient to use a special notation for the sums. A success in reducing an inequality in this form means that the only condition for testing it is to verify whether one exponent sequence ($\alpha_1, \ldots, \alpha_n$) majorizes the other one.

$$\sum_\text{sym} x_1^{\alpha_1} \cdots x_n^{\alpha_n}$$

This notation requires developing every permutation, developing an expression made of *n*! [monomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomials), for instance:

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_\text{sym} x^3 y^2 z^0 &= x^3 y^2 z^0 + x^3 z^2 y^0 + y^3 x^2 z^0 + y^3 z^2 x^0 + z^3 x^2 y^0 + z^3 y^2 x^0 \\ &= x^3 y^2 + x^3 z^2 + y^3 x^2 + y^3 z^2 + z^3 x^2 + z^3 y^2 \end{aligned}$$

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

::: {#o13 .statement cue=named-heading name="Arithmetic-geometric mean inequality"}

### Arithmetic-geometric mean inequality {#Arithmetic-geometric_mean_inequality}

Let

$$a_G = \left( \frac 1 n , \ldots , \frac 1 n \right)$$

and

$$a_A = ( 1 , 0, 0, \ldots , 0 ).$$

We have

$$\begin{aligned} a_{A1} = 1 & > a_{G1} = \frac 1 n, \\ a_{A1} + a_{A2} = 1 & > a_{G1} + a_{G2} = \frac 2 n, \\ & \,\,\, \vdots \\ a_{A1} + \cdots + a_{An} & = a_{G1} + \cdots + a_{Gn} = 1. \end{aligned}$$

Then

\[*a<sub>A</sub>*\] ≥ \[*a<sub>G</sub>*\],

which is

$$\frac 1 {n!} (x_1^1 \cdot x_2^0 \cdots x_n^0 + \cdots + x_1^0 \cdots x_n^1) (n-1)! \geq \frac 1 {n!} (x_1 \cdot \cdots \cdot x_n)^{1/n} n!$$

yielding the inequality.

:::

### Other examples {#Other_examples}

We seek to prove that *x*<sup>2</sup> + *y*<sup>2</sup> ≥ 2*xy* by using bunching (Muirhead's inequality). We transform it in the symmetric-sum notation:

$$\sum_ \mathrm{sym} x^2 y^0 \ge \sum_\mathrm{sym} x^1 y^1.$$

The sequence (2, 0) majorizes the sequence (1, 1), thus the inequality holds by bunching.

Similarly, we can prove the inequality

$$x^3+y^3+z^3 \ge 3 x y z$$

by writing it using the symmetric-sum notation as

$$\sum_ \mathrm{sym} x^3 y^0 z^0 \ge \sum_\mathrm{sym} x^1 y^1 z^1,$$

which is the same as

$$2 x^3 + 2 y^3 + 2 z^3 \ge 6 x y z.$$

Since the sequence (3, 0, 0) majorizes the sequence (1, 1, 1), the inequality holds by bunching.

:::
