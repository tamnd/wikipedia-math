# Sub-Riemannian manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sub-Riemannian manifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **sub-Riemannian manifold** is a certain type of generalization of a [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652). Roughly speaking, to measure distances in a sub-Riemannian manifold, you are allowed to go only along curves tangent to so-called *horizontal subspaces*.

:::

Sub-Riemannian manifolds (and so, *a fortiori*, Riemannian manifolds) carry a natural [intrinsic metric](wikipedia://en/475335) called the **metric of Carnot–Carathéodory**. The [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) of such [metric spaces](wikipedia://en/20018) is always an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) and larger than its [topological dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_dimension) (unless it is actually a Riemannian manifold).

Sub-Riemannian manifolds often occur in the study of constrained systems in [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586), such as the motion of vehicles on a surface, the motion of robot arms, and the orbital dynamics of satellites. Geometric quantities such as the [Berry phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berry_phase) may be understood in the language of sub-Riemannian geometry. The [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518), important to [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), carries a natural sub-Riemannian structure.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

By a *distribution* on $M$ we mean a [subbundle](wikipedia://en/2517151) of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) of $M$ (see also [distribution](wikipedia://en/2976052)).

Given a distribution $H(M)\subseteq T(M)$ a vector field in $H(M)$ is called *horizontal*. A curve $\gamma$ on $M$ is called horizontal if $\dot\gamma(t)\in H_{\gamma(t)}(M)$ for any $t$.

A distribution $H(M)$ is called *completely non-integrable* or *bracket generating* if for any $x\in M$ we have that any tangent vector can be presented as a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of [Lie brackets](wikipedia://en/10282799) of horizontal fields, i.e. vectors of the form

$$A(x),\ [A,B](x),\ [A,[B,C]](x),\ [A,[B,[C,D]]](x),\dotsc\in T_x(M)$$

where all vector fields $A,B,C,D, \dots$ are horizontal. This requirement is also known as [Hörmander's condition](wikipedia://en/13659915).

A sub-Riemannian manifold is a triple $(M, H, g)$, where $M$ is a differentiable [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), $H$ is a completely non-integrable "horizontal" distribution and $g$ is a smooth section of positive-definite [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478) on $H$.

Any (connected) sub-Riemannian manifold carries a natural [intrinsic metric](wikipedia://en/475335), called the metric of Carnot–Carathéodory, defined as

$$d(x, y) = \inf\int_0^1 \sqrt{g(\dot\gamma(t),\dot\gamma(t))} \, dt,$$

where infimum is taken along all *horizontal curves* $\gamma: [0, 1] \to M$ such that $\gamma(0)=x$, $\gamma(1)=y$. Horizontal curves can be taken either [Lipschitz continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous), [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous) or in the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) $H^1([0,1],M)$ producing the same metric in all cases.

The fact that the distance between two points is always finite (i.e. any two points are connected by a horizontal curve) is a consequence of Hörmander's condition known as [Chow–Rashevskii theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chow–Rashevskii_theorem).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

A position of a car on the plane is determined by three parameters: two coordinates $x$ and $y$ for the location and an angle $\alpha$ which describes the orientation of the car. Therefore, the position of the car can be described by a point in a manifold

$$\mathbb R^2\times S^1.$$

One can ask, what is the minimal distance one should drive to get from one position to another? This defines a [Carnot–Carathéodory metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carnot–Carathéodory_metric) on the manifold

$$\mathbb R^2\times S^1.$$

A closely related example of a sub-Riemannian metric can be constructed on a [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518): Take two elements $\alpha$ and $\beta$ in the corresponding Lie algebra such that

$$\{ \alpha,\beta,[\alpha,\beta]\}$$

spans the entire algebra. The distribution $H$ spanned by left shifts of $\alpha$ and $\beta$ is *completely non-integrable*. Then choosing any smooth positive quadratic form on $H$ gives a sub-Riemannian metric on the group.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

For every sub-Riemannian manifold, there exists a [Hamiltonian](wikipedia://en/198319), called the **sub-Riemannian Hamiltonian**, constructed out of the metric for the manifold. Conversely, every such quadratic Hamiltonian induces a sub-Riemannian manifold.

Solutions of the corresponding [Hamilton–Jacobi equations](wikipedia://en/897539) for the sub-Riemannian Hamiltonian are called geodesics, and generalize [Riemannian geodesics](wikipedia://en/91096).
