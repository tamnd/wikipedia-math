# Laguerre polynomials

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Laguerre polynomials"}

*Image: Complex color plot of the Laguerre polynomial L n(x) with n as -1 divided by 9 and x as z to the power of 4 from -2-2i to 2+2i.svg.* Complex color plot of $L_{-1/9}(z^{4})\text{ from } -2-2i\text{ to } 2+2i$

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Laguerre polynomials**, named after [Edmond Laguerre](wikipedia://en/1011080) (1834–1886), are nontrivial solutions of **Laguerre's differential equation:**

$$xy'' + (1 - x)y' + ny = 0,\ y = L(x)$$

which is a second-order [linear differential equation](wikipedia://en/379868). This equation has [nonsingular solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonsingular_solution) only if $n$ is a non-negative integer.

:::

Sometimes the name **Laguerre polynomials** is used for solutions of

$$xy'' + (\alpha + 1 - x)y' + ny = 0~.$$

where $n$ is still a non-negative integer. Then they are also named **generalized Laguerre polynomials**, as will be done here (alternatively **associated Laguerre polynomials** or, rarely, **Sonine polynomials**, after their inventor[^o2] [Nikolay Yakovlevich Sonin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikolay_Yakovlevich_Sonin)).

More generally, a **Laguerre function** is a solution when $n$ is not necessarily a non-negative integer.

The Laguerre polynomials are also used for [Gauss–Laguerre quadrature](wikipedia://en/18511187) to numerically compute integrals of the form

$$\int_0^\infty f(x) e^{-x} \, dx.$$

These polynomials, usually denoted $L_{0}$, $L_{1}$, ..., are a [polynomial sequence](wikipedia://en/195990) which may be defined by the [Rodrigues formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_formula#Rodrigues_formula),

$$L_n(x)=\frac{e^x}{n!}\frac{d^n}{dx^n}\left(e^{-x} x^n\right) =\frac{1}{n!} \left( \frac{d}{dx} -1 \right)^n x^n,$$

reducing to the closed form of a following section.

They are [orthogonal polynomials](wikipedia://en/32811718) with respect to an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product)

$$\langle f,g \rangle = \int_0^\infty f(x) g(x) e^{-x}\,dx.$$

The [rook polynomials](wikipedia://en/14031635) in combinatorics are more or less the same as Laguerre polynomials, up to elementary changes of variables. Further see the [Tricomi–Carlitz polynomials](wikipedia://en/32757711).

The Laguerre polynomials arise in quantum mechanics, in the radial part of the solution of the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874) for a one-electron atom. They also describe the static Wigner functions of oscillator systems in [quantum mechanics in phase space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_space_formulation#Simple_harmonic_oscillator). They further enter in the quantum mechanics of the [Morse potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_potential) and of the [3D isotropic harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719#Example:_3D_isotropic_harmonic_oscillator).

Physicists sometimes use a definition for the Laguerre polynomials that is larger by a factor of *n*! than the definition used here. (Likewise, some physicists may use somewhat different definitions of the so-called associated Laguerre polynomials.)

## Recursive definition, closed form, and generating function {#Recursive_definition,_closed_form,_and_generating_function}

One can also define the Laguerre polynomials recursively, defining the first two polynomials as

$$L_0(x) = 1$$

$$L_1(x) = 1 - x$$

and then using the following [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/32811718#Recurrence_relations) for any $k \ge 1$:

$$L_{k + 1}(x) = \frac{(2k + 1 - x)L_k(x) - k L_{k - 1}(x)}{k + 1}.$$

Furthermore,

$$x L'_n(x) = nL_n (x) - nL_{n-1}(x).$$

In solution of some boundary value problems, the characteristic values can be useful:

$$L_{k}(0) = 1, L_{k}'(0) = -k.$$

The **[closed form](wikipedia://en/585143)** is

$$L_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k}\frac{(-1)^k}{k!} x^k .$$

The [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) for them likewise follows,

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty t^n L_n(x)= \frac{1}{1-t} e^{-tx/(1-t)}.$$

The operator form is

$$L_n(x) = \frac{1}{n!}e^x \frac{d^n}{dx^n} (x^n e^{-x})$$

Polynomials of negative index can be expressed using the ones with positive index:

$$L_{-n}(x)=e^xL_{n-1}(-x).$$

## Generalized Laguerre polynomials {#Generalized_Laguerre_polynomials}

For arbitrary real α the polynomial solutions of the differential equation[^o6]

$$x\,y'' + \left(\alpha +1 - x\right) y' + n\,y = 0$$

are called **generalized Laguerre polynomials**, or **associated Laguerre polynomials**.

One can also define the generalized Laguerre polynomials recursively, defining the first two polynomials as

$$L^{(\alpha)}_0(x) = 1$$

$$L^{(\alpha)}_1(x) = 1 + \alpha - x$$

and then using the following [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/32811718#Recurrence_relations) for any $k \ge 1$:

$$L^{(\alpha)}_{k + 1}(x) = \frac{(2k + 1 + \alpha - x)L^{(\alpha)}_k(x) - (k + \alpha) L^{(\alpha)}_{k - 1}(x)}{k + 1}.$$

The simple Laguerre polynomials are the special case $\alpha = 0$ of the generalized Laguerre polynomials:

$$L^{(0)}_n(x) = L_n(x).$$

The [Rodrigues formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rodrigues_formula) for them is

$$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x) = {x^{-\alpha} e^x \over n!}{d^n \over dx^n} \left(e^{-x} x^{n+\alpha}\right) = \frac{x^{-\alpha}}{n!}\left( \frac{d}{dx}-1\right)^nx^{n+\alpha}.$$

The [generating function](wikipedia://en/160993) for them is

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty t^n L^{(\alpha)}_n(x)= \frac{1}{(1-t)^{\alpha+1}} e^{-tx/(1-t)}.$$

### Properties {#Properties}

- Laguerre functions are defined by [confluent hypergeometric functions](wikipedia://en/1516095) and Kummer's transformation as[^o9] $$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x) := {n+ \alpha \choose n} M(-n,\alpha+1,x).$$ where ${n+ \alpha \choose n}$ is a generalized [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668). When $n$ is an integer the function reduces to a polynomial of degree $n$. It has the alternative expression[^o10] $$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)= \frac {(-1)^n}{n!} U(-n,\alpha+1,x)$$ in terms of [Kummer's function of the second kind](wikipedia://en/1516095).
- The closed form for these generalized Laguerre polynomials of degree $n$ is[^o11] $$L_n^{(\alpha)} (x) = \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i {n+\alpha \choose n-i} \frac{x^i}{i!}$$ derived by applying [Leibniz's theorem for differentiation of a product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leibniz_rule_%28generalized_product_rule%29) to Rodrigues' formula.
- Laguerre polynomials have a [differential operator](wikipedia://en/263886) representation, much like the closely related Hermite polynomials. Namely, let $D = \frac{d}{dx}$ and consider the differential operator $M=xD^2+(\alpha+1)D$. Then $\exp(-tM)x^n=(-1)^nt^nn!L^{(\alpha)}_n\left(\frac{x}{t}\right)$.

### As a contour integral {#As_a_contour_integral}

Given the generating function specified above, the polynomials may be expressed in terms of a [contour integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contour_integral)

$$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i}\oint_C\frac{e^{-xt/(1-t)}}{(1-t)^{\alpha+1}\,t^{n+1}} \; dt,$$

where the contour circles the origin once in a counterclockwise direction without enclosing the [essential singularity](wikipedia://en/80862) at 1

### Recurrence relations {#Recurrence_relations}

The addition formula for Laguerre polynomials:[^o16]

$$L^{(\alpha_{1}+\dots+\alpha_{r}+r-1)}_{n}\left(x_{1}+\dots+x_{r}\right)=\sum_{m_{1}+\dots+m_{r}=n}L^{(\alpha_{1})}_{m_{1}}\left(x_{1}\right)\cdots L^{(\alpha_{r})}_{m_{r}}\left(x_{r}\right).$$

Laguerre's polynomials satisfy the recurrence relations

$$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)= \sum_{i=0}^n L_{n-i}^{(\alpha+i)}(y)\frac{(y-x)^i}{i!},$$

in particular

$$L_n^{(\alpha+1)}(x)= \sum_{i=0}^n L_i^{(\alpha)}(x)$$

and

$$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)= \sum_{i=0}^n {\alpha-\beta+n-i-1 \choose n-i} L_i^{(\beta)}(x),$$

or

$$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)=\sum_{i=0}^n {\alpha-\beta+n \choose n-i} L_i^{(\beta- i)}(x);$$

moreover

$$\begin{aligned} L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)- \sum_{j=0}^{\Delta-1} {n+\alpha \choose n-j} (-1)^j \frac{x^j}{j!}&= (-1)^\Delta\frac{x^\Delta}{(\Delta-1)!} \sum_{i=0}^{n-\Delta} \frac{{n+\alpha \choose n-\Delta-i}}{(n-i){n \choose i}}L_i^{(\alpha+\Delta)}(x)\\[6pt] &=(-1)^\Delta\frac{x^\Delta}{(\Delta-1)!} \sum_{i=0}^{n-\Delta} \frac{{n+\alpha-i-1 \choose n-\Delta-i}}{(n-i){n \choose i}}L_i^{(n+\alpha+\Delta-i)}(x) \end{aligned}$$

They can be used to derive the four 3-point-rules

$$\begin{aligned} L_n^{(\alpha)}(x) &= L_n^{(\alpha+1)}(x) - L_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1)}(x) = \sum_{j=0}^k {k \choose j}(-1)^j L_{n-j}^{(\alpha+k)}(x), \\[10pt] n L_n^{(\alpha)}(x) &= (n + \alpha )L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x) - x L_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1)}(x), \\[10pt] & \text{or } \\ \frac{x^k}{k!}L_n^{(\alpha)}(x) &= \sum_{i=0}^k (-1)^i {n+i \choose i} {n+\alpha \choose k-i} L_{n+i}^{(\alpha-k)}(x), \\[10pt] n L_n^{(\alpha+1)}(x) &= (n-x) L_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1)}(x) + (n+\alpha)L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x) \\[10pt] x L_n^{(\alpha+1)}(x) &= (n+\alpha)L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x)-(n-x)L_n^{(\alpha)}(x); \end{aligned}$$

combined they give this additional, useful recurrence relations

$$\begin{aligned} L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)&= \left(2+\frac{\alpha-1-x}n \right)L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x)- \left(1+\frac{\alpha-1}n \right)L_{n-2}^{(\alpha)}(x)\\[10pt] &= \frac{\alpha+1-x}n L_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1)}(x)- \frac x n L_{n-2}^{(\alpha+2)}(x) \end{aligned}$$

Since $L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)$ is a [monic polynomial](wikipedia://en/314730) of degree $n$ in $\alpha$, there is the [partial fraction decomposition](wikipedia://en/247288)

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{n!\,L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)}{(\alpha+1)_n} &= 1- \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^j \frac{j}{\alpha + j} {n \choose j}L_n^{(-j)}(x) \\ &= 1- \sum_{j=1}^n \frac{x^j}{\alpha + j}\,\,\frac{L_{n-j}^{(j)}(x)}{(j-1)!} \\ &= 1-x \sum_{i=1}^n \frac{L_{n-i}^{(-\alpha)}(x) L_{i-1}^{(\alpha+1)}(-x)}{\alpha +i}. \end{aligned}$$

The second equality follows by the following identity, valid for integer *i* and $n$ and immediate from the expression of $L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)$ in terms of [Charlier polynomials](wikipedia://en/19585960):

$$\frac{(-x)^i}{i!} L_n^{(i-n)}(x) = \frac{(-x)^n}{n!} L_i^{(n-i)}(x).$$

For the third equality apply the fourth and fifth identities of this section.

### Derivatives {#Derivatives}

Differentiating the [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) representation of a generalized Laguerre polynomial $k$ times leads to

$$\frac{d^k}{d x^k} L_n^{(\alpha)} (x) = \begin{cases} (-1)^k L_{n-k}^{(\alpha+k)}(x) & \text{if } k\le n, \\ 0 & \text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

This points to a special case ($\alpha = 0$) of the formula above: for integer $\alpha = k$ the generalized polynomial may be written

$$L_n^{(k)}(x)=(-1)^k\frac{d^kL_{n+k}(x)}{dx^k},$$

the shift by $k$ sometimes causing confusion with the usual parenthesis notation for a derivative.

Moreover, the following equation holds:

$$\frac{1}{k!} \frac{d^k}{d x^k} x^\alpha L_n^{(\alpha)} (x) = {n+\alpha \choose k} x^{\alpha-k} L_n^{(\alpha-k)}(x),$$

which generalizes with [Cauchy's formula](wikipedia://en/2823#Techniques_of_integration) to

$$L_n^{(\alpha')}(x) = (\alpha'-\alpha) {\alpha'+ n \choose \alpha'-\alpha} \int_0^x \frac{t^\alpha (x-t)^{\alpha'-\alpha-1}}{x^{\alpha'}} L_n^{(\alpha)}(t)\,dt.$$

The derivative with respect to the second variable $\alpha$ has the form,[^o18]

$$\frac{d}{d \alpha}L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)= \sum_{i=0}^{n-1} \frac{L_i^{(\alpha)}(x)}{n-i}.$$

The generalized Laguerre polynomials obey the differential equation

$$x L_n^{(\alpha) \prime\prime}(x) + (\alpha+1-x)L_n^{(\alpha)\prime}(x) + n L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)=0,$$

which may be compared with the equation obeyed by the *k*th derivative of the ordinary Laguerre polynomial,

$$x L_n^{[k] \prime\prime}(x) + (k+1-x)L_n^{[k]\prime}(x) + (n-k) L_n^{[k]}(x)=0,$$

where $L_n^{[k]}(x)\equiv\frac{d^kL_n(x)}{dx^k}$ for this equation only.

In [Sturm–Liouville form](wikipedia://en/490990) the differential equation is

$$-\left(x^{\alpha+1} e^{-x}\cdot L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)^\prime\right)' = n\cdot x^\alpha e^{-x}\cdot L_n^{(\alpha)}(x),$$

which shows that $L^{(\alpha)}_{n}$ is an eigenvector for the eigenvalue $n$.

### Orthogonality {#Orthogonality}

The generalized Laguerre polynomials are [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) over 0, ∞ with respect to the measure with [weighting function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weighting_function) $x^{\alpha} e^{-x}$:[^o20]

$$\int_0^\infty x^\alpha e^{-x} L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)L_m^{(\alpha)}(x)dx=\frac{\Gamma(n+\alpha+1)}{n!} \delta_{n,m},$$

which follows from

$$\int_0^\infty x^{\alpha'-1} e^{-x} L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)dx= {\alpha-\alpha'+n \choose n} \Gamma(\alpha').$$

If $\Gamma(x,\alpha+1,1)$ denotes the [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079) then the orthogonality relation can be written as

$$\int_0^{\infty} L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)L_m^{(\alpha)}(x)\Gamma(x,\alpha+1,1) dx={n+ \alpha \choose n}\delta_{n,m}.$$

The associated, symmetric kernel polynomial has the representations ([Christoffel–Darboux formula](wikipedia://en/25164285))

$$\begin{aligned} K_n^{(\alpha)}(x,y) &:= \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)} \sum_{i=0}^n \frac{L_i^{(\alpha)}(x) L_i^{(\alpha)}(y)}{{\alpha+i \choose i}}\\[4pt] & =\frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)} \frac{L_n^{(\alpha)}(x) L_{n+1}^{(\alpha)}(y) - L_{n+1}^{(\alpha)}(x) L_n^{(\alpha)}(y)}{\frac{x-y}{n+1} {n+\alpha \choose n}} \\[4pt] &= \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)}\sum_{i=0}^n \frac{x^i}{i!} \frac{L_{n-i}^{(\alpha+i)}(x) L_{n-i}^{(\alpha+i+1)}(y)}{{\alpha+n \choose n}{n \choose i}}; \end{aligned}$$

recursively

$$K_n^{(\alpha)}(x,y)=\frac{y}{\alpha+1} K_{n-1}^{(\alpha+1)}(x,y)+ \frac{1}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)} \frac{L_n^{(\alpha+1)}(x) L_n^{(\alpha)}(y)}{{\alpha+n \choose n}}.$$

Moreover, by the orthogonality and completeness relation for the generalized Laguerre polynomials, the kernel converges in the sense of distributions:

$$y^\alpha e^{-y} K_n^{(\alpha)}(\cdot,y) \to \delta(y-\cdot), \qquad n\to\infty.$$

[Turán's inequalities](wikipedia://en/14989249) can be derived here, which is

$$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)^2- L_{n-1}^{(\alpha)}(x) L_{n+1}^{(\alpha)}(x)= \sum_{k=0}^{n-1} \frac{{\alpha+n-1\choose n-k}}{n{n\choose k}} L_k^{(\alpha-1)}(x)^2>0.$$

The following [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) is needed in the [quantum mechanical](wikipedia://en/25202) treatment of the [hydrogen atom](wikipedia://en/14225#Wavefunction),

$$\int_0^{\infty}x^{\alpha+1} e^{-x} \left[L_n^{(\alpha)} (x)\right]^2 dx= \frac{(n+\alpha)!}{n!}(2n+\alpha+1).$$

### Series expansions {#Series_expansions}

Let a function have the (formal) series expansion

$$f(x)= \sum_{i=0}^\infty f_i^{(\alpha)} L_i^{(\alpha)}(x).$$

Then

$$f_i^{(\alpha)}=\int_0^\infty \frac{L_i^{(\alpha)}(x)}{{i+ \alpha \choose i}} \cdot \frac{x^\alpha e^{-x}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)} \cdot f(x) \,dx .$$

The series converges in the associated [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $L^{2}[0, \infty)$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922)

$$\| f \|_{L^2}^2 := \int_0^\infty \frac{x^\alpha e^{-x}}{\Gamma(\alpha+1)} | f(x)|^2 \, dx = \sum_{i=0}^\infty {i+\alpha \choose i} |f_i^{(\alpha)}|^2 < \infty.$$

#### Further examples of expansions {#Further_examples_of_expansions}

[Monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) are represented as

$$\frac{x^n}{n!}= \sum_{i=0}^n (-1)^i {n+ \alpha \choose n-i} L_i^{(\alpha)}(x),$$

while [binomials](wikipedia://en/4668) have the parametrization

$${n+x \choose n}= \sum_{i=0}^n \frac{\alpha^i}{i!} L_{n-i}^{(x+i)}(\alpha).$$

This leads directly to

$$e^{-\gamma x}= \sum_{i=0}^\infty \frac{\gamma^i}{(1+\gamma)^{i+\alpha+1}} L_i^{(\alpha)}(x) \qquad \text{convergent iff } \Re(\gamma) > -\tfrac{1}{2}$$

for the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678). The [incomplete gamma function](wikipedia://en/456050) has the representation

$$\Gamma(\alpha,x)=x^\alpha e^{-x} \sum_{i=0}^\infty \frac{L_i^{(\alpha)}(x)}{1+i} \qquad \left(\Re(\alpha)>-1 , x > 0\right).$$

### Asymptotics {#Asymptotics}

#### In terms of elementary functions {#In_terms_of_elementary_functions}

For any fixed positive integer $M$, fixed [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\alpha$, fixed and bounded interval $[c, d] \subset (0, + \infty)$, uniformly for $x \in [c, d]$, at $n \to \infty$:

$$L^{(\alpha)}_{n}\left(x\right)=\frac{n^{\frac{1}{2}\alpha-\frac{1}{4}}{\mathrm{e}}^{\frac{1}{2}x}}{{\pi}^{\frac{1}{2}}x^{\frac{1}{2}\alpha+\frac{1}{4}}}\left(\cos\theta_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)\left(\sum_{m=0}^{M-1}\frac{a_{m}(x)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}m}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{1}{2}M}}\right)\right)+\sin\theta_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x)\left(\sum_{m=1}^{M-1}\frac{b_{m}(x)}{n^{\frac{1}{2}m}}+O\left(\frac{1}{n^{\frac{1}{2}M}}\right)\right)\right)$$

where

$$\theta_{n}^{(\alpha)}(x) :=2(nx)^{\frac{1}{2}}-\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\alpha+\tfrac{1}{4}\right)\pi.$$

and $a_0, b_1, a_1, b_2, \dots$ are functions depending on $\alpha, x$ but not $n$, and regular for $x > 0$. The first few ones are:

$$\begin{aligned} & a_0(x)=1 \\ & a_1(x)=0 \\ & b_1(x)=\frac{1}{48 x^{\frac{1}{2}}}\left(4 x^2-24 (\alpha + 1) x+3-12 \alpha^2\right) \end{aligned}$$

This is [Perron](wikipedia://en/1485469)'s formula.[^o25][^o26] There is also a generalization for $x \in \mathbb C \setminus [0, \infty)$.[^o27] [Fejér](wikipedia://en/3934357)'s formula is a special case of Perron's formula with $M = 1$.[^o28][^o27][^o29]

#### In terms of Bessel functions {#In_terms_of_Bessel_functions}

The [Mehler–Heine formula](wikipedia://en/32798823) states:

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} n^{-\alpha}L_n^{(\alpha)}\left(\frac{z^2}{4n}\right) = \left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{-\alpha} J_\alpha(z),$$

where $J_\alpha$ is a [Bessel function of the first kind](wikipedia://en/4700#Bessel_functions_of_the_first_kind:_Jα).

See also:.[^o25]

#### In terms of Airy functions {#In_terms_of_Airy_functions}

Let $\nu =4 n+2 \alpha+2$. Let $\operatorname{Ai}$ be the [Airy function](wikipedia://en/406896). Let $\alpha$ be arbitrary and real, $\epsilon$ and $\omega$ be positive and fixed.

The [Plancherel–Rotach asymptotics](wikipedia://en/73964350) formulas:[^o32][^o25]

- for $x =\nu\cos^2\varphi$ and $\epsilon\leq \varphi \leq \tfrac{\pi}{2} -\epsilon n^{-1/2}$, uniformly at $n \to \infty$:

$$e^{-x/2}L^{(\alpha )}_n(x) =(-1)^{n}(\pi \sin \varphi)^{-1/2}x^{-\alpha/2-1/4}n^{\alpha/2-1/4} \big\{\sin\left[\left(n+\tfrac{\alpha+1}{2}\right)(\sin 2\varphi-2\varphi)+3\pi/4\right] +(nx)^{-1/2}\mathcal{O}(1)\big\}$$

- for $x = \nu\cosh^2\varphi$ and $\epsilon\leq \varphi \leq \omega$, uniformly at $n \to \infty$:

$$e^{-x/2}L^{(\alpha )}_n(x) =\tfrac{1}{2}(-1)^{n}(\pi \sinh \varphi )^{-1/2}x^{-\alpha/2-1/4}n^{\alpha /2-1/4} \exp\left[\left(n+\tfrac{\alpha+1}{2}\right)(2\varphi-\sinh 2\varphi)\right] \{1+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-1}\right)\}$$

- for $x =\nu -2(2n/3)^{1/3}t$ and $t$ complex and bounded, uniformly at $n \to \infty$:

$$e^{-x/2}L^{(\alpha)}_n(x) =(-1)^n\pi^{-1}2^{-\alpha-1/3}3^{1/3}n^{-1/3} \bigg\{\pi \operatorname{Ai}(-3^{-1/3}t)+\mathcal{O}\left(n^{-2/3}\right)\bigg\}$$

See DLMF for higher-order terms.[^o25]

## Zeroes {#Zeroes}

::: {#o35 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

### Notation {#Notation}

$j_{\alpha, m}$ is the $m$-th positive zero of the [Bessel function](wikipedia://en/4700) $J_\alpha(x)$.

$a_m$ is the $m$-th zero of the Airy function $\operatorname{Ai}(x)$, in descending order: $0 > a_1 > a_2 > \cdots$.

$$\nu =4 n+2 \alpha+2.$$

If $\alpha > -1$, then $L_n^{(\alpha)}$ has $n$ real roots. Thus in this section we assume $\alpha > -1$ by default.

$x_1 < \dots < x_n$ are the real roots of $L_n^{(\alpha)}$.

Note that $\left((-1)^{n-i} L_{n-i}^{(\alpha)}\right)_{i=0}^n$ is a [Sturm chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sturm_chain).

:::

### Inequalities {#Inequalities}

For $\alpha > -1$, we have these bounds:[^o37][^o38][^o13][^o39]

- $$x_1 < \frac{(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)}{n+\alpha+1}$$
- $$x_1 < \frac{(\alpha+1)(\alpha+3)}{2 n+\alpha+1}$$
- $$x_1 < \frac{(\alpha+1)(\alpha+2)(\alpha+4)(2 n+\alpha+1)}{(\alpha+1)^2(\alpha+2)+n(5 \alpha+11)(n+\alpha+1)}$$
- $x_n \leq 2 n+\alpha-1+2 \sqrt{(n-2)(n+\alpha-1)}$ when $n \geq 2$
- $$x_n > 4n + \alpha - 16 \sqrt{2n}$$
- $$x_n > 3n-4$$
- $$x_n > 2n + \alpha - 1$$
- $$x_n > 2 n+\alpha-2+\sqrt{n^2-2 n+\alpha n+2}$$
- $$\begin{aligned} & (n+2) x_1 &\geq\left(n-1-\sqrt{n^2+(n+2)(\alpha+1)}\right)^2-1 \\ & (n+2) x_n &\leq\left(n-1+\sqrt{n^2+(n+2)(\alpha+1)}\right)^2-1 \end{aligned}$$
- $$\begin{aligned} x_1 &> \frac 12 \nu - 3 -\sqrt{1+4(n-1)(n+\alpha-1)} \\ x_n &< \frac 12 \nu - 3 +\sqrt{1+4(n-1)(n+\alpha-1)} \end{aligned}$$

For fixed $k = 1, \dots, n$,[^o37][^o13][^o38]

$$\begin{aligned} \nu x_k&>j_{\alpha, k}^2 \\ x_k&< \frac{j_{\alpha, k}^2}{\nu / 2+\sqrt{(\nu / 2)^2-j_{\alpha, k}^2}} \quad \text{ if } \nu / 2 > j_{\alpha, k}\\ x_k &< \left[\nu^{1 / 2}+2^{-1 / 3}\nu^{-1 / 6} a_{n-k+1}\right]^2 \quad \text{ if }|\alpha| \geqslant 1 / 4\\ x_k &< \nu+2^{\frac{2}{3}} a_k \nu^{\frac{1}{3}}+2^{-\frac{2}{3}} a_k^2 \nu^{-\frac{1}{3}} \end{aligned}$$

For fixed $k$, we have $\lim_{n \to \infty} \nu x_k = j_{\alpha, k}^2$, so the first inequality is sharp.

See also.[^o40]

### Electrostatics {#Electrostatics}

The zeroes satisfy the **[Stieltjes](wikipedia://en/40589) relations**:[^o42][^o43]

$$\begin{aligned} \sum_{1 \leq j \leq n, i \neq j} \frac{1}{x_{i} - x_{j}} &= \frac 12 \left(1 - \frac{\alpha + 1}{x_i}\right)\\ \sum_{1 \leq j \leq n} \frac{1}{x_{j}} &= \frac{n}{\alpha + 1}\\ \sum_{1 \leq j \leq n, i \neq j} \frac{1}{(x_{i} - x_{j})^2} &= -\frac{(\alpha + 1)(\alpha + 5)}{12 x_i^2} + \frac{2n + \alpha + 1}{6x_i}- \frac{1}{12}\\ \sum_{1 \leq j \leq n, i \neq j} \frac{1}{(x_{i} - x_{j})^3} &= -\frac{(\alpha + 1)(\alpha + 3)}{8 x_i^3} + \frac{2n + \alpha + 1}{8x_i^2}\\ \end{aligned}$$

The first relation can be interpreted physically. Fix an electric particle at origin with charge $+\frac{\alpha + 1}{2}$, and produce a constant [electric field](wikipedia://en/41092) of strength $-\frac 12$. Then, place $n$ electric particles with charge $+1$. The first relation states that the zeroes of $L_n^{(\alpha)}$ are the equilibrium positions of the particles.

As the zeroes specify the polynomial up to scaling, this provides an alternative way to uniquely characterize the Laguerre polynomials.

The zeroes also satisfy[^o44]

$$\sum_{i=1}^n \frac{1}{x-x_i }=-\sum_{k=0}^{\infty} S_{k+1} x^k, \quad S_k := \sum_{i = 1}^n x_i^{-k}$$

which allows the following bound

$$S_m^{-1 / m}<x_1<S_m / S_{m+1}, \quad m=1,2, \ldots$$

### Limit distribution {#Limit_distribution}

Let $F_n(t) := \frac 1n \#\{i : x_{i} \leq t\}$ be the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) for the roots, then we have the limit law[^o46]

$$\lim_{n \to \infty} F_n(4n t) = \frac 2\pi \int_{0}^t \sqrt{\frac{1-s}{s}} ds \quad \forall t \in (0, 1]$$

which can be interpreted as the limit distribution of the [Wishart ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wishart_ensemble) spectrum.

For fixed $\alpha > -1$ and fixed $k$, as $n \to \infty$,[^o38]

$$\begin{aligned} x_{n+1-k}= & \nu+2^{2 / 3} a_k \nu^{1 / 3}+\frac{1}{5} 2^{4 / 3} a_k^2 \nu^{-1 / 3}+\left(\frac{11}{35}-\alpha^2-\frac{12}{175} a_k^3\right) \nu^{-1} \\ & +\left(\frac{16}{1575} a_k+\frac{92}{7875} a_k^4\right) 2^{2 / 3} \nu^{-5 / 3}-\left(\frac{15152}{3031875} a_k^5+\frac{1088}{121275} a_k^2\right) 2^{1 / 3} \nu^{-7 / 3}+\mathcal{O}\left(\nu^{-3}\right), \end{aligned}$$

For $\alpha \in (-1, 0)$,[^o44]

$$\begin{aligned} x_1=\frac{\alpha+1}{n} & +\frac{n-1}{2}\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{n}\right)^2-\frac{n^2+3 n-4}{12}\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{n}\right)^3 \\ & +\frac{7 n^3+6 n^2+23 n-36}{144}\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{n}\right)^4 \\ & -\frac{293 n^4+210 n^3+235 n^2+990 n-1728}{8640}\left(\frac{\alpha+1}{n}\right)^5+\cdots \end{aligned}$$

## In quantum mechanics {#In_quantum_mechanics}

In quantum mechanics the Schrödinger equation for the [hydrogen-like atom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen-like_atom) is exactly solvable by separation of variables in spherical coordinates. The radial part of the wave function is a (generalized) Laguerre polynomial.[^o48]

[Vibronic transitions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vibronic_coupling) in the Franck-Condon approximation can also be described using Laguerre polynomials.[^o49]

::: {#o51 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Multiplication theorems"}

## Multiplication theorems {#Multiplication_theorems}

[Erdélyi](wikipedia://en/4388684) gives the following two [multiplication theorems](wikipedia://en/8720712) [^o52]

$$\begin{aligned} & t^{n+1+\alpha} e^{(1-t) z} L_n^{(\alpha)}(z t)=\sum_{k=n}^\infty {k \choose n}\left(1-\frac 1 t\right)^{k-n} L_k^{(\alpha)}(z), \\[6pt] & e^{(1-t)z} L_n^{(\alpha)}(z t)=\sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{(1-t)^k z^k}{k!}L_n^{(\alpha+k)}(z). \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Relation to Hermite polynomials {#Relation_to_Hermite_polynomials}

The generalized Laguerre polynomials are related to the [Hermite polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomial):

$$\begin{aligned} H_{2n}(x) &= (-1)^n 2^{2n} n! L_n^{(-1/2)} (x^2) \\[4pt] H_{2n+1}(x) &= (-1)^n 2^{2n+1} n! x L_n^{(1/2)} (x^2) \end{aligned}$$

where the $H_{n}(x)$ are the [Hermite polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomial) based on the weighting function $\exp(-x^{2})$, the so-called "physicist's version."

Because of this, the generalized Laguerre polynomials arise in the treatment of the [quantum harmonic oscillator](wikipedia://en/50719).

Applying the addition formula,

$$(-1)^n 2^{2n} n! \, L^{\left(\frac{r}{2}-1\right)}_{n}\Bigl(z_1^2+\cdots+z_r^2\Bigr) =\sum_{m_1+\cdots+m_r=n} \prod_{i=1}^r H_{2m_i}(z_i).$$

## Relation to hypergeometric functions {#Relation_to_hypergeometric_functions}

The Laguerre polynomials may be defined in terms of [hypergeometric functions](wikipedia://en/2055223), specifically the [confluent hypergeometric functions](wikipedia://en/1516095), as

$$L^{(\alpha)}_n(x) = {n+\alpha \choose n} M(-n,\alpha+1,x) =\frac{(\alpha+1)_n} {n!} \,_1F_1(-n,\alpha+1,x)$$

where $(a)_n$ is the [Pochhammer symbol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pochhammer_symbol) (which in this case represents the rising factorial).

::: {#o56 .statement cue=named-heading name="Hardy–Hille formula"}

## Hardy–Hille formula {#Hardy–Hille_formula}

The generalized Laguerre polynomials satisfy the [Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599)–[Hille](wikipedia://en/1643924) formula[^o57][^o58]

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{n!\,\Gamma\left(\alpha + 1\right)}{\Gamma\left(n+\alpha+1\right)}L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)L_n^{(\alpha)}(y)t^n=\frac{1}{(1-t)^{\alpha + 1}}e^{-(x+y)t/(1-t)}\,_0F_1\left(;\alpha + 1;\frac{xyt}{(1-t)^2}\right),$$

where the series on the left converges for $\alpha>-1$ and $|t|<1$. Using the identity

$$\,_0F_1(;\alpha + 1;z)=\,\Gamma(\alpha + 1) z^{-\alpha/2} I_\alpha\left(2\sqrt{z}\right),$$

(see [generalized hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/361184#The_series_0F1)), this can also be written as

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{n!}{\Gamma(1+\alpha+n)}L_n^{(\alpha)}(x)L_n^{(\alpha)}(y) t^n = \frac{1}{(xyt)^{\alpha/2}(1-t)}e^{-(x+y)t/(1-t)} I_\alpha \left(\frac{2\sqrt{xyt}}{1-t}\right).$$

where $I_\alpha$ denotes the modified Bessel function of the first kind, defined as

$$I_\alpha(z) = \sum_{k=0}^\infty \frac{1}{k!\, \Gamma(k+\alpha+1)} \left(\frac{z}{2}\right)^{2k+\alpha}$$

This formula is a generalization of the [Mehler kernel](wikipedia://en/31393808) for [Hermite polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_polynomial), which can be recovered from it by setting the Hermite polynomials as a special case of the associated Laguerre polynomials.

Substitute $t \mapsto -t/y$ and take the $y \to \infty$ limit, we obtain [^o59]

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty \frac{t^n}{\Gamma(n+1+\alpha)} L_n^{(\alpha)}(x) = \frac{e^t}{(-xt)^{\alpha/2}}I_{\alpha}(2\sqrt{-xt}).$$

The formula is named after [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) and [Einar Hille](wikipedia://en/1643924).[^o60][^o61]

:::

## Physics convention {#Physics_convention}

The generalized Laguerre polynomials are used to describe the quantum wavefunction for [hydrogen atom](wikipedia://en/14225) orbitals.[^o63][^o64][^o65] The convention used throughout this article expresses the generalized Laguerre polynomials as [^o66]

$$L_n^{(\alpha)}(x) = \frac{\Gamma(\alpha + n + 1)}{\Gamma(\alpha + 1) n!} \,_1F_1(-n; \alpha + 1; x),$$

where $\,_1F_1(a;b;x)$ is the [confluent hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/1516095). In the physics literature,[^o65] the generalized Laguerre polynomials are instead defined as

$$\bar{L}_n^{(\alpha)}(x) = \frac{\left[\Gamma(\alpha + n + 1)\right]^2}{\Gamma(\alpha + 1)n!} \,_1F_1(-n; \alpha + 1; x).$$

The physics version is related to the standard version by

$$\bar{L}_n^{(\alpha)}(x) = (n+\alpha)! L_n^{(\alpha)}(x).$$

There is yet another, albeit less frequently used, convention in the physics literature [^o67][^o68][^o69]

$$\tilde{L}_n^{(\alpha)}(x) = (-1)^{\alpha}\bar{L}_{n-\alpha}^{(\alpha)}.$$

## Umbral calculus convention {#Umbral_calculus_convention}

Generalized Laguerre polynomials are linked to [umbral calculus](wikipedia://en/214124) by being [Sheffer sequences](wikipedia://en/218596) for $D/(D-I)$ when multiplied by $n!$. In umbral calculus convention,[^o71] the default Laguerre polynomials are defined to be

$$\mathcal L_n(x) = n!L_n^{(-1)}(x) = \sum_{k=0}^n L(n,k) (-x)^k$$

where $L(n,k) = \binom{n-1}{k-1} \frac{n!}{k!}$ are the signless [Lah numbers](wikipedia://en/85752). $(\mathcal L_n(x))_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ is a sequence of polynomials of [binomial type](wikipedia://en/200077), *i.e.* they satisfy

$$\mathcal L_n(x+y) = \sum_{k=0}^n \binom{n}{k} \mathcal L_k(x) \mathcal L_{n-k}(y)$$
