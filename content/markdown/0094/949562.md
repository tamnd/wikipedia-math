# Compound Poisson distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Compound Poisson distribution"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), a **compound Poisson distribution** is the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the sum of a number of [independent identically-distributed random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Independent_identically-distributed_random_variables), where the number of terms to be added is itself a [Poisson-distributed](wikipedia://en/23009144) variable. The result can be either a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_distribution) or a [discrete distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_distribution).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose that

$$N\sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda),$$

i.e., *N* is a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) whose distribution is a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) with [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) λ, and that

$$X_1, X_2, X_3, \dots$$

are identically distributed random variables that are mutually independent and also independent of *N*. Then the probability distribution of the sum of $N$ i.i.d. random variables

$$Y = \sum_{n=1}^N X_n$$

is a compound Poisson distribution.

In the case *N* = 0, then this is a sum of 0 terms, so the value of *Y* is 0. Hence the conditional distribution of *Y* given that *N* = 0 is a [degenerate distribution](wikipedia://en/101843).

The compound Poisson distribution is obtained by marginalising the joint distribution of (*Y*,*N*) over *N*, and this joint distribution can be obtained by combining the conditional distribution *Y* | *N* with the marginal distribution of *N*.

:::

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) and the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) of the compound distribution can be derived in a simple way from [law of total expectation](wikipedia://en/312397) and the [law of total variance](wikipedia://en/312408). Thus

$$\operatorname{E}(Y)= \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{E}(Y \mid N)\right]= \operatorname{E}\left[N \operatorname{E}(X)\right]= \operatorname{E}(N) \operatorname{E}(X) ,$$

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{Var}(Y) & = \operatorname{E}\left[\operatorname{Var}(Y\mid N)\right] + \operatorname{Var}\left[\operatorname{E}(Y \mid N)\right] =\operatorname{E} \left[N\operatorname{Var}(X)\right] + \operatorname{Var}\left[N\operatorname{E}(X)\right] , \\[6pt] & = \operatorname{E}(N)\operatorname{Var}(X) + \left(\operatorname{E}(X) \right)^2 \operatorname{Var}(N). \end{aligned}$$

Then, since E(*N*) = Var(*N*) if *N* is Poisson-distributed, these formulae can be reduced to

$$\operatorname{E}(Y)= \operatorname{E}(N)\operatorname{E}(X) = \lambda\operatorname{E}(X) ,$$

$$\operatorname{Var}(Y) = \operatorname{E}(N)(\operatorname{Var}(X) + (\operatorname{E}(X))^2)= \operatorname{E}(N){\operatorname{E}(X^2)} = \lambda{\operatorname{E}(X^2)}.$$

The probability distribution of *Y* can be determined in terms of [characteristic functions](wikipedia://en/3229401):

$$\varphi_Y(t) = \operatorname{E}(e^{itY})= \operatorname{E} \left( \left(\operatorname{E} (e^{itX}\mid N) \right)^N \right)= \operatorname{E} \left((\varphi_X(t))^N\right), \,$$

and hence, using the [probability-generating function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probability-generating_function) of the Poisson distribution, we have

$$\varphi_Y(t) = \textrm{e}^{\lambda(\varphi_X(t) - 1)}.\,$$

An alternative approach is via [cumulant generating functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cumulant_generating_function):

$$K_Y(t)=\ln \operatorname{E}[e^{tY}]=\ln \operatorname E[\operatorname E[e^{tY}\mid N]]=\ln \operatorname E[e^{NK_X(t)}]=K_N(K_X(t)) . \,$$

Via the [law of total cumulance](wikipedia://en/1611766) it can be shown that, if the mean of the Poisson distribution *λ* = 1, the [cumulants](wikipedia://en/359684) of *Y* are the same as the [moments](wikipedia://en/368684) of *X*<sub>1</sub>.

Every [infinitely divisible](wikipedia://en/13588803) probability distribution is a limit of compound Poisson distributions.[^o6] And compound Poisson distributions is infinitely divisible by the definition.

:::

## Discrete compound Poisson distribution {#Discrete_compound_Poisson_distribution}

When $X_1, X_2, X_3, \dots$ are positive integer-valued i.i.d random variables with $P(X_1 = k) = \alpha_k,\ (k =1,2, \ldots )$, then this compound Poisson distribution is named **discrete compound Poisson distribution**[^o8][^o9][^o10] (or stuttering-Poisson distribution[^o11]) . We say that the discrete random variable $Y$ satisfying [probability generating function](wikipedia://en/236841) characterization

$$P_Y(z) = \sum\limits_{i = 0}^\infty P(Y = i)z^i = \exp\left(\sum\limits_{k = 1}^\infty \alpha_k \lambda (z^k - 1)\right), \quad (|z| \le 1)$$

has a discrete compound Poisson(DCP) distribution with parameters $(\alpha_1 \lambda,\alpha_2 \lambda, \ldots ) \in \mathbb{R}^\infty$ (where $\sum_{i = 1}^\infty \alpha_i = 1$, with $\alpha_i \ge 0,\lambda > 0$), which is denoted by

$$X \sim {\text{DCP}}(\lambda {\alpha _1},\lambda {\alpha _2}, \ldots )$$

Moreover, if $X \sim {\operatorname{DCP}}(\lambda {\alpha _1}, \ldots ,\lambda {\alpha _r})$, we say $X$ has a discrete compound Poisson distribution of order $r$ . When $r = 1,2$, DCP becomes [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) and [Hermite distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermite_distribution), respectively. When $r = 3,4$, DCP becomes triple stuttering-Poisson distribution and quadruple stuttering-Poisson distribution, respectively.[^o12] Other special cases include: shift [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922), [negative binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/45177), [Geometric Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/43163472), [Neyman type A distribution](wikipedia://en/75712890), Luria–Delbrück distribution in [Luria–Delbrück experiment](wikipedia://en/1647936). For more special case of DCP, see the reviews paper[^o13] and references therein.

Feller's characterization of the compound Poisson distribution states that a non-negative integer valued r.v. $X$ is [infinitely divisible](wikipedia://en/13588803) if and only if its distribution is a discrete compound Poisson distribution.[^o14] The [negative binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/45177) is discrete [infinitely divisible](wikipedia://en/13588803), i.e., if *X* has a negative binomial distribution, then for any positive integer *n*, there exist discrete i.i.d. random variables *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> whose sum has the same distribution that *X* has. The shift [geometric distribution](wikipedia://en/45922) is discrete compound Poisson distribution since it is a trivial case of [negative binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/45177).

This distribution can model batch arrivals (such as in a [bulk queue](wikipedia://en/36484704)[^o11][^o15]). The discrete compound Poisson distribution is also widely used in [actuarial science](wikipedia://en/189373) for modelling the distribution of the total claim amount.[^o9]

When some $\alpha_k$ are negative, it is the discrete pseudo compound Poisson distribution.[^o9] We define that any discrete random variable $Y$ satisfying [probability generating function](wikipedia://en/236841) characterization

$$G_Y(z) = \sum\limits_{i = 0}^\infty P(Y = i)z^i = \exp\left(\sum\limits_{k = 1}^\infty \alpha_k \lambda (z^k - 1)\right), \quad (|z| \le 1)$$

has a discrete pseudo compound Poisson distribution with parameters $(\lambda_1 ,\lambda_2, \ldots )=:(\alpha_1 \lambda,\alpha_2 \lambda, \ldots ) \in \mathbb{R}^\infty$ where $\sum_{i = 1}^\infty {\alpha_i} = 1$ and $\sum_{i = 1}^\infty {\left| {{\alpha _i}} \right|} < \infty$, with ${\alpha_i} \in \mathbb{R},\lambda > 0$.

## Compound Poisson Gamma distribution {#Compound_Poisson_Gamma_distribution}

If *X* has a [gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079), of which the [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906) is a special case, then the conditional distribution of *Y* | *N* is again a gamma distribution. The marginal distribution of *Y* is a [Tweedie distribution](wikipedia://en/15204099) with variance power 1 \< *p* \< 2 (proof via comparison of [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/3229401)).[^o17] To be more explicit, if

$$N \sim\operatorname{Poisson}(\lambda) ,$$

and

$$X_i \sim \operatorname{\Gamma}(\alpha, \beta)$$

i.i.d., then the distribution of

$$Y = \sum_{i=1}^N X_i$$

is a reproductive [exponential dispersion model](wikipedia://en/15670753) $ED(\mu, \sigma^2)$ with

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{E}[Y] & = \lambda \frac{\alpha}{\beta} =: \mu , \\[4pt] \operatorname{Var}[Y]& = \lambda \frac{\alpha(1+\alpha)}{\beta^2}=: \sigma^2 \mu^p . \end{aligned}$$

The mapping of parameters Tweedie parameter $\mu, \sigma^2, p$ to the Poisson and Gamma parameters $\lambda, \alpha, \beta$ is the following:

$$\begin{aligned} \lambda &= \frac{\mu^{2-p}}{(2-p)\sigma^2} , \\[4pt] \alpha &= \frac{2-p}{p-1} , \\[4pt] \beta &= \frac{\mu^{1-p}}{(p-1)\sigma^2} . \end{aligned}$$

## Compound Poisson processes {#Compound_Poisson_processes}

A [compound Poisson process](wikipedia://en/1082294) with rate $\lambda>0$ and jump size distribution *G* is a continuous-time [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895) $\{\,Y(t) : t \geq 0 \,\}$ given by

$$Y(t) = \sum_{i=1}^{N(t)} D_i,$$

where the sum is by convention equal to zero as long as *N*(*t*) = 0. Here, $\{\,N(t) : t \geq 0\,\}$ is a [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process) with rate $\lambda$, and $\{\,D_i : i \geq 1\,\}$ are independent and identically distributed random variables, with distribution function *G*, which are also independent of $\{\,N(t) : t \geq 0\,\}.\,$[^o19]

For the discrete version of compound Poisson process, it can be used in [survival analysis](wikipedia://en/419259) for the frailty models.[^o20]

::: {#o22 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

A compound Poisson distribution, in which the summands have an [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906), was used by Revfeim to model the distribution of the total rainfall in a day, where each day contains a Poisson-distributed number of events each of which provides an amount of rainfall which has an exponential distribution.[^o23] Thompson applied the same model to monthly total rainfalls.[^o24]

There have been applications to [insurance claims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insurance)[^o25][^o26] and [x-ray computed tomography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CT_scan).[^o27][^o28][^o29]
