# Liouville's formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Liouville's formula"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Liouville's formula**, also known as the **Abel–Jacobi–Liouville identity**, is an equation that expresses the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of a [square-matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859#Square_matrices) solution of a first-order system of homogeneous [linear differential equations](wikipedia://en/379868) in terms of the sum of the diagonal coefficients of the system. The formula is named after the [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Joseph Liouville](wikipedia://en/341810). [Jacobi's formula](wikipedia://en/2350347) provides another representation of the same mathematical relationship.

:::

Liouville's formula is a generalization of [Abel's identity](wikipedia://en/3261224) and can be used to prove it. Since Liouville's formula relates the different [linearly independent](wikipedia://en/101863) solutions of the [system of differential equations](wikipedia://en/63871781), it can help to find one solution from the other(s), see the example application below.

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement of Liouville's formula"}

## Statement of Liouville's formula {#Statement_of_Liouville's_formula}

Consider the $n$-dimensional first-order homogeneous linear differential equation

$$y'=A(t)y$$

on an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $I$ of the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), where $A(t)$ for $t \in I$ denotes a square matrix of dimension $n$ with [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) entries. Let $\Phi$ denote a matrix-valued solution on $I$, meaning that $\Phi(t)$ is the so-called [fundamental matrix](wikipedia://en/10125619), a square matrix of dimension $n$ with real or complex entries and the [derivative](wikipedia://en/1765852) satisfies

$$\Phi'(t)=A(t)\Phi(t),\qquad t\in I.$$

Let

$$\operatorname{tr} A(s)=\sum_{i=1}^n a_{i,i}(s),\qquad s\in I,$$

denote the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of $A(s) = (a_{i,\,j\,}(s))_{i,\,j\,\in\,\{1,...,n\}}$, the sum of its diagonal entries. If the trace of $A$ is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122), then the determinant of $\Phi$ satisfies

$$\det\Phi(t)=\det\Phi(t_0)\,\exp\left(\int_{t_0}^t \operatorname{tr} A(s) \,\textrm{d}s\right)$$

for all $t$ and $t_{0}$ in $I$.

:::

## Example application {#Example_application}

This example illustrates how Liouville's formula can help to find the general solution of a first-order system of homogeneous linear differential equations. Consider

$$y'=\underbrace{\begin{pmatrix}1&-1/x\\1+x&-1\end{pmatrix}}_{=\,A(x)}y$$

on the open interval $I = (0,\,\infty)$. Assume that the easy solution

$$y(x)=\begin{pmatrix}1\\x\end{pmatrix},\qquad x\in I,$$

is already found. Let

$$y(x)=\begin{pmatrix}y_1(x)\\y_2(x)\end{pmatrix}$$

denote another solution, then

$$\Phi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}y_1(x)&1\\y_2(x)&x\end{pmatrix},\qquad x\in I,$$

is a square-matrix-valued solution of the above differential equation. Since the trace of $A(x)$ is zero for all $x \in I$, Liouville's formula implies that the determinant

::: {#o5 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$c_1:=\det\Phi(x)=x\,y_1(x)-y_2(x),\qquad x\in I, \tag{1}$$

:::

is actually a constant independent of $x$. Writing down the first component of the differential equation for $y$, we obtain using ((1)) that

$$y'_1(x)=y_1(x)-\frac{y_2(x)}x=\frac{x\,y_1(x)-y_2(x)}x=\frac{c_1}x,\qquad x\in I.$$

Therefore, by integration, we see that

$$y_1(x)=c_1\ln x+c_2,\qquad x\in I,$$

involving the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476) and the [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939) $c_{2}$. Solving equation ((1)) for $y_{2}(x)$ and substituting for $y_{1}(x)$ gives

$$y_2(x)=x\,y_1(x)-c_1=\,c_1x\ln x+c_2x-c_1,\qquad x\in I,$$

which is the general solution for $y$. With the special choice $c_{1} = 0$ and $c_{2} = 1$ we recover the easy solution we started with, the choice $c_{1} = 1$ and $c_{2} = 0$ yields a linearly independent solution. Therefore,

$$\Phi(x)=\begin{pmatrix}\ln x&1\\x\ln x-1&x\end{pmatrix},\qquad x\in I,$$

is a so-called [fundamental solution](wikipedia://en/1820858) of the system.

::: {#o7 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of Liouville's formula"}

## Proof of Liouville's formula {#Proof_of_Liouville's_formula}

We omit the argument $x$ for brevity. By the [Leibniz formula for determinants](wikipedia://en/5361510), the derivative of the determinant of $\Phi = (\Phi_{i,\,j\,})_{i,\,j\,\in\,\{1,...,n\}}$ can be calculated by differentiating one row at a time and taking the sum, i.e.

::: {#o8 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$(\det\Phi)'=\sum_{i=1}^n\det\begin{pmatrix} \Phi_{1,1}&\Phi_{1,2}&\cdots&\Phi_{1,n}\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ \Phi'_{i,1}&\Phi'_{i,2}&\cdots&\Phi'_{i,n}\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ \Phi_{n,1}&\Phi_{n,2}&\cdots&\Phi_{n,n} \end{pmatrix}. \tag{2}$$

:::

Since the matrix-valued solution $\Phi$ satisfies the equation $\Phi' = A\Phi$, we have for every entry of the matrix $\Phi'$

$$\Phi'_{i,k}=\sum_{j=1}^n a_{i,j}\Phi_{j,k}\,,\qquad i,k\in\{1,\ldots,n\},$$

or for the entire row

$$(\Phi'_{i,1},\dots,\Phi'_{i,n}) =\sum_{j=1}^n a_{i,j}(\Phi_{j,1},\ldots,\Phi_{j,n}), \qquad i\in\{1,\ldots,n\}.$$

When we subtract from the $i$-th row the linear combination

$$\sum_{\scriptstyle j=1\atop\scriptstyle j \neq i}^n a_{i,j}(\Phi_{j,1},\ldots,\Phi_{j,n}),$$

of all the other rows, then the value of the determinant remains unchanged, hence

$$\det\begin{pmatrix} \Phi_{1,1}&\Phi_{1,2}&\cdots&\Phi_{1,n}\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ \Phi'_{i,1}&\Phi'_{i,2}&\cdots&\Phi'_{i,n}\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ \Phi_{n,1}&\Phi_{n,2}&\cdots&\Phi_{n,n} \end{pmatrix} =\det\begin{pmatrix} \Phi_{1,1}&\Phi_{1,2}&\cdots&\Phi_{1,n}\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ a_{i,i}\Phi_{i,1}&a_{i,i}\Phi_{i,2}&\cdots&a_{i,i}\Phi_{i,n}\\ \vdots&\vdots&&\vdots\\ \Phi_{n,1}&\Phi_{n,2}&\cdots&\Phi_{n,n} \end{pmatrix} =a_{i,i}\det\Phi$$

for every $i \in \{1, . . . , n$} by the linearity of the determinant with respect to every row. Hence

::: {#o9 .equation cue=numblk name="3"}

$$(\det\Phi)'=\sum_{i=1}^n a_{i,i}\det\Phi=\mathrm{tr}\,A\,\det\Phi \tag{3}$$

:::

by ((2)) and the definition of the trace. It remains to show that this representation of the derivative implies Liouville's formula.

Fix $x_{0} \in I$. Since the trace of $A$ is assumed to be continuous function on $I$, it is bounded on every closed and bounded subinterval of $I$ and therefore integrable, hence

$$g(x):=\det\Phi(x) \exp\left(-\int_{x_0}^x \mathrm{tr}\,A(\xi) \,\textrm{d}\xi\right), \qquad x\in I,$$

is a well defined function. Differentiating both sides, using the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988), the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113), the derivative of the [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) and the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696), we obtain

$$g'(x)=\bigl((\det\Phi(x))'-\det\Phi(x)\,\mathrm{tr}\,A(x)\bigr)\exp\left(-\int_{x_0}^x \operatorname{tr}\,A(\xi) \,\textrm{d}\xi\right)=0,\qquad x\in I,$$

due to the derivative in ((3)). Therefore, $g$ has to be constant on $I$, because otherwise we would obtain a contradiction to the [mean value theorem](wikipedia://en/19662) (applied separately to the real and imaginary part in the complex-valued case). Since $g(x_{0}) = \det \Phi(x_{0})$, Liouville's formula follows by solving the definition of $g$ for $\det \Phi(x)$.

:::
