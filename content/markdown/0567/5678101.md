# Local time (mathematics)

*Image: Local times surface.png.* A sample path of an Itō process together with its surface of local times.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Local time"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) theory of [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895), **local time** is a stochastic process associated with [semimartingale](wikipedia://en/9653775) processes such as [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436), that characterizes the amount of time a particle has spent at a given level. Local time appears in various [stochastic integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_integration) formulas, such as [Tanaka's formula](wikipedia://en/9666599), if the integrand is not sufficiently smooth. It is also studied in statistical mechanics in the context of [random fields](wikipedia://en/540838).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

For a continuous real-valued semimartingale $(B_s)_{s\ge 0}$, the local time of $B$ at the point $x$ is the stochastic process which is informally defined by

$$L^x(t) =\int_0^t \delta(x-B_s)\,d[B]_s,$$

where $\delta$ is the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021) and $[B]$ is the [quadratic variation](wikipedia://en/2143889). It was introduced by [Paul Lévy](wikipedia://en/556462). The basic idea is that $L^x(t)$ is an (appropriately rescaled and time-parametrized) measure of how much time $B_s$ has spent at $x$ up to time $t$. More rigorously, it may be written as the almost sure limit

$$L^x(t) =\lim_{\varepsilon\downarrow 0} \frac{1}{2\varepsilon} \int_0^t 1_{\{ x- \varepsilon < B_s < x+\varepsilon \}} \, d[B]_s,$$

which may be shown to always exist. Note that in the special case of Brownian motion (or more generally a real-valued [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ito_diffusion) of the form $dB = b(t,B)\,dt+ dW$ where $W$ is a Brownian motion), the term $d[B]_s$ simply reduces to $ds$, which explains why it is called the local time of $B$ at $x$. For a discrete state-space process $(X_s)_{s\ge 0}$, the local time can be expressed more simply as[^o4]

$$L^x(t) =\int_0^t 1_{\{x\}}(X_s) \, ds.$$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement cue=named-heading name="Tanaka's formula"}

## Tanaka's formula {#Tanaka's_formula}

Tanaka's formula also provides a definition of local time for an arbitrary continuous semimartingale $(X_s)_{s\ge 0}$ on $\mathbb R:$[^o7]

$$L^x(t) = |X_t - x| - |X_0 - x| - \int_0^t \left( 1_{(0,\infty)}(X_s - x) - 1_{(-\infty, 0]}(X_s-x) \right) \, dX_s, \qquad t \geq 0.$$

A more general form was proven independently by Meyer[^o8] and Wang;[^o9] the formula extends Itô's lemma for twice differentiable functions to a more general class of functions. If $F:\mathbb R \rightarrow \mathbb R$ is absolutely continuous with derivative $F',$ which is of bounded variation, then

$$F(X_t) = F(X_0) + \int_0^t F'_{-}(X_s) \, dX_s + \frac12 \int_{-\infty}^\infty L^x(t) \, dF'_{-}(x),$$

where $F'_{-}$ is the left derivative.

If $X$ is a Brownian motion, then for any $\alpha\in(0,1/2)$ the field of local times $L = (L^x(t))_{x \in \mathbb R, t \geq 0}$ has a modification which is a.s. Hölder continuous in $x$ with exponent $\alpha$, uniformly for bounded $x$ and $t$.[^o10] In general, $L$ has a modification that is a.s. continuous in $t$ and [càdlàg](wikipedia://en/30862839) in $x$.

Tanaka's formula provides the explicit [Doob–Meyer decomposition](wikipedia://en/3651135) for the one-dimensional [reflecting Brownian motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reflecting_Brownian_motion), $(|B_s|)_{s \geq 0}$.

:::

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Ray–Knight theorems"}

## Ray–Knight theorems {#Ray–Knight_theorems}

The field of local times $L_t = (L^x_t)_{x \in E}$ associated to a stochastic process on a space $E$ is a well studied topic in the area of random fields. Ray–Knight type theorems relate the field *L*<sub>*t*</sub> to an associated [Gaussian process](wikipedia://en/302944).

In general Ray–Knight type theorems of the first kind consider the field *L*<sub>*t*</sub> at a hitting time of the underlying process, whilst theorems of the second kind are in terms of a stopping time at which the field of local times first exceeds a given value.

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="First Ray–Knight theorem"}

### First Ray–Knight theorem {#First_Ray–Knight_theorem}

Let (*B*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>*t* ≥ 0</sub> be a one-dimensional Brownian motion started from *B*<sub>0</sub> = *a* > 0, and (*W*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>*t*≥0</sub> be a standard two-dimensional Brownian motion started from *W*<sub>0</sub> = 0 ∈ **R**<sup>2</sup>. Define the stopping time at which *B* first hits the origin, $T = \inf\{t \geq 0 \colon B_t = 0\}$. Ray[^o15] and Knight[^o16] (independently) showed that

::: {#o17 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\left\{ L^x(T) \colon x \in [0,a] \right\} \stackrel{\mathcal{D}}{=} \left\{ |W_x|^2 \colon x \in [0,a] \right\} \, \tag{1}$$

:::

where (*L*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>*t* ≥ 0</sub> is the field of local times of (*B*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>*t* ≥ 0</sub>, and equality is in distribution on *C*\[0, *a*\]. The process |*W*<sub>*x*</sub>|<sup>2</sup> is known as the squared [Bessel process](wikipedia://en/7646799).

:::

::: {#o19 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Second Ray–Knight theorem"}

### Second Ray–Knight theorem {#Second_Ray–Knight_theorem}

Let (*B*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>t ≥ 0</sub> be a standard one-dimensional Brownian motion *B*<sub>0</sub> = 0 ∈ **R**, and let (*L*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>*t* ≥ 0</sub> be the associated field of local times. Let *T*<sub>*a*</sub> be the first time at which the local time at zero exceeds *a* > 0

$$T_a = \inf \{ t \geq 0 \colon L^0_t > a \}.$$

Let (*W*<sub>*t*</sub>)<sub>*t* ≥ 0</sub> be an independent one-dimensional Brownian motion started from *W*<sub>0</sub> = 0, then[^o20]

::: {#o21 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$\left \{ L^x_{T_a} + W_x^2 \colon x \geq 0 \right \} \stackrel{\mathcal{D}}{=} \left\{ (W_x + \sqrt a )^2 \colon x \geq 0 \right \}. \, \tag{2}$$

:::

Equivalently, the process $(L^x_{T_a})_{x \geq 0}$ (which is a process in the spatial variable $x$) is equal in distribution to the square of a 0-dimensional [Bessel process](wikipedia://en/7646799) started at $a$, and as such is Markovian.

:::

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Generalized Ray–Knight theorems"}

### Generalized Ray–Knight theorems {#Generalized_Ray–Knight_theorems}

Results of Ray–Knight type for more general stochastic processes have been intensively studied, and analogue statements of both ((1)) and ((2)) are known for strongly symmetric Markov processes.

:::
