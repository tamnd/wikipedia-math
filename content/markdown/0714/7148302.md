# Ptolemy's inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ptolemy's inequality"}

*Image: Ptolemy Inequality.svg.* Four points and their six distances. The points are not co-circular, so Ptolemy's inequality is strict for these points.

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), **Ptolemy's inequality** relates the six [distances](wikipedia://en/53932) determined by four points in the [plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) or in a higher-dimensional space. It states that, for any four points $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$, the following [inequality](wikipedia://en/89489) holds:

:::

$$\overline{AB}\cdot \overline{CD}+\overline{BC}\cdot \overline{DA} \ge \overline{AC}\cdot \overline{BD}.$$

It is named after the [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Greece) [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979).

The four points can be ordered in any of three distinct ways (counting reversals as not distinct) to form three different [quadrilaterals](wikipedia://en/25278), for each of which the sum of the products of opposite sides is at least as large as the product of the diagonals. Thus, the three product terms in the inequality can be additively permuted to put any one of them on the right side of the inequality, so the three products of opposite sides or of diagonals of any one of the quadrilaterals must obey the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941).[^o2]

As a special case, [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971) states that the inequality becomes an equality when the four points lie in cyclic order on a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220). The other case of equality occurs when the four points are [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581) in order. The inequality does not generalize from [Euclidean spaces](wikipedia://en/9697) to arbitrary [metric spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_spaces). The spaces where it remains valid are called the *Ptolemaic spaces*; they include the [inner product spaces](wikipedia://en/14856), [Hadamard spaces](wikipedia://en/2850665), and [shortest path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_path) distances on [Ptolemaic graphs](wikipedia://en/50730814).

## Assumptions and derivation {#Assumptions_and_derivation}

Ptolemy's inequality is often stated for a special case, in which the four points are the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of a [convex](wikipedia://en/660019) [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278), given in cyclic order.[^o4][^o5] However, the theorem applies more generally to any four points; it is not required that the quadrilateral they form be convex, simple, or even planar.

For points in the plane, Ptolemy's inequality can be derived from the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) by an [inversion](wikipedia://en/295844) centered at one of the four points.[^o6][^o7] Alternatively, it can be derived by [interpreting the four points as complex numbers](wikipedia://en/1521971#Proof_using_complex_numbers), using the complex number identity:

$$(A-B)(C-D)+(A-D)(B-C)=(A-C)(B-D)$$

to construct a triangle whose side lengths are the products of sides of the given quadrilateral, and applying the triangle inequality to this triangle.[^n1] One can also view the points as belonging to the complex [projective line](wikipedia://en/398578), express the inequality in the form that the [absolute values](wikipedia://en/991) of two [cross-ratios](wikipedia://en/401767) of the points sum to at least one, and deduce this from the fact that the cross-ratios themselves add to exactly one.[^o9]

A proof of the inequality for points in three-dimensional space can be reduced to the planar case, by observing that for any non-planar quadrilateral, it is possible to rotate one of the points around the diagonal until the quadrilateral becomes planar, increasing the other diagonal's length and keeping the other five distances constant.[^n1] In spaces of higher dimension than three, any four points lie in a three-dimensional subspace, and the same three-dimensional proof can be used.

## Four concyclic points {#Four_concyclic_points}

For four [points in order around a circle](wikipedia://en/504404), Ptolemy's inequality becomes an equality, known as [Ptolemy's theorem](wikipedia://en/1521971):

$$\overline{AB}\cdot \overline{CD}+\overline{AD}\cdot\overline{BC} = \overline{AC}\cdot \overline{BD}.$$

In the inversion-based proof of Ptolemy's inequality, transforming four co-circular points by an inversion centered at one of them causes the other three to become collinear, so the triangle equality for these three points (from which Ptolemy's inequality may be derived) also becomes an equality.[^o7] For any other four points, Ptolemy's inequality is strict.

## In three dimensions {#In_three_dimensions}

Four non-coplanar points $A$, $B$, $C$, and $D$ in 3D form a tetrahedron. In this case, the strict inequality holds: $\overline{AB}\cdot \overline{CD}+\overline{BC}\cdot \overline{DA} > \overline{AC}\cdot \overline{BD}$.[^o13]

## In general metric spaces {#In_general_metric_spaces}

*Image: Circle graph C4.svg.* A [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962) in which the distances disobey Ptolemy's inequality

Ptolemy's inequality holds more generally in any [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856),[^o2][^o15] and whenever it is true for a real [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538), that space must be an inner product space.[^o15][^o16]

For other types of [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018), the inequality may or may not be valid. A space in which it holds is called *Ptolemaic*. For instance, consider the four-vertex [cycle graph](wikipedia://en/878962), shown in the figure, with all edge lengths equal to 1. The sum of the products of opposite sides is 2. However, diagonally opposite [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) are at distance 2 from each other, so the product of the diagonals is 4, bigger than the sum of products of sides. Therefore, the [shortest path](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shortest_path) distances in this graph are not Ptolemaic. The graphs in which the distances obey Ptolemy's inequality are called the [Ptolemaic graphs](wikipedia://en/50730814) and have a restricted structure compared to arbitrary graphs; in particular, they disallow [induced cycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Induced_cycle) of length greater than three, such as the one shown.[^o17]

The Ptolemaic spaces include all [CAT(0) spaces](wikipedia://en/12941912) and in particular all [Hadamard spaces](wikipedia://en/2850665). If a complete [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) is Ptolemaic, it is necessarily a Hadamard space.[^o18]

## Inner product spaces {#Inner_product_spaces}

Suppose that $\|\cdot\|$ is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) on a vector space $X.$ Then this norm satisfies Ptolemy's inequality:

$$\|x - y\| \, \|z\| ~+~ \|y - z\| \, \|x\| ~\geq~ \|x - z\| \, \|y\| \qquad \text{ for all vectors } x, y, z.$$

if and only if there exists an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) $\langle \cdot, \cdot \rangle$ on $X$ such that $\|x\|^2 = \langle x,\ x\rangle$ for all vectors $x \in X.$[^o20] Another necessary and sufficient condition for there to exist such an inner product is for the norm to satisfy the [parallelogram law](wikipedia://en/362953):

$$\|x+y\|^2 ~+~ \|x-y\|^2 ~=~ 2\|x\|^2 + 2\|y\|^2 \qquad \text{ for all vectors } x, y.$$

If this is the case then this inner product will be unique and it can be defined in terms of the norm by using the [polarization identity](wikipedia://en/2047965).
