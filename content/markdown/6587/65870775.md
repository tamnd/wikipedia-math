# Fátima de Madrid

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fátima de Madrid"}

**Fátima de Madrid** is the name given to an [Arab](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab) [Muslim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muslim) [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) who supposedly lived during the late 10th and early 11th centuries in [Islamic Spain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus). She was purportedly the daughter of the astronomer [Maslama al-Majriti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maslama_al-Majriti), with whom she is said to have worked on several astronomical and mathematical treatises, including the astronomical tables of [Muhammad ibn Musa al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Musa_al-Khwarizmi). The earliest known account of her is the 1924 edition of the [Enciclopedia Espasa-Calpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enciclopedia_universal_ilustrada_europeo-americana), and historians have cast doubt on whether she actually existed.[^o3]

:::

## Purported career {#Purported_career}

Fátima de Madrid is said to have lived in [Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Córdoba,_Spain), under the [Caliphate of Córdoba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliphate_of_Córdoba), during the late 10th and early 11th centuries. She is said to have been the daughter of the famous Islamic astronomer and scientist [Maslama al-Majriti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maslama_al-Majriti).[^o3]

Her most famous supposed work, known as the "Corrections from Fátima," are a series of astronomical and mathematical treatises, though no copy of it has ever been found. She also supposedly co-authored "A Treatise on the Astrolabe" with her father, which contains information about how to use [astrolabes](wikipedia://en/73664). Today, the manuscript is still allegedly preserved in the library of the monastery of [El Escorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Escorial).[^o3]

Fátima supposedly helped her father edit and adapt the astronomical tables of [al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Musa_al-Khwarizmi), replacing the [Persian solar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_Hijri_calendar) used in his models with the [Islamic lunar calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_calendar). They supposedly corrected the tables also to account for the geographical location of Córdoba, as well as for the [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29) passing through it. With her father, she also supposedly translated the numeration of Persian to Arab years and determined the positions of the planets on the day of the [Hijra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hegira). Fátima also supposedly helped her father correct [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979)'s *[Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060)*, which contained mistakes in the calculations of eclipses.[^n1]

In addition, Fátima supposedly wrote several [zījes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zij), a type of Islamic astronomical treatise. These covered topics including calendars, ephemerides of the planets, the Sun, and the Moon, and solar and lunar eclipses.[^n1]

Apart from her work on astronomy, Fátima was said to be able to speak, read, or write in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic), [Spanish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_language), [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_language), [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek), and [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin).[^o3]

## Historicity {#Historicity}

Whether Fátima de Madrid actually existed is contested. The earliest known reference to her existence is the 1924 edition of the [Enciclopedia Espasa-Calpe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enciclopedia_universal_ilustrada_europeo-americana). As Ángel Requena Fraile, a [historian of mathematics](wikipedia://en/14220), explains: Arabist, historian, and biographer of [Al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) Manuela Marín similarly holds that Fátima is a historical invention from the Espasa-Calpe. Marín attributes continued discussion about her, such as on the internet and with her inclusion in a 2009 calendar titled "Astronomers who made history", to uncritical repetition of the Espasa-Calpe's content.[^o9]
