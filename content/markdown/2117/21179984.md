# Lumpability

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lumpability"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), **lumpability** is a method for reducing the size of the state space of some [continuous-time Markov chains](wikipedia://en/1125342), first published by [Kemeny](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_George_Kemeny) and [Snell](wikipedia://en/33962324).[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose that the complete state-space of a [Markov chain](wikipedia://en/60876) is divided into disjoint subsets of states, where these subsets are denoted by *t<sub>i</sub>*. This forms a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) $\scriptstyle{T = \{ t_1, t_2, \ldots \}}$ of the states. Both the state-space and the collection of subsets may be either finite or countably infinite. A continuous-time Markov chain $\{ X_i \}$ is **lumpable** with respect to the partition *T* if and only if, for any subsets *t<sub>i</sub>* and *t<sub>j</sub>* in the partition, and for any states *n,n’* in subset *t<sub>i</sub>*,

$$\sum_{m \in t_j} q(n,m) = \sum_{m \in t_j} q(n',m) ,$$

where *q*(*i,j*) is the transition rate from state *i* to state *j*.[^o5]

Similarly, for a [stochastic matrix](wikipedia://en/217313) *P*, *P* is a **lumpable matrix** on a partition *T* if and only if, for any subsets *t<sub>i</sub>* and *t<sub>j</sub>* in the partition, and for any states *n,n’* in subset *t<sub>i</sub>*,

$$\sum_{m \in t_j} p(n,m) = \sum_{m \in t_j} p(n',m) ,$$

where *p*(*i,j*) is the probability of moving from state *i* to state *j*.[^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider the matrix

$$P = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{1}{2} & \frac{3}{8} & \frac{1}{16} & \frac{1}{16} \\ \frac{7}{16} & \frac{7}{16} & 0 & \frac{1}{8} \\ \frac{1}{16} & 0 & \frac{1}{2} & \frac{7}{16} \\ 0 & \frac{1}{16} & \frac{3}{8} & \frac{9}{16} \end{pmatrix}$$

and notice it is lumpable on the partition *t* = {(1,2),(3,4)} so we write

$$P_t = \begin{pmatrix} \frac{7}{8} & \frac{1}{8} \\ \frac{1}{16} & \frac{15}{16} \end{pmatrix}$$

and call *P*<sub>*t*</sub> the lumped matrix of *P* on *t*.

:::

## Successively lumpable processes {#Successively_lumpable_processes}

In 2012, [Katehakis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_N._Katehakis) and Smit discovered the Successively Lumpable processes for which the stationary probabilities can be obtained by successively computing the stationary probabilities of a propitiously constructed sequence of Markov chains. Each of the latter chains has a (typically much) smaller state space and this yields significant computational improvements. These results have many applications reliability and queueing models and problems.[^o10]

::: {#o12 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Quasi-lumpability"}

## Quasi–lumpability {#Quasi–lumpability}

Franceschinis and Muntz introduced quasi-lumpability, a property whereby a small change in the rate matrix makes the chain lumpable.[^o13]

:::
