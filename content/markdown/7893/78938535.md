# Limit group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Limit group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) and [logics](wikipedia://en/19636), **limit groups** are the [finitely generated groups](wikipedia://en/963042) that admit a [presentation](wikipedia://en/99494) which is a limit of [free group](wikipedia://en/59735) presentations in the discrete [Chabauty topology](wikipedia://en/7683674).[^o2] Formerly known as **fully residually free groups**, they arise naturally in the study of equations in free groups and have gained significance through the work of [Sela](wikipedia://en/19309289) on [Tarski's problem](wikipedia://en/59735#Tarski's_problems). They now form a well-studied class of examples in [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) and have led to generalizations such as limit groups over [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/3172817) and certain [relatively hyperbolic groups](wikipedia://en/21993809).[^o3][^o4]

:::

Basic examples include free groups themselves, [hyperbolic orientable surface groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_group), and [free products](wikipedia://en/360036) of [free abelian groups](wikipedia://en/247296). A concrete classification is provided by the hierarchy of constructible limit groups.

## Definitions and characterizations {#Definitions_and_characterizations}

### The space of marked groups and the Chabauty topology {#The_space_of_marked_groups_and_the_Chabauty_topology}

For $n\ge 1$, the *space of marked groups* $\mathcal{G}_n$ is the set of [normal subgroups](wikipedia://en/21918) of the free group $F_n$. Because $F_n$ is a [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536), the *Chabauty topology* is the [topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topology_%28structure%29) on $\mathcal{G}_n$ induced by the [product topology](wikipedia://en/24910), or *Tychonoff topology*, on the [power set](wikipedia://en/23799) $\{0, 1\}^{F_n}$ (where $\{0, 1\}$ is discrete). Thus one can say that two elements $N, N'$ of $\mathcal{G}_n$ are "close" if one has $S\cap N = S\cap N'$ for a "big" finite subset $S\subset F_n$. Since a [group presentation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_presentation) with $n$ generators can be regarded as an [epimorphism](wikipedia://en/59539) from $F_n$, which is the same as a quotient of $F_n$, the set of all group presentations involving a set of $n$ letters is naturally in bijection with $\mathcal{G}_n$ and thus inherits its topology. One may regard elements of $\mathcal{G}_n$ either as subgroups, presentations or epimorphisms.

For $1\le k\le n$, a *limit group* *over* $F_k$ is the quotient of $F_n$ by an element of the [topological closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of the set of normal subgroups $N\triangleleft F_n$ such that $F_n / N$ is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to $F_k$. As the space $\mathcal{G}_n$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738), this is the same as a limit of a sequence of epimorphisms $\phi_i: F_n\longrightarrow F_k$. A *limit group* is a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) for which a presentation arises in this way for some $1\le k\le n$.

### Fully residually free groups {#Fully_residually_free_groups}

A finitely generated group $G$ is said to be *fully residually free* if for all finite subset $B\subset G$, there exists a free group $F$ and a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396) $f: G\longrightarrow F$ whose restriction to $B$ is injective.

One can see that finitely generated fully residually free groups are limit groups, as follows. If $G$ is generated by $n$ elements, then there is an epimorphism $g: F_n\longrightarrow G$. Taking an increasing [countable](wikipedia://en/6026) [exhaustion](wikipedia://en/3521238) of $G$ by finite subsets $B_i$, one has homomorphisms $f_i: G\longrightarrow F_{k_i}$ whose restriction to $B_i$ is injective, and since any $n$-generated subgroup of a free group is a free group of rank at most $n$, one can assume that $f_i$s are epimorphisms and $k_i \leq n$. A subsequence of $f_i\circ g$ tends to $g$ and has constant $k_i=k$, hence $G$ is a limit group over $F_k$.

The converse also holds (but is harder to prove), therefore limit groups are characterized as the finitely generated, fully residually free groups.[^o2]

### Constructibility {#Constructibility}

Any limit group is obtained by iterating constructions called *free extension of centralizer*, then passing to a subgroup.[^o2]

Given a group $G$ and an element $c\in G$ with [centralizer](wikipedia://en/145661) $Z_c \le G$, a free extension of the centralizer $Z_c$ is a [free amalgamated product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_product_with_amalgamated_subgroup) $G \underset{Z_c}{\ast} (Z_c \times \mathbb{Z}^d)$ for some $d \ge 1$. If $G$ is a limit group and $c\ne 1$, one can obtain the free extension of centralizer as a limit of the homomorphisms sending the generators of the $\mathbb{Z}^d$ to powers of $c$ tending to infinity in a way that removes the relations other than commutation with $c$.

## Properties {#Properties}

- Limit groups are [finitely presented](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely-presented_group)
- Any finitely generated subgroup of a limit group is itself a limit group (hence limit groups are coherent)
- Limit groups are [commutative-transitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative-transitive) and satisfy the *CSA property*: for all $g, g', h$, if $hgh^{-1}$ and $g'$ commute, then $g$ and $h$ commute
- Limit groups are [bi-orderable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bi-orderable_group)
- Limit groups are [CAT(0)](wikipedia://en/12942017) with isolated flats[^o11]
- Limit groups act isometrically on [real trees](wikipedia://en/371914) for which [Rips machine](wikipedia://en/31275945) techniques can be used
- Limit groups admit abelian [JSJ decompositions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JSJ_theory_%28group_theory%29)

:::

## Makanin-Razborov diagrams and equations {#Makanin-Razborov_diagrams_and_equations}

Limit groups over a free group of fixed rank form a finite diagram, the *Makanin-Razborov diagram*, that can be used to parametrize the solution set of a system of equations in a free group. In particular, free groups are equationally noetherian, meaning that any system of equations is equivalent to a finite system (this was already known from their linearity).[^o13]

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Most of the theory for limit groups over free groups has been generalized to limit groups over Gromov-hyperbolic groups,[^o16] and much of it still adapts to torsion-free toral relatively hyperbolic groups.[^o17]

:::
