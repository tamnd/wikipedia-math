# AVA Radio Company

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="AVA Radio Company"}

*Image: Cyklometr.jpg.* Reconstruction of the appearance of the Cyclometer on the basis of sketches from the memories of Marian Rejewski

The **AVA Radio Company** ([Polish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_language): *Wytwórnia Radiotechniczna AVA*) was a Polish [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics) firm founded in 1929 in [Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw), Poland. AVA designed and built radio equipment for the [Polish General Staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_General_Staff)'s [Cipher Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biuro_Szyfrów), which was responsible for the radio communications of the General Staff's *Oddział II* (Section II, the General Staff's [intelligence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_intelligence) section).[^n1]

:::

After the Cipher Bureau's [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902)-[cryptologist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptologist) [Marian Rejewski](wikipedia://en/4349420) in late December 1932 deduced the wiring in the [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) military [Enigma](wikipedia://en/9256) [rotor cipher machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotor_cipher_machine), AVA built Enigma "doubles" as well as all the electro-mechanical equipment designed at the Cipher Bureau to expedite [decryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decryption) of Enigma ciphers.[^n2]

## History {#History}

AVA's four directors were [Edward Fokczyński](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Fokczyński), [Antoni Palluth](wikipedia://en/2294303), [Ludomir Danilewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludomir_Danilewicz), and his younger brother [Leonard Danilewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Danilewicz). The company took its name from the combined radio [callsigns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callsign) of the Danilewicz brothers: (TPAV [^o5] later SP3AV[^o6]) and Palluth (*TPVA*). SP3AV was named in 1930 as an amateur who worked on the 28 Megacycle band [^o7] When the company was being formed in 1929, the Danilewicz brothers were [short-wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short-wave) "[hams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amateur_radio_operator)" and students at the [Warsaw Polytechnic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw_Polytechnic).[^n4]

In 1927, Fokczyński had opened a small radio workshop on [Warsaw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Warsaw)'s [New World Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_World_Street). Sporadically he had received orders from the Cipher Bureau, whose Captain [Maksymilian Ciężki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maksymilian_Ciężki) knew Fokczyński from his 1919–22 army service. Beginning in 1929, the modest shop, ten minutes' walk from the [General Staff building](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saxon_Palace), which housed the [Cipher Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biuro_Szyfrów), was transformed into AVA. The company subsequently moved to new facilities in southern Warsaw's [Mokotów](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mokotów) district.[^n4]

Early on, the fledgling company was severely under-capitalized. All four directors, however, had other jobs; and soon the Cipher Bureau came to the rescue with an order for eight 10-watt short-wave radio stations, which became the embryo of Section II's radio network. AVA also won other clients, including the [Polish Navy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_Navy) and Professor Lugeon of the Warsaw Meteoroglogical Institute, for whom AVA built, to his design, an *atmoradiograf* that registered disturbances in the atmosphere; this, Leonard Danilewicz later recalled, was the unheralded beginning of [radioastronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radioastronomy).[^n1]

The [General Staff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polish_General_Staff)'s [Cipher Bureau](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biuro_Szyfrów) was responsible for the radio communications of the General Staff's Section II (Intelligence section). The Cipher Bureau entrusted the design and construction of the equipment to AVA. The work was carried out on a [cost-plus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost-plus_contract) basis, to the benefit of both AVA and the General Staff.[^n5]

In December 1932, the Cipher Bureau's mathematician-cryptologist Marian Rejewski reconstructed the wiring of the German [Enigma machine](wikipedia://en/9256). The Poles' subsequent reading of German [ciphers](wikipedia://en/5244) laid the foundation for the [western Allies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allies_in_World_War_II)' [Ultra](wikipedia://en/31748) cipher-breaking operations, beginning seven years later, during [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II). Now, in January 1933, just as [Hitler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hitler) was coming to power in Germany, AVA quickly produced a "double" of the Enigma machine; by mid-1934, it had made over a dozen.[^n6]

In 1934 or 1935, AVA built the [cyclometer](wikipedia://en/2774229), a device designed by Rejewski to prepare a "[card catalog](wikipedia://en/3153239)" that facilitated the decryption of Enigma messages.[^n7] In 1938 AVA built the "[cryptologic bomb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptologic_bomb)," invented by Rejewski about October 1938. This was an electrically powered aggregate of six Enigmas that took the place of some one hundred workers. Six bombs were built before September 1939.[^n8]

Actual assembly of the cyclometers, "bombs" and [Lacida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lacida) cipher machines was carried out not at the AVA facilities but in Room 13 (the "Clock Room") at the Cipher Bureau in the General Staff building—and, from 1937, at the new Cipher Bureau center in the [Kabaty Woods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kabaty) south of Warsaw. No one had access to this room except the head of the Cipher Bureau, [Gwido Langer](wikipedia://en/2919669), the head of its German section (*B.S. 4*), [Maksymilian Ciężki](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maksymilian_Ciężki), and personnel employed there: [Antoni Palluth](wikipedia://en/2294303), [Ludomir Danilewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludomir_Danilewicz), his younger brother [Leonard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Danilewicz), [Edward Fokczyński](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Fokczyński), and the specialist in precision mechanics, Czesław Betlewski.[^n9]

In July 1941, Marian Rejewski and Henryk Zygalski were asked to test the security of the Lacida at the Cadiz Center, near [Uzès](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uzès) (free zone). The device had apparently not been rigorously tested before approval, because the two mathematicians decrypted a message in less than two hours. Their dismayed boss gave up using the machine.[^n10]
