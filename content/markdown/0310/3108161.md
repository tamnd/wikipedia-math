# Parallelizable manifold

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Parallelizable manifold"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a differentiable manifold $M$ of dimension *n* is called **parallelizable**[^o2] if there exist [smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) [vector fields](wikipedia://en/62641)

$$\{V_1, \ldots,V_n\}$$

on the manifold, such that at every point $p$ of $M$ the [tangent vectors](wikipedia://en/191999)

$$\{V_1(p), \ldots, V_n(p)\}$$

provide a [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_of_a_vector_space) of the [tangent space](wikipedia://en/30807) at $p$. Equivalently, the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) is a [trivial bundle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_bundle),[^o3] so that the associated [principal bundle](wikipedia://en/310950) of [linear frames](wikipedia://en/366550) has a global section on $M.$

:::

A particular choice of such a basis of vector fields on $M$ is called a **[parallelization](wikipedia://en/33569276)** (or an **absolute parallelism**) of $M$.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- An example with $n = 1$ is the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220): we can take *V*<sub>1</sub> to be the unit tangent vector field, say pointing in the anti-clockwise direction. The [torus](wikipedia://en/74800) of dimension $n$ is also parallelizable, as can be seen by expressing it as a [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of circles. For example, take $n = 2,$ and construct a torus from a square of [graph paper](wikipedia://en/293606) with opposite edges glued together, to get an idea of the two tangent directions at each point. More generally, every [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) *G* is parallelizable, since a basis for the tangent space at the [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) can be moved around by the action of the translation group of *G* on *G* (every translation is a diffeomorphism and therefore these translations induce linear isomorphisms between tangent spaces of points in *G*).
- A classical problem was to determine which of the [spheres](wikipedia://en/27859) *S*<sup>*n*</sup> are parallelizable. The zero-dimensional case *S*<sup>0</sup> is trivially parallelizable. The case *S*<sup>1</sup> is the circle, which is parallelizable as has already been explained. The [hairy ball theorem](wikipedia://en/485168) shows that *S*<sup>2</sup> is not parallelizable. However *S*<sup>3</sup> is parallelizable, since it is the Lie group [SU(2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%282%29). The only other parallelizable sphere is *S*<sup>7</sup>; this was proved in 1958, by [Friedrich Hirzebruch](wikipedia://en/987495), [Michel Kervaire](wikipedia://en/13644049), and by [Raoul Bott](wikipedia://en/337457) and [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632), in independent work. The parallelizable spheres correspond precisely to elements of unit norm in the [normed division algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_division_algebra) of the real numbers, complex numbers, [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440), and [octonions](wikipedia://en/51436), which allows one to construct a parallelism for each. Proving that other spheres are not parallelizable is more difficult, and requires [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).
- The product of parallelizable [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) is parallelizable.
- Every [orientable](wikipedia://en/187446) [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) [three-dimensional manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) is parallelizable.[^o6]

:::

## Remarks {#Remarks}

- Any parallelizable [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) is [orientable](wikipedia://en/187446).
- The term *[framed manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Framed_manifold)* (occasionally *[rigged manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rigged_manifold)*) is most usually applied to an embedded manifold with a given trivialisation of the [normal bundle](wikipedia://en/2370200), and also for an abstract (that is, non-embedded) manifold with a given stable trivialisation of the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794).
- A related notion is the concept of a **π-manifold**.[^o8] A smooth manifold $M$ is called a [π-manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Π-manifold) if, when embedded in a high dimensional euclidean space, its normal bundle is trivial. In particular, every parallelizable manifold is a π-manifold.
