# Thomson problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Thomson problem"}

The objective of the **Thomson problem** is to determine the minimum [electrostatic potential energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostatic_potential_energy) configuration of $N$ [electrons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron) constrained to the surface of a [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090) that repel each other with a force given by [Coulomb's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law). The physicist [J. J. Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._J._Thomson) posed the problem in 1904[^o2] after proposing an [atomic model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atomic_model), later called the [plum pudding model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plum_pudding_model), based on his knowledge of the existence of negatively charged electrons within neutrally-charged atoms.

:::

Related problems include the study of the geometry of the minimum energy configuration and the study of the large $N$ behavior of the minimum energy.

## Mathematical statement {#Mathematical_statement}

The electrostatic interaction energy occurring between each pair of electrons of equal charges ($e_i = e_j = e$, with $e$ the [elementary charge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_charge) of an electron) is given by [Coulomb's law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law),

$$U_{ij}(N)={e_i e_j \over 4\pi\epsilon_0 r_{ij}},$$

where $\epsilon_0$ is the [electric constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_constant) and $r_{ij}=|\mathbf{r}_i - \mathbf{r}_j|$ is the distance between each pair of electrons located at points on the sphere defined by vectors $\mathbf{r}_i$ and $\mathbf{r}_j$, respectively.

Simplified units of $e=1$ and $k_e=1/4\pi\epsilon_0=1$ (the [Coulomb constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coulomb's_law#Coulomb_constant)) are used without loss of generality. Then,

$$U_{ij}(N) = {1 \over r_{ij}}.$$

The total electrostatic potential energy of each *N*-electron configuration may then be expressed as the sum of all pair-wise interaction energies

$$U(N) = \sum_{i < j} \frac{1}{r_{ij}}.$$

The global minimization of $U(N)$ over all possible configurations of *N* distinct points is typically found by numerical minimization algorithms.

Thomson's problem is related to the 7th of the [eighteen unsolved mathematics problems](wikipedia://en/8294746) proposed by the mathematician [Steve Smale](wikipedia://en/40294) — "Distribution of points on the 2-sphere".[^o4] The main difference is that in Smale's problem the function to minimise is not the electrostatic potential $1 \over r_{ij}$ but a logarithmic potential given by $-\log r_{ij}.$ A second difference is that Smale's question is about the asymptotic behaviour of the total potential when the number *N* of points goes to infinity, not for concrete values of *N*.

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

### Example {#Example}

The solution of the Thomson problem for two electrons is obtained when both electrons are as far apart as possible on opposite sides of the origin, $r_{ij} = 2r = 2$, or

$$U(2) = {1 \over 2}.$$

:::

## Known exact solutions {#Known_exact_solutions}

*Image: N 2 to 5 ThomsonSolutions.png.* Schematic geometric solutions of the mathematical Thomson Problem for up to *N* = 5 electrons.

Mathematically exact minimum energy configurations have been rigorously identified in only a handful of cases.

- For *N* = 1, the solution is trivial. The single electron may reside at any point on the surface of the unit sphere. The total energy of the configuration is defined as zero because the charge of the electron is subject to no electric field due to other sources of charge.
- For *N* = 2, the optimal configuration consists of electrons at [antipodal points](wikipedia://en/367577).
- For *N* = 3, electrons reside at the vertices of an equilateral triangle about any [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082).[^o8] The great circle is often considered to define an [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) about the sphere and the two points perpendicular to the plane are often considered poles to aid in discussions about the electrostatic configurations of many-*N* electron solutions.
- For *N* = 4, electrons reside at the vertices of a regular [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606).
- For *N* = 5, electrons reside at the vertices of a [triangular bipyramid](wikipedia://en/646933).[^o9]
- For *N* = 6, electrons reside at vertices of a regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635).[^o10] The configuration may also be described as four electrons residing at the corners of a square about the equator and the remaining two residing at the poles.
- For *N* = 12, electrons reside at the vertices of a regular [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084).[^o11]

The solutions of the Thomson problem for *N* = 4, 6, and 12 electrons are [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) whose faces are all congruent equilateral triangles. Numerical solutions for *N* = 8 and 20 are not the regular convex polyhedral configurations of the remaining two Platonic solids (the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) and [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407), respectively).[^o12]

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

One can also ask for ground states of particles interacting with arbitrary potentials. To be mathematically precise, let *f* be a decreasing real-valued function, and define the energy functional

$$\sum_{i < j} f(|x_i-x_j|).$$

Traditionally, one considers $f(x)=x^{-\alpha}$ also known as Riesz $\alpha$-kernels. For integrable Riesz kernels see the 1972 work of Landkof.[^o15] For non-integrable Riesz kernels, the [poppy-seed bagel theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poppy-seed_bagel_theorem) holds, see the 2004 work of Hardin and Saff.[^o16] Notable cases include:[^o17]

- *α* = ∞, the [Tammes problem](wikipedia://en/19332734) (packing);
- *α* = 1, the Thomson problem;
- *α* = 0, to maximize the product of distances, latterly known as [Whyte's problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whyte's_problem);
- *α* = −1 : maximum average distance problem.

One may also consider configurations of *N* points on a [sphere of higher dimension](wikipedia://en/39782). See [spherical design](wikipedia://en/9730285).

:::

## Solution algorithms {#Solution_algorithms}

Several [algorithms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithms) have been applied to this problem. The focus since the millennium has been on [local optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_search_%28optimization%29) methods applied to the energy function, although [random walks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Random_walks) have made their appearance:[^o17]

- constrained global optimization (Altschuler et al. 1994),
- steepest descent (Claxton and Benson 1966, Erber and Hockney 1991),
- random walk (Weinrach et al. 1990),
- genetic algorithm (Morris et al. 1996)

While the objective is to minimize the global electrostatic potential energy of each *N*-electron case, several algorithmic starting cases are of interest.

### Continuous spherical shell charge {#Continuous_spherical_shell_charge}

*Image: ThomsonProblem Related Energies LaFave.png.* The extreme upper energy limit of the Thomson Problem is given by $N^2/2$ for a continuous shell charge followed by N(N − 1)/2, the energy associated with a random distribution of *N* electrons. Significantly lower energy of a given *N*-electron solution of the Thomson Problem with one charge at its origin is readily obtained by $U(N) + N$, where $U(N)$ are solutions of the Thomson Problem.

The energy of a continuous spherical shell of charge distributed across its surface is given by

$$U_{\text{shell}}(N)=\frac{N^2}{2}$$

and is, in general, greater than the energy of every Thomson problem solution. Note: Here *N* is used as a continuous variable that represents the infinitely divisible charge, *Q*, distributed across the spherical shell. For example, a spherical shell of $N=1$ represents the uniform distribution of a single electron's charge, $-e$, across the entire shell.

### Randomly distributed point charges {#Randomly_distributed_point_charges}

The expected global energy of a system of electrons distributed in a purely random manner across the surface of the sphere is given by

$$U_{\text{rand}}(N)=\frac{N(N-1)}{2}$$

and is, in general, greater than the energy of every Thomson problem solution.

Here, *N* is a discrete variable that counts the number of electrons in the system. As well, $U_{\text{rand}}(N) < U_{\text{shell}}(N)$.

### Charge-centered distribution {#Charge-centered_distribution}

For every *N*th solution of the Thomson problem there is an $(N+1)$th configuration that includes an electron at the origin of the sphere whose energy is simply the addition of *N* to the energy of the *N*th solution. That is,[^o22]

$$U_0(N+1)=U_{\text{Thom}}(N)+N.$$

Thus, if $U_{\text{Thom}}(N)$ is known exactly, then $U_0(N+1)$ is known exactly.

In general, $U_0(N+1)$ is greater than $U_{\text{Thom}}(N+1)$, but is remarkably closer to each $(N+1)$th Thomson solution than $U_{\text{shell}}(N+1)$ and $U_{\text{rand}}(N+1)$. Therefore, the charge-centered distribution represents a smaller "energy gap" to cross to arrive at a solution of each Thomson problem than algorithms that begin with the other two charge configurations.

## Relations to other scientific problems {#Relations_to_other_scientific_problems}

The Thomson problem is a natural consequence of J. J. Thomson's [plum pudding model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plum_pudding_model) in the absence of its uniform positive background charge.[^o24]

Though experimental evidence led to the abandonment of Thomson's plum pudding model as a complete atomic model, irregularities observed in numerical energy solutions of the Thomson problem have been found to correspond with electron shell-filling in naturally occurring atoms throughout the [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table) of elements.[^o26]

The Thomson problem also plays a role in the study of other physical models including [multi-electron bubbles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_bubble) and the surface ordering of liquid metal drops confined in [Paul traps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_trap).

The generalized Thomson problem arises, for example, in determining arrangements of protein subunits that comprise the shells of spherical [viruses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Virus). The "particles" in this application are clusters of protein subunits arranged on a shell. Other realizations include regular arrangements of [colloid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colloid) particles in *colloidosomes*, proposed for encapsulation of active ingredients such as drugs, nutrients or living cells, [fullerene](wikipedia://en/10628) patterns of carbon atoms, and [VSEPR theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VSEPR_theory). An example with long-range logarithmic interactions is provided by [Abrikosov vortices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abrikosov_vortices) that form at low temperatures in a [superconducting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superconductivity) metal shell with a large monopole at its center.
