# 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ⋯

*Image: Partial sums of 1+2+4+8.jpg.* The first four partial sums of 1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ⋯.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ⋯"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + ⋯** is the [infinite series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_series) whose terms are the successive [powers of two](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Powers_of_two). As a [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630), it is characterized by its first term, 1, and its [common ratio](wikipedia://en/12630#Common_ratio_r), 2. As a series of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) it [diverges](wikipedia://en/876428) to [infinity](wikipedia://en/20110824), so in the usual sense it has no sum. However, it can be manipulated to yield a number of mathematically interesting results. For example, many [summation methods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summation_method) are used in mathematics to assign numerical values even to divergent series. In particular, the [Ramanujan summation](wikipedia://en/9052595) of this series is −1, which is the limit of the series using the [2-adic metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_numbers).

:::

## Summation {#Summation}

The partial sum of the first $n$ terms of $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots$ is

$$\sum_{k=0}^{n - 1} 2^k = 2^0+2^1 + \cdots + 2^{n - 1} = 2^n - 1.$$

Since the sequence $1, 3, 7, 15, \ldots$ of these partial sums diverges to infinity, so does the series. Therefore, any [totally regular summation method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_regular_summation_method) gives a sum of infinity, including the [Cesàro sum](wikipedia://en/705600) and [Abel sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abel_summation).[^o3] On the other hand, there is at least one generally useful method that sums $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots$ to the finite value of −1. The associated [power series](wikipedia://en/59958)

$$f(x) = 1 + 2x + 4x^2 + 8x^3+ \cdots + 2^n{}x^n + \cdots = \frac{1}{1-2x}$$

has a [radius of convergence](wikipedia://en/61476) around 0 of only $\frac{1}{2}$ so it does not converge at $x = 1.$ Nonetheless, the so-defined function $f$ has a unique [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) to the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) with the point $x = \frac{1}{2}$ deleted, and it is given by the same rule $f(x) = \frac{1}{1 - 2 x}.$ Since $f(1) = -1,$ the original series $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots$ is said to be summable ([E](wikipedia://en/9780066)) to −1, and −1 is the (E) sum of the series. (The notation is due to [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) in reference to [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902)'s approach to divergent series.)[^o4]

An almost identical approach (the one taken by Euler himself) is to consider the power series whose coefficients are all 1, that is,

$$1 + y + y^2 + y^3 + \cdots = \frac{1}{1-y}$$

and plugging in $y = 2.$ These two series are related by the substitution $y = 2 x.$

The fact that (E) summation assigns a finite value to $1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + \cdots$ shows that the general method is not totally regular. On the other hand, it possesses some other desirable qualities for a summation method, including stability and linearity. These latter two axioms actually force the sum to be −1, since they make the following manipulation valid:

$$\begin{aligned} s = {} & 1+2+4+8+16+\cdots \\ = {} & 1+2(1+2+4+8+\cdots) \\ = {} & 1+2s \end{aligned}$$

In a useful sense, $s = \infty$ is a root of the equation $s = 1 + 2 s.$ (For example, $\infty$ is one of the two [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) of the [Möbius transformation](wikipedia://en/314493) $z \mapsto 1 + 2 z$ on the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799)). If some summation method is known to return an ordinary number for $s$; that is, not $\infty,$ then it is easily determined. In this case $s$ may be subtracted from both sides of the equation, yielding $0 = 1 + s,$ so $s = -1.$[^o5]

The above manipulation might be called on to produce −1 outside the context of a sufficiently powerful summation procedure. For the most well-known and straightforward sum concepts, including the fundamental convergent one, it is absurd that a series of positive terms could have a negative value. A similar phenomenon occurs with the divergent geometric series $1 - 1 + 1 - 1 + \cdots$ ([Grandi's series](wikipedia://en/7830441)), where a series of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) appears to have the non-integer sum $\frac{1}{2}.$ These examples illustrate the potential danger in applying similar arguments to the series implied by such [recurring decimals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recurring_decimal) as $0.111\ldots$ and most notably [$0.999\ldots$](wikipedia://en/1849799). The arguments are ultimately justified for these convergent series, implying that $0.111\ldots = \frac{1}{9}$ and $0.999\ldots = 1,$ but the underlying [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) demand careful thinking about the interpretation of endless sums.[^o6]

It is also possible to view this series as convergent in a number system different from the real numbers, namely, the [2-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423). As a series of 2-adic numbers this series converges to the same sum, −1, as was derived above by analytic continuation.[^o7]
