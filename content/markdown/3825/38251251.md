# Strain-rate tensor

article

*Image: Vel\_decomp\_field01\_term6\_s.png.* A two-dimensional flow that, at the highlighted point, has only a strain rate component, with no mean velocity or rotational component.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Strain-rate tensor"}

In [continuum mechanics](wikipedia://en/5918), the **strain-rate tensor** or **rate-of-strain tensor** is a [physical quantity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity) that describes the [rate of change](wikipedia://en/7921) of the [strain](wikipedia://en/22707107) (i.e., the relative [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29)) of a material in the neighborhood of a certain point, at a certain moment of time. It can be defined as the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the [strain tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_tensor) with respect to time, or as the symmetric component of the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) (derivative with respect to position) of the [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity). In [fluid mechanics](wikipedia://en/2684988) it also can be described as the **velocity gradient**, a measure of how the [velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_field) of a fluid changes between different points within the fluid.[^o2] Though the term can refer to a [velocity profile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Velocity_profile) (variation in velocity across layers of flow in a pipe),[^o3] it is often used to mean the [gradient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spatial_gradient) of a flow's velocity with respect to its [coordinates](wikipedia://en/81931#Common_coordinate_systems).[^o4] The concept has implications in a variety of areas of [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), including [magnetohydrodynamics](wikipedia://en/40310), mining and water treatment.[^o5][^o6][^o7]

:::

The strain rate tensor is a purely [kinematic](wikipedia://en/65914) concept that describes the [macroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic) motion of the material. Therefore, it does not depend on the nature of the material, or on the forces and stresses that may be acting on it; and it applies to any [continuous medium](wikipedia://en/5918), whether [solid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solid), [liquid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liquid) or [gas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gas).

On the other hand, for any [fluid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid) except [superfluids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superfluid), any gradual change in its deformation (i.e. a non-zero strain rate tensor) gives rise to [viscous forces](wikipedia://en/18963754) in its interior, due to [friction](wikipedia://en/11062) between adjacent [fluid elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_element), that tend to oppose that change. At any point in the fluid, these stresses can be described by a [viscous stress tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscous_stress_tensor) that is, almost always, completely determined by the strain rate tensor and by certain intrinsic properties of the fluid at that point. Viscous stress also occur in solids, in addition to the [elastic stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elastic_stress_tensor) observed in static deformation; when it is too large to be ignored, the material is said to be [viscoelastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Viscoelastic).

## Dimensional analysis {#Dimensional_analysis}

By performing [dimensional analysis](wikipedia://en/8267), the dimensions of velocity gradient can be determined. The dimensions of velocity are $\mathsf {L^1 T^{-1}}$, and the dimensions of distance are $\mathsf{L^1}$. Since the velocity gradient can be expressed as $\frac{\Delta \text{velocity}}{\Delta \text{distance}}$. Therefore, the velocity gradient has the same dimensions as this ratio, i.e., $\mathsf{ T^{-1} }$.

## In continuum mechanics {#In_continuum_mechanics}

In 3 dimensions, the gradient $\nabla\mathbf{v}$ of the velocity $\mathbf{v}$ is a second-order [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965) which can be expressed as the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) $\mathbf{L}$:

$$\mathbf{L} = \nabla\mathbf{v} = \begin{bmatrix} {\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial x}} & {\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial x}} & {\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial x}} \\ {\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial y}} & {\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial y}} & {\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial y}\ } \\ {\frac{\partial v_x}{\partial z}} & {\frac{\partial v_y}{\partial z}} & {\frac{\partial v_z}{\partial z}} \end{bmatrix}$$

$\mathbf{L}$ can be decomposed into the sum of a [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) $\textbf{E}$ and a [skew-symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/174055) $\textbf{W}$ as follows

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{E} &= \frac{1}{2} \left(\mathbf{L} + \mathbf{L}^\textsf{T}\right) \\ \mathbf{W} &= \frac{1}{2} \left(\mathbf{L} - \mathbf{L}^\textsf{T}\right) \end{aligned}$$

$\textbf{E}$ is called the strain rate tensor and describes the rate of stretching and shearing. $\textbf{W}$ is called the spin tensor and describes the rate of rotation.[^o10]

## Relationship between shear stress and the velocity field {#Relationship_between_shear_stress_and_the_velocity_field}

[Sir Isaac Newton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_Isaac_Newton) proposed that [shear stress](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shear_stress) is directly proportional to the velocity gradient:[^o12]

$$\tau = \mu\frac{\partial u} {\partial y}.$$

The [constant of proportionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_of_proportionality), $\mu$, is called the [dynamic viscosity](wikipedia://en/18963754#Definition).

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Consider a material body, solid or fluid, that is flowing and/or moving in space. Let $\mathbf{v}$ be the velocity [field](wikipedia://en/62641) within the body; that is, a [smooth](wikipedia://en/330206) function from $\mathbf{R}^{3} \times \mathbf{R}$ such that $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}, t)$ is the [macroscopic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroscopic) velocity of the material that is passing through the point $\mathbf{p}$ at time $t$.

The velocity $\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{r}, t)$ at a point displaced from $\mathbf{p}$ by a small vector $\mathbf{r}$ can be written as a [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448):

$$\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{r}, t) = \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}, t) + (\nabla \mathbf{v})(\mathbf{p}, t)(\mathbf{r}) + \text{higher order terms},$$

where $\nabla\mathbf{v}$ the gradient of the velocity field, understood as a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) that takes a displacement vector $\mathbf{r}$ to the corresponding change in the velocity.

In an arbitrary [reference frame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frame_of_reference), $\nabla\mathbf{v}$ is related to the [Jacobian matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_matrix) of the field, namely in 3 dimensions it is the 3 × 3 matrix

$$\left(\nabla \mathbf{v}\right)^{\mathrm{T}} = \begin{bmatrix} \partial_1 v_1 & \partial_2 v_1 & \partial_3 v_1 \\ \partial_1 v_2 & \partial_2 v_2 & \partial_3 v_2 \\ \partial_1 v_3 & \partial_2 v_3 & \partial_3 v_3 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{J}.$$

where $v_{i}$ is the component of $\mathbf{v}$ parallel to [axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_axis) $i$ and $\partial_{j}f$ denotes the [partial derivative](wikipedia://en/52565) of a function $f$ with respect to the space coordinate $x_{j}$. Note that $\mathbf{J}$ is a function of $\mathbf{p}$ and $t$.

In this coordinate system, the Taylor approximation for the velocity near $\mathbf{p}$ is

$$v_i(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{r}, t) = v_i(\mathbf{p}, t) + \sum_j J_{i j}(\mathbf{p}, t) r_j = v_i(\mathbf{p}, t) + \sum_j \partial_j v_i(\mathbf{p}, t) r_j;$$

or simply

$$\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{r}, t) = \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}, t) + \mathbf{J}(\mathbf{p}, t) \mathbf{r}$$

if $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{r}$ are viewed as 3 × 1 matrices.

### Symmetric and antisymmetric parts {#Symmetric_and_antisymmetric_parts}

Any matrix can be decomposed into the sum of a [symmetric matrix](wikipedia://en/126474) and an [antisymmetric matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antisymmetric_matrix). Applying this to the Jacobian matrix with symmetric and antisymmetric components $\mathbf{E}$ and $\mathbf{R}$ respectively:

$$\begin{aligned} \mathbf{E} &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{J} + \mathbf{J}^\textsf{T}\right) & \mathbf{R} &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\mathbf{J} - \mathbf{J}^\textsf{T}\right) \\ E_{ij} &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_j v_i + \partial_i v_j\right) & R_{ij} &= \frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_j v_i - \partial_i v_j\right) \end{aligned}$$

This decomposition is independent of coordinate system, and so has physical significance. Then the velocity field may be approximated as

$$\mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{r}, t) \approx \mathbf{v}(\mathbf{p}, t) + \mathbf{E}(\mathbf{p}, t)(\mathbf{r}) + \mathbf{R}(\mathbf{p}, t)(\mathbf{r}),$$

that is,

$$\begin{aligned} v_i(\mathbf{p} + \mathbf{r}, t) &= v_i(\mathbf{p}, t) + \sum_j E_{i j}(\mathbf{p}, t) r_j + \sum_j R_{i j}(\mathbf{p}, t) r_j \\ &= v_i(\mathbf{p}, t) + \frac{1}{2}\sum_j \left(\partial_j v_i(\mathbf{p}, t) + \partial_i v_j(\mathbf{p}, t)\right)r_j + \frac{1}{2}\sum_j \left(\partial_j v_i(\mathbf{p}, t) - \partial_i v_j(\mathbf{p}, t)\right)r_j \end{aligned}$$

The antisymmetric term $\mathbf{R}$ represents a rigid-like rotation of the fluid about the point $\mathbf{p}$. Its angular velocity $\vec{\omega}$ is

$$\vec{\omega} = \frac{1}{2} \nabla \times \mathbf{v} = \frac{1}{2} \begin{bmatrix} \partial_2 v_3 - \partial_3 v_2 \\ \partial_3 v_1 - \partial_1 v_3 \\ \partial_1 v_2 - \partial_2 v_1 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The product $\nabla \times \mathbf{v}$ is called the **[vorticity](wikipedia://en/166010)** of the vector field. A rigid rotation does not change the relative positions of the fluid elements, so the antisymmetric term $\mathbf{R}$ of the velocity gradient does not contribute to the rate of change of the deformation. The actual strain rate is therefore described by the symmetric $\mathbf{E}$ term, which is the **strain rate tensor**.

### Shear rate and compression rate {#Shear_rate_and_compression_rate}

The symmetric term $\mathbf{E}$ (the rate-of-strain tensor) can be broken down further as the sum of a scalar times the unit tensor, that represents a gradual isotropic expansion or contraction; and a [traceless](wikipedia://en/43270) symmetric tensor which represents a gradual shearing deformation, with no change in volume:[^o17]

$$\mathbf{E}(\mathbf{p}, t)(\mathbf{r}) = \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{p}, t)(\mathbf{r}) + \mathbf{D}(\mathbf{p}, t)(\mathbf{r}).$$

That is,

$$E_{ij} = \underbrace{\frac{1}{3}\left(\sum_k\partial_k v_k\right) \delta_{ij}}_{\text{rate-of-expansion tensor } S_{ij}} + \underbrace{\overbrace{\frac{1}{2}\left(\partial_i v_j + \partial_j v_i\right)}^{E_{ij}}-S_{ij}}_{\text{rate-of-shear tensor } D_{ij}},$$

Here $\mathbf{\delta}$ is the [unit tensor](wikipedia://en/182890), such that $\delta_{ij}$ is 1 if $i = j$ and 0 if $i \ne j$. This decomposition is independent of the choice of coordinate system, and is therefore physically significant.

The trace of the expansion rate tensor is the [divergence](wikipedia://en/8328) of the velocity field:

$$\nabla \cdot \mathbf{v} = \partial_1 v_1 + \partial_2 v_2 + \partial_3 v_3;$$

which is the rate at which the volume of a fixed amount of fluid increases at that point.

The shear rate tensor is represented by a symmetric 3 × 3 matrix, and describes a flow that combines compression and expansion flows along three orthogonal axes, such that there is no change in volume. This type of flow occurs, for example, when a [rubber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rubber) strip is stretched by pulling at the ends, or when [honey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Honey) falls from a spoon as a smooth unbroken stream.

For a two-dimensional flow, the divergence of $\mathbf{v}$ has only two terms and quantifies the change in area rather than volume. The factor 1/3 in the expansion rate term should be replaced by $\tfrac{1}{2}$ in that case.

## Examples {#Examples}

The study of velocity gradients is useful in analysing path dependent materials and in the subsequent study of stresses and strains; e.g., [Plastic deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plastic_deformation) of [metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metals).[^o4] The near-wall velocity gradient of the unburned reactants flowing from a tube is a key parameter for characterising flame stability.[^o6] The velocity gradient of a [plasma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plasma_%28physics%29) can define conditions for the solutions to fundamental equations in magnetohydrodynamics.[^o5]

### Fluid in a pipe {#Fluid_in_a_pipe}

Consider the velocity field of a fluid flowing through a [pipe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pipe_%28fluid_conveyance%29). The layer of fluid in contact with the pipe tends to be at rest with respect to the pipe. This is called the [no slip condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/No_slip_condition).[^o21] If the velocity difference between fluid layers at the centre of the pipe and at the sides of the pipe is sufficiently small, then the fluid flow is observed in the form of continuous layers. This type of flow is called [laminar flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laminar_flow).

The [flow velocity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity) difference between adjacent layers can be measured in terms of a velocity gradient, given by $\Delta u / \Delta y$. Where $\Delta u$ is the difference in flow velocity between the two layers and $\Delta y$ is the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) between the layers.
