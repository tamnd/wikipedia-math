# Lissajous knot

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lissajous knot"}

In [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008), a **Lissajous knot** is a [knot](wikipedia://en/366808) defined by [parametric equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parametric_equations) of the form

:::

$$x = \cos(n_x t + \phi_x),\qquad y = \cos(n_y t + \phi_y), \qquad z = \cos(n_z t + \phi_z),$$

*Image: Lissajous 8 21 Knot.png.* A Lissajous 8<sub>21</sub> knot

where $n_x$, $n_y$, and $n_z$ are [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) and the [phase shifts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_shift) $\phi_x$, $\phi_y$, and $\phi_z$ may be any [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).[^o2]

The projection of a Lissajous knot onto any of the three coordinate planes is a [Lissajous curve](wikipedia://en/753756), and many of the properties of these knots are closely related to properties of Lissajous curves.

Replacing the cosine function in the parametrization by a [triangle wave](wikipedia://en/52151) transforms every Lissajous knot isotopically into a billiard curve inside a cube, the simplest case of so-called *billiard knots*. Billiard knots can also be studied in other domains, for instance in a cylinder[^o3] or in a (flat) [solid torus](wikipedia://en/1251473) ([Lissajous-toric knot](wikipedia://en/67038444)).

## Form {#Form}

Because a knot cannot be self-intersecting, the three integers $n_x, n_y, n_z$ must be pairwise [relatively prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relatively_prime), and none of the quantities

$$n_x \phi_y - n_y \phi_x,\quad n_y \phi_z - n_z \phi_y,\quad n_z \phi_x - n_x \phi_z$$

may be an integer multiple of [pi](wikipedia://en/23601). Moreover, by making a substitution of the form $t' = t+c$, one may assume that any of the three phase shifts $\phi_x$, $\phi_y$, $\phi_z$ is equal to zero.

## Symmetry {#Symmetry}

Lissajous knots are highly symmetric, though the type of symmetry depends on whether or not the numbers $n_x$, $n_y$, and $n_z$ are all odd.

### Odd case {#Odd_case}

If $n_x$, $n_y$, and $n_z$ are all odd, then the [point reflection](wikipedia://en/12487536) across the origin $(x,y,z)\mapsto (-x,-y,-z)$ is a symmetry of the Lissajous knot which preserves the knot orientation.

In general, a knot that has an orientation-preserving point [reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873) is known as **strongly positive [amphicheiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amphicheiral_knot)**.[^o14] This is a fairly rare property: only seven [prime knots](wikipedia://en/315030) with twelve or fewer crossings are strongly positive amphicheiral (10<sub>99</sub>, 10<sub>123</sub>, 12a427, 12a1019, 12a1105, 12a1202, 12n706).[^o15] Since this is so rare, ′most′ prime Lissajous knots lie in the even case.

### Even case {#Even_case}

If one of the frequencies (say $n_x$) is even, then the 180° rotation around the *x*-axis $(x,y,z)\mapsto (x,-y,-z)$ is a symmetry of the Lissajous knot. In general, a knot that has a symmetry of this type is called **2-periodic**, so every even Lissajous knot must be 2-periodic.

### Consequences {#Consequences}

*Image: Lissajous knot with parameters (4, 5, 41, 0.01, 0.16).svg.* A Lissajous knot with three factors: $(n_x,n_y,n_z)=(4,5,41)$, $(\phi_x,\phi_y)=(0.01,0.16)$

The symmetry of a Lissajous knot puts severe constraints on the [Alexander polynomial](wikipedia://en/1107647). In the odd case, the Alexander polynomial of the Lissajous knot must be a perfect [square](wikipedia://en/659942).[^o18] In the even case, the Alexander polynomial must be a perfect square [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) 2.[^o19] In addition, the [Arf invariant](wikipedia://en/6950659) of a Lissajous knot must be zero. It follows that:

- The [trefoil knot](wikipedia://en/404773) and [figure-eight knot](wikipedia://en/59565) are not Lissajous.
- No [torus knot](wikipedia://en/1165182) can be Lissajous.
- No [fibered](wikipedia://en/1358592) [2-bridge knot](wikipedia://en/3396136) can be Lissajous.
