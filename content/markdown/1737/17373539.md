# Freshman's dream

*Image: Freshman's Dream.svg.* An illustration of the Freshman's dream in two dimensions. Each side of the square is X+Y in length. The area of the square is the sum of the area of the yellow region (=X<sup>2</sup>), the area of the green region (=Y<sup>2</sup>), and the area of the two white regions (=2×X×Y).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Freshman's dream"}

In mathematics, the **freshman's dream**,[^o2][^o3][^o4][^o5] also known as **freshman exponentiation**,[^o5][^o6] the **[child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child)'s binomial [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977)**,[^o7] (rarely) the **[schoolboy](wikipedia://en/155526) binomial theorem**,[^o8] or the **Frobenius identity**[^o4] is the generally-false [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) $(x+y)^n=x^n+y^n$. Beginning students commonly make this error in computing the [power](wikipedia://en/99491) of a [sum](wikipedia://en/246160) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), falsely assuming powers [distribute](wikipedia://en/103118) over sums.

:::

The correct result is given by the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677),[^o6] which has additional [terms](wikipedia://en/61338#Notation_and_terminology) in the middle when $n\ge 2$.[^o2] For example, when $n=2$, the correct result is *x*<sup>2</sup> + 2*xy* + *y*<sup>2</sup>, which can also be shown by [multiplying](wikipedia://en/20845) (*x* + *y*)(*x* + *y*) by using the distributive property properly, or the [FOIL method](wikipedia://en/1797141).

The freshman's dream is actually valid in commutative [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) *p*, such as the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) $\mathbb{F}_p = \mathbb{Z}/p\mathbb{Z}$, where *p* is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), provided that the exponent *n* is *p* or more generally a power of *p*. Equivalently, the [Frobenius map](wikipedia://en/1088270) of the ring is an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569). One way to prove this is to show that *p* [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) all the [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668) except for the first and the last, so all the intermediate terms are equal to [zero](wikipedia://en/34513).[^o2][^o6] Another way to prove the common [special case](wikipedia://en/31308010) of this for $\mathbb{F}_p$ is to use [Fermat's little theorem](wikipedia://en/53453) that *a<sup>p</sup>* [≡](wikipedia://en/48167) *a* [mod](wikipedia://en/1352428) *p* for all integers *a*.[^o7] (This can be [iterated](wikipedia://en/68833) for powers of *p*, using the [property of exponentiation](wikipedia://en/99491#Identities_and_properties) that taking a power of a power multiplies the exponents, and thereby proven in general using [induction](wikipedia://en/18881).)

The freshman's dream is valid for all *n* in [tropical geometry](wikipedia://en/867041)[^o3][^o4] (where multiplication is replaced with [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), so exponentiation becomes multiplication, and addition is replaced with [minimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum_%28mathematics%29)).

The freshman's dream equation is also true in some [degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) cases, such as when *n* = 1, when $n \ge 1$ and at least one of *x* and *y* is zero, and when *n* is an [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) integer and $y=-x$. These are all of the true cases for *n* ∈ {0, 1, 2, 3}, but when *n* ≥ 4 or *n* is [negative](wikipedia://en/154616) or non-[integer](wikipedia://en/14563), there are generally additional pairs of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) *x*, *y* that satisfy the equation.

::: {#o10 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- $(1+4)^2 = 5^2 = 25$, but $1^2+4^2 = 17$.
- $\sqrt{x^2+y^2}$ does not equal $\sqrt{x^2}+\sqrt{y^2}=|x|+|y|$. For example, $\sqrt{9+16}=\sqrt{25}=5$, which does not equal 3 + 4 = 7. In this example, the error is being committed with the exponent *n* = $\tfrac{1}{2}$.

:::

## Prime characteristic {#Prime_characteristic}

When $p$ is a prime number and $x$ and $y$ are members of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) of [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) $p$, then $(x+y)^p=x^p+y^p$. This can be seen by examining the prime factors of the binomial coefficients: the *n*th binomial coefficient is

$$\binom{p}{n} = \frac{p!}{n!(p-n)!}.$$

The [numerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerator) is *p* [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606)(!), which is divisible by *p*. However, when 0 \< *n* \< *p*, both *n*! and (*p* − *n*)! are coprime with *p* since all the factors are less than *p* and *p* is prime. Since a binomial coefficient is always an integer, the *n*th binomial coefficient is divisible by *p* and hence equal to 0 in the ring. We are left with the zeroth and *p*th coefficients, which both equal 1, yielding the desired equation.

Thus in characteristic *p* the freshman's dream is a valid identity. This result demonstrates that exponentiation by *p* produces an [endomorphism](wikipedia://en/9569), known as the [Frobenius endomorphism](wikipedia://en/1088270) of the ring.

The demand that the characteristic *p* be a prime number is central to the truth of the freshman's dream. A related theorem states that a number *n* is prime [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) (*x* + 1)<sup>*n*</sup> = *x<sup>n</sup>* + 1 in the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $(\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z})[x]$. This theorem is a key fact in modern primality testing.[^o7]

## History {#History}

The history of the [term](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terminology) "freshman's dream" is somewhat unclear.

The phrase "freshman's dream" is recorded in non-mathematical contexts since at least the 1840s.[^o13][^o14]

On September 6, 1938, *[The New York Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sun_%28New_York_City%29)* published a 16-line [poem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poetry) by [Harold Willard Gleason](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Willard_Gleason) titled «"Dark and Bloody Ground---" (*The Freshman's Dream*)» that bears some resemblance to this equation. It begins with "In [minuends](wikipedia://en/74141#Notation_and_terminology) of [Algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) / Wild [corollaries](wikipedia://en/168865) twine;" and ends with "Or you shall [factor](wikipedia://en/82341) [cubes](wikipedia://en/451445), for terms / Of [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824) progression!" It mentions "binomial" and "[parenthesis](wikipedia://en/59338)" and cautions to "Remove the brackets, [radicals](wikipedia://en/235029) \[...\] with [discretion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discretion#In_ethics_and_social_behavior)". However, it has no context or explanation to confirm or refute whether it actually refers to this equation. This poem was reproduced by other [periodicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodical_literature) over the following two [months](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Month), including the *[National Mathematics Magazine](wikipedia://en/4230329)* published by the [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) (MAA).[^o15]

On December 30, 1939, [Saunders Mac Lane](wikipedia://en/336574) delivered an [address](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_speaking) to the MAA in [Columbus, Ohio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbus,_Ohio), wherein he explained the theorem for fields of prime characteristic, then stated that "As [S. C. Kleene](wikipedia://en/28805) has remarked, a [knowledge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knowledge) of the case *p*=2 of this equation would corrupt freshman students of algebra!"[^o16] This may be the first connection between "freshman" and binomial expansion in fields of positive characteristic.[^o5] Since then, [authors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Author) of [undergraduate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undergraduate_education) algebra texts took note of the common error.

In 1974, in a [textbook](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textbook) about [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) for [graduate students](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Postgraduate_education), [Thomas W. Hungerford](wikipedia://en/30933181) published an exercise with a title of "The Freshman's Dream" with a footnote stating, "Terminology due to [V\[incent\] O. McBrien](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincent_O._McBrien)."[^o17]
