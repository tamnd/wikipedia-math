# Richards' theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Richards' theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Richards' theorem** is a result due to [Paul I. Richards](wikipedia://en/48966280) in 1947. The theorem states that for

:::

$$R(s) = \frac{kZ(s)-sZ(k)}{kZ(k)-sZ(s)},$$

if $Z(s)$ is a [positive-real function](wikipedia://en/23437839) (PRF) then $R(s)$ is a PRF for all [real](wikipedia://en/20646438), positive values of $k$.[^o2]

The theorem has applications in electrical [network synthesis](wikipedia://en/59441761). The PRF property of an impedance function determines whether or not a passive network can be realised having that impedance. Richards' theorem led to a new method of realising such networks in the 1940s.

::: {#o4 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

$$R(s) = \frac{kZ(s)-sZ(k)}{kZ(k)-sZ(s)}$$

where $Z(s)$ is a PRF, $k$ is a positive real constant, and $s= \sigma + i \omega$ is the [complex frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_frequency) variable, can be written as,

$$R(s) = \dfrac{1-W(s)}{1+W(s)}$$

where,

$$W(s) = {1 - \dfrac{Z(s)}{Z(k)} \over 1 + \dfrac{Z(s)}{Z(k)}} \left( \frac{k+s}{k-s} \right)$$

Since $Z(s)$ is PRF then

$$1 + \dfrac{Z(s)}{Z(k)}$$

is also PRF. The [zeroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28complex_analysis%29) of this function are the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) of $W(s)$. Since a PRF can have no zeroes in the right-half [*s*-plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S-plane), then $W(s)$ can have no poles in the right-half *s*-plane and hence is analytic in the right-half *s*-plane.

Let

$$Z(i \omega) = r (\omega) + ix(\omega)$$

Then the magnitude of $W(i \omega)$ is given by,

$$\left| W(i \omega) \right| = \sqrt{ \dfrac{ (Z(k) - r(\omega))^2 +x(\omega)^2 }{ (Z(k) + r(\omega))^2 +x(\omega)^2 }}$$

Since the PRF condition requires that $r(\omega) \ge 0$ for all $\omega$ then $\left| W(i \omega) \right| \le 1$ for all $\omega$. The maximum magnitude of $W(s)$ occurs on the $i \omega$ axis because $W(s)$ is analytic in the right-half *s*-plane. Thus $|W(s)| \le 1$ for $\sigma \ge 0$.

Let $W(s) = u( \sigma, \omega) + iv( \sigma, \omega)$, then the real part of $R(s)$ is given by,

$$\Re (R(s)) = \dfrac{1 - |W(s)|^2}{ (1 + u( \sigma, \omega))^2 + v^2(\sigma, \omega)}$$

Because $W(s) \le 1$ for $\sigma \ge 0$ then $\Re (R(s)) \ge 0$ for $\sigma \ge 0$ and consequently $R(s)$ must be a PRF.[^o5]

Richards' theorem can also be derived from [Schwarz's lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schwarz's_lemma).[^o6]

:::

## Uses {#Uses}

The theorem was introduced by [Paul I. Richards](wikipedia://en/48966280) as part of his investigation into the properties of PRFs. The term *PRF* was coined by [Otto Brune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Brune) who [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) that the PRF property was a [necessary and sufficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_and_sufficient) condition for a function to be realisable as a passive [electrical network](wikipedia://en/9559), an important result in [network synthesis](wikipedia://en/59441761).[^o9] Richards gave the theorem in his 1947 paper in the reduced form,[^o10]

$$R(s) = \frac{Z(s)-sZ(1)}{Z(1)-sZ(s)}$$

that is, the special case where $k=1$

The theorem (with the more general casse of $k$ being able to take on any value) formed the basis of the [network synthesis](wikipedia://en/59441761) technique presented by [Raoul Bott](wikipedia://en/337457) and [Richard Duffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Duffin) in 1949.[^o11] In the Bott-Duffin synthesis, $Z(s)$ represents the electrical network to be synthesised and $R(s)$ is another (unknown) network incorporated within it ($R(s)$ is unitless, but $R(s)Z(k)$ has units of impedance and $R(s)/Z(k)$ has units of admittance). Making $Z(s)$ the subject gives

$$Z(s) = \left( \frac{R(s)}{Z(k)} + \frac{k}{sZ(k)} \right)^{-1} + \left(\frac{1}{Z(k) R(s)} + \frac{s}{k Z(k)} \right)^{-1}$$

Since $Z(k)$ is merely a positive [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438), $Z(s)$ can be synthesised as a new network proportional to $R(s)$ in parallel with a capacitor all in series with a network proportional to the inverse of $R(s)$ in parallel with an inductor. By a suitable choice for the value of $k$, a resonant circuit can be extracted from $R(s)$ leaving a function $Z'(s)$ two degrees lower than $Z(s)$. The whole process can then be applied iteratively to $Z'(s)$ until the degree of the function is reduced to something that can be realised directly.[^o12]

The advantage of the Bott-Duffin synthesis is that, unlike other methods, it is able to synthesise any PRF. Other methods have limitations such as only being able to deal with two kinds of [element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_element) in any single network. Its major disadvantage is that it does not result in the minimal number of elements in a network. The number of elements grows exponentially with each iteration. After the first iteration there are two $Z'$ and associated elements, after the second, there are four $Z''$ and so on.[^o13]

Hubbard notes that Bott and Duffin appeared not to know the relationship of Richards' theorem to Schwarz's lemma and offers it as his own discovery,[^o14] but it was certainly known to Richards who used it in his own proof of the theorem.[^o15]
