# Moving least squares

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Moving least squares"}

**Moving least squares** is a method of reconstructing [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) from a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of unorganized point samples via the calculation of a [weighted least squares](wikipedia://en/2407860) [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) biased towards the region around the point at which the reconstructed value is requested.

:::

In [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics), the moving least squares method is useful for reconstructing a surface from a set of points. Often it is used to create a 3D surface from a [point cloud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_cloud) through either [downsampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Downsampling) or [upsampling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upsampling).

In numerical analysis to handle contributions of geometry where it is difficult to obtain discretizations, the moving least squares methods have also been used and generalized to solve [PDEs](wikipedia://en/52564) on curved surfaces and other geometries.[^o2][^o3][^o4] This includes numerical methods developed for curved surfaces for solving scalar parabolic PDEs[^o2][^o4] and vector-valued hydrodynamic PDEs.[^o3]

In machine learning, moving least squares methods have also been used to develop model classes and learning methods. This includes function regression methods[^o5] and neural network function and operator regression approaches, such as GMLS-Nets.[^o6]

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Moving\_Least\_Squares2.png.* Here is a 1D example. The circles are the sample points and the polygon is a linear interpolation. The blue curve is a smooth approximation of order 3.

Consider a function $f: \mathbb{R}^n \to \mathbb{R}$ and a set of sample points $S = \{ (x_i,f_i) | f(x_i) = f_i \}$. Then, the moving least square approximation of degree $m$ at the point $x$ is $\tilde{p}(x)$ where $\tilde{p}$ minimizes the weighted least-square error

$$\sum_{i \in I} (p(x_i)-f_i)^2\theta(\|x-x_i\|)$$

over all polynomials $p$ of degree $m$ in $\mathbb{R}^n$. $\theta(s)$ is the weight and it tends to zero as $s\to \infty$.

In the example $\theta(s) = e^{-s^2}$. The smooth interpolator of "order 3" is a quadratic interpolator.

:::
