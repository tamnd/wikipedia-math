# Paley–Wiener integral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paley–Wiener integral"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Paley–Wiener integral** is a simple [stochastic integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_integral). When applied to [classical Wiener space](wikipedia://en/6592746), it is less general than the [Itô integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Itô_integral), but the two agree when they are both defined.

:::

The integral is named after its discoverers, [Raymond Paley](wikipedia://en/2788568) and [Norbert Wiener](wikipedia://en/63185).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $i : H \to E$ be an [abstract Wiener space](wikipedia://en/6592746) with abstract Wiener measure $\gamma$ on $E$. Let $j : E^* \to H$ be the [adjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_of_an_operator) of $i$. (We have abused notation slightly: strictly speaking, $j : E^* \to H^*$, but since $H$ is a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), it is [isometrically isomorphic](wikipedia://en/254777) to its [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) $H^*$, by the [Riesz representation theorem](wikipedia://en/26452).)

It can be shown that $j$ is an [injective function](wikipedia://en/45196) and has [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29) [image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_%28function%29) in $H$. Furthermore, it can be shown that every [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) $f \in E^*$ is also [square-integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square-integrable): in fact,

$$\| f \|_{L^{2} (E, \gamma; \mathbb{R})} = \| j(f) \|_{H}$$

This defines a natural [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) from $j(E^*)$ to $L^2(E, \gamma; \mathbb{R})$, under which $j(f) \in j(E^*) \subseteq H$ goes to the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) $[f]$ of $f$ in $L^2(E, \gamma; \mathbb{R})$. This is well-defined since $j$ is injective. This map is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777), so it is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122).

However, since a continuous linear map between [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989) such as $H$ and $L^2(E, \gamma; \mathbb{R})$ is uniquely determined by its values on any dense subspace of its domain, there is a unique continuous linear extension $I : H \to L^2(E, \gamma; \mathbb{R})$ of the above natural map $j(E^*) \to L^2(E, \gamma; \mathbb{R})$ to the whole of $H$.

This isometry $I : H \to L^2(E, \gamma; \mathbb{R})$ is known as the **Paley–Wiener map**. $I(h)$, also denoted $\langle h, x \rangle^\sim$, is a function on $E$ and is known as the **Paley–Wiener integral** (with respect to $h \in H$).

The Paley–Wiener integral for a particular element $h \in H$ is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) on $E$. The notation $\langle h, x \rangle^\sim$ does not really denote an inner product (since $h$ and $x$ belong to two different spaces), but is a convenient [abuse of notation](wikipedia://en/1698066) in view of the [Cameron–Martin theorem](wikipedia://en/6573013). For this reason, many authors prefer to write $\langle h, - \rangle^\sim (x)$ or $I(h)(x)$ rather than using the more compact but potentially confusing $\langle h, x \rangle^\sim$ notation.
