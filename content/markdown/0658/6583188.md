# Banach–Mazur theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Banach–Mazur theorem"}

In [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), a field of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a key object of study is a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space), which is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) equipped with a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534), which allows vectors to be measured. When they are [infinite dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_dimensional), normed spaces can be very complicated. A standard normed space is the space $C([0,1],\mathbb R)$ of [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) on the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324), $f:[0,1]\to\mathbb R$, which is equipped with the norm $\|f\| = \max_{x\in [0,1]}|f(x)|$, the [maximum value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_value) of the function.

:::

The **Banach–Mazur theorem** is a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) that provides one way of bounding the complexity of certain [well-behaved](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Well-behaved) normed spaces ([separable](wikipedia://en/27855)). It states that every such normed space can be embedded into the normed space $C([0,1],\mathbb R)$, in such a way that the norm (length) of every vector is preserved (a property known as an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777)). It is named after [Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) and [Stanisław Mazur](wikipedia://en/1023162).

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Every [real](wikipedia://en/20646438), [separable](wikipedia://en/27855) [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $(X, || \cdot ||)$ is [isometrically isomorphic](wikipedia://en/254777) to a [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) subspace of $C^{0}([0, 1], \mathbf{R})$, the space of all [continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) from the unit [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) into the real line.

:::

## Comments {#Comments}

On the one hand, the Banach–Mazur theorem seems to tell us that the seemingly vast collection of all separable Banach spaces is not that vast or difficult to work with, since a separable Banach space is "only" a collection of continuous paths. On the other hand, the theorem tells us that $C^{0}([0, 1], \mathbf{R})$ is a "really big" space, big enough to contain every possible separable Banach space.

Non-separable Banach spaces cannot embed isometrically in the separable space $C^{0}([0, 1], \mathbf{R})$, but for every Banach space $X$, one can find a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) $K$ and an isometric linear embedding $j$ of $X$ into the space $C(K)$ of scalar continuous functions on $K$. The simplest choice is to let $K$ be the [unit ball](wikipedia://en/27033090) of the [continuous dual](wikipedia://en/7988#Continuous_dual_space) $X\,'$, equipped with the [w\*-topology](wikipedia://en/33662). This unit ball $K$ is then compact by the [Banach–Alaoglu theorem](wikipedia://en/577366). The embedding $j$ is introduced by saying that for every $x \in X$, the continuous function $j(x)$ on $K$ is defined by

$$\forall x' \in K: \qquad j(x)(x') = x'(x).$$

The mapping $j$ is linear, and it is isometric by the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860).

Another generalization was given by Kleiber and Pervin (1969): a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) of [density](wikipedia://en/23523594) equal to an infinite cardinal $\alpha$ is isometric to a subspace of $C^{0}([0,1]^{\alpha}, \mathbf{R})$, the space of real continuous functions on the [product](wikipedia://en/24104095#Infinite_products) of $\alpha$ copies of the unit interval.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Stronger versions of the theorem"}

## Stronger versions of the theorem {#Stronger_versions_of_the_theorem}

Let us write $C^{k}[0, 1]$ for $C^{k}([0, 1], \mathbf{R})$. In 1995, Luis Rodríguez-Piazza proved that the isometry $i : X \to C^{0}[0, 1]$ can be chosen so that every non-zero function in the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) $i(X)$ is [nowhere differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowhere_differentiable). Put another way, if $D \subset C^{0}[0, 1]$ consists of functions that are differentiable at at least one point of $[0, 1]$, then $i$ can be chosen so that $i(X) \cap D = \{0\}.$ This conclusion applies to the space $C^{0}[0, 1]$ itself, hence there exists a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102) $i : C^{0}[0, 1] \to C^{0}[0, 1]$ that is an isometry onto its image, such that image under $i$ of $C^{0}[0, 1]$ (the subspace consisting of functions that are everywhere differentiable with continuous derivative) intersects $D$ only at $0$: thus the space of smooth functions (with respect to the uniform distance) is isometrically isomorphic to a space of nowhere-differentiable functions. Note that the (metrically incomplete) space of smooth functions is dense in $C^{0}[0, 1]$.

:::
