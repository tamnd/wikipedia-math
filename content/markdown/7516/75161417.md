# Finite subgroups of SU(2)

*Image: Frobenius\_character\_tables\_binary\_subgroups\_1899.pdf.* The character tables of the binary tetrahedral, octahedral and icosahedral groups following [Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Frobenius) (1899)[^o1]

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Finite subgroups of SU(2)"}

In [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969), finite subgroups of the [special unitary group](wikipedia://en/173997) $\operatorname{SU}(2)$ are [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) composed of rotations and related transformations, employed particularly in the field of [physical chemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_chemistry). The [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of a [physical body](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_body) generally contains a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) (typically finite) of the [3D rotation group](wikipedia://en/173965). It may occur that the group $\{\pm1\}$ with two elements acts also on the body; this is typically the case in [magnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetism) for the exchange of north and south poles, or in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) for the change of [spin](wikipedia://en/19593829) sign. In this case, the symmetry group of a body may be a [central extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_extension_%28mathematics%29) of the group of spatial symmetries by the group with two elements. [Hans Bethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Bethe) introduced the term "[double group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_group)" (*Doppelgruppe*) for such a group, in which two different elements induce the spatial identity, and a rotation of $2\pi$ may correspond to an element of the double group that is not the identity.

:::

The classification of the finite double groups and their [character tables](wikipedia://en/911520) is therefore physically meaningful and is thus the main part of the theory of double groups. Finite double groups include the [binary polyhedral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_polyhedral_group).

In physical chemistry, double groups are used in the treatment of the [magnetochemistry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetochemistry) of [complexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordination_complex) of metal ions that have a single unpaired electron in the d-shell or f-shell.[^o3][^o4] Instances when a double group is commonly used include 6-coordinate complexes of [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper)(II), [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium)(III) and [cerium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerium)(III). In these double groups rotation by 360° is treated as a symmetry operation separate from the identity operation; the double group is formed by combining these two symmetry operations with a [point group](wikipedia://en/1138942) such as a [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/2922089) or the [full octahedral group](wikipedia://en/2900805#Full_octahedral_symmetry).

## Definition and theory {#Definition_and_theory}

Let $\Gamma$ be a finite subgroup of [SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%283%29), the three-dimensional rotation group. There is a natural homomorphism $f$ of [SU(2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%282%29) onto SO(3) which has kernel $\{\pm I\}$.[^n1] This [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_cover_%28topology%29) can be realised using the [adjoint action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjoint_action) of SU(2) on its [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{su}(2)$ of traceless 2-by-2 [skew-adjoint](wikipedia://en/509788) matrices or using the action by conjugation of [unit quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_quaternions). The **double group** $\Gamma$ is defined as $f^{-1}(\Gamma)$. By construction, $\{\pm I\}$ is a [central subgroup](wikipedia://en/4938766) of $\Gamma$ and the quotient is isomorphic to $\Gamma$. Thus $\Gamma$ is a [central extension](wikipedia://en/345807#central_extension) of the group $\Gamma$ by $\{\pm1\}$, the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 2. Ordinary representations of $\Gamma$ are just mappings of $\Gamma$ into the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) that are homomorphisms up to a sign; equivalently, they are [projective representations](wikipedia://en/348860) of $\Gamma$ with a [factor system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_system) or [Schur multiplier](wikipedia://en/1121587) in $\{\pm1\}$. Two projective representations of $\Gamma$ are closed under the tensor product operation, with their corresponding factor systems in $\{\pm1\}$ multiplying. The central extensions of $\Gamma$ by $\{\pm1\}$ also have a natural product.[^n2]

The finite subgroups of SU(2) and SO(3) were determined in 1876 by [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) in an article in *[Mathematische Annalen](wikipedia://en/1423381)*, later incorporated in his celebrated 1884 "Lectures on the Icosahedron": for SU(2), the subgroups correspond to the cyclic groups, the [binary dihedral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_dihedral_group), the [binary tetrahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997586), the [binary octahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997598), and the [binary icosahedral group](wikipedia://en/3195145); and for SO(3), they correspond to the cyclic groups, the [dihedral groups](wikipedia://en/100267), the [tetrahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tetrahedral_group), the [octahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Octahedral_group) and the [icosahedral group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icosahedral_group). The correspondence can be found in numerous text books, and goes back to the classification of [platonic solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_solids). From Klein's classifications of binary subgroups, it follows that, if $\Gamma$ a finite subgroup of SO(3), then, up to equivalence, there are exactly two central extensions of $\Gamma$ by $\{\pm1\}$: the one obtained by lifting the double cover $\Gamma = f^{-1}(\Gamma)$; and the trivial extension $\Gamma \times \{\pm1\}$.[^n2][^o31][^o32][^o33][^o9]

The [character tables](wikipedia://en/911520) of the finite subgroups of SU(2) and SO(3) were determined and tabulated by [F. G. Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Frobenius) in 1898,[^o1] with alternative derivations by [I. Schur](wikipedia://en/617522) and H. E. Jordan in 1907 independently. [Branching rules](wikipedia://en/880754) and [tensor product formulas](wikipedia://en/43079145) were also determined. For each binary subgroup, i.e. finite subgroup of SU(2), the [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of $\Gamma$ are labelled by [extended Dynkin diagrams](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_Dynkin_diagram) of type A, D and E; the rules for tensoring with the two-dimensional vector representation are given graphically by an [undirected graph](wikipedia://en/34080241).[^o31][^o32][^o33] By [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696), irreducible representations of $\Gamma \times \{\pm1\}$ are just irreducible representations of $\Gamma$ multiplied by either the trivial or the sign character of $\{\pm1\}$. Likewise, irreducible representations of $\Gamma$ that send $-1$ to $I$ are just ordinary representations of $\Gamma$; while those which send $-1$ to $-I$ are genuinely double-valued or spinor representations.[^n2]

The [representation theory of SU(2)](wikipedia://en/1378619) goes back to the nineteenth century and the theory of invariants of binary forms, with the figures of [Alfred Clebsch](wikipedia://en/1369957) and [Paul Gordan](wikipedia://en/1059850) prominent.[^o15][^n4][^o16][^o17][^o18][^o19][^o20][^o21][^o22] The irreducible representations of SU(2) are indexed by non-negative half integers $j$. If $V$ is the two-dimensional vector representation, then $V_{j}$ = S<sup>2*j*</sup> $V$, the $2j$th [symmetric power](wikipedia://en/57047243) of $V$, a $(2j + 1)$-dimensional vector space. Letting $G$ be the compact group SU(2), the group $G$ acts irreducibly on each $V_{j}$ and satisfies the Clebsch-Gordan rules:

$$V_i \otimes V_j \cong V_{|i-j|} \oplus V_{|i-j|+1} \oplus \cdots \oplus V_{i+j -1} \oplus V_{i+j}.$$

In particular $V_j \otimes V_{{1\over 2}} \cong V_{j-{1\over 2}} \oplus V_{j+{1\over 2}}$ for $j$ > 0, and $V_0 \otimes V_{{1\over 2}} \cong V_{{1\over 2}}.$ By definition, the matrix representing $g$ in $V_{j}$ is just S<sup>2*j*</sup> ( $g$ ). Since every $g$ is conjugate to a diagonal matrix with diagonal entries $\zeta$ and $\zeta^{-1}$ (the order being immaterial), in this case S<sup>2*j*</sup> ( $g$ ) has diagonal entries $\zeta^{2j}$, $\zeta^{2j-1}$, ... , $\zeta^{-2j+1}$, $\zeta^{-2j}$. Setting $\pi_j(g) =S^{2j}(g),$ this yields the character formula

$$\chi_j(g) := {\rm Tr} \, \pi_j(g) = \sum_{k=-2j}^{2j} \zeta^k = {\zeta^{2j+1} - \zeta^{-2j-1}\over \zeta -\zeta^{-1}}.$$

Substituting $\zeta =e^{i\alpha/2}$, it follows that, if $g$ has diagonal entries $e^{\pm i\alpha/2},$ then

$$\chi_j(g) = {\sin (j + {1/2})\alpha \over \sin \alpha/2}.$$

The representation theory of SU(2), including that of SO(3), can be developed in many different ways:[^n5]

- using the complexification *G*<sub>c</sub> = [SL(2,C)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SL%282,C%29) and the double coset decomposition *G*<sub>c</sub> = *B* ⋅ *w* ⋅ *B* ∐ *B*, where *B* denotes upper triangular matrices and $w = \begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ -1 & 0\end{pmatrix}$;
- using the infinitesimal action of the Lie algebras of SU(2) and SL(2,C) where they appear as raising and lowering operators $E$, $F$, $H$ of [angular momentum](wikipedia://en/3797203) in [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202): here *E* = $\begin{pmatrix} 0 & 1\\ 0 & 0\end{pmatrix}$, *F* = *E*\* and *H* = \[*E*, *F*\] so that \[*H*, *E*\] = 2*E* and \[*H*, *F*\] = −2*F*;
- using integration of [class functions](wikipedia://en/1001329) over SU(2), identifying the unit quaternions with 3-sphere and [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645) as the volume form: this reduces to integration over the diagonal matrices, i.e. the circle group **T**.

The properties of [matrix coefficients](wikipedia://en/11017946) or [representative functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_function) of the compact group SU(2) (and SO(3)) are well documented as part of the theory of special functions:[^n6][^n7] the [Casimir operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Casimir_operator) $C = H^{2} + 2EF + 2FE$ commutes with the Lie algebras and groups. The operator $\tfrac{1}{4}C$ can be identified with the Laplacian $\Delta$, so that on a matrix coefficient $\varphi$ of $V_{j}$, $\Delta\varphi = (j^{2} + j)\varphi$.

The representative functions $A$ form a non-commutative algebra under [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) with respect to Haar measure $\mu$. The analogue for a finite subgroup of $\Gamma$ of SU(2) is the finite-dimensional group algebra **C**\[$\Gamma$\] From the Clebsch-Gordan rules, the convolution algebra $A$ is isomorphic to a direct sum of $n \times n$ matrices, with $n = 2j + 1$ and $j \ge 0$. The matrix coefficients for each irreducible representation $V_{j}$ form a set of [matrix units](wikipedia://en/1239237). This direct sum decomposition is the [Peter-Weyl theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter-Weyl_theorem). The corresponding result for **C**\[$\Gamma$\] is [Maschke's theorem](wikipedia://en/30876007). The algebra $A$ has eigensubspaces $a(g\zeta) = a(g)$ or $a(g)\zeta$, exhibiting them as direct sum of $V_{j}$, summed over $j$ non-negative integers or positive half-integers – these are examples of [induced representations](wikipedia://en/357452). It allows the computations of branching rules from SU(2) to $\Gamma$, so that $V_{j}$ can be decomposed as direct sums of irreducible representations of $\Gamma$.[^n8][^n9][^n6][^n3]

## History {#History}

[Georg Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Frobenius) derived and listed in 1899 the [character tables](wikipedia://en/911520) of the finite subgroups of [SU(2)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SU%282%29), the double cover of the rotation group [SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%283%29). In 1875, [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887) had already classified these finite "binary" subgroups into the [cyclic groups](wikipedia://en/52327), the [binary dihedral groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_dihedral_group), the [binary tetrahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997586), the [binary octahedral group](wikipedia://en/10997598) and the [binary icosahedral group](wikipedia://en/3195145). Alternative derivations of the character tables were given by [Issai Schur](wikipedia://en/617522) and H. E. Jordan in 1907; further [branching rules](wikipedia://en/880754) and [tensor product formulas](wikipedia://en/43079145) were also determined.[^o31][^o32][^o33]

In a 1929 article on splitting of atoms in crystals, the [physicist](wikipedia://en/22939) [H. Bethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Bethe) first coined the term "double group" (*Doppelgruppe*),[^o34][^o35] a concept that allowed [double-valued or spinor representations](wikipedia://en/348860) of finite subgroups of the rotation group to be regarded as ordinary linear representations of their double covers. In particular, Bethe applied his theory to [relativistic quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/19389837) and [crystallographic point groups](wikipedia://en/663786), where a natural [physical restriction](wikipedia://en/2218040) to 32 point groups occurs. Subsequently, the non-crystallographic icosahedral case has also been investigated more extensively, resulting most recently in groundbreaking advances on [carbon 60](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carbon_60) and [fullerenes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fullerenes) in the 1980s and 90s.[^o36][^o37][^o38] In 1982–1984, there was another breakthrough involving the icosahedral group, this time through [materials scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materials_science) [Dan Shechtman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dan_Shechtman)'s remarkable work on [quasicrystals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystals), for which he was awarded a Nobel Prize in Chemistry in 2011.[^o39][^o40][^o41]

## Applications {#Applications}

### Magnetochemistry {#Magnetochemistry}

In magnetochemistry, the need for a double group arises in a very particular circumstance, namely, in the treatment of the magnetic properties of complexes of a metal ion in whose electronic structure there is a single unpaired electron (or its equivalent, a single vacancy) in a metal ion's d- or f- shell. This occurs, for example, with the elements [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper), [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver) and [gold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gold) in the +2 oxidation state, where there is a single vacancy in the d-electron shell, with [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium)(III) which has a single electron in the 3d shell and with [cerium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerium)(III) which has a single electron in the 4f shell.

In [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the [character](wikipedia://en/293418) $\chi$, for rotation, by an angle $\alpha$, of a wavefunction for half-integer angular momentum is given by

$$\chi^J (\alpha) = \frac{\sin [J+1/2] \alpha } {\sin (1/2) \alpha }$$

where angular momentum is the [vector sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_sum) of spin and orbital momentum, $J = L + S$. This formula applies with angular momentum in general.

In atoms with a single unpaired electron the character for a rotation through an angle of $2\pi + \alpha$ is equal to $-\chi^J (\alpha)$. The change of sign cannot be true for an identity operation in any point group. Therefore, a double group, in which rotation by $2\pi$ is classified as being distinct from the identity operation, is used. A character table for the double group D<sub>4</sub>′ is as follows. The new operation is labelled R in this example. The character table for the point group D<sub>4</sub> is shown for comparison.

The need for a double group occurs, for example, in the treatment of [magnetic properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetochemistry) of 6-coordinate complexes of [copper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copper)(II). The electronic configuration of the central Cu<sup>2+</sup> ion can be written as \[Ar\]3d<sup>9</sup>. It can be said that there is a single vacancy, or hole, in the copper 3d-electron shell, which can contain up to 10 electrons. The ion Cu(H<sub>2</sub>O)<sub>6</sub><sup>2+</sup> is a typical example of a compound with this characteristic.

1. Six-coordinate complexes of the Cu(II) ion, with the generic formula \[CuL<sub>6</sub>\]<sup>2+</sup>, are subject to the [Jahn–Teller effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jahn–Teller_effect) so that the symmetry is reduced from octahedral (point group O<sub>h</sub>) to tetragonal (point group D<sub>4h</sub>). Since d orbitals are centrosymmetric the related atomic term symbols can be classified in the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) D<sub>4</sub>.
1. To a first approximation [spin–orbit coupling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin–orbit_coupling) can be ignored and the magnetic moment is then predicted to be \1.73 [*μ*<sub>B</sub>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bohr_magneton), the so-called spin-only value. However, for a more accurate prediction spin–orbit coupling must be taken into consideration. This means that the relevant quantum number is $J$, where $J = L + S$.
1. When *J* is half-integer, the *character* for a rotation by an angle of $\alpha + 2\pi$ [radians](wikipedia://en/26003) is equal to minus the *character* for rotation by an angle $\alpha$. This cannot be true for an identity in a point group. Consequently, a group must be used in which rotations by $\alpha + 2\pi$ are classed as symmetry operations distinct from rotations by an angle $\alpha$. This group is known as the double group, D<sub>4</sub>′.

With species such as the square-planar complex of the [silver](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Silver)(II) ion \[AgF<sub>4</sub>\]<sup>2−</sup> the relevant double group is also D<sub>4</sub>′; deviations from the spin-only value are greater as the magnitude of spin–orbit coupling is greater for silver(II) than for copper(II).[^o51]

A double group is also used for some compounds of [titanium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titanium) in the +3 oxidation state. Compounds of titanium(III) have a single electron in the 3d shell. The magnetic moments of octahedral complexes with the generic formula \[TiL<sub>6</sub>\]<sup>*n*+</sup> have been found to lie in the range 1.63–1.81 B.M. at room temperature. The double group O′ is used to classify their electronic states.

The [cerium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerium)(III) ion, Ce<sup>3+</sup>, has a single electron in the 4f shell. The magnetic properties of octahedral complexes of this ion are treated using the double group O′.

When a cerium(III) ion is encapsulated in a C<sub>60</sub> cage, the formula of the [endohedral fullerene](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endohedral_fullerene) is written as Ce<sup>3+</sup>@C<sub>60</sub><sup>3−</sup>.[^o53]

### Free radicals {#Free_radicals}

Double groups may be used in connection with [free radicals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_radicals). This has been illustrated for the species CH<sub>3</sub>F<sup>\+</sup> and CH<sub>3</sub>BF<sub>2</sub><sup>\+</sup> which both contain a single unpaired electron.[^o55]
