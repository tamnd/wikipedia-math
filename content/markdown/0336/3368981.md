# Weak formulation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weak formulation"}

**Weak formulations** are tools for the analysis of mathematical [equations](wikipedia://en/9284) that permit the transfer of concepts of [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) to solve problems in other fields such as [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564). In a weak formulation, equations or conditions are no longer required to hold absolutely (and this is not even well defined) and has instead *[weak solutions](wikipedia://en/3555416)* only with respect to certain "test vectors" or "[test functions](wikipedia://en/301505)". In a **strong formulation**, the solution space is constructed such that these equations or conditions are already fulfilled.

:::

The **Lax–Milgram theorem**, named after [Peter Lax](wikipedia://en/486365) and [Arthur Milgram](wikipedia://en/21029542) who proved it in 1954, provides weak formulations for certain systems on [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932).

## General concept {#General_concept}

Let $V$ be a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), let $V'$ be the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of $V$, let $A\colon V \to V'$ be a [linear map](wikipedia://en/18102), and let $f \in V'$. A vector $u \in V$ is a solution of the equation

$$Au = f$$

if and only if for all $v \in V$,

$$(Au)(v) = f(v).$$

A particular choice of $v$ is called a *test vector* (in general) or a *test function* (if $V$ is a function space).

To bring this into the generic form of a weak formulation, find $u\in V$ such that

$$a(u,v) = f(v) \quad \forall v \in V,$$

by defining the [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477)

$$a(u,v) := (Au)(v).$$

## Example 1: linear system of equations {#Example_1:_linear_system_of_equations}

Now, let $V = \mathbb R^n$ and $A:V \to V$ be a [linear mapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_mapping). Then, the weak formulation of the equation

$$Au = f$$

involves finding $u\in V$ such that for all $v \in V$ the following equation holds:

$$\langle Au,v \rangle = \langle f,v \rangle,$$

where $\langle \cdot,\cdot \rangle$ denotes an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product).

Since the inner product is bilinear, it is sufficient to test with [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector), and we get

$$\langle Au,e_i\rangle = \langle f,e_i\rangle, \quad i=1,\ldots,n.$$

Actually, expanding $u = \sum_{j=1}^n u_je_j$, we obtain the [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) form of the equation

$$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{u} = \mathbf{f},$$

where $a_{ij} = \langle Ae_j, e_i\rangle$ and $f_i = \langle f,e_i \rangle$.

The bilinear form associated to this weak formulation is

$$a(u,v) = \mathbf{v}^T\mathbf{A} \mathbf{u}.$$

## Example 2: Poisson's equation {#Example_2:_Poisson's_equation}

To solve [Poisson's equation](wikipedia://en/193735)

$$-\nabla^2 u = f,$$

on a domain $\Omega\subset \mathbb R^d$ with $u=0$ on its [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824), and to specify the solution space $V$ later, one can use the $L^2$-scalar product

$$\langle u,v\rangle = \int_\Omega uv\,dx$$

to derive the weak formulation. Then, testing with [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206) $v$ yields

$$-\int_\Omega ( \nabla^2 u ) v \,dx = \int_\Omega fv \,dx.$$

The left side of this equation can be made more symmetric by [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) using [Green's identity](wikipedia://en/805700) and assuming that $v=0$ on $\partial\Omega$:

$$\int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v \,dx = \int_\Omega f v \,dx.$$

This is what is usually called the weak formulation of [Poisson's equation](wikipedia://en/193735). [Functions](wikipedia://en/185427) in the solution space $V$ must be zero on the boundary, and have square-integrable [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921). The appropriate space to satisfy these requirements is the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) $H^1_0(\Omega)$ of functions with [weak derivatives](wikipedia://en/1411087) in $L^2(\Omega)$ and with zero boundary conditions, so $V = H^1_0(\Omega)$.

The generic form is obtained by assigning

$$a(u,v) = \int_\Omega \nabla u \cdot \nabla v \,dx$$

and

$$f(v) = \int_\Omega f v \,dx.$$

## The Lax–Milgram theorem {#The_Lax–Milgram_theorem}

This is a formulation of the **Lax–Milgram theorem** which relies on properties of the symmetric part of the [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477). It is not the most general form.

Let $V$ be a real [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) and $a( \cdot ,\cdot )$ a [bilinear form](wikipedia://en/449477) on $V$, which is

1. [bounded](wikipedia://en/449477#On_normed_vector_spaces): $|a(u,v)| \le C \|u\| \|v\|\,;$ and
1. [coercive](wikipedia://en/8971904#Coercive_operators_and_forms): $a(u,u) \ge c \|u\|^2\,.$

Then, for any bounded $f\in V'$, there is a unique solution $u\in V$ to the equation

$$a(u,v) = f(v) \quad \forall v \in V$$

and it holds

$$\|u\| \le \frac1c \|f\|_{V'}\,.$$

### Application to example 1 {#Application_to_example_1}

Here, application of the Lax–Milgram theorem is a stronger result than is needed.

- Boundedness: all bilinear forms on $\mathbb{R}^n$ are bounded. In particular, we have $$|a(u,v)| \le \|A\|\,\|u\|\,\|v\|$$
- Coercivity: this actually means that the [real parts](wikipedia://en/5826) of the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of $A$ are not smaller than $c$. Since this implies in particular that no eigenvalue is zero, the system is solvable.

Additionally, this yields the estimate

$$\|u\| \le \frac1c \|f\|,$$

where $c$ is the minimal real part of an eigenvalue of $A$.

### Application to example 2 {#Application_to_example_2}

Here, choose $V = H^1_0(\Omega)$ with the norm

$$\|v\|_V := \|\nabla v\|,$$

where the norm on the right is the $L^2$-norm on $\Omega$ (this provides a true norm on $V$ by the [Poincaré inequality](wikipedia://en/4722074)). But, we see that $|a(u,u)| = \|\nabla u\|^2$ and by the [Cauchy–Schwarz inequality](wikipedia://en/38128), $|a(u,v)| \le \|\nabla u\|\,\|\nabla v\|$.

Therefore, for any $f \in [H^1_0(\Omega)]'$, there is a unique solution $u\in V$ of [Poisson's equation](wikipedia://en/193735) and we have the estimate

$$\|\nabla u\| \le \|f\|_{[H^1_0(\Omega)]'}.$$
