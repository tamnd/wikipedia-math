# Torsor (algebraic geometry)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Torsor"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **torsor** or a **principal bundle** is an analogue of a [principal bundle](wikipedia://en/310950) in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801). Because there are few open sets in [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744), it is more common to consider torsors in [étale topology](wikipedia://en/5707590) or some other flat topologies. The notion also generalizes a [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064) in [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384). Though other notions of torsors are known in more general context (e.g. over [stacks](wikipedia://en/13185596)) this article will focus on torsors over [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754), the original setting where torsors have been thought for. The word *torsor* comes from the French *torseur*. They are indeed widely discussed, for instance, in [Michel Demazure](wikipedia://en/32535595)'s and [Pierre Gabriel](wikipedia://en/30716698)'s famous book *Groupes algébriques, Tome I*.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $\mathcal{T}$ be a [Grothendieck topology](wikipedia://en/12910) and $X$ a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754). Moreover let $G$ be a [group scheme](wikipedia://en/510523) over $X$, a $G$-torsor (or principal $G$-bundle) over $X$ for the topology $\mathcal{T}$ (or simply a $G$-torsor when the topology is clear from the context) is the data of a scheme $P$ and a morphism $f:P\to X$ with a $G$-invariant (right) [action](wikipedia://en/56600253) on $P$ that is locally trivial in $\mathcal{T}$ i.e. there exists a covering $\{ U_i \to X \}$ such that the [base change](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_change_%28scheme_theory%29) $U_i \times_X P$ over $P$ is isomorphic to the trivial torsor $U_i \times G \to U_i$ [^o5]

:::

::: {#o7 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notations"}

## Notations {#Notations}

When $\mathcal{T}$ is the [étale topology](wikipedia://en/5707590) (resp. [fpqc](wikipedia://en/10538912), etc.) instead of *a torsor for the étale topology* we can also say an *étale-torsor* (resp. fpqc-torsor etc.).

:::

## Étale, fpqc and fppf topologies {#Étale,_fpqc_and_fppf_topologies}

Unlike in the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) in many Grothendieck topologies a torsor can be itself a covering. This happens in some of the most common Grothendieck topologies, such as the [fpqc-topology](wikipedia://en/10538912) the [fppf-topology](wikipedia://en/10538912) but also the [étale topology](wikipedia://en/5707590) (and many less famous ones). So let $\mathcal{T}$ be any of those topologies (étale, fpqc, fppf). Let $X$ be a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) and $G$ a [group scheme](wikipedia://en/510523) over $X$. Then $P\to X$ is a $G$-torsor if and only if $P \times_X P$ over $P$ is isomorphic to the trivial torsor $P \times G$ over $P$. In this case we often say that a torsor trivializes itself (as it becomes a trivial torsor when pulled back over itself).

## Correspondence vector bundles-${GL}_n$-torsors {#Correspondence_vector_bundles-{GL}_n-torsors}

Over a given scheme $X$ there is a bijection, between [vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_bundles) over $X$ (i.e. locally free sheaves) and ${GL}_n$-torsors, where $n=rk(V)\in \mathbb{N}$, the rank of $V$. Given $V$ one can take the (representable) sheaf of local isomorphisms $Isom(V,\mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus n})$ which has a structure of a $Isom(\mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus n},\mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus n})$-torsor. It is easy to prove that $Isom(\mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus n},\mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus n})\simeq GL_{n,X}$. We often refer to $Isom(V,\mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus n})$ as the *frame bundle* (or *frame torsor*) associated to $V$ and hence denote it as $Fr(V):=Isom(V,\mathcal{O}_X^{\oplus n})$.

## Trivial torsors and sections {#Trivial_torsors_and_sections}

A $G$-torsor $f:P\to X$ is isomorphic to a trivial torsor if and only if $P(X) = \operatorname{Mor}(X, P)$ is nonempty, i.e. the morphism $f$ admits at least a section $s:X\to P$. Indeed, if there exists a section $s: X \to P$, then $X \times G \to P, (x, g) \mapsto s(x)g$ is an isomorphism. On the other hand if $f:P\to X$ is isomorphic to a trivial $G$-torsor, then $P\simeq X\times G$; the identity lement $1_G\in G$ gives the required section $s=id_X\times 1_G$.

## Examples and basic properties {#Examples_and_basic_properties}

- If $L/K$ is a [finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_extension) [Galois extension](wikipedia://en/457064), then $\operatorname{Spec} L \to \operatorname{Spec} K$ is a $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$-torsor (roughly because the [Galois group](wikipedia://en/12673) acts simply transitively on the roots.) By abuse of notation we have still denoted by $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$ the finite constant group scheme over $K$ associated to the abstract group $\operatorname{Gal}(L/K)$. This fact is a basis for [Galois descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_descent). See [integral extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_extension) for a generalization.
- If $X$ is an [abelian variety](wikipedia://en/247152) over a field $k$ then the multiplication by $n\in \mathbb{N}$, $n_X:X\to X$ is a torsor for the fpqc-topology under the action of the finite $k$-group scheme $ker(n_X)$. That happens for instance when $X$ is an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225).
- An [abelian torsor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_torsor), a $G$-torsor where $G$ is an abelian variety.

## Torsors and cohomology {#Torsors_and_cohomology}

Let $P$ be a $G$-torsor for the étale topology and let $\{ U_i \to X \}$ be a covering trivializing $P$, as in the definition. A trivial torsor admits a section: thus, there are elements $s_i \in P(U_i)$. Fixing such sections $s_i$, we can write uniquely $s_i g_{ij} = s_j$ on $U_{ij}$ with $g_{ij} \in G(U_{ij})$. Different choices of $s_i$ amount to 1-coboundaries in cohomology; that is, the $g_{ij}$ define a cohomology class in the [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357) (more precisely [Čech cohomology](wikipedia://en/856614) with sheaf coefficient) group $H^1(X, G)$.[^o13] A trivial torsor corresponds to the identity element. Conversely, it is easy to see any class in $H^1(X, G)$ defines a $G$-torsor over $X$, unique up to a unique isomorphism.

## The universal torsor of a scheme and the fundamental group scheme {#The_universal_torsor_of_a_scheme_and_the_fundamental_group_scheme}

In this context torsors have to be taken in the [fpqc topology](wikipedia://en/10538912). Let $S$ be a Dedekind scheme (e.g. the spectrum of a field) and $f:X\to S$ a faithfully [flat morphism](wikipedia://en/542520), locally of finite type. Assume $f$ has a section $x\in X(S)$. We say that $X$ has a [fundamental group scheme](wikipedia://en/27509612) $\pi_1(X,x)$ if there exist a pro-finite and flat $\pi_1(X,x)$-torsor $\hat{X}\to X$, called the **universal torsor of $X$**, with a section $\hat{x}\in \hat{X}_x(S)$ such that for any finite $G$-torsor $Y\to X$ with a section $y\in Y_x(S)$ there is a unique morphism of torsors $\hat{X}\to Y$ sending $\hat{x}$ to $y$. Its existence, conjectured by [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042), has been proved by [Madhav V. Nori](wikipedia://en/71398482)[^o15][^o16][^o17] for $S$ the spectrum of a field and by [Marco Antei](wikipedia://en/74584293), Michel Emsalem and Carlo Gasbarri when $S$ is a Dedekind scheme of dimension 1.[^o18][^o19]

## The contracted product {#The_contracted_product}

The contracted product is an operation allowing to build a new torsor from a given one, inflating or deflating its structure with some particular procedure also known as push forward. Though the construction can be presented in a wider generality we are only presenting here the following, easier and very common situation: we are given a right $G$-torsor $f:P\to X$ and a group scheme morphism $u:G\to M$. Then $G$ acts to the left on $M$ via left multiplication: $g\star m:= u(g)m$. We say that two elements $(p,m)\in P\times M$ and $(p',m')\in P\times M$ are equivalent if there exists $g\in G$ such that $(pg^{-1},g\star m)=(p',m')$. The space of orbits $P\times^G M:=\frac{P\times M}{G}$ is called the contracted product of $P$ through $u:G\to M$. Elements are denoted as $p\wedge m$. The contracted product is a scheme and has a structure of a right $M$-torsor when provided with the action $(p\wedge m)*m':=p\wedge (mm')$. Of course all the operations have to be intended functorially and not set theoretically. The name contracted product comes from the French *produit contracté* and in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) it is preferred to its topological equivalent push forward.

## Morphisms of torsors and sub-torsors {#Morphisms_of_torsors_and_sub-torsors}

Let $f:Y\to X$ and $h:T\to X$ be respectively a (right) $G$-torsor and a (right) $H$-torsor in some Grothendieck topology $\mathcal{T}$ where $G$ and $H$ are $X$-group schemes. A morphism (of torsors) from $Y$ to $T$ is a pair of morphisms $(a,b)$ where $a:Y\to T$ is a $X$-morphism and $b:G\to H$ is group-scheme morphism such that $\sigma_H\circ(a\times b)=a\circ \sigma_G$ where $\sigma_G$ and $\sigma_H$ are respectively the action of $G$ on $Y$ and of $H$ on $T$.

In this way $T$ can be proved to be isomorphic to the contracted product $Y\times^GH$. If the morphism $b:G\to H$ is a [closed immersion](wikipedia://en/1623986) then $Y$ is said to be a sub-torsor of $T$. We can also say, inheriting the language from topology, that $T$ admits a [reduction of structure group scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduction_of_structure_group) from $H$ to $G$.

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Structure reduction theorem"}

### Structure reduction theorem {#Structure_reduction_theorem}

An important result by [Vladimir Drinfeld](wikipedia://en/1053747) and [Carlos Simpson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carlos_Simpson) goes as follows: let $X$ be a smooth projective curve over an algebraically closed field $k$, $G$ a semisimple, split and simply connected [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) (then a group scheme) and $P$ a $G$-torsor on $X_R = X \times_{\operatorname{Spec}k} \operatorname{Spec}R$, $R$ being a finitely generated $k$-algebra. Then there is an [étale morphism](wikipedia://en/3022771) $R \to R'$ such that $P \times_{X_R} X_{R'}$ admits a reduction of structure group scheme to a Borel subgroup-scheme of $G$.[^o24][^o25]

:::

## Further remarks {#Further_remarks}

- It is common to consider a torsor for not just a group scheme but more generally for a [group sheaf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_sheaf) (e.g., fppf group sheaf).
- The category of torsors over a fixed base forms a [stack](wikipedia://en/13185596). Conversely, a [prestack](wikipedia://en/39547490) can be [stackified](wikipedia://en/39547490#Stacks_associated_to_prestacks) by taking the category of torsors (over the prestack).
- If $G$ is a connected algebraic group over a finite field $\mathbf{F}_q$, then any $G$-torsor over $\operatorname{Spec} \mathbf{F}_q$ is trivial. ([Lang's theorem](wikipedia://en/42146937).)

## Invariants {#Invariants}

If *P* is a parabolic subgroup of a smooth affine group scheme *G* with connected fibers, then its degree of instability, denoted by $\deg_i(P)$, is the degree of its Lie algebra $\operatorname{Lie}(P)$ as a vector bundle on *X*. The degree of instability of *G* is then $\deg_i(G) = \max \{ \deg_i(P) \mid P \subset G \text{ parabolic subgroups} \}$. If *G* is an algebraic group and *E* is a *G*-torsor, then the degree of instability of *E* is the degree of the [inner form](wikipedia://en/35205225) ${}^E G = \operatorname{Aut}_G(E)$ of *G* induced by *E* (which is a group scheme over *X*); i.e., $\deg_i (E) = \deg_i ({}^E G)$. *E* is said to be *semi-stable* if $\deg_i (E) \le 0$ and is *stable* if $\deg_i (E) < 0$.

## Examples of torsors in applied mathematics {#Examples_of_torsors_in_applied_mathematics}

According to [John Baez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Baez), [energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Energy), [voltage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Voltage), [position](wikipedia://en/1345771) and the phase of a quantum-mechanical [wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction) are all examples of torsors in everyday physics; in each case, only relative comparisons can be measured, but a reference point must be chosen arbitrarily to make absolute values meaningful. However, the comparative values of relative energy, voltage difference, displacements and phase differences are *not* torsors, but can be represented by simpler structures such as real numbers, vectors or angles.[^o29]

In basic calculus, he cites [indefinite integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indefinite_integral) as being examples of torsors.[^o29]
