# Bivector (complex)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bivector"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **bivector** is the vector part of a [biquaternion](wikipedia://en/1207070). For biquaternion *q* = *w* + *x*i + *y*j + *z*k, *w* is called the **biscalar** and *x*i + *y*j + *z*k is its **bivector** part. The coordinates *w*, *x*, *y*, *z* are [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) with [imaginary unit](wikipedia://en/52358) h:

:::

$$x = x_1 + \mathrm{h} x_2,\ y = y_1 + \mathrm{h} y_2,\ z = z_1 + \mathrm{h} z_2, \quad \mathrm{h}^2 = -1 = \mathrm{i}^2 = \mathrm{j}^2 = \mathrm{k}^2 .$$

A bivector may be written as the sum of real and imaginary parts:

$$(x_1 \mathrm{i} + y_1 \mathrm{j} + z_1 \mathrm{k}) + \mathrm{h} (x_2 \mathrm{i} + y_2 \mathrm{j} + z_2 \mathrm{k})$$

where $r_1 = x_1 \mathrm{i} + y_1 \mathrm{j} + z_1 \mathrm{k}$ and $r_2 = x_2 \mathrm{i} + y_2 \mathrm{j} + z_2 \mathrm{k}$ are [vectors](wikipedia://en/32533). Thus the bivector $q = x \mathrm{i} + y \mathrm{j} + z \mathrm{k} = r_1 + \mathrm{h} r_2 .$[^o2]

In the standard linear representation of [biquaternions as 2 × 2 complex matrices](wikipedia://en/1207070#Linear_representation) acting on the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) with [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) {1, h},

$\begin{pmatrix}hv & w+hx\\-w+hx & -hv\end{pmatrix}$ represents bivector *q* = *v*i + *w*j + *x*k.

The [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) of this matrix corresponds to −*q*, so the representation of bivector *q* is a [skew-Hermitian matrix](wikipedia://en/509788).

## History {#History}

[William Rowan Hamilton](wikipedia://en/39817) coined both the terms *vector* and *bivector*. The first term was named with quaternions, and the second about a decade later, as in *Lectures on Quaternions* (1853).[^o2] [Willard Gibbs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willard_Gibbs) included a [a note on bivectors](https://archive.org/details/elementsvectora00gibb/page/76/mode/2up) in his *Elements of Vector Analysis* (1884). He used bivectors for [Edwin Bidwell Wilson](wikipedia://en/11692574)'s textbook *[Vector Analysis](wikipedia://en/12125059)* (1901) based on his lectures.[^o4] For instance, given a bivector *r* = *r*<sub>1</sub> + h*r*<sub>2</sub>, the [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277) for which *r*<sub>1</sub> and *r*<sub>2</sub> are a pair of [conjugate semi-diameters](wikipedia://en/24531598) is called the *directional ellipse of the bivector* *r*.[^o4]

[Ludwik Silberstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ludwik_Silberstein) studied a [complexified](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexified) electromagnetic field *E* + h*B*, where there are three components, each a complex number, known as the [Riemann–Silberstein vector](wikipedia://en/36821570).[^o5][^o6]

A consideration of [biquaternion](wikipedia://en/1207070) representation of special relativity, using [Lie theory](wikipedia://en/1595681), brings bivectors into prominence: The [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) of the [Lorentz group](wikipedia://en/230489) is expressed by bivectors. In particular, if *r*<sub>1</sub> and *r*<sub>2</sub> are [right versors](wikipedia://en/2644621) so that $r_1^2 = -1 = r_2^2$, then the biquaternion curve {exp *θr*<sub>1</sub> : *θ* ∈ **R**} traces over and over the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) in the plane {*x* + *yr*<sub>1</sub> : *x*, *y* ∈ **R**}. Such a circle corresponds to the space rotation parameters of the Lorentz group.

Now (h*r*<sub>2</sub>)<sup>2</sup> = (−1)(−1) = +1, and the biquaternion curve {exp *θ*(h*r*<sub>2</sub>) : *θ* ∈ **R**} is a [unit hyperbola](wikipedia://en/26205122) in the plane {*x* + *yr*<sub>2</sub> : *x*, *y* ∈ **R**}. The spacetime transformations in the Lorentz group that lead to [FitzGerald contractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/FitzGerald_contraction) and [time dilation](wikipedia://en/297839) depend on a [hyperbolic angle](wikipedia://en/1139981) parameter. In the words of Ronald Shaw, "Bivectors are logarithms of Lorentz transformations."[^o7]

The [commutator](wikipedia://en/7193#Ring_theory) product of this Lie algebra is just twice the [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) on **R**<sup>3</sup>, for instance, \[i,j\] = ij − ji = 2k, which is twice i × j. As Shaw wrote in 1970:

Now it is well known that the Lie algebra of the homogeneous Lorentz group can be considered to be that of bivectors under commutation. \[...\] The Lie algebra of bivectors is essentially that of complex 3-vectors, with the Lie product being defined to be the familiar cross product in (complex) 3-dimensional space.[^o8]

"Bivectors \[...\] help describe elliptically polarized homogeneous and inhomogeneous plane waves – one vector for direction of propagation, one for amplitude."[^o9]
