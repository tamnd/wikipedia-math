# Star coloring

*Image: Graph star coloring.svg.* The star chromatic number of the [Dyck graph](wikipedia://en/24041180) is 4, although its chromatic number is 2.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Star coloring"}

In the [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a **star coloring** of a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ is a (proper) [vertex coloring](wikipedia://en/426743) in which every [path](wikipedia://en/638889) on four vertices uses at least three distinct colors. Equivalently, in a star coloring, the [induced subgraphs](wikipedia://en/1655188) formed by the vertices of any two colors has [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28graph_theory%29) that are [star graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_graph).[^n1] Star coloring has been introduced by (Grünbaum 1973).[^n2] The **star chromatic number** $\chi_s(G)$ of $G$ is the fewest colors needed to star color $G$.

:::

## In special classes of graphs {#In_special_classes_of_graphs}

(Grünbaum 1973) observed that the star chromatic number is bounded for [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314).[^n2] More precisely, the star chromatic number of planar graphs is at most 20, and some planar graphs have star chromatic number at least 10.[^n1] More generally, the star chromatic number is bounded on every proper minor closed class.[^n3] This result has been generalized to all low-tree-depth colorings (standard coloring and star coloring being low-tree-depth colorings with respective parameter 1 and 2).[^n4]

For every graph of maximum degree $d,$ the star chromatic number is $O(d^{3/2}).$ There exist graphs for which this bound is close to tight: they have star chromatic number $\Omega(d^{3/2}/\log^{1/2} n).$[^n5]

## Complexity {#Complexity}

It is [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) to determine whether $\chi_s(G) \leq 3$, even when *G* is a graph that is both [planar](wikipedia://en/24314) and [bipartite](wikipedia://en/244431).[^n1] Finding an optimal star coloring is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) even when *G* is a bipartite graph.[^n6]

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

Star coloring is the special case for $q=3$ of $q$-[centered coloring](wikipedia://en/81758007), colorings in which every connected subgraph either uses at least $q$ colors or has at least one color that is used for exactly one vertex. For such a coloring, a connected subgraph with only two colors must be a star, with the vertex of a unique color at its center. There can be no edges between the remaining vertices in the component, because they would form two-vertex connected subgraphs without a uniquely used color.[^o16]

Another generalization of star coloring is the closely related concept of [acyclic coloring](wikipedia://en/690769), where it is required that every cycle uses at least three colors, so the two-color induced subgraphs are [forests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_%28graph_theory%29). If we denote the acyclic chromatic number of a graph $G$ by $\chi_a(G)$, we have that $\chi_a(G) \leq \chi_s(G)$, and in fact every star coloring of $G$ is an acyclic coloring. In the other direction, $\chi_s(G)\le 2\chi_a(G)^2-\chi_a(G),$ so each of the two kinds of chromatic number is bounded if and only if the other one is.[^n1]
