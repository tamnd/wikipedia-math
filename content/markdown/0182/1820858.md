# Fundamental solution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fundamental solution"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **fundamental solution** for a linear [partial differential operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) $L$ is a formulation in the language of [distribution theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) of the older idea of a [Green's function](wikipedia://en/311001) (although unlike Green's functions, fundamental solutions do not address boundary conditions).

:::

In terms of the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021) $\delta(x)$, a fundamental solution $F$ is a solution of the [inhomogeneous equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inhomogeneous_equation)

Here $F$ is *a priori* only assumed to be a [distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29).

This concept has long been utilized for the [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian) in two and three dimensions. It was investigated for all dimensions for the Laplacian by [Marcel Riesz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Riesz).

The existence of a fundamental solution for any operator with [constant coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_coefficients) — the most important case, directly linked to the possibility of using [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) to solve an [arbitrary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arbitrary) [right hand side](wikipedia://en/166352) — was shown by [Bernard Malgrange](wikipedia://en/9170142) and [Leon Ehrenpreis](wikipedia://en/14162704), and a proof is available in [Joel Smoller](wikipedia://en/60496463) (1994).[^o2] In the context of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), fundamental solutions are usually developed via the [Fredholm alternative](wikipedia://en/3050954) and explored in [Fredholm theory](wikipedia://en/4355120).

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider the following differential equation $Lf = \sin(x)$ with

$$L = \frac{d^2}{d x^2} .$$

The fundamental solutions can be obtained by solving $LF = \delta(x)$, explicitly,

$$\frac{d^2}{d x^2} F(x) = \delta(x) \,.$$

Since for the unit step function (also known as the [Heaviside function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heaviside_function)) $H$ we have

$$\frac{d}{d x} H(x) = \delta(x) \,,$$

there is a solution

$$\frac{d}{d x} F(x) = H(x) + C \,.$$

Here $C$ is an arbitrary constant introduced by the integration. For convenience, set $C = -1/2$.

After integrating $\frac{dF}{dx}$ and choosing the new integration constant as zero, one has

$$F(x) = x H(x) - \frac{1}{2}x = \frac{1}{2} |x| ~.$$

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

Once the fundamental solution is found, it is straightforward to find a solution of the original equation, through [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of the fundamental solution and the desired right hand side.

Fundamental solutions also play an important role in the numerical solution of partial differential equations by the [boundary element method](wikipedia://en/1678795).

### Application to the example {#Application_to_the_example}

Consider the operator $L$ and the [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) mentioned in the example,

$$\frac{d^2}{d x^2} f(x) = \sin(x) \,.$$

We can find the solution $f(x)$ of the original equation by [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) (denoted by an asterisk) of the right-hand side $\sin(x)$ with the fundamental solution $F(x) = \frac{1}{2}|x|$:

$$f(x) = (F * \sin)(x) := \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \frac{1}{2}|x - y|\sin(y) \, dy \,.$$

This shows that some care must be taken when working with functions which do not have enough regularity (e.g. compact support, *L*<sup>1</sup> integrability) since, we know that the desired solution is $f(x) = -\sin(x)$, while the above integral diverges for all $x$. The two expressions for $f$ are, however, equal as distributions.

### An example that more clearly works {#An_example_that_more_clearly_works}

$$\frac{d^2}{d x^2} f(x) = I(x) \,,$$

where $I$ is the [characteristic (indicator) function](wikipedia://en/240790) of the unit interval 0,1. In that case, it can be verified that the convolution of $I$ with $F(x) = |x|/2$ is

$$(I * F)(x) = \begin{cases} \frac{1}{2}x^2-\frac{1}{2}x+\frac{1}{4}, & 0 \le x \le 1 \\ |\frac{1}{2}x-\frac{1}{4}|, & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

which is a solution, i.e., has second derivative equal to $I$.

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof that the convolution is a solution"}

### Proof that the convolution is a solution {#Proof_that_the_convolution_is_a_solution}

Denote the [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) of functions $F$ and $g$ as $F \ast g$. Say we are trying to find the solution of $Lf = g(x)$. We want to prove that $F \ast g$ is a solution of the previous equation, i.e. we want to prove that $L(F \ast g) = g$. When applying the differential operator with constant coefficients, $L$, to the convolution, it is known that

$$L(F*g) = (LF)*g \,,$$

provided $L$ has constant coefficients.

If $F$ is the fundamental solution, the right side of the equation reduces to

$$\delta * g~.$$

But since the delta function is an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) for convolution, this is simply $g(x)$. Summing up,

$$L(F*g) = (LF)*g = \delta(x)*g(x) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \delta (x-y) g(y) \, dy = g(x) \,.$$

Therefore, if $F$ is the fundamental solution, the convolution $F \ast g$ is one solution of $Lf = g(x)$. This does not mean that it is the only solution. Several solutions for different initial conditions can be found.

:::

## Fundamental solutions for some partial differential equations {#Fundamental_solutions_for_some_partial_differential_equations}

The following can be obtained by means of [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247):

### Laplace equation {#Laplace_equation}

For the [Laplace equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplace_equation),

$$[-\Delta] \Phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')$$

the fundamental solutions in two and three dimensions, respectively, are

$$\Phi_\textrm{2D}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = -\frac{1}{2\pi}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|,\qquad \Phi_\textrm{3D}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \frac{1}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|} ~.$$

### Screened Poisson equation {#Screened_Poisson_equation}

For the [screened Poisson equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Screened_Poisson_equation),

$$[-\Delta+k^2] \Phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'), \quad k \in \mathbb{R},$$

the fundamental solutions are

$$\Phi_\textrm{2D}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \frac{1}{2\pi}K_0(k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|),\qquad \Phi_\textrm{3D}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \frac{\exp(-k|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|)}{4\pi|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|},$$

where $K_0$ is a [modified Bessel function](wikipedia://en/4700#Modified_Bessel_functions) of the second kind.

In higher dimensions the fundamental solution of the screened Poisson equation is given by the [Bessel potential](wikipedia://en/24696809).

### Biharmonic equation {#Biharmonic_equation}

For the [Biharmonic equation](wikipedia://en/3010562),

$$[-\Delta^2] \Phi(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \delta(\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}')$$

the biharmonic equation has the fundamental solutions

$$\Phi_\textrm{2D}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = -\frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|^2}{8\pi}\ln|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|,\qquad \Phi_\textrm{3D}(\mathbf{x},\mathbf{x}') = \frac{|\mathbf{x}-\mathbf{x}'|}{8\pi} ~.$$

## Signal processing {#Signal_processing}

In [signal processing](wikipedia://en/29324), the analog of the fundamental solution of a differential equation is called the [impulse response](wikipedia://en/545825) of a filter.
