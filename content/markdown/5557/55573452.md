# GRIM test

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="GRIM test"}

The **granularity-related inconsistency of means (GRIM) test** is a simple [statistical test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_test) used to identify inconsistencies in the analysis of data sets. The test relies on the fact that, given a dataset containing *N* [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) values, the [arithmetic mean](wikipedia://en/612) (commonly called simply the average) is restricted to a few possible values: it must always be expressible as a [fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) whose [denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) is *N* and whose [numerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerator) is an integer. If the reported mean does not fit this description, there must be an error somewhere; the preferred term for such errors is "inconsistencies", to emphasise that their origin is, on first discovery, typically unknown. GRIM inconsistencies can result from inadvertent data-entry or [typographical errors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typographical_error), or from [scientific fraud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_fraud). The GRIM test is most useful in fields such as [psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychology) where researchers typically use small [groups](wikipedia://en/1765232) and measurements are often [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). The GRIM test was proposed by Nick Brown (psychologist) and James Heathers in 2016, following increased awareness of the [replication crisis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Replication_crisis) in some fields of science.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Procedure"}

## Procedure {#Procedure}

The GRIM test is straightforward to perform. For each reported mean in a paper, the [sample size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_size) (*N*) is found, and all fractions with denominator *N* are calculated. The mean is then checked against this list (being aware of the fact that values may be [rounded](wikipedia://en/169945) inconsistently: depending on the context, a mean of 1.125 may be reported as 1.12 or 1.13). If the mean is not in this list, it is highlighted as mathematically impossible.[^o5][^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Consider an experiment in which a [fair die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice) is rolled 20 times. Each roll will produce one whole number between 1 and 6, and the [hypothesized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypothesis_testing) [mean value](wikipedia://en/9653) is 3.5. The results of the rolls are then averaged together, and the mean is reported as 3.48. This is close to the expected value, and appears to support the hypothesis. However, a GRIM test reveals that the reported mean is mathematically impossible: the result of dividing any whole number by 20, written to 2 [decimal places](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_place), must be of the form X.X0 or X.X5; it is impossible to divide any integer by 20 and produce a result with an "8" in the second decimal place.[^o9]

:::

## Interpretation and limitations {#Interpretation_and_limitations}

Even if the data fails the GRIM test, this is not automatically a sign of manipulation. Errors in the mean can come about innocently as a result of an error on the part of the tester, typographical errors, calculation and programming mistakes, or improper reporting of the sample size.[^o5] However, it can be a sign that some data has been improperly excluded or that the mean has been illegitimately fudged [in order to make the results appear more significant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-hacking). The location of failures can be indicative of the underlying cause: an isolated impossible mean may be caused by an error, multiple impossible values in the same row of a table indicate a poor [response rate](wikipedia://en/3095637), and multiple impossible values in the same column indicate the given sample size is incorrect. Multiple errors scattered throughout a table can be a sign of deeper problems, and other statistical tests can be used to analyze the suspect data.[^o11]

The GRIM test works best with data sets in which: the sample size is relatively small, the number of subcomponents in [composite measures](wikipedia://en/2988563) is also small, and the mean is reported to multiple decimal places.[^o5] In some cases, a valid mean may appear to fail the test if the input data is not [discretized](wikipedia://en/330017) as expected – for example, if people are asked how many slices of pizza they ate at a buffet, some people may respond with a fraction such as "three and a half" instead of a whole number as expected.[^o11]

::: {#o13 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Brown and Heathers applied the test to 260 articles published in *[Psychological Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psychological_Science)*, *[Journal of Experimental Psychology: General](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Experimental_Psychology:_General)*, and *[Journal of Personality and Social Psychology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Personality_and_Social_Psychology)*. Of these articles, 71 were amenable to GRIM test analysis; 36 of these contained at least one impossible value and 16 contained multiple impossible values.[^o6]

GRIM testing also played a significant role in uncovering errors in publications by [Cornell University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cornell_University)'s Food and Brand Lab under [Brian Wansink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brian_Wansink). GRIM testing revealed that a series of articles on the effect of price on consumption at an all-you-can-eat pizza buffet contained many impossible means – deeper analysis of the raw data revealed that in many cases, sample sizes were incorrectly stated and values incorrectly calculated.[^o2][^o11]
