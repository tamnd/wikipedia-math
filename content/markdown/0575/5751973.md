# Pasch's axiom

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pasch's axiom"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), **Pasch's axiom** is a statement in [plane geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_geometry), used implicitly by [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331), which cannot be derived from the [postulates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid's_postulates) as Euclid gave them.[^o2] Its essential role was discovered by [Moritz Pasch](wikipedia://en/4449765) in 1882.[^o3]

:::

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

*Image: Paschaxiom.svg.* Two lines (in black) meeting a triangle side red and meeting the other sides red and blue

The axiom states that,[^o6]

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=math-theorem name="Pasch's axiom"}

**Pasch's axiom.**

Let *A*, *B*, *C* be three points that do not lie on a [line](wikipedia://en/946975) and let $a$ be a line in the [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) *ABC* which does not meet any of the points *A*, *B*, *C*. If the line $a$ passes through a point of the segment *AB*, it also passes through a point of the segment *AC*, or through a point of segment *BC*.

:::

The fact that segments *AC* and *BC* are not both intersected by the line $a$ is proved in Supplement I,1, which was written by [P. Bernays](wikipedia://en/2556580).[^o8]

A more modern version of this axiom is as follows:[^o9]

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=math-theorem name="A more modern version of Pasch's axiom"}

**A more modern version of Pasch's axiom.**

In the plane, if a line intersects one side of a [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654) *internally* then it intersects precisely one other side *internally* and the third side *externally*, if it does not pass through a vertex of the triangle.

:::

(In case the third side is [parallel](wikipedia://en/664497) to our line, we count an "intersection at infinity" as external.) A more informal version of the axiom is often seen:

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=math-theorem name="A more informal version of Pasch's axiom"}

**A more informal version of Pasch's axiom.**

If a line, not passing through any vertex of a triangle, meets one side of the triangle then it meets another side.

:::

:::

## History {#History}

Pasch published this axiom in 1882,[^o3] and showed that Euclid's axioms were incomplete. The axiom was part of Pasch's approach to introducing the concept of order into plane geometry.

## Equivalences {#Equivalences}

In other treatments of elementary geometry, using different sets of axioms, Pasch's axiom can be proved as a theorem;[^o14] it is a consequence of the plane separation axiom when that is taken as one of the axioms. [Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert) uses Pasch's axiom in [his axiomatic treatment](wikipedia://en/838151) of [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417).[^o15] Given the remaining axioms in Hilbert's system, it can be shown that Pasch's axiom is logically equivalent to the plane separation axiom.[^o16]

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="Hilbert's use of Pasch's axiom"}

## Hilbert's use of Pasch's axiom {#Hilbert's_use_of_Pasch's_axiom}

[David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) uses Pasch's axiom in his book *Foundations of Geometry* which provides an [axiomatic](wikipedia://en/928) basis for Euclidean geometry. Depending upon the edition, it is numbered either II.4 or II.5.[^o15] His statement is given above.

In Hilbert's treatment, this axiom appears in the section concerning axioms of order and is referred to as a *plane axiom of order*. Since he does not phrase the axiom in terms of the sides of a triangle (considered as lines rather than line segments) there is no need to talk about internal and external intersections of the line $a$ with the sides of the triangle *ABC*.

:::

## Caveats {#Caveats}

Pasch's axiom is distinct from [Pasch's theorem](wikipedia://en/1171759) which is a statement about the order of four points on a line. However, in literature there are many instances where Pasch's axiom is referred to as Pasch's theorem. A notable instance of this is (Greenberg 1974, p. 67).

Pasch's axiom should not be confused with the Veblen-Young axiom for [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849),[^o21] which may be stated as:

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=math-theorem name="Veblen-Young axiom for projective geometry"}

**Veblen-Young axiom for projective geometry.**

If a line intersects two sides of a triangle, then it also intersects the third side.

:::

There is no mention of internal and external intersections in the statement of the Veblen-Young axiom which is only concerned with the [incidence property](wikipedia://en/387224) of the lines meeting. In projective geometry the concept of betweeness (required to define internal and external) is not valid and all lines meet (so the issue of parallel lines does not arise).
