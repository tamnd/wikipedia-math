# Proth prime

::: {#o3 .subject cue=lead name="Proth prime"}

A **Proth number** is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) *N* of the form $N = k \times 2^n+1$ where *k* and *n* are positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), *k* is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) and $2^n > k$. A **Proth prime** is a Proth number that is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). They are named after the French mathematician [François Proth](wikipedia://en/12423134).[^o4] The first few Proth primes are

:::

3, 5, 13, 17, 41, 97, 113, 193, 241, 257, 353, 449, 577, 641, 673, 769, 929, 1153, 1217, 1409, 1601, 2113, 2689, 2753, 3137, 3329, 3457, 4481, 4993, 6529, 7297, 7681, 7937, 9473, 9601, 9857 ().

It is still an open question whether an infinite number of Proth primes exist. It was shown in 2022 that the reciprocal sum of Proth primes converges to a real number near 0.747392479, substantially less than the value of 1.093322456 for the reciprocal sum of Proth numbers.[^o2]

The primality of Proth numbers can be tested more easily than many other numbers of similar magnitude.

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A Proth number takes the form $N=k \times 2^n +1$ where *k* and *n* are positive integers, $k$ is odd and $2^n>k$. A Proth prime is a Proth number that is prime.[^o4][^o7] Without the condition that $2^n > k$, all odd integers larger than 1 would be Proth numbers.[^o8]

:::

## Primality testing {#Primality_testing}

The primality of a Proth number can be tested with [Proth's theorem](wikipedia://en/3225985), which states that a Proth number $p$ is prime if and only if there exists an integer $a$ for which

$a^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\equiv -1 \pmod{p}.$[^o7]

This theorem can be used as a probabilistic test of primality, by checking for many random choices of $a$ whether $a^{\frac{p-1}{2}}\equiv -1 \pmod{p}.$ If this fails to hold for several random $a$, then it is very likely that the number $p$ is [composite](wikipedia://en/82289). This test is a [Las Vegas algorithm](wikipedia://en/537519): it never returns a [false positive](wikipedia://en/43286898) but can return a [false negative](wikipedia://en/43286898); in other words, it never reports a composite number as "[probably prime](wikipedia://en/160560)" but can report a prime number as "possibly composite".

In 2008, Sze created a [deterministic algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deterministic_algorithm) that runs in at most $\tilde{O}((k\log k+\log N)(\log N)^2)$ time, where Õ is the [soft-O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soft_O) notation. For typical searches for Proth primes, usually $k$ is either fixed (e.g. 321 Prime Search or Sierpinski Problem) or of order $O(\log N)$ (e.g. [Cullen prime](wikipedia://en/321895) search). In these cases algorithm runs in at most $\tilde{O}((\log N)^3)$, or $O((\log N)^{3+\epsilon})$ time for all $\epsilon>0$. There is also an algorithm that runs in $\tilde{O}((\log N)^{24/7})$ time.[^o4][^o11]

Fermat numbers are a special case of Proth numbers, wherein $k=1$. In such a scenario [Pépin's test](wikipedia://en/3266846) proves that only base $a=3$ need to be checked to deterministically verify or falsify the primality of a Fermat number.

## Large primes {#Large_primes}

, the largest known Proth prime is $10223 \times 2^{31172165} + 1$. It is 9,383,761 digits long.[^o13] It was found by Szabolcs Peter in the [PrimeGrid](wikipedia://en/3255603) volunteer computing project which announced it on 6 November 2016.[^o14] It is also the third largest known non-[Mersenne prime](wikipedia://en/18908).[^o15]

The project [Seventeen or Bust](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seventeen_or_Bust), searching for Proth primes with a certain $t$ to prove that 78557 is the smallest [Sierpinski number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_number) ([Sierpinski problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_number#Sierpiński_problem)), has found 11 large Proth primes by 2007. Similar resolutions to the [prime Sierpiński problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_number#Prime_Sierpiński_problem) and [extended Sierpiński problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_number#Extended_Sierpiński_problem) have yielded several more numbers.

Since divisors of [Fermat numbers](wikipedia://en/91127) $F_n = 2^{2^n} + 1$ are always of the form $k \times 2^{n+2} + 1$, it is customary to determine if a new Proth prime divides a Fermat number.[^o16]

As of January 2025, [PrimeGrid](wikipedia://en/3255603) is the leading computing project for searching for Proth primes. Its main projects include:

- general Proth prime search
- 321 Prime Search (searching for primes of the form $3\times2^n+1$, also called [Thabit primes of the second kind](wikipedia://en/1817053#Properties))
- 27121 Prime Search (searching for primes of the form $27\times2^n+1$ and $121\times2^n+1$)
- [Cullen prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cullen_prime) search (searching for primes of the form $n\times2^n+1$)
- [Sierpinski problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_problem) (and their prime and extended generalizations) – searching for primes of the form $k \times 2^n+1$ where *k* is in this list:

> *k* ∈ {21181, 22699, 24737, 55459, 67607, 79309, 79817, 91549, 99739, 131179, 152267, 156511, 163187, 200749, 209611, 222113, 225931, 227723, 229673, 237019, 238411}

## Proth prime of the second kind {#Proth_prime_of_the_second_kind}

A **Proth number of the second kind** is a [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) *N* of the form $N = k \times 2^n-1$ where *k* and *n* are positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), *k* is [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) and $2^n > k$. A **Proth prime of the second kind** is a Proth number of the second kind that is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666). The first few Proth primes of the second kind are

3, 7, 11, 23, 31, 47, 79, 127, 191, 223, 239, 383, 479, 607, 863, 991, 1087, 1151, 1279, 1471, 1663, 2111, 2239, 2687, 2879, 3391, 3583, 3967, 5119, 5503, 6143, 6271, 6911, 7039, 8191, 8447, 8831, 9343 ().

The largest Proth primes of the second kind can be primality testing use the [Lucas–Lehmer–Riesel test](wikipedia://en/15371861).

As of January 2025, [PrimeGrid](wikipedia://en/3255603) is the leading computing project for searching for Proth primes of the second kind. Its main projects include:

- general Proth prime of the second kind search
- 321 Prime Search (searching for primes of the form $3\times2^n-1$, also called [Thabit primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thabit_prime))
- 27121 Prime Search (searching for primes of the form $27\times2^n-1$ and $121\times2^n-1$)
- [Woodall prime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woodall_prime) search (searching for primes of the form $n\times2^n-1$)
- [Riesel problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riesel_problem) (and their prime and extended generalizations) – searching for primes of the form $k \times 2^n-1$ where *k* is in this list:

> *k* ∈ {23669, 31859, 38473, 46663, 67117, 74699, 81041, 121889, 129007, 143047, 161669, 206231, 215443, 226153, 234343, 245561, 250027, 315929, 319511, 324011, 325123, 327671, 336839, 342847, 344759, 362609, 363343, 364903, 365159, 368411, 371893, 384539, 386801, 397027, 409753, 444637, 470173, 474491, 477583, 485557, 494743 }

::: {#o28 .application cue=heading name="Uses"}

## Uses {#Uses}

Small Proth primes (less than 10<sup>200</sup>) have been used in constructing prime ladders, sequences of prime numbers such that each term is "close" (within about 10<sup>11</sup>) to the previous one. Such ladders have been used to empirically verify prime-related [conjectures](wikipedia://en/6138). For example, [Goldbach's weak conjecture](wikipedia://en/147164) was verified in 2008 up to 8.875 × 10<sup>30</sup> using prime ladders constructed from Proth primes.[^o29] (The conjecture was later proved by [Harald Helfgott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harald_Helfgott).[^o30][^o31])

Also, Proth primes can optimize [den Boer reduction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Den_Boer_reduction) between the [Diffie–Hellman problem](wikipedia://en/3237741) and the [Discrete logarithm problem](wikipedia://en/181334). The prime number 55 × 2<sup>286</sup> + 1 has been used in this way.[^o32]

As Proth primes have simple [binary](wikipedia://en/238686) representations, they have also been used in fast modular reduction without the need for pre-computation, for example by [Microsoft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft).[^o33]
