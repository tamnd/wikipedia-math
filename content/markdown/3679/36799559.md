# Finite subdivision rule

*Image: Hyperbolic orthogonal dodecahedral honeycomb.png.* A perspective projection of a [dodecahedral tessellation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_small_dodecahedral_honeycomb) in **[H<sup>3</sup>](wikipedia://en/1237700)**. Note the recursive structure: each pentagon contains smaller pentagons, which contain smaller pentagons. This is an example of a subdivision rule arising from a finite universe (i.e. a [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257)).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Finite subdivision rule"}

In mathematics, a **finite subdivision rule** is a recursive way of dividing a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) or other two-dimensional shape into smaller and smaller pieces. Subdivision rules in a sense are generalizations of regular geometric [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals). Instead of repeating exactly the same design over and over, they have slight variations in each stage, allowing a richer structure while maintaining the elegant style of fractals.[^o8] Subdivision rules have been used in architecture, biology, and computer science, as well as in the study of [hyperbolic manifolds](wikipedia://en/1589135). [Substitution tilings](wikipedia://en/5558956) are a well-studied type of subdivision rule.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A subdivision rule takes a [tiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tiling_%28mathematics%29) of the plane by polygons and turns it into a new tiling by subdividingeach polygon into smaller polygons. It is **finite**if there are only finitely many ways that every polygon can subdivide. Each way of subdividing a tile is called a **tile type**. Each tile type is represented by a label (usually a letter). Every tile type subdivides into smaller tile types. Each edge also gets subdivided according to finitely many **edge types**. Finite subdivision rules can only subdivide tilings that are made up of polygons labelled by tile types. Such tilings are called **subdivision complexes** for the subdivision rule. Given any subdivision complex for a subdivision rule, we can subdivide it over and over again to get a sequence of tilings.[^n1]

For instance, **binary subdivision** has one tile type and one edge type. All quadrilaterals are type A tiles. [^n2]

*Image: The binary subdivision rule.png.* The binary subdivision rule

Since the only tile type is a quadrilateral, binary subdivision can only subdivide tilings made up of quadrilaterals. This means that the only subdivision complexes are tilings by quadrilaterals. The tiling can be [regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_tiling), but doesn't have to be. The following example starts with a complex made of four quadrilaterals and subdivides it twice.[^o6]

*Image: BinarySubdivisionComplex2.png.* We start with a complex with four quadrilaterals and subdivide twice. All squares are type A tiles.

Certain [rational maps](wikipedia://en/2783119) give rise to finite subdivision rules.[^o11] This includes most [Lattès maps](wikipedia://en/28886032).[^o12]

Every prime, non-split alternating [knot or link complement](wikipedia://en/1350120) has a subdivision rule, with some tiles that do not subdivide, corresponding to the boundary of the link complement.[^o13] The subdivision rules show what the night sky would look like to someone living in a [knot complement](wikipedia://en/1350120); because the universe wraps around itself (i.e. is not [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected)), an observer would see the visible universe repeat itself in an infinite pattern.[^o14] The subdivision rule describes that pattern.[^o13]

## In higher dimensions {#In_higher_dimensions}

Subdivision rules can easily be generalized to other dimensions.[^o16] For instance, [barycentric subdivision](wikipedia://en/583651) is used in all dimensions.[^o17] Also, binary subdivision can be generalized to other dimensions (where [hypercubes](wikipedia://en/39783) get divided by every midplane), as used in certain proofs in higher-dimensional [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396) such as for the [Bolzano–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/31211) and [Heine–Borel theorem](wikipedia://en/59595).[^o18]

## Rigorous definition {#Rigorous_definition}

*Image: FourTorusSubdivision.svg.* A subdivision rule for the four-torus. The faces of the B tiles that subdivide can only touch C tiles, and the faces of the B tiles that don't only touch A tiles.

A finite subdivision rule $R$ consists of the following.[^n1]

1. A finite 2-dimensional [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022) $S_R$, called the **subdivision complex**, with a fixed cell structure such that $S_R$ is the union of its closed 2-cells. We assume that for each closed 2-cell $\tilde{s}$ of $S_R$ there is a CW structure $s$ on a closed 2-disk such that $s$ has at least two vertices, the vertices and edges of $s$ are contained in $\partial s$, and the characteristic map $\psi_s:s\rightarrow S_R$ which maps onto $\tilde{s}$ restricts to a homeomorphism onto each open cell.[^n1]
1. A finite two dimensional CW complex $R(S_R)$, which is a subdivision of $S_R$.[^n1]
1. A continuous cellular map $\phi_R:R(S_R)\rightarrow S_R$ called the **subdivision map**, whose restriction to every open cell is a homeomorphism onto an open cell.[^n1]

Each CW complex $s$ in the definition above (with its given characteristic map $\psi_s$) is called a **tile type**.[^n1]

An $R$-complex for a subdivision rule $R$ is a 2-dimensional CW complex $X$ which is the union of its closed 2-cells, together with a continuous cellular map $f:X\rightarrow S_R$ whose restriction to each open cell is a homeomorphism. We can subdivide $X$ into a complex $R(X)$ by requiring that the induced map $f:R(X)\rightarrow R(S_R)$ restricts to a homeomorphism onto each open cell. $R(X)$ is again an $R$-complex with map $\phi_R \circ f:R(X)\rightarrow S_R$. Repeating this process produces a sequence of subdivided $R$-complexes $R^n(X)$ with maps $\phi_R^n\circ f:R^n(X)\rightarrow S_R$.[^n1]

## Quasi-isometry properties {#Quasi-isometry_properties}

*Image: CantorHistoryGraph.png.* The history graph of the [middle thirds](wikipedia://en/6172) subdivision rule.

Subdivision rules can be used to study the [quasi-isometry](wikipedia://en/12747259) properties of certain spaces.[^o36] Given a subdivision rule $R$ and subdivision complex $X$, one can construct a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) called the "history graph" that records the action of the subdivision rule. The graph consists of the [dual graphs](wikipedia://en/2536864) of every stage $R^n(X)$, together with edges connecting each tile in $R^n(X)$ with its subdivisions in $R^{n+1}(X)$.[^o27]

The quasi-isometry properties of the history graph can be studied using subdivision rules. For instance, the history graph is quasi-isometric to [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) exactly when the subdivision rule is "conformal", as described in the [combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_Riemann_mapping_theorem).[^o36]

## Applications {#Applications}

Islamic [Girih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girih) tiles in Islamic architecture are self-similar tilings that can be modeled with finite subdivision rules.[^o30] In 2007, [Peter J. Lu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lu) of [Harvard University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harvard_University) and Professor [Paul J. Steinhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Steinhardt) of [Princeton University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Princeton_University) published a paper in the journal *[Science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_%28journal%29)* suggesting that girih tilings possessed properties consistent with [self-similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-similar) [fractal](wikipedia://en/10913) [quasicrystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystalline) tilings such as [Penrose tilings](wikipedia://en/26611936) (presentation 1974, predecessor works starting in about 1964) predating them by five centuries.[^o30]

[Subdivision surfaces](wikipedia://en/1079432) in computer graphics use subdivision rules to refine a surface to any given level of precision. These subdivision surfaces (such as the [Catmull-Clark subdivision surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catmull-Clark_subdivision_surface)) take a [polygon mesh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polygon_mesh) (the kind used in 3D animated movies) and refine it to a mesh with more polygons by adding and shifting points according to different recursive formulas. Although many points get shifted in this process, each new mesh is combinatorially a subdivision of the old mesh (meaning that for every edge and vertex of the old mesh, one can identify a corresponding edge and vertex in the new one, plus several more edges and vertices).[^o31]

Subdivision rules were applied by (Cannon Floyd Parry 2000) to the study of large-scale growth patterns of biological organisms.[^o33] They produced a mathematical growth model which demonstrated that some systems determined by simple finite subdivision rules can result in objects (in their example, a tree trunk) whose large-scale form oscillates wildly over time, even though the local subdivision laws remain the same. They also applied their model to the analysis of the growth patterns of rat tissue. They suggested that the "negatively curved" (or non-euclidean) nature of microscopic growth patterns of biological organisms is one of the key reasons why large-scale organisms do not look like crystals or polyhedral shapes but in fact in many cases resemble self-similar [fractals](wikipedia://en/10913). In particular, they suggested that such "negatively curved" local structure is manifested in the highly folded and highly connected nature of the brain and the lung tissue.[^o33]

## Cannon's conjecture {#Cannon's_conjecture}

[Cannon](wikipedia://en/19404886), [Floyd](wikipedia://en/18986817), and [Parry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Walter_Parry) first studied finite subdivision rules as an attempt to prove the following conjecture:[^o36]

Every [Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Leonidovich_Gromov) [hyperbolic group](wikipedia://en/3172817) with a 2-sphere at infinity [acts geometrically](wikipedia://en/12746935) on [hyperbolic 3-space](wikipedia://en/318742).

Here, a geometric action is a cocompact, properly discontinuous action by isometries. This conjecture was partially solved by [Grigori Perelman](wikipedia://en/225266) in his proof of the [geometrization conjecture](wikipedia://en/220642), which states (in part) that any Gromov hyperbolic group that is a 3-manifold group must act geometrically on hyperbolic 3-space. However, it still remains to be shown that a Gromov hyperbolic group with a 2-sphere at infinity is a 3-manifold group.[^o37][^o38][^o39]

Cannon and Swenson showed [^o40] that a hyperbolic group with a 2-sphere at infinity has an associated subdivision rule. If this subdivision rule is conformal in a certain sense, the group will be a 3-manifold group with the geometry of hyperbolic 3-space.[^o36]

:::

::: {#o42 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem"}

## Combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem {#Combinatorial_Riemann_mapping_theorem}

Subdivision rules provide a sequence of tilings of a surface, and tilings offer an intuitive understanding of distance, length, and area (by assigning each tile a length and area of 1). In the limit, the distances that come from these tilings may converge in some sense to an [analytic structure](wikipedia://en/173181) on the surface. The combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem gives necessary and sufficient conditions for this to occur.[^o36]

Its statement needs some background. A tiling $T$ of a ring $R$ (i.e., a closed annulus) gives two invariants, $M_{\sup} (R, T)$ and $m_{\inf} (R ,T)$, called [approximate moduli](wikipedia://en/16819101#Discrete_extremal_length). These are similar to the classical [modulus of a ring](wikipedia://en/16819101#extremal_distance_in_an_annulus). They are defined by the use of "weight functions". A weight function $\rho$ assigns a non-negative number called a "weight" to each tile of $T$. Every path in $R$ can be given a length, defined to be the sum of the weights of all tiles in the path. Define the height $H(\rho)$ of $R$ under $\rho$ to be the infimum of the length of all possible paths connecting the inner boundary of $R$ to the outer boundary. The circumference $C(\rho)$ of $R$ under $\rho$ is the infimum of the length of all possible paths circling the ring (i.e., not nullhomotopic in R). The area $A(\rho)$ of $R$ under $\rho$ is defined to be the sum of the squares of all weights in $R$. Invariant under scaling of the metric, they can be defined as:[^o36]

$$\begin{aligned} M_{\sup} (R,T) &= \sup \frac{H(\rho)^2}{A(\rho)}, \\ m_{\inf} (R,T) &= \inf \frac{A(\rho)}{C(\rho)^2}. \end{aligned}$$

A sequence $T_1,T_2,\ldots$ of tilings is **conformal ($K$)** if mesh approaches 0 and:[^o36]

1. For each ring $R$, the approximate moduli $M_{\sup}(R,T_i)$ and $m_{\inf}(R,T_i)$, for all $i$ sufficiently large, lie in a single interval of the form $[r,Kr]$; and
1. Given a point $x$ in the surface, a neighborhood $N$ of $x$, and an integer $I$, there is a ring $R$ in $N\smallsetminus\{x\}$ separating *x* from the complement of $N$, such that for all large $i$ the approximate moduli of $R$ are all greater than $I$.

::: {#o44 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Statement of theorem"}

### Statement of theorem {#Statement_of_theorem}

If a sequence $T_1,T_2,\ldots$ of tilings of a surface is conformal ($K$) in the above sense, then there is a [conformal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_structure) on the surface and a constant $K'$ depending only on $K$ in which the classical moduli and approximate moduli (from $T_i$ for $i$ sufficiently large) of any given annulus are $K'$-comparable, meaning that they lie in a single interval $[r,K'r]$.[^o36]

:::

### Consequences {#Consequences}

The combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem implies that a group $G$ acts geometrically on $\mathbb{H}^3$ if and only if it is Gromov hyperbolic, it has a sphere at infinity, and the natural subdivision rule on the sphere gives rise to a sequence of tilings that is conformal in the sense above. Thus, Cannon's conjecture would be true if all such subdivision rules were conformal.[^o40]

:::
