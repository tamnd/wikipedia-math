# Trace diagram

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Trace diagram"}

*Image: trace diagram adjugate.png.* A trace diagram representing the [adjugate of a matrix](wikipedia://en/202840).

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **trace diagrams** are a graphical means of performing computations in [linear](wikipedia://en/18422) and [multilinear algebra](wikipedia://en/243946). They can be represented as (slightly modified) [graphs](wikipedia://en/12401) in which some edges are labeled by [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859). The simplest trace diagrams represent the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) and [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of a matrix. Several results in linear algebra, such as [Cramer's Rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cramer's_Rule) and the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547), have simple diagrammatic proofs. They are closely related to [Penrose's graphical notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrose's_graphical_notation).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Let *V* be a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of [dimension](wikipedia://en/32370) *n* over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *F* (with *n*≥2), and let Hom(*V*,*V*) denote the [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) on *V*. An ***n*-trace diagram** is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $\mathcal{D}=(V_1\sqcup V_2\sqcup V_n, E)$, where the sets *V*<sub>*i*</sub> (*i* = 1, 2, *n*) are composed of [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) of [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) *i*, together with the following additional structures:

- a *ciliation* at each vertex in the graph, which is an explicit ordering of the adjacent edges at that vertex;
- a labeling *V*<sub>2</sub> → Hom(*V*,*V*) associating each degree-2 vertex to a linear transformation.

Note that *V*<sub>2</sub> and *V<sub>n</sub>* should be considered as distinct sets in the case *n* = 2. A **framed trace diagram** is a trace diagram together with a partition of the degree-1 vertices *V*<sub>1</sub> into two disjoint ordered collections called the *inputs* and the *outputs*.

The "graph" underlying a trace diagram may have the following special features, which are not always included in the standard definition of a graph:

- [Loops](wikipedia://en/1449083) are permitted (a loop is an edge that connects a vertex to itself).
- Edges that have no vertices are permitted, and are represented by small circles.
- Multiple edges between the same two vertices are permitted.

### Drawing conventions {#Drawing_conventions}

- When trace diagrams are drawn, the ciliation on an *n*-vertex is commonly represented by a small mark between two of the incident edges (in the figure above, a small red dot); the specific ordering of edges follows by proceeding counter-clockwise from this mark.
- The ciliation and labeling at a degree-2 vertex are combined into a single directed node that allows one to differentiate the first edge (the *incoming* edge) from the second edge (the *outgoing* edge).
- Framed diagrams are drawn with *inputs* at the bottom of the diagram and *outputs* at the top of the diagram. In both cases, the ordering corresponds to reading from left to right.

### Correspondence with multilinear functions {#Correspondence_with_multilinear_functions}

Every framed trace diagram corresponds to a [multilinear](wikipedia://en/221525) function between [tensor](wikipedia://en/29965) powers of the vector space *V*. The degree-1 vertices correspond to the inputs and outputs of the function, while the degree-*n* vertices correspond to the generalized [Levi-Civita symbol](wikipedia://en/195982) (which is an [anti-symmetric tensor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anti-symmetric_tensor) related to the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468)). If a diagram has no output strands, its function maps tensor products to a scalar. If there are no degree-1 vertices, the diagram is said to be **closed** and its corresponding function may be identified with a scalar.

By definition, a trace diagram's function is computed using [signed graph](wikipedia://en/2472618) coloring. For each [edge coloring](wikipedia://en/690647) of the graph's edges by *n* labels, so that no two edges adjacent to the same vertex have the same label, one assigns a *weight* based on the labels at the vertices and the labels adjacent to the matrix labels. These weights become the coefficients of the diagram's function.

In practice, a trace diagram's function is typically computed by *decomposing* the diagram into smaller pieces whose functions are known. The overall function can then be computed by re-composing the individual functions.

## Properties {#Properties}

Let *G* be the group of n×n matrices. If a closed trace diagram is labeled by *k* different matrices, it may be interpreted as a function from $G^k$ to an algebra of multilinear functions. This function is [invariant](wikipedia://en/1126638) under simultaneous [conjugation](wikipedia://en/49176), that is, the function corresponding to $(g_1,\ldots,g_k)$ is the same as the function corresponding to $(a g_1 a^{-1}, \ldots, a g_k a^{-1})$ for any invertible $a\in G$.

:::

## Extensions and applications {#Extensions_and_applications}

Trace diagrams may be specialized for particular [Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_groups) by altering the definition slightly. In this context, they are sometimes called [birdtracks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birdtracks), [tensor diagrams](wikipedia://en/2973987), or [Penrose graphical notation](wikipedia://en/2973987).

Trace diagrams have primarily been used by physicists as a tool for studying [Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_groups). The most common applications use [representation theory](wikipedia://en/19378200) to construct [spin networks](wikipedia://en/173196) from trace diagrams. In mathematics, they have been used to study [character varieties](wikipedia://en/14688049).
