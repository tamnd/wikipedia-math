# Real projective space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Real projective space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **real projective space**, denoted $\mathbb{RP}^n$ or $\mathbb{P}_n(\mathbb{R}),$ is the [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) of [lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29) passing through the origin 0 in the [real space](wikipedia://en/1482083) $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}.$ It is a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [smooth manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold) of [dimension](wikipedia://en/8398) $n$, and is a special case $\mathbf{Gr}(1, \mathbb{R}^{n+1})$ of a [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810) space.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Basic properties"}

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

::: {#o5 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

### Construction {#Construction}

Like all [projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135), $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is formed by taking the [quotient](wikipedia://en/237213) of $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus \{0\}$ under the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) $x\sim \lambda x$ for all [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\lambda\neq 0$. For all $x$ in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}\setminus \{0\}$ one can always find a $\lambda$ such that $\lambda x$ has [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) 1. There are precisely two such $\lambda$ differing by sign. Thus, $\mathbb{RP}^n$ has the topology that is obtained by identifying [antipodal points](wikipedia://en/367577) of the unit $n$-[sphere](wikipedia://en/27859), $S^n$, in $\mathbb{R}^{n+1}$.

One can alternatively restrict to the upper hemisphere of $S^n$ and merely identify antipodal points on the bounding equator. This shows that $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is also topologically equivalent to the closed $n$-dimensional disk, $D^n$, with antipodal points on the boundary, $\partial D^n=S^{n-1}$, identified.

:::

### Low-dimensional examples {#Low-dimensional_examples}

- $\mathbb{RP}^1$ is called the [real projective line](wikipedia://en/46379102), which is [topologically](wikipedia://en/29954) equivalent to a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220). Thinking of points of $\mathbb{RP}^1$ as unit-norm complex numbers $z$ up to sign, the diffeomorphism $\mathbb{RP}^1 \to S^1$ is given by $z \mapsto z^2$. Geometrically, a line in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is parameterized by an angle $\theta \in [0, \pi]$ and the endpoints of this closed interval correspond to the same line.
- $\mathbb{RP}^2$ is called the [real projective plane](wikipedia://en/411325). This space cannot be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in $\mathbb{R}^3$. It can however be embedded in $\mathbb{R}^4$ and can be [immersed](wikipedia://en/6993953) in $\mathbb{R}^3$ (see [here](wikipedia://en/61361)). The questions of embeddability and immersibility for projective $n$-space have been well-studied.[^o7]
- $\mathbb{RP}^3$ is [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic) to [SO(3)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SO%283%29), hence admits a group structure; the covering map $S^3\to\mathbb{RP}^3$ is a map of groups Spin(3) → SO(3), where [Spin(3)](wikipedia://en/411231) is a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) that is the [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) of SO(3).

### Topology {#Topology}

The antipodal map on the $n$-sphere (the map sending $x$ to $-x$) generates a [**Z**<sub>2</sub>](wikipedia://en/52327) [group action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on $S^n$. As mentioned above, the orbit space for this action is $\mathbb{RP}^n$. This action is actually a [covering space](wikipedia://en/250323) action giving $S^n$ as a [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_cover_%28topology%29) of $\mathbb{RP}^n$. Since $S^n$ is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) for $n\geq 2$, it also serves as the [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover) in these cases. It follows that the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is $\mathbb{Z}_2$ when $n> 1$. (When $n=1$ the fundamental group is $\mathbb{Z}$ due to the homeomorphism with $S^1$). A generator for the fundamental group is the closed [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) obtained by projecting any curve connecting antipodal points in $S^n$ down to $\mathbb{RP}^n$.

The projective $n$-space is compact, connected, and has a fundamental group isomorphic to the cyclic group of order 2: its [universal covering space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_covering_space) is given by the antipody quotient map from the $n$-sphere, a [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) space. It is a [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_covering_group). The antipode map on $\mathbb{R}^p$ has sign $(-1)^p$, so it is orientation-preserving if and only if $p$ is even. The [orientation character](wikipedia://en/9903344) is thus: the non-trivial loop in $\pi_1(\mathbb{RP}^n)$ acts as $(-1)^{n+1}$ on orientation, so $\mathbb{RP}^n$ is orientable if and only if $n+1$ is even, i.e., $n$ is odd.[^o9]

The projective $n$-space is in fact diffeomorphic to the submanifold of $\mathbb{R}^{(n+1)^2}$ consisting of all symmetric $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ matrices of [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) 1 that are also idempotent linear transformations.

:::

## Geometry of real projective spaces {#Geometry_of_real_projective_spaces}

Real projective space admits a constant positive scalar curvature metric, coming from the double cover by the standard round sphere (the antipodal map is locally an isometry).

For the standard round metric, this has [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) identically 1.

In the standard round metric, the measure of projective space is exactly half the measure of the sphere.

### Smooth structure {#Smooth_structure}

Real projective spaces are [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifold). On *S<sup>n</sup>*, in homogeneous coordinates, (*x*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *x*<sub>*n*+1</sub>), consider the subset *U<sub>i</sub>* with *x<sub>i</sub>* ≠ 0. Each *U<sub>i</sub>* is homeomorphic to the disjoint union of two open unit balls in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> that map to the same subset of **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> and the coordinate transition functions are smooth. This gives **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> a [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1542238).

### Structure as a CW complex {#Structure_as_a_CW_complex}

Real projective space **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> admits the structure of a [CW complex](wikipedia://en/353022) with 1 cell in every dimension.

In homogeneous coordinates (*x*<sub>1</sub> ... *x*<sub>*n*+1</sub>) on *S<sup>n</sup>*, the coordinate neighborhood *U*<sub>1</sub> = {(*x*<sub>1</sub> ... *x*<sub>*n*+1</sub>) | *x*<sub>1</sub> ≠ 0} can be identified with the interior of *n*-disk *D<sup>n</sup>*. When *x<sub>i</sub>* = 0, one has **RP**<sup>*n*−1</sup>. Therefore the *n*−1 skeleton of **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> is **RP**<sup>*n*−1</sup>, and the attaching map *f* : *S*<sup>*n*−1</sup> → **RP**<sup>*n*−1</sup> is the 2-to-1 covering map. One can put

$$\mathbf{RP}^n = \mathbf{RP}^{n-1} \cup_f D^n.$$

Induction shows that **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a CW complex with 1 cell in every dimension up to *n*.

The cells are [Schubert cells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schubert_cell), as on the [flag manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_manifold). That is, take a complete [flag](wikipedia://en/390646) (say the standard flag) 0 = *V*<sub>0</sub> \< *V*<sub>1</sub> \<...\< *V<sub>n</sub>*; then the closed *k*-cell is lines that lie in *V<sub>k</sub>*. Also the open *k*-cell (the interior of the *k*-cell) is lines in $V_{k} \backslash V_{k-1}$ (lines in *V<sub>k</sub>* but not *V*<sub>*k*−1</sub>).

In homogeneous coordinates (with respect to the flag), the cells are

$$\begin{array}{c} [*:0:0:\dots:0] \\ {[}*:*:0:\dots:0] \\ \vdots \\ {[}*:*:*:\dots:*]. \end{array}$$

This is not a regular CW structure, as the attaching maps are 2-to-1. However, its cover is a regular CW structure on the sphere, with 2 cells in every dimension; indeed, the minimal regular CW structure on the sphere.

In light of the smooth structure, the existence of a [Morse function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_function) would show **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a CW complex. One such function is given by, in homogeneous coordinates,

$$g(x_1, \ldots, x_{n+1}) = \sum_{i=1} ^{n+1} i \cdot |x_i|^2.$$

On each neighborhood *U<sub>i</sub>*, *g* has nondegenerate critical point (0,...,1,...,0) where 1 occurs in the *i*-th position with Morse index *i*. This shows **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> is a CW complex with 1 cell in every dimension.

### Tautological bundles {#Tautological_bundles}

Real projective space has a natural [line bundle](wikipedia://en/361366) over it, called the [tautological bundle](wikipedia://en/2913502). More precisely, this is called the tautological subbundle, and there is also a dual *n*-dimensional bundle called the tautological quotient bundle.

## Algebraic topology of real projective spaces {#Algebraic_topology_of_real_projective_spaces}

### Homotopy groups {#Homotopy_groups}

The higher homotopy groups of **RP**<sup>*n*</sup> are exactly the higher homotopy groups of *S<sup>n</sup>*, via the long exact sequence on homotopy associated to a [fibration](wikipedia://en/316826).

Explicitly, the fiber bundle is:

$$\mathbf{Z}_2 \to S^n \to \mathbf{RP}^n.$$

You might also write this as

$$S^0 \to S^n \to \mathbf{RP}^n$$

or

$$O(1) \to S^n \to \mathbf{RP}^n$$

by analogy with [complex projective space](wikipedia://en/762977).

The homotopy groups are:

$$\pi_i (\mathbf{RP}^n) = \begin{cases} 0 & i = 0\\ \mathbf{Z} & i = 1, n = 1\\ \mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z} & i = 1, n > 1\\ \pi_i (S^n) & i > 1, n > 0. \end{cases}$$

### Homology {#Homology}

The cellular chain complex associated to the above CW structure has 1 cell in each dimension 0, ..., *n*. For each dimensional *k*, the boundary maps *d<sub>k</sub>* : δ*D<sup>k</sup>* → **RP**<sup>*k*−1</sup>/**RP**<sup>*k*−2</sup> is the map that collapses the equator on *S*<sup>*k*−1</sup> and then identifies antipodal points. In odd (resp. even) dimensions, this has degree 0 (resp. 2):

$$\deg(d_k) = 1 + (-1)^k.$$

Thus the integral [homology](wikipedia://en/2113969) is

$$H_i(\mathbf{RP}^n) = \begin{cases} \mathbf{Z} & i = 0 \text{ or } i = n \text{ odd,}\\ \mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z} & 0<i<n,\ i\ \text{odd,}\\ 0 & \text{else.} \end{cases}$$

**RP**<sup>*n*</sup> is orientable if and only if *n* is odd, as the above homology calculation shows.

## Infinite real projective space {#Infinite_real_projective_space}

The infinite real projective space is constructed as the [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) or union of the finite projective spaces:

$$\mathbf{RP}^\infty := \lim_n \mathbf{RP}^n.$$

This space is [classifying space of *O*(1)](wikipedia://en/11441746), the first [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954).

The double cover of this space is the infinite sphere $S^\infty$, which is contractible. The infinite projective space is therefore the [Eilenberg–MacLane space](wikipedia://en/1073230) *K*(**Z**<sub>2</sub>, 1).

For each nonnegative integer *q*, the modulo 2 homology group $H_q(\mathbf{RP}^\infty; \mathbf{Z}/2) = \mathbf{Z}/2$.

Its [cohomology ring](wikipedia://en/1069723) [modulo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modulo_%28jargon%29) 2 is

$$H^*(\mathbf{RP}^\infty; \mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}[w_1],$$

where $w_1$ is the first [Stiefel–Whitney class](wikipedia://en/877761): it is the free $\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z}$-algebra on $w_1$, which has degree 1.

Its [cohomology ring](wikipedia://en/1069723) with $\mathbf{Z}$ coefficients is

$$H^*(\mathbf{RP}^{\infty};\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbf{Z}[\alpha]/(2\alpha),$$

where $\alpha$ has degree 2. It can be deduced from the [chain map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_map) between cellular cochain complexes with $\mathbf{Z}$ and $\mathbf{Z}/2$ coefficients, which yield a ring homomorphism

$$H^*(\mathbf{RP}^{\infty};\mathbf{Z}) \rightarrow H^*(\mathbf{RP}^{\infty};\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})$$

injective in positive dimensions, with image the even dimensional part of $H^*(\mathbf{RP}^{\infty};\mathbf{Z}/2\mathbf{Z})$. Alternatively, the result can also be obtained using the [Universal coefficient theorem](wikipedia://en/996107).
