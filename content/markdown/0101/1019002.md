# Dirac measure

*Image: Hasse diagram of powerset of 3.svg.* A diagram showing all possible subsets of a 3-point set $\{x,y,z$}. The Dirac measure $\delta_{x}$ assigns a size of 1 to all sets in the upper-left half of the diagram and 0 to all sets in the lower-right half.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirac measure"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Dirac measure** assigns a size to a set based solely on whether it contains a fixed element *x* or not. It is one way of formalizing the idea of the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021), an important tool in physics and other technical fields.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A **Dirac measure** is a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) $\delta_{x}$ on a set $X$ (with any [$\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma_algebra) of [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of $X$) defined for a given $x \in X$ and any [(measurable) set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable_set) $A \subseteq X$ by

$$\delta_x (A) = 1_A(x)= \begin{cases} 0, & x \not \in A; \\ 1, & x \in A. \end{cases}$$

where $1_{A}$ is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $A$.

The Dirac measure is a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437), and in terms of probability it represents the [almost sure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_sure) outcome $x$ in the [sample space](wikipedia://en/22958) $X$. We can also say that the measure is a single [atom](wikipedia://en/3058373) at $x$. The Dirac measures are the [extreme points](wikipedia://en/454968) of the convex set of probability measures on $X$.

The name is a back-formation from the [Dirac delta function](wikipedia://en/37021); considered as a [Schwartz distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29), for example on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), measures can be taken to be a special kind of distribution. The identity

$$\int_{X} f(y) \, \mathrm{d} \delta_x (y) = f(x),$$

which, in the form

$$\int_X f(y) \delta_x (y) \, \mathrm{d} y = f(x),$$

is often taken to be part of the definition of the "delta function", holds as a theorem of [Lebesgue integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration).

:::

## Properties of the Dirac measure {#Properties_of_the_Dirac_measure}

Let $\delta_{x}$ denote the Dirac measure centred on some fixed point $x$ in some [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(X, \Sigma)$.

- $\delta_{x}$ is a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437), and hence a [finite measure](wikipedia://en/4316185).

Suppose that $(X, T)$ is a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) and that $\Sigma$ is at least as fine as the [Borel $\sigma$-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_sigma_algebra) $\sigma(T)$ on $X$.

- $\delta_{x}$ is a [strictly positive measure](wikipedia://en/6607051) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the topology $T$ is such that $x$ lies within every non-empty open set, e.g. in the case of the [trivial topology](wikipedia://en/382447) $\{\emptyset, X\}$.
- Since $\delta_{x}$ is probability measure, it is also a [locally finite measure](wikipedia://en/6621919).
- If $X$ is a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) topological space with its Borel $\sigma$-algebra, then $\delta_{x}$ satisfies the condition to be an [inner regular measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_regular_measure), since [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) sets such as $\{x\}$ are always [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). Hence, $\delta_{x}$ is also a [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933).
- Assuming that the topology $T$ is fine enough that $\{x\}$ is closed, which is the case in most applications, the [support](wikipedia://en/6591796) of $\delta_{x}$ is $\{x\}$. (Otherwise, $\operatorname{supp}(\delta_{x})$ is the closure of $\{x\}$ in $(X, T)$.) Furthermore, $\delta_{x}$ is the only probability measure whose support is $\{x\}$.
- If $X$ is $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ with its usual $\sigma$-algebra and $n$-dimensional [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) $\lambda^{n}$, then $\delta_{x}$ is a [singular measure](wikipedia://en/4304500) with respect to $\lambda^{n}$: simply decompose $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ as $A = \mathbf{R}^{n} \backslash \{x\}$ and $B = \{x\}$ and observe that $\delta_{x}(A) = \lambda^{n}(B) = 0$.
- The Dirac measure is a [sigma-finite measure](wikipedia://en/4316174).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

A [discrete measure](wikipedia://en/8912350) is similar to the Dirac measure, except that it is concentrated at countably many points instead of a single point. More formally, a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) is called a **discrete measure** (in respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198)) if its [support](wikipedia://en/6591796) is at most a [countable set](wikipedia://en/6026).
