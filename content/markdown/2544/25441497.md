# Stokes' theorem

Vector

*Image: Stokes' Theorem.svg.* An illustration of Stokes' theorem. The surface $\Sigma$ has boundary $\partial\Sigma$ and normal vector $n$. The positive direction around the boundary and the positive normal direction are related by the [right-hand rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule).

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Stokes' theorem"}

**Stokes' theorem**, also known as the **[Kelvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin)–[Stokes](wikipedia://en/212838) theorem**, is a theorem in [vector calculus](wikipedia://en/32640) that relates the behavior of a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) along the edge of a surface to the behavior of its [curl](wikipedia://en/6123) on the surface itself.[^o2] In its usual three-dimensional form, it says that the total circulation of a vector field around a closed curve is equal to the total curl of the field through a surface bounded by that curve.

:::

If $\Sigma$ is an oriented surface with boundary $\partial\Sigma$, Stokes' theorem is commonly written as

$$\oint_{\partial\Sigma} \mathbf F\cdot d\mathbf r = \iint_{\Sigma} (\nabla\times \mathbf F)\cdot \mathbf n\,dS.$$

Here the left side is the [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) of the vector field around the boundary curve, while the right side is the [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) of its curl over the surface. Informally, the theorem says that adding up the local rotation of a vector field across a surface gives the net circulation around its edge.

The theorem is also called the **fundamental theorem for curls**, the **curl theorem**, or the **rotor theorem**.[^o3] It is a special case of the [generalized Stokes theorem](wikipedia://en/29040).[^o4][^o5] In the language of [differential forms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_forms), the vector field corresponds to a 1-form and its curl corresponds to the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782) of that form.

::: {#o7 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

Let $\Sigma$ be a smooth oriented surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$, parametrized by $\mathbf\Sigma(u,v)$, with boundary $\partial \Sigma \equiv \Gamma$, parametrized by $\mathbf\Gamma(t)$. If a vector field

$$\mathbf{F}(x,y,z) = (F_x(x, y, z), F_y(x, y, z), F_z(x, y, z))$$

has continuous first-order [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives) in $\Sigma$, then

$$\iint_\Sigma (\nabla \times \mathbf{F}) \cdot d\mathbf{\Sigma} = \oint_{\partial\Sigma} \mathbf{F} \cdot d\mathbf{\Gamma}$$

with the shorthands for the line element $d\mathbf\Gamma = \frac{d\mathbf\Gamma}{dt}dt$ and the surface element

$$d\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{n} d\Sigma = \left( \frac{\partial \mathbf\Sigma}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial \mathbf\Sigma}{\partial v} \right) du dv$$

where $\mathbf{n}(u,v)$ is the vector orthogonal to the surface at the point $\mathbf\Sigma(u,v)$.

The equality can be expressed in terms of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519), with $\wedge$ being the [wedge product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wedge_product) and $\text{d}$ the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782):

$$\begin{aligned} &\iint_\Sigma \left(\left(\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial y}-\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial z} \right)\,\mathrm{d}y\wedge \mathrm{d}z +\left(\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial z}-\frac{\partial F_z}{\partial x}\right)\, \mathrm{d}z\wedge \mathrm{d}x +\left (\frac{\partial F_y}{\partial x}-\frac{\partial F_x}{\partial y}\right)\, \mathrm{d}x\wedge \mathrm{d}y\right) \\ & = \oint_{\partial\Sigma} \Bigl(F_x\, \mathrm{d}x+F_y\, \mathrm{d}y+F_z\, \mathrm{d}z\Bigr). \end{aligned}$$

The main challenge in a precise statement of Stokes' theorem is in defining the notion of a boundary. Surfaces such as the [Koch snowflake](wikipedia://en/46959), for example, are well-known not to exhibit a Riemann-integrable boundary, and the notion of surface measure in [Lebesgue theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration) cannot be defined for a non-[Lipschitz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_function) surface. One (advanced) technique is to pass to a [weak formulation](wikipedia://en/3368981) and then apply the machinery of [geometric measure theory](wikipedia://en/12169694); for that approach see the [coarea formula](wikipedia://en/17915846). In this article, we instead use a more elementary definition, based on the fact that a boundary can be discerned for full-dimensional subsets of $\mathbb{R}^2$.

A more detailed statement will be given for subsequent discussions. Let $\gamma:[a,b]\to\mathbb{R}^2$ be a [piecewise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise) smooth [Jordan plane curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve): a simple closed curve in the plane. The [Jordan curve theorem](wikipedia://en/267444) implies that $\gamma$ divides $\mathbb{R}^2$ into two components, a [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) one and another that is non-compact. Let $D$ denote the compact part; then $D$ is bounded by $\gamma$. It now suffices to transfer this notion of boundary along a continuous map to our surface in $\mathbb{R}^3$. But we already have such a map: the [parametrization](wikipedia://en/453198) of $\Sigma$.

Suppose $\psi:D\to\mathbb{R}^3$ is [piecewise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise) smooth at the [neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of $D$[^o8] , with $\Sigma=\psi(D)$.[^o9] If $\Gamma$ is the [space curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_curve) defined by $\Gamma(t)=\psi(\gamma(t))$[^o10] then we call $\Gamma$ the boundary of $\Sigma$, written $\partial\Sigma$ [^o11]

With the above notation, if $\mathbf{F}$ is any smooth vector field on $\mathbb{R}^3$, then[^o12][^o13]

$$\oint_{\partial\Sigma} \mathbf{F}\, \cdot\, d{\mathbf{\Gamma}} = \iint_{\Sigma} \nabla\times\mathbf{F}\, \cdot\, d\mathbf{\Sigma}.$$

Here, the "$\cdot$" represents the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) in $\mathbb{R}^3$.

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Special case of a more general theorem"}

### Special case of a more general theorem {#Special_case_of_a_more_general_theorem}

Stokes' theorem can be viewed as a special case of the following identity:[^o16]

$$\oint_{\partial\Sigma} (\mathbf{F}\, \cdot\, d{\mathbf{\Gamma}})\,\mathbf{g} = \iint_{\Sigma}\left[ d\mathbf{\Sigma}\cdot\left( \nabla\times\mathbf{F}- \mathbf{F}\times\nabla\right)\right]\mathbf{g},$$

where $\mathbf{g}$ is any smooth vector or scalar field in $\mathbb{R}^3$. When $\mathbf{g}$ is a uniform scalar field, the standard Stokes' theorem is recovered.

:::

:::

## Proof {#Proof}

The proof of the theorem consists of 4 steps. We assume [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), so what is of concern is how to boil down the three-dimensional complicated problem (Stokes' theorem) to a two-dimensional rudimentary problem (Green's theorem).[^o19] When proving this theorem, mathematicians normally deduce it as a special case of a [more general result](wikipedia://en/29040), which is stated in terms of [differential forms](wikipedia://en/221519), and proved using more sophisticated machinery. While powerful, these techniques require substantial background, so the proof below avoids them, and does not presuppose any knowledge beyond a familiarity with basic vector calculus and linear algebra.[^o13] At the end of this section, a short alternative proof of Stokes' theorem is given, as a corollary of the generalized Stokes' theorem.

::: {#o21 .proof cue=heading name="Elementary proof"}

### Elementary proof {#Elementary_proof}

#### First step of the elementary proof (parametrization of integral) {#First_step_of_the_elementary_proof_(parametrization_of_integral)}

As in *\#Theorem*, we reduce the dimension by using the natural parametrization of the surface. Let $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ and $\gamma$ be as in that section, and note that by change of variables

$$\oint_{\partial\Sigma}{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\Gamma}} = \oint_{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{\gamma}))\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{\gamma})} = \oint_{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{y}))\cdot J_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\,\mathrm{d}\gamma}$$

where $J_{y}\psi$ stands for the [Jacobian matrix](wikipedia://en/195351) of $\psi$ at $y = \gamma(t)$.

Now let $\{\mathbf{e}_{u}, \mathbf{e}_{v}\}$ be an orthonormal basis in the coordinate directions of $\mathbf{R}^{2}$.[^o23]

Recognizing that the columns of $J_{\mathbf{y}}\boldsymbol{\psi}$ are precisely the partial derivatives of $\boldsymbol{\psi}$ at $\mathbf{y}$, we can expand the previous equation in coordinates as

$$\begin{aligned} \oint_{\partial\Sigma}{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\Gamma}} &= \oint_{\gamma}{\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{y}))\cdot J_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\mathbf{e}_u(\mathbf{e}_u\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}) + \mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{y}))\cdot J_{\mathbf{y}}(\boldsymbol{\psi})\mathbf{e}_v(\mathbf{e}_v\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y})} \\ &=\oint_{\gamma}{\left(\left(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{y}))\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial u}(\mathbf{y})\right)\mathbf{e}_u + \left(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(\mathbf{y}))\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial v}(\mathbf{y})\right)\mathbf{e}_v\right)\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{y}} \end{aligned}$$

#### Second step in the elementary proof (defining the pullback) {#Second_step_in_the_elementary_proof_(defining_the_pullback)}

The previous step suggests we define the function

$$\mathbf{P}(u,v) = \left(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(u,v))\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial u}(u,v)\right)\mathbf{e}_u + \left(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(u,v))\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial v}(u,v) \right)\mathbf{e}_v$$

Now, if the scalar value functions $P_u$ and $P_v$ are defined as follows,

$${P_u}(u,v) = \left(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(u,v))\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial u}(u,v)\right)$$

$${P_v}(u,v) =\left(\mathbf{F}(\boldsymbol{\psi}(u,v))\cdot\frac{\partial\boldsymbol{\psi}}{\partial v}(u,v) \right)$$

then,

$$\mathbf{P}(u,v) = {P_u}(u,v) \mathbf{e}_u + {P_v}(u,v) \mathbf{e}_v .$$

This is the [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) of $\mathbf{F}$ along $\boldsymbol{\psi}$, and, by the above, it satisfies

$$\oint_{\partial\Sigma}{\mathbf{F}(\mathbf{x})\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}}=\oint_{\gamma}{\mathbf{P}(\mathbf{y})\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}} =\oint_{\gamma}{( {P_u}(u,v) \mathbf{e}_u + {P_v}(u,v) \mathbf{e}_v)\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}}$$

We have successfully reduced one side of Stokes' theorem to a 2-dimensional formula; we now turn to the other side.

#### Third step of the elementary proof (second equation) {#Third_step_of_the_elementary_proof_(second_equation)}

First, calculate the partial derivatives appearing in [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334), via the [product rule](wikipedia://en/1116115):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial P_u}{\partial v} &= \frac{\partial (\mathbf{F}\circ \boldsymbol{\psi})}{\partial v}\cdot\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u} + (\mathbf{F}\circ \boldsymbol\psi) \cdot\frac{\partial^2 \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v \, \partial u} \\[5pt] \frac{\partial P_v}{\partial u} &= \frac{\partial (\mathbf{F}\circ \boldsymbol{\psi})}{\partial u}\cdot\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v} + (\mathbf{F}\circ \boldsymbol\psi) \cdot\frac{\partial^2 \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u \, \partial v} \end{aligned}$$

Conveniently, the second term vanishes in the difference, by [equality of mixed partials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equality_of_mixed_partials). So,[^o26]

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial P_v}{\partial u} - \frac{\partial P_u}{\partial v} &= \frac{\partial (\mathbf{F}\circ \boldsymbol\psi)}{\partial u}\cdot\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v} - \frac{\partial (\mathbf{F}\circ \boldsymbol\psi)}{\partial v}\cdot\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u} \\[5pt] &= \frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v}\cdot(J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F})\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u} - \frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u}\cdot(J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F})\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v} && \text{(chain rule)}\\[5pt] &= \frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v}\cdot\left(J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F}-{(J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F})}^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u} \end{aligned}$$

But now consider the matrix in that quadratic form—that is, $J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F}-(J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F})^{\mathsf{T}}$. We claim this matrix in fact describes a cross product. Here the superscript "${}^{\mathsf{T}}$" represents the [transposition of matrices](wikipedia://en/173844).

To be precise, let $A=(A_{ij})_{ij}$ be an arbitrary $3 \times 3$ matrix and let

$$\mathbf{a}= \begin{bmatrix}a_1 \\ a_2 \\ a_3\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}A_{32}-A_{23} \\ A_{13}-A_{31} \\ A_{21}-A_{12}\end{bmatrix}$$

Note that $\mathbf{x} \mapsto \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x}$ is linear, so it is determined by its action on basis elements. But by direct calculation

$$\begin{aligned} \left(A-A^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\mathbf{e}_1 &= \begin{bmatrix} 0 \\ a_3 \\ -a_2 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{e}_1\\ \left(A-A^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\mathbf{e}_2 &= \begin{bmatrix} -a_3 \\ 0 \\ a_1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{e}_2\\ \left(A-A^{\mathsf{T}}\right)\mathbf{e}_3 &= \begin{bmatrix} a_2 \\ -a_1 \\ 0 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{e}_3 \end{aligned}$$

Here, $\{\mathbf{e}_{1}, \mathbf{e}_{2}, \mathbf{e}_{3}\}$ represents an orthonormal basis in the coordinate directions of $\mathbb{R}^3$.[^o27]

Thus $(A - A^{T})\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{x}$ for any $\mathbf{x}$.

Substituting ${(J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F})}$ for $A$, we obtain

$$\left({(J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F})} - {(J_{\boldsymbol\psi(u,v)}\mathbf{F})}^{\mathsf{T}} \right) \mathbf{x} =(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\times \mathbf{x}, \quad \text{for all}\, \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{3}$$

We can now recognize the difference of partials as a [(scalar) triple product](wikipedia://en/978650):

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial P_v}{\partial u} - \frac{\partial P_u}{\partial v} &= \frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v}\cdot(\nabla\times\mathbf{F}) \times \frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u} = (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot \frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u} \times \frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v} \end{aligned}$$

On the other hand, the definition of a [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) also includes a triple product—the very same one!

$$\begin{aligned} \iint_\Sigma (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot \, d\mathbf{\Sigma} &=\iint_D {(\nabla\times\mathbf{F})(\boldsymbol\psi(u,v))\cdot\frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial u}(u,v)\times \frac{\partial \boldsymbol\psi}{\partial v}(u,v)\,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v} \end{aligned}$$

So, we obtain

$$\iint_\Sigma (\nabla\times\mathbf{F})\cdot \,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\Sigma } = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial P_v}{\partial u} - \frac{\partial P_u}{\partial v} \right) \,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v$$

::: {#o29 .proof cue=named-heading name="Fourth step of the elementary proof (reduction to Green's theorem)"}

#### Fourth step of the elementary proof (reduction to Green's theorem) {#Fourth_step_of_the_elementary_proof_(reduction_to_Green's_theorem)}

Combining the second and third steps and then applying [Green's theorem](wikipedia://en/243334) completes the proof. Green's theorem asserts the following: for any region D bounded by the Jordans closed curve γ and two scalar-valued smooth functions $P_u(u,v), P_v(u,v)$ defined on D;

$$\oint_{\gamma}{( {P_u}(u,v) \mathbf{e}_u + {P_v}(u,v) \mathbf{e}_v)\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{l}} = \iint_D \left( \frac{\partial P_v}{\partial u} - \frac{\partial P_u}{\partial v} \right) \,\mathrm{d}u\,\mathrm{d}v$$

We can substitute the conclusion of STEP2 into the left-hand side of Green's theorem above, and substitute the conclusion of STEP3 into the right-hand side. [Q.E.D.](wikipedia://en/25228)

:::

### Proof via differential forms {#Proof_via_differential_forms}

The functions $\mathbb{R}^3\to\mathbb{R}^3$ can be identified with the differential 1-forms on $\mathbb{R}^3$ via the map

$$F_x\mathbf{e}_1+F_y\mathbf{e}_2+F_z\mathbf{e}_3 \mapsto F_x\,\mathrm{d}x + F_y\,\mathrm{d}y + F_z\,\mathrm{d}z .$$

Write the differential 1-form associated to a function $\mathbf{F}$ as $\omega_{\mathbf{F}}$. Then one can calculate that

$$\star\omega_{\nabla\times\mathbf{F}}=\mathrm{d}\omega_{\mathbf{F}},$$

where $\star$ is the [Hodge star](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_star) and $\mathrm{d}$ is the [exterior derivative](wikipedia://en/220782). Thus, by generalized Stokes' theorem,[^o32]

$$\oint_{\partial\Sigma}{\mathbf{F}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\gamma}} =\oint_{\partial\Sigma}{\omega_{\mathbf{F}}} =\int_{\Sigma}{\mathrm{d}\omega_{\mathbf{F}}} =\int_{\Sigma}{\star\omega_{\nabla\times\mathbf{F}}} =\iint_{\Sigma}{\nabla\times\mathbf{F}\cdot\,\mathrm{d}\mathbf{\Sigma}}$$

## History {#History}

The theorem first appears in a letter from [William Thomson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Thomson,_1st_Baron_Kelvin) to [George Stokes](wikipedia://en/212838) dated 2 July 1850. Stokes set it as question 8 on the 1854 [Smith's Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smith's_Prize) examination at Cambridge, which led to the result bearing his name.[^o34]

## Applications {#Applications}

### Irrotational fields {#Irrotational_fields}

In this section, we will discuss the irrotational field ([lamellar vector field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamellar_vector_field)) based on Stokes' theorem.

::: {#o38 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition 2-1 (irrotational field)"}

**Definition 2-1 (irrotational field).** A smooth vector field $\mathbf{F}$ on an [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^3$ is *irrotational* ([lamellar vector field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lamellar_vector_field)) if $\nabla \times \mathbf{F} = 0$.

This concept is very fundamental in mechanics; as we'll prove later, if $\mathbf{F}$ is *irrotational* and the domain of $\mathbf{F}$ is [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected), then $\mathbf{F}$ is a [conservative vector field](wikipedia://en/461454).

:::

::: {#o40 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Helmholtz's theorem"}

#### Helmholtz's theorem {#Helmholtz's_theorem}

In this section, we will introduce a theorem that is derived from Stokes' theorem and characterizes vortex-free vector fields. In classical mechanics and fluid dynamics it is called [Helmholtz's theorem](wikipedia://en/477513).

::: {#o41 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem 2-1 (Helmholtz's theorem in fluid dynamics)"}

**Theorem 2-1 (Helmholtz's theorem in fluid dynamics).**[^o4][^o42] Let $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^3$ be an [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) with a lamellar vector field $\mathbf{F}$ and let $c_{0}, c_{1}: [0, 1] \to U$ be piecewise smooth loops. If there is a function $H: [0, 1] \times [0, 1] \to U$ such that

- **\[TLH0\]** $H$ is piecewise smooth,
- **\[TLH1\]** $H(t, 0) = c_{0}(t)$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$,
- **\[TLH2\]** $H(t, 1) = c_{1}(t)$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$,
- **\[TLH3\]** $H(0, s) = H(1, s)$ for all $s \in [0, 1]$.

Then,

$$\int_{c_0} \mathbf{F} \, \mathrm{d}c_0=\int_{c_1} \mathbf{F} \, \mathrm{d}c_1$$

Some textbooks such as Lawrence[^o4] call the relationship between $c_{0}$ and $c_{1}$ stated in theorem 2-1 as "homotopic" and the function $H: [0, 1] \times [0, 1] \to U$ as "homotopy between $c_{0}$ and $c_{1}$". However, "homotopic" or "homotopy" in above-mentioned sense are different (stronger than) [typical definitions](wikipedia://en/212250) of "homotopic" or "homotopy"; the latter omit condition \[TLH3\]. So from now on we refer to homotopy (homotope) in the sense of theorem 2-1 as a *tubular homotopy (resp. tubular-homotopic)*.

:::

::: {#o44 .proof cue=heading name="Proof of Helmholtz's theorem"}

##### Proof of Helmholtz's theorem {#Proof_of_Helmholtz's_theorem}

*Image: Domain of singular 2 cube.svg.* The definitions of $\gamma_{1}, ..., \gamma_{4}$

In what follows, we [abuse notation](wikipedia://en/1698066) and use "$\oplus$" for concatenation of paths in the [fundamental groupoid](wikipedia://en/55264190) and "$\ominus$" for reversing the orientation of a path.

Let $D = [0, 1] \times [0, 1]$, and split $\partial D$ into four line segments $\gamma_{j}$.

$$\begin{aligned} \gamma_1:[0,1] \to D;\quad&\gamma_1(t) = (t, 0) \\ \gamma_2:[0,1] \to D;\quad&\gamma_2(s) = (1, s) \\ \gamma_3:[0,1] \to D;\quad&\gamma_3(t) = (1-t, 1) \\ \gamma_4:[0,1] \to D;\quad&\gamma_4(s) = (0, 1-s) \end{aligned}$$

so that

$$\partial D = \gamma_1 \oplus \gamma_2 \oplus \gamma_3 \oplus \gamma_4$$

By our assumption that $c_{0}$ and $c_{1}$ are piecewise smooth homotopic, there is a piecewise smooth homotopy $H: D \to M$

$$\begin{aligned} \Gamma_i(t) &= H(\gamma_{i}(t)) && i=1, 2, 3, 4 \\ \Gamma(t) &= H(\gamma(t)) =(\Gamma_1 \oplus \Gamma_2 \oplus \Gamma_3 \oplus \Gamma_4)(t) \end{aligned}$$

Let $S$ be the image of $D$ under $H$. That

$$\iint_S \nabla\times\mathbf{F}\, \mathrm{d}S = \oint_\Gamma \mathbf{F}\, \mathrm{d}\Gamma$$

follows immediately from Stokes' theorem. $\mathbf{F}$ is lamellar, so the left side vanishes, i.e.

$$0=\oint_\Gamma \mathbf{F}\, \mathrm{d}\Gamma = \sum_{i=1}^4 \oint_{\Gamma_i} \mathbf{F} \, \mathrm{d}\Gamma$$

As $H$ is tubular(satisfying \[TLH3\]),$\Gamma_2 = \ominus \Gamma_4$ and $\Gamma_2 = \ominus \Gamma_4$. Thus the line integrals along $\Gamma_{2}(s)$ and $\Gamma_{4}(s)$ cancel, leaving

$$0=\oint_{\Gamma_1} \mathbf{F} \, \mathrm{d}\Gamma +\oint_{\Gamma_3} \mathbf{F} \, \mathrm{d}\Gamma$$

On the other hand, $c_{1} = \Gamma_{1}$, $c_3 = \ominus \Gamma_3$, so that the desired equality follows almost immediately.

:::

### Conservative forces {#Conservative_forces}

Above Helmholtz's theorem gives an explanation as to why the work done by a conservative force in changing an object's position is path independent. First, we introduce the Lemma 2-2, which is a corollary of and a special case of Helmholtz's theorem.

::: {#o46 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=inline-head name="Lemma 2-2"}

**Lemma 2-2.**[^o4][^o5] Let $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^3$ be an [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631), with a Lamellar vector field $\mathbf{F}$ and a piecewise smooth loop $c_{0}: [0, 1] \to U$. Fix a point $\mathbf{p} \in U$, if there is a homotopy $H: [0, 1] \times [0, 1] \to U$ such that

- **\[SC0\]** $H$ is *piecewise smooth*,
- **\[SC1\]** $H(t, 0) = c_{0}(t)$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$,
- **\[SC2\]** $H(t, 1) = \mathbf{p}$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$,
- **\[SC3\]** $H(0, s) = H(1, s) = \mathbf{p}$ for all $s \in [0, 1]$.

Then,

$$\int_{c_0} \mathbf{F} \, \mathrm{d}c_0=0$$

Above Lemma 2-2 follows from theorem 2–1. In Lemma 2-2, the existence of $H$ satisfying \[SC0\] to \[SC3\] is crucial;the question is whether such a homotopy can be taken for arbitrary loops. If $U$ is simply connected, such $H$ exists. The definition of [simply connected space](wikipedia://en/523879) follows:

:::

::: {#o47 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition 2-2 (simply connected space)"}

**Definition 2-2 (simply connected space).**[^o4][^o5] Let $M\subseteq\mathbb{R}^n$ be non-empty and [path-connected](wikipedia://en/6233#Path_connectedness). $M$ is called [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) if and only if for any continuous loop, $c: [0, 1] \to M$ there exists a continuous tubular homotopy $H: [0, 1] \times [0, 1] \to M$ from $c$ to a fixed point $p \in c$; that is,

- **\[SC0'\]** $H$ is *continuous*,
- **\[SC1\]** $H(t, 0) = c(t)$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$,
- **\[SC2\]** $H(t, 1) = \mathbf{p}$ for all $t \in [0, 1]$,
- **\[SC3\]** $H(0, s) = H(1, s) = \mathbf{p}$ for all $s \in [0, 1]$.

The claim that "for a conservative force, the work done in changing an object's position is path independent" might seem to follow immediately if the M is simply connected. However, recall that simple-connection only guarantees the existence of a *continuous* homotopy satisfying \[SC1-3\]; we seek a piecewise smooth homotopy satisfying those conditions instead.

Fortunately, the gap in regularity is resolved by the [Whitney's approximation theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whitney's_approximation_theorem).[^o5][^o48] In other words, the possibility of finding a continuous homotopy, but not being able to integrate over it, is actually eliminated with the benefit of higher mathematics. We thus obtain the following theorem.

:::

::: {#o49 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem 2-2"}

**Theorem 2-2.**[^o4][^o5] Let $U\subseteq\mathbb{R}^3$ be [open](wikipedia://en/39358) and simply connected with an irrotational vector field $\mathbf{F}$. For all piecewise smooth loops $c: [0, 1] \to U$

$$\int_{c_0} \mathbf{F} \, \mathrm{d}c_0 = 0$$

### Maxwell's equations {#Maxwell's_equations}

In the physics of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism), Stokes' theorem provides the justification for the equivalence of the differential form of the [Maxwell–Faraday equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell–Faraday_equation) and the [Maxwell–Ampère equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ampère's_circuital_law) and the integral form of these equations. For Faraday's law, Stokes theorem is applied to the electric field, $\mathbf{E}$:

$$\oint_{\partial\Sigma} \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{l}= \iint_\Sigma \mathbf{\nabla}\times \mathbf{E} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S} .$$

For Ampère's law, Stokes' theorem is applied to the magnetic field, $\mathbf{B}$:

$$\oint_{\partial\Sigma} \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d}\boldsymbol{l}= \iint_\Sigma \mathbf{\nabla}\times \mathbf{B} \cdot \mathrm{d} \mathbf{S} .$$
