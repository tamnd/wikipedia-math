# Smooth number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Smooth number"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), an ***n*-smooth** (or ***n*-friable**) **number** is an [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) whose [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factors) are all less than or equal to *n*.[^o2][^o3] For example, a 7-smooth number is a number in which every prime factor is at most 7. Therefore, 49 = 7<sup>2</sup> and 15750 = 2 × 3<sup>2</sup> × 5<sup>3</sup> × 7 are both 7-smooth, while 11 and 702 = 2 × 3<sup>3</sup> × 13 are not 7-smooth. The term seems to have been coined by [Leonard Adleman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Adleman).[^o4] Smooth numbers are especially important in [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432), which relies on factorization of integers. 2-smooth numbers are simply the [powers of 2](wikipedia://en/376948), while 5-smooth numbers are also known as [regular numbers](wikipedia://en/5941535).

:::

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_and_positive_numbers) [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) is called B-**smooth** if none of its [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) are greater than B. For example, 1,620 has prime factorization 2<sup>2</sup> × 3<sup>4</sup> × 5; therefore 1,620 is 5-smooth because none of its prime factors are greater than 5. This definition includes numbers that lack some of the smaller prime factors; for example, both 10 and 12 are 5-smooth, even though they miss out the prime factors 3 and 5, respectively. All 5-smooth numbers are of the form 2<sup>*a*</sup> × 3<sup>*b*</sup> × 5<sup>*c*</sup>, where *a*, *b* and *c* are non-negative integers.

The 3-smooth numbers have also been called "harmonic numbers", although that name has other more widely used meanings, most notably for [the sum of the reciprocals of the natural numbers](wikipedia://en/214729). 5-smooth numbers are also called [**regular numbers**](wikipedia://en/5941535) or Hamming numbers;[^o8] 7-smooth numbers are also called **humble numbers**,[^o9] and sometimes called *highly composite*, although this conflicts with another meaning of [highly composite numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highly_composite_numbers).

Here, note that B itself is not required to appear among the factors of a B-smooth number. If the largest prime factor of a number is p then the number is B-smooth for any B ≥ p. In many scenarios B is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666), but [composite numbers](wikipedia://en/82289) are permitted as well. A number is B-smooth [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it is p-smooth, where p is the largest prime less than or equal to B.

:::

::: {#o12 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

An important practical application of smooth numbers is the [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) (FFT) algorithms (such as the [Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm](wikipedia://en/352702)), which operates by recursively breaking down a problem of a given size *n* into problems the size of its factors. By using *B*-smooth numbers, one ensures that the base cases of this recursion are small primes, for which efficient algorithms exist. (Large prime sizes require less-efficient algorithms such as [Bluestein's FFT algorithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bluestein's_FFT_algorithm).)

Numbers that are 5-smooth or [regular numbers](wikipedia://en/5941535) play a special role in [Babylonian mathematics](wikipedia://en/3861353).[^o13] They are also important in [music theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory) (see [Limit (music)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_%28music%29)),[^o14] and the problem of generating these numbers efficiently has been used as a test problem for [functional programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functional_programming).[^o15]

Smooth numbers have a number of applications to cryptography.[^o16] While most applications center around [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) (e.g. the fastest known [integer factorization](wikipedia://en/15491) algorithms, for example: the [general number field sieve](wikipedia://en/152734)), the [VSH](wikipedia://en/24892363) hash function is another example of a constructive use of smoothness to obtain a [provably secure design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Provably_secure_cryptographic_hash_function).

In music, a [*p*-limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_limit) tuning is the set of [music intervals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_interval) that are ratios of two *p*-smooth numbers.[^o17]

:::

## Distribution {#Distribution}

Let $\Psi(x,y)$ denote the number of *y*-smooth integers less than or equal to *x* (the de Bruijn function).

If the smoothness bound *B* is fixed and small, there is a good estimate for $\Psi(x,B)$:

$$\Psi(x,B) \sim \frac{1}{\pi(B)!} \prod_{p\le B}\frac{\log x}{\log p}.$$

where $\pi(B)$ denotes [the number of primes less than or equal to](wikipedia://en/331826) $B$.

Otherwise, define the parameter *u* as *u* = log *x* / log *y*: that is, *x* = *y*<sup>*u*</sup>. Then,

$$\Psi(x,y) = x\cdot \rho(u) + O\left(\frac{x}{\log y}\right)$$

where $\rho(u)$ is the [Dickman function](wikipedia://en/7635648).

For any *k*, [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) natural numbers will not be *k*-smooth.

If $n=n_1 n_2$ where $n_1$ is $B$-smooth and $n_2$ is not (or is equal to 1), then $n_1$ is called the $B$-smooth part of $n$. The relative size of the $x^{1/u}$-smooth part of a random integer less than or equal to $x$ is known to decay much more slowly than $\rho(u)$.[^o19]

## Powersmooth numbers {#Powersmooth_numbers}

Further, *m* is called *n*-**powersmooth** (or *n*-**ultrafriable**) if all prime *powers* $p^{\nu}$ dividing *m* satisfy:

$$p^{\nu} \leq n.\,$$

For example, 720 (2<sup>4</sup> × 3<sup>2</sup> × 5<sup>1</sup>) is 5-smooth but not 5-powersmooth (because there are several prime powers greater than 5, *e.g.* $3^2 = 9 \nleq 5$ and $2^4 = 16 \nleq 5$). It is 16-powersmooth since its greatest prime factor power is 2<sup>4</sup> = 16. The number is also 17-powersmooth, 18-powersmooth, etc.

Unlike *n*-smooth numbers, for any positive integer *n* there are only finitely many *n*-powersmooth numbers. In fact, the *n*-powersmooth numbers are exactly the positive divisors of “the [least common multiple](wikipedia://en/17961) of 1, 2, 3, …, *n*” , e.g. the 9-powersmooth numbers (also the 10-powersmooth numbers) are exactly the positive divisors of 2520.

*n*-smooth and *n*-powersmooth numbers have applications in number theory, such as in [Pocklington *n* − 1 primality test](wikipedia://en/25850348) and [Morrison *n* + 1 primality test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morrison_n_+_1_primality_test) and [ECPP](wikipedia://en/25904365) and [Pollard's *p* − 1 algorithm](wikipedia://en/578753) and [Williams's *p* + 1 algorithm](wikipedia://en/1916573) and [ECM](wikipedia://en/154212). Such applications are often said to work with "smooth numbers," with no *n* specified; this means the numbers involved must be *n*-powersmooth, for some unspecified small number *n. A*s *n* increases, the performance of the algorithm or method in question degrades rapidly. For example, the [Pohlig–Hellman algorithm](wikipedia://en/1995624) for computing [discrete logarithms](wikipedia://en/181334) has a running time of [O](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asymptotic_notation)(*n*<sup>1/2</sup>)—for [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) of *n*-smooth [order](wikipedia://en/493527).

:::

## Smooth over a set *A* {#Smooth_over_a_set_A}

Moreover, *m* is said to be smooth over a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) *A* if there exists a factorization of *m* where the factors are powers of elements in *A*. For example, since 12 = 4 × 3, 12 is smooth over the sets *A*<sub>1</sub> = {4, 3}, *A*<sub>2</sub> = {2, 3}, and $\mathbb{Z}$, however it would not be smooth over the set *A*<sub>3</sub> = {3, 5}, as 12 contains the factor 4 = 2<sup>2</sup>, and neither 4 nor 2 are in *A*<sub>3</sub>.

Note the set *A* does not have to be a set of prime factors, but it is typically a proper [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the primes as seen in the [factor base](wikipedia://en/8026273) of [Dixon's factorization method](wikipedia://en/1033877) and the [quadratic sieve](wikipedia://en/582340). Likewise, it is what the [general number field sieve](wikipedia://en/152734) uses to build its notion of smoothness, under the [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564) $\varphi:\mathbb{Z}[\theta]\to\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$.[^o23]
