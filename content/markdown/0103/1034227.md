# Insolubilia

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Insolubilia"}

In the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages), variations on the [liar paradox](wikipedia://en/17967) were studied under the name of ***insolubilia*** ("insolubles").

:::

## Overview {#Overview}

Although the liar paradox was well known in [antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_history), interest seems to have lapsed until the twelfth century, when it appears to have been reinvented independently of ancient authors. Medieval interest may have been inspired by a passage in the *[Sophistical Refutations](wikipedia://en/1692779)* of [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308). Although the *Sophistical Refutations* are consistently cited by medieval logicians from the earliest *insolubilia* literature, medieval studies of *insolubilia* go well beyond Aristotle. Other ancient sources which could suggest the liar paradox, including [Saint Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo), [Cicero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cicero), and the quotation of [Epimenides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epimenides) appearing in the [Epistle to Titus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistle_to_Titus), were not cited in discussions of *insolubilia*.

[Adam of Balsham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adam_of_Balsham) mentioned, in passing, some paradoxical statements (dated to 1132), but he did not dwell on the difficulties raised by these statements. [Alexander Neckham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Neckham), writing later in the twelfth century, explicitly recognized the paradoxical nature of *insolubilia*, but did not attempt to resolve the inconsistent implications of the paradox. The first resolution was given by an anonymous author at the end of the twelfth or beginning of the thirteenth century. There was an established literature on the topic by about 1320, when [Thomas Bradwardine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Bradwardine) prefaced his own discussion of *insolubilia* with nine views then current. Interest in *insolubilia* continued throughout the fourteenth century, especially by [Jean Buridan](wikipedia://en/172466).[^o3]

The medieval *insolubilia* literature seems to treat these paradoxes as difficult but not truly "insoluble", and, though interesting and meriting investigation, not central to the study of logic. This may be contrasted with modern studies of [self-referential paradoxes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_self–referential_paradoxes) such as [Russell's paradox](wikipedia://en/46095), in which the problems are seen as fundamentally insoluble, and central to the foundations of logic.
