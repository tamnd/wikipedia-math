# Fermat (computer algebra system)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fermat"}

**Fermat** (named after [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966)) is a [computer algebra system](wikipedia://en/54427) developed by Prof. Robert H. Lewis of [Fordham University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fordham_University). It can work on [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) (of arbitrary size), [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024), [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), modular numbers, [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) elements, multivariable [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000), [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210), or polynomials [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) other polynomials. The main areas of application are multivariate rational function arithmetic and [matrix algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_algebra) over [rings](wikipedia://en/48404) of multivariate polynomials or rational functions. Fermat does not do simplification of [transcendental functions](wikipedia://en/297021) or [symbolic integration](wikipedia://en/1652672).

:::

A session with Fermat usually starts by choosing rational or modular "mode" to establish the ground [field](wikipedia://en/10603) (or ground ring) $F$ as [$\mathbb{Z}$](wikipedia://en/14563) or $\mathbb{Z}/n$.[^o3] On top of this may be attached any number of symbolic variables $t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n,$ thereby creating the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $F[t_1, t_2, \dots, t_n]$ and its quotient field. Further, some polynomials $p, q, \dots$ involving some of the $t_i$ can be chosen to mod out with, creating the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $F(t_1, t_2, \dots)/(p, q, \dots).$ Finally, it is possible to allow [Laurent polynomials](wikipedia://en/61351), those with negative as well as positive exponents. Once the computational ring is established in this way, all computations are of elements of this ring. The computational ring can be changed later in the session.

The [polynomial gcd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatest_common_divisor_of_two_polynomials) procedures, which call each other in a highly recursive manner, are about 7000 lines of code.

Fermat has extensive built-in primitives for array and matrix manipulations, such as [submatrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submatrix), [sparse matrix](wikipedia://en/341015), [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468), [normalize](wikipedia://en/170366), column reduce, [row echelon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Row_echelon), [Smith normal form](wikipedia://en/1106564), and [matrix inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_inverse). It is consistently faster than some well known computer algebra systems, especially in [multivariate polynomial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multivariate_polynomial) gcd. It is also space efficient.

The basic data item in Fermat is a multivariate rational function or polynomial. The numerator and denominator are polynomials with no common factor. Polynomials are implemented recursively as general linked lists, unlike some systems that implement polynomials as lists of [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416). To implement (most) finite fields, the user finds an irreducible monic polynomial in a symbolic variable, say $p(t_1),$ and commands Fermat to mod out by it. This may be continued recursively, $q(t_2, t_1),$ etc. Low level data structures are set up to facilitate arithmetic and gcd over this newly created [ground field](wikipedia://en/27161748). Two special fields, $GF(2^{8})$ and $GF(2^{16}),$ are more efficiently implemented at the bit level. Since 2024, factoring of multivariate polynomials can be done, though this is not a key feature.

## History {#History}

With [Windows 10](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Windows_10), and thanks to Bogdan Radu, it has been possible since May 2021 to run Fermat Linux natively on Windows.[^o5]

Fermat was last updated on 20 September 2025 (Mac and Linux; latest dedicated Windows version: 1 November 2011).

In an earlier version, called FFermat (Float Fermat), the basic number type is [floating point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floating_point) numbers of 18 digits. That version allows for numerical computing techniques, has extensive graphics capabilities, no sophisticated polynomial gcd algorithms, and is available only for Mac OS 9.

Fermat was originally written in [Pascal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pascal_%28programming_language%29) for a [DEC](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Equipment_Corporation) [VAX](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/VAX), then for the [classic Mac OS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classic_Mac_OS) during 1985–1996. It was [ported](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porting) to [Microsoft Windows](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microsoft_Windows) in 1998. In 2003 it was translated into [C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C_%28programming_language%29) and ported to [Linux](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linux) (Intel machines) and [Unix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unix) (Sparc/Sun). It is about 120,000 lines of C code.

The FFermat and (old) Windows Fermat Pascal [source code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Source_code) have been made available to the public under a restrictive license.

The manual was extensively revised and updated on 25 July 2011 (small revision in June 2016, another revision on 25 March 2020; latest small revision June 2024).

Fermat is used extensively by physicists working on Feynman integral reduction. See the references to FIRE 6.5 and to Kira.
