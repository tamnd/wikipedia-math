# Logical NOR

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Logical NOR"}

Peirce arrow

In [Boolean logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_logic), **logical NOR**,**[^o3]** **non-disjunction**, or **joint denial[^o3]** is a truth-functional operator which produces a result that is the negation of [logical or](wikipedia://en/7962). That is, a sentence of the form (*p* NOR *q*) is true precisely when neither *p* nor *q* is true—i.e. when both *p* and *q* are *false*. It is logically equivalent to $\neg(p \lor q)$ and $\neg p \land \neg q$, where the symbol $\neg$ signifies logical [negation](wikipedia://en/161019), $\lor$ signifies [OR](wikipedia://en/7962), and $\land$ signifies [AND](wikipedia://en/18152).

:::

Non-disjunction is usually denoted as $\downarrow$ or $\overline{\vee}$ or $X$ (prefix) or $\operatorname{NOR}$.

As with its [dual](wikipedia://en/609737), the [NAND operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NAND_operator) (also known as the [Sheffer stroke](wikipedia://en/29388)—symbolized as either $\uparrow$, $\mid$ or $/$), NOR can be used by itself, without any other logical operator, to constitute a logical [formal system](wikipedia://en/396102) (making NOR [functionally complete](wikipedia://en/5279259)).

The [computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) used in the spacecraft that first carried humans to the [moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon), the [Apollo Guidance Computer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_Guidance_Computer), was constructed entirely using NOR gates with three inputs.[^o29]

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The **NOR operation** is a [logical operation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_operation) on two [logical values](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_value), typically the values of two [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094), that produces a value of *true* if and only if both operands are false. In other words, it produces a value of *false* if and only if at least one operand is true.

## Alternative notations and names {#Alternative_notations_and_names}

[Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) is the first to show the functional completeness of non-disjunction while he doesn't publish his result.[^o9][^o10] Peirce used $\overline{\curlywedge}$ for [non-conjunction](wikipedia://en/29388) and $\curlywedge$ for non-disjunction (in fact, what Peirce himself used is $\curlywedge$ and he didn't introduce $\overline{\curlywedge}$ while Peirce's editors made such disambiguated use).[^o10] Peirce called $\curlywedge$ the **ampheck** (from Ancient Greek ἀμφήκης, grc, "cutting both ways").[^o10]

In 1911, Edward Stamm was the first to publish a description of both non-conjunction (using $\sim$, the Stamm hook), and non-disjunction (using $*$, the Stamm star), and showed their functional completeness.[^o28][^o11] Note that most uses in logical notation of $\sim$ use this for negation.

In 1913, [Sheffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Maurice_Sheffer) described non-disjunction and showed its functional completeness. Sheffer used $\mid$ for non-conjunction, and $\wedge$ for non-disjunction.

In 1935, Donald L. Webb described non-disjunction for $n$-valued logic, and use $\mid$ for the operator. So some people call it **Webb operator**,[^o25] **Webb operation**[^o27] or **Webb function**.[^o26]

In 1940, [Quine](wikipedia://en/33833) also described non-disjunction and use $\downarrow$ for the operator.[^o12] So some people call the operator **Peirce arrow** or **Quine dagger**.

In 1944, [Church](wikipedia://en/49731) also described non-disjunction and use $\overline{\vee}$ for the operator.[^o13]

In 1954, [Bocheński](wikipedia://en/1029376) used $X$ in $Xpq$ for non-disjunction in [Polish notation](wikipedia://en/25056).[^o14]

[APL](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/APL_%28programming_language%29) uses a glyph ⍱ that combines a ∨ with a ~.[^o15]

## Properties {#Properties}

NOR is commutative but not associative, which means that $P \downarrow Q \leftrightarrow Q \downarrow P$ but $(P \downarrow Q) \downarrow R \not\leftrightarrow P \downarrow (Q \downarrow R)$.[^o18]

### Functional completeness {#Functional_completeness}

The logical NOR, taken by itself, is a [functionally complete](wikipedia://en/5279259) set of connectives.[^o20] This can be proved by first showing, with a [truth table](wikipedia://en/25512250), that $\neg A$ is truth-functionally equivalent to $A \downarrow A$.[^o21] Then, since $A \downarrow B$ is truth-functionally equivalent to $\neg (A \lor B)$,[^o21] and $A \lor B$ is equivalent to $\neg(\neg A \land \neg B)$,[^o21] the logical NOR suffices to define the set of connectives $\{\land, \lor, \neg\}$,[^o21] which is shown to be truth-functionally complete by the [Disjunctive Normal Form Theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunctive_Normal_Form_Theorem).[^o21]

This may also be seen from the fact that Logical NOR does not possess any of the five qualities (truth-preserving, false-preserving, [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear#Boolean_functions), [monotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonic#In_Boolean_functions), self-dual) required to be absent from at least one member of a set of [functionally complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Functionally_complete) operators.

:::

## Other Boolean operations in terms of the logical NOR {#Other_Boolean_operations_in_terms_of_the_logical_NOR}

NOR has the interesting feature that all other logical operators can be expressed by interlaced NOR operations. The [logical NAND](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_NAND) operator also has this ability.

Expressed in terms of NOR $\downarrow$, the usual operators of propositional logic are:
