# Spectrum of a matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Spectrum of a matrix"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **spectrum** of a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of its [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue).[^o2][^o3][^o4] (More precisely, it is its [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) of eigenvalues, where each eigenvalue comes with an associated multiplicity, and two spectra are only considered to be equal if each eigenvalue has the same multiplicity in each spectrum.) More generally, if $T\colon V \to V$ is a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) on any [finite-dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370), its spectrum is the set of scalars $\lambda$ such that $T-\lambda I$ is not [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible_function). The [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the matrix equals the product of its eigenvalues. Similarly, the [trace](wikipedia://en/43270) of the matrix equals the sum of its eigenvalues.[^o5][^o6][^o7] From this point of view, we can define the [pseudo-determinant](wikipedia://en/22519386) for a [singular matrix](wikipedia://en/245528) to be the product of its nonzero eigenvalues (the density of [multivariate normal distribution](wikipedia://en/50347) will need this quantity).

:::

In many applications, such as [PageRank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PageRank), one is interested in the dominant eigenvalue, i.e. that which is largest in [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991). In other applications, the smallest eigenvalue is important, but in general, the whole spectrum provides valuable information about a matrix.

::: {#o9 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $V'$ be a finite-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) over some [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$ and suppose $T:V\to V$ is a linear map. The *spectrum* of $T$, denoted $\operatorname{Spec}{T}$ or σ<sub>*T*</sub>, is the [multiset](wikipedia://en/305303) of [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of *T*. Thus the elements of the spectrum are precisely the eigenvalues of *T*, and the multiplicity of an eigenvalue *λ* in the spectrum equals the dimension of the [generalized eigenspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_eigenspace) of *T* for *λ* (also called the [algebraic multiplicity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_multiplicity) of *λ*).

Now, fix a [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) *B* of *V* over *K* and suppose *M* ∈ Mat<sub>*K*</sub>(*V*) is a matrix. Define the linear map *T* : *V* → *V* pointwise by *Tx* = *Mx*, where on the right-hand side *x* is interpreted as a column vector and *M* acts on *x* by [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280). We now say that *x* ∈ *V* is an [eigenvector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of *M* if *x* is an eigenvector of *T*. Similarly, λ ∈ *K* is an eigenvalue of *M* if it is an eigenvalue of *T*, and with the same multiplicity, and the spectrum of *M*, written σ<sub>*M*</sub>, is the multiset of all such eigenvalues.

:::

## Related notions {#Related_notions}

The [eigendecomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigendecomposition) (or spectral decomposition) of a [diagonalizable matrix](wikipedia://en/246325) is a [decomposition](wikipedia://en/253873) of a diagonalizable matrix into a specific canonical form whereby the matrix is represented in terms of its eigenvalues and eigenvectors.

The [spectral radius](wikipedia://en/386169) of a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) is the largest absolute value of its eigenvalues. In [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713), the spectral radius of a [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) is the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of the absolute values of the elements in the spectrum of that operator.
