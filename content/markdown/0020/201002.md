# Magnetostriction

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Magnetostriction"}

**Magnetostriction** is a property of [magnetic materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnet) that causes them to change their shape or dimensions during the process of [magnetization](wikipedia://en/2404348). The variation of materials' magnetization due to the applied [magnetic field](wikipedia://en/36563) changes the magnetostrictive strain until reaching its saturation value, λ. The effect was first identified in 1842 by [James Joule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Joule) when observing a sample of [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron).[^o2]

:::

Magnetostriction applies to magnetic fields, while [electrostriction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrostriction) applies to electric fields.

Magnetostriction causes energy loss due to frictional heating in susceptible ferromagnetic cores, and is also responsible for the low-pitched humming sound that can be heard coming from transformers, where alternating currents produce a changing magnetic field.[^o3]

## Explanation {#Explanation}

Internally, ferromagnetic materials have a structure that is divided into *[domains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain)*, each of which is a region of uniform magnetization. When a magnetic field is applied, the boundaries between the domains shift and the domains rotate; both of these effects cause a change in the material's dimensions. The reason that a change in the magnetic domains of a material results in a change in the material's dimensions is a consequence of [magnetocrystalline anisotropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetocrystalline_anisotropy); it takes more energy to magnetize a crystalline material in one direction than in another. If a magnetic field is applied to the material at an angle to an easy axis of magnetization, the material will tend to rearrange its structure so that an easy axis is aligned with the field to minimize the [free energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_free_energy) of the system. Since different crystal directions are associated with different lengths, this effect induces a [strain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28mechanics%29) in the material.[^o5]

The reciprocal effect, the change of the magnetic susceptibility (response to an applied field) of a material when subjected to a mechanical stress, is called the [Villari effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villari_effect). Two other effects are related to magnetostriction: the [Matteucci effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matteucci_effect) is the creation of a helical anisotropy of the susceptibility of a magnetostrictive material when subjected to a [torque](wikipedia://en/30400) and the [Wiedemann effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiedemann_effect) is the twisting of these materials when a helical magnetic field is applied to them.

The Villari reversal is the change in sign of the magnetostriction of [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron) from positive to negative when exposed to magnetic fields of approximately 40 [kA/m](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amperes_per_meter).

On magnetization, a magnetic material undergoes changes in volume which are small: of the order 10<sup>−6</sup>.

## Magnetostrictive hysteresis loop {#Magnetostrictive_hysteresis_loop}

*Image: Magnetostrictive hysteresis loop of Mn-Zn ferrite.png.* Magnetostrictive hysteresis loop of Mn-Zn ferrite for power applications measured by semiconductor strain gauges

Like [flux density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flux_density), the magnetostriction also exhibits [hysteresis](wikipedia://en/147003) versus the strength of the magnetizing field. The shape of this hysteresis loop (called "dragonfly loop") can be reproduced using the [Jiles-Atherton model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jiles-Atherton_model).[^o7]

## Magnetostrictive materials {#Magnetostrictive_materials}

*Image: Magnetostrictive transducer.PNG.* Cut-away of a transducer comprising: magnetostrictive material (inside), magnetising coil, and magnetic enclosure completing the magnetic circuit (outside)

Magnetostrictive materials can convert magnetic energy into [kinetic energy](wikipedia://en/17327), or the reverse, and are used to build [actuators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Actuator) and [sensors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sensor). The property can be quantified by the magnetostrictive coefficient, λ, which may be positive or negative and is defined as the fractional change in length as the magnetization of the material increases from zero to the [saturation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturation_%28magnetic%29) value. The effect is responsible for the familiar "[electric hum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mains_hum)" (Mains hum 60 Hz.ogg) which can be heard near [transformers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transformer) and high power electrical devices.

Cobalt exhibits the largest room-temperature magnetostriction of a pure element at 60 microstrains. Among alloys, the highest known magnetostriction is exhibited by [Terfenol-D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terfenol-D), (Ter for [terbium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terbium), Fe for [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron), NOL for [Naval Ordnance Laboratory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Ordnance_Laboratory), and D for [dysprosium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dysprosium)). Terfenol-D, Tb\_{*x*}Dy\_{1−*x*}Fe2, exhibits about 2,000 microstrains in a field of 160 kA/m (2 kOe) at room temperature and is the most commonly used engineering magnetostrictive material.[^o9] [Galfenol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galfenol), Fe\_{*x*}Ga\_{1−*x*}, and [Alfer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfer), Fe\_{*x*}Al\_{1−*x*} , are newer alloys that exhibit 200-400 microstrains at lower applied fields (~200 Oe) and have enhanced mechanical properties from the brittle Terfenol-D. Both of these alloys have \<100> easy axes for magnetostriction and demonstrate sufficient ductility for sensor and actuator applications.[^o10]

*Image: Magnetostrictive flow sensor.png.* Schematic of a whisker flow sensor developed using thin-sheet magnetostrictive alloys.

Another very common magnetostrictive composite is the amorphous alloy Fe81Si\_{3.5}B\_{13.5}C2 with its trade name [Metglas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metglas) 2605SC. Favourable properties of this material are its high saturation-magnetostriction constant, λ, of about 20 microstrains and more, coupled with a low [magnetic-anisotropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_anisotropy) field strength, H<sub>A</sub>, of less than 1 kA/m (to reach [magnetic saturation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_saturation)). [Metglas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metglas) 2605SC also exhibits a very strong ΔE-effect with reductions in the effective [Young's modulus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Young's_modulus) up to about 80% in bulk. This helps build energy-efficient magnetic [MEMS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microelectromechanical_systems).

Cobalt [ferrite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrite_%28magnet%29), CoFe2O4 (CoO·Fe<sub>2</sub>O<sub>3</sub>), is also mainly used for its magnetostrictive applications like sensors and actuators, thanks to its high saturation magnetostriction (~200 parts per million).[^o11] In the absence of [rare-earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rare-earth) elements, it is a good substitute for [Terfenol-D](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terfenol-D).[^o12] Moreover, its magnetostrictive properties can be tuned by inducing a magnetic uniaxial anisotropy.[^o13] This can be done by magnetic annealing,[^o14] magnetic field assisted compaction,[^o15] or reaction under uniaxial pressure.[^o16] This last solution has the advantage of being ultrafast (20 min), thanks to the use of [spark plasma sintering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spark_plasma_sintering).

In early [sonar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sonar) transducers during World War II, [nickel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nickel) was used as a magnetostrictive material. To alleviate the shortage of nickel, the Japanese navy used an [iron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iron)-[aluminium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aluminium) alloy from the [Alperm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alperm) family.

### Mechanical behaviors of magnetostrictive alloys {#Mechanical_behaviors_of_magnetostrictive_alloys}

#### Effect of microstructure on elastic strain alloys {#Effect_of_microstructure_on_elastic_strain_alloys}

[Single-crystal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Single_crystal) alloys exhibit superior microstrain, but are vulnerable to yielding due to the anisotropic mechanical properties of most metals. It has been observed that for [polycrystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polycrystalline) alloys with a high area coverage of preferential grains for microstrain, the mechanical properties ([ductility](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ductility)) of magnetostrictive alloys can be significantly improved. Targeted metallurgical processing steps promote [abnormal grain growth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abnormal_grain_growth) of {011} grains in [galfenol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galfenol) and [alfenol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alperm) thin sheets, which contain two easy axes for magnetic domain alignment during magnetostriction. This can be accomplished by adding particles such as [boride](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boride) species [^o19] and [niobium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niobium) carbide (NbC) [^o20] during initial chill casting of the [ingot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingot).

For a polycrystalline alloy, an established formula for the magnetostriction, λ, from known directional microstrain measurements is:[^o21]

λ<sub>s</sub> = 1/5(2λ<sub>100</sub>+3λ<sub>111</sub>)

*Image: Tensiletestgallium.png.* Magnetostrictive alloy deformed to fracture

During subsequent [hot rolling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hot_rolling) and [recrystallization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recrystallization_%28metallurgy%29) steps, particle strengthening occurs in which the particles introduce a "pinning" force at [grain boundaries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grain_boundaries) that hinders normal ([stochastic](wikipedia://en/292222)) grain growth in an annealing step assisted by a H2S atmosphere. Thus, single-crystal-like texture (~90% {011} grain coverage) is attainable, reducing the interference with [magnetic domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_domain) alignment and increasing microstrain attainable for polycrystalline alloys as measured by semiconducting [strain gauges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strain_gauges).[^o22] These surface textures can be visualized using [electron backscatter diffraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_backscatter_diffraction) (EBSD) or related diffraction techniques.

#### Compressive stress to induce domain alignment {#Compressive_stress_to_induce_domain_alignment}

For actuator applications, maximum rotation of magnetic moments leads to the highest possible magnetostriction output. This can be achieved by processing techniques such as stress annealing and field annealing. However, mechanical pre-stresses can also be applied to thin sheets to induce alignment perpendicular to actuation as long as the stress is below the buckling limit. For example, it has been demonstrated that applied compressive pre-stress of up to ~50 MPa can result in an increase of magnetostriction by ~90%. This is hypothesized to be due to a "jump" in initial alignment of domains perpendicular to applied stress and improved final alignment parallel to applied stress.[^o24]

### Constitutive behavior of magnetostrictive materials {#Constitutive_behavior_of_magnetostrictive_materials}

These materials generally show non-linear behavior with a change in applied magnetic field or stress. For small magnetic fields, linear piezomagnetic constitutive[^o26] behavior is enough. Non-linear magnetic behavior is captured using a classical macroscopic model such as the [Preisach model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preisach_model_of_hysteresis)[^o27] and Jiles-Atherton model.[^o28] For capturing magneto-mechanical behavior, Armstrong[^o29] proposed an "energy average" approach. More recently, Wahi *et al.*[^o30] have proposed a computationally efficient [constitutive](wikipedia://en/1034699) model wherein constitutive behavior is captured using a "locally linearizing" scheme.
