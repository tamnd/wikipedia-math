# Binomial type

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Binomial type"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [polynomial sequence](wikipedia://en/195990), i.e., a sequence of [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) [indexed](wikipedia://en/23624339) by non-negative [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) $\left\{0, 1, 2, 3, \ldots \right\}$ in which the index of each polynomial equals its [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652), is said to be of **binomial type** if it satisfies the sequence of identities

:::

$$p_n(x+y)=\sum_{k=0}^n{n \choose k}\, p_k(x)\, p_{n-k}(y).$$

Many such sequences exist. The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all such sequences forms a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) under the operation of [umbral composition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Umbral_composition), explained below. Every sequence of binomial type may be expressed in terms of the [Bell polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_polynomial). Every sequence of binomial type is a [Sheffer sequence](wikipedia://en/218596) (but most Sheffer sequences are not of binomial type). Polynomial sequences put on firm footing the vague 19th century notions of [umbral calculus](wikipedia://en/214124).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- In consequence of this definition the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677) can be stated by saying that the sequence $\{x^n : n= 0, 1, 2, \ldots \}$ is of binomial type.
- The sequence of "[lower factorials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_factorial)" is defined by $$(x)_n=x(x-1)(x-2)\cdot\cdots\cdot(x-n+1).$$ (In the theory of special [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), this same notation denotes [upper factorials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_factorial), but this present usage is universal among [combinatorialists](wikipedia://en/5170).) The [product](wikipedia://en/24977) is understood to be 1 if *n* = 0, since it is in that case an [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289). This polynomial sequence is of binomial type.[^n1]
- Similarly the "[upper factorials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_factorial)" $$x^{(n)}=x(x+1)(x+2)\cdot\cdots\cdot(x+n-1)$$ are a polynomial sequence of binomial type.
- The [Abel polynomials](wikipedia://en/2094497) $$p_n(x)=x(x-an)^{n-1}$$ are a polynomial sequence of binomial type.
- The [Touchard polynomials](wikipedia://en/342592) $$p_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n S(n,k)x^k$$ where $S(n,k)$ is the number of [partitions of a set](wikipedia://en/340240) of [size](wikipedia://en/6174) $n$ into $k$ [disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359) [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631), is a polynomial sequence of binomial type. [Eric Temple Bell](wikipedia://en/255236) called these the "exponential polynomials" and that term is also sometimes seen in the literature. The [coefficients](wikipedia://en/51203) $S(n,k)$ are "[Stirling numbers](wikipedia://en/95465) of the second kind". This sequence has a curious connection with the [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144): If $X$ is a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) with a Poisson distribution with [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) $\lambda$ then $E(X^n)= p_n(\lambda)$. In particular, when $\lambda = 1$, we see that the $n$th [moment](wikipedia://en/790823) of the Poisson distribution with expected value $1$ is the number of partitions of a set of size $n$, called the $n$th [Bell number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_numbers). This fact about the $n$th moment of that particular Poisson distribution is "[Dobinski's formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bell_numbers)".

:::

## Characterization by delta operators {#Characterization_by_delta_operators}

It can be shown that a polynomial sequence { *p*<sub>*n*</sub>(x): *n* = 0, 1, 2, … } is of binomial type if and only if all three of the following conditions hold:

- The [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation) on the space of polynomials in *x* that is characterized by $$p_n(x) \mapsto n p_{n-1}(x)$$ is [shift-equivariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shift-equivariant), and
- *p*<sub>0</sub>(*x*) = 1 for all *x*, and
- *p*<sub>*n*</sub>(0) = 0 for *n* > 0.

(The statement that this operator is shift-equivariant is the same as saying that the polynomial sequence is a [Sheffer sequence](wikipedia://en/218596); the set of sequences of binomial type is properly included within the set of Sheffer sequences.)

### Delta operators {#Delta_operators}

That linear transformation is clearly a [delta operator](wikipedia://en/229528), i.e., a shift-equivariant linear transformation on the space of polynomials in *x* that reduces degrees of polynomials by 1. The most obvious examples of delta operators are [difference operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_operator)[^n1] and [differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation_%28calculus%29). It can be shown that every delta operator can be written as a [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) of the form: $Q=\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n D^n$ where *D* is differentiation (note that the [lower bound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_bound) of [summation](wikipedia://en/246160) is 1). Each delta operator *Q* has a unique sequence of "basic polynomials", i.e., a polynomial sequence satisfying

1. $$p_0(x)=1,$$
1. $$p_n(0)=0\quad{\rm for\ }n\geq 1,{\rm\ and}$$
1. $$Qp_n(x)=np_{n-1}(x).$$

It was shown in 1973 by [Rota](wikipedia://en/101891), Kahaner, and [Odlyzko](wikipedia://en/1986042), that a polynomial sequence is of binomial type if and only if it is the sequence of basic polynomials of some delta operator.[^o8] Therefore, this paragraph amounts to a recipe for generating as many polynomial sequences of binomial type as one may wish.

## Characterization by Bell polynomials {#Characterization_by_Bell_polynomials}

For any sequence *a*<sub>1</sub>, *a*<sub>2</sub>, *a*<sub>3</sub>, … of [scalars](wikipedia://en/3588331), let

$$p_n(x)=\sum_{k=1}^n B_{n,k}(a_1,\dots,a_{n-k+1}) x^k$$

where *B*<sub>*n*,*k*</sub>(*a*<sub>1</sub>, …, *a*<sub>*n*−*k*+1</sub>) is the [Bell polynomial](wikipedia://en/572498). Then this polynomial sequence is of binomial type. Note that for each *n* ≥ 1,

$$p_n'(0)=a_n.$$

Here is the main result of this section:

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem:** All polynomial sequences of binomial type are of this form.

A result in Mullin and Rota, repeated in Rota, Kahaner, and Odlyzko[^o8] states that every polynomial sequence { *p*<sub>*n*</sub>(*x*) }<sub>*n*</sub> of binomial type is determined by the sequence { *p*<sub>*n*</sub>′(0) }<sub>*n*</sub>, but those sources do not mention Bell polynomials.

This sequence of scalars is also related to the delta operator. Let

$$P(t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty {a_n \over n!} t^n.$$

Then

$$P^{-1}\left({d \over dx}\right),$$

where $P^{-1}(P(x)) = P(P^{-1}(x)) = 1$, is the delta operator of this sequence.

:::

::: {#o12 .statement cue=named-heading name="Characterization by a convolution identity"}

## Characterization by a convolution identity {#Characterization_by_a_convolution_identity}

For sequences *a*<sub>*n*</sub>, *b*<sub>*n*</sub>, *n* = 0, 1, 2, …, define a sort of [convolution](wikipedia://en/7519) by

$$(a \mathbin{\diamondsuit} b)_n = \sum_{j=0}^n {n \choose j} a_j b_{n-j}.$$

Let $a_n^{k\diamondsuit}$ be the *n*th term of the sequence

$$\underbrace{a\mathbin{\diamondsuit}\cdots\mathbin{\diamondsuit} a}_{k\text{ factors}}.$$

Then for any sequence *a*<sub>*i*</sub>, *i* = 0, 1, 2, ..., with *a*<sub>0</sub> = 0, the sequence defined by *p*<sub>0</sub>(*x*) = 1 and

$$p_n(x) = \sum_{k=1}^n {a_n^{k\diamondsuit} x^k \over k!}\,$$

for *n* ≥ 1, is of binomial type, and every sequence of binomial type is of this form.

:::

## Characterization by generating functions {#Characterization_by_generating_functions}

Polynomial sequences of binomial type are precisely those whose [generating functions](wikipedia://en/160993) are formal (not necessarily [convergent](wikipedia://en/1743431)) [power series](wikipedia://en/59958) of the form

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty {p_n(x) \over n!}t^n = e^{x f(t)}$$

where *f*(*t*) is a [formal power series](wikipedia://en/60012) whose [constant term](wikipedia://en/79861) is zero and whose first-degree term is not zero.[^n2] It can be shown by the use of the power-series version of [Faà di Bruno's formula](wikipedia://en/425943) that

$$f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty {p_n'(0) \over (n-1)!}t^{n-1}.$$

The delta operator of the sequence is the compositional inverse $f^{-1}(D)$, so that

$$f^{-1}(D)p_n(x)=np_{n-1}(x).$$

### A way to think about these generating functions {#A_way_to_think_about_these_generating_functions}

The coefficients in the product of two formal power series

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty {a_n \over n!}t^n$$

and

$$\sum_{n=0}^\infty {b_n \over n!}t^n$$

are

$$c_n=\sum_{k=0}^n {n \choose k} a_k b_{n-k}$$

(see also [Cauchy product](wikipedia://en/863791)). If we think of *x* as a parameter indexing a family of such power series, then the binomial identity says in effect that the power series indexed by *x* + *y* is the product of those indexed by *x* and by *y*. Thus the *x* is the argument to a function that maps sums to products: an [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678)

$$g(t)^x=e^{x f(t)}$$

where *f*(*t*) has the form given above.

## Umbral composition of polynomial sequences {#Umbral_composition_of_polynomial_sequences}

The set of all polynomial sequences of binomial type is a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) in which the group operation is "umbral composition" of polynomial sequences. That operation is defined as follows. Suppose { *p*<sub>*n*</sub>(*x*): *n* = 0, 1, 2, 3, ... } and { *q*<sub>*n*</sub>(*x*): *n* = 0, 1, 2, 3, ... } are polynomial sequences, and

$$p_n(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n a_{n,k}\, x^k.$$

Then the umbral composition *p* o *q* is the polynomial sequence whose *n*th term is

$$(p_n\circ q)(x)=\sum_{k=0}^n a_{n,k}\, q_k(x)$$

(the subscript *n* appears in *p*<sub>*n*</sub>, since this is the *n* term of that sequence, but not in *q*, since this refers to the sequence as a whole rather than one of its terms).

With the delta operator defined by a power series in *D* as above, the natural [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between delta operators and polynomial sequences of binomial type, also defined above, is a [group isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397), in which the group operation on power series is formal composition of formal power series.

## Cumulants and moments {#Cumulants_and_moments}

The sequence κ<sub>*n*</sub> of coefficients of the first-degree terms in a polynomial sequence of binomial type may be termed the [cumulants](wikipedia://en/359684) of the polynomial sequence. It can be shown that the whole polynomial sequence of binomial type is determined by its cumulants, in a way discussed in the article titled *[cumulant](wikipedia://en/359684)*. Thus

$p_n'(0)=\kappa_n=$ the *n*th cumulant

and

$p_n(1)=\mu_n'=$ the *n*th moment.

These are "formal" cumulants and "formal" [moments](wikipedia://en/368684), as opposed to cumulants of a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) and moments of a probability distribution.

Let

$$f(t)=\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{\kappa_n}{n!}t^n$$

be the (formal) cumulant-generating function. Then

$$f^{-1}(D)$$

is the delta operator associated with the polynomial sequence, i.e., we have

$$f^{-1}(D)p_n(x)=n p_{n-1}(x).$$
