# Translation (geometry)

*Image: Traslazione OK.svg.* A translation moves every point of a figure or a space by the same amount in a given direction.

*Image: Traslazione.gif.* Translation of three objects

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Translation"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), a **translation** is a [geometric transformation](wikipedia://en/43194879) that moves every point of a figure, shape or space by the same [distance](wikipedia://en/654387) in a given [direction](wikipedia://en/54516626). A translation can also be interpreted as the addition of a constant [vector](wikipedia://en/32370) to every point, or as shifting the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) of the [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931). In a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), any translation is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777).

:::

A translation is an isometry that displaces the original figure according to a direction, a sense, and a length ([vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28mathematics%29)). Translations preserve the direction and length of line segments, and the amplitudes of angles.

A **slide** is a translation along a [screw axis](wikipedia://en/3656192), around which a [rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_%28geometry%29) may also occur.

We can cite as practical examples of translation, [elevators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevator), [escalators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escalator), and even [slides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slide_%28playground%29).

In translational symmetry, the figure "slides" along a line, remaining unchanged. In all translations, it is observed that the same element moves in a certain direction and always parallel to itself, that is, without ever rotating. In a frieze, there is a motif that repeats periodically, in a certain direction and always parallel to itself.

"Let AB be an oriented segment, in the plane π or in the space E. (Oriented means that the order in which the endpoints are cited is relevant: first A, and then B.) The translation determined by AB is the transformation (bijective correspondence) τ : π → π, or τ : E → E, defined by τ(X) = X', such that (AB, XX') and (AX, BX') are pairs of opposite sides of a parallelogram".[^o2]

## As a function {#As_a_function}

If $\mathbf{v}$ is a fixed vector, known as the *translation vector*, and $\mathbf{p}$ is the initial position of some object, then the translation function $T_{\mathbf{v}}$ will work as $T_{\mathbf{v}}(\mathbf{p})=\mathbf{p}+\mathbf{v}$.

If $T$ is a translation, then the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) of a subset $A$ under the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $T$ is the **translate** of $A$ by $T$. The translate of $A$ by $T_{\mathbf{v}}$ is often written as $A+\mathbf{v}$.

::: {#o5 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Translation (physics)"}

### Application in classical physics {#Application_in_classical_physics}

In [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics), translational motion is movement that changes the [position](wikipedia://en/1345771) of an object, as opposed to [rotation](wikipedia://en/39789). For example, according to Whittaker:[^o6]

If a body is moved from one position to another, and if the lines joining the initial and final points of each of the points of the body are a set of parallel straight lines of length *ℓ*, so that the orientation of the body in space is unaltered, the displacement is called a *translation parallel to the direction of the lines, through a distance ℓ*.

A translation is the operation changing the positions of all points $(x, y, z)$ of an object according to the formula

$$(x,y,z) \to (x+\Delta x,y+\Delta y, z+\Delta z)$$

where $(\Delta x,\ \Delta y,\ \Delta z)$ is the same [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) for each point of the object. The translation vector $(\Delta x,\ \Delta y,\ \Delta z)$ common to all points of the object describes a particular type of [displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Displacement_%28vector%29) of the object, usually called a *linear* displacement to distinguish it from displacements involving rotation, called *angular* displacements.

When considering [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758), a change of [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) coordinate is considered to be a translation.

:::

## As an operator {#As_an_operator}

The [translation operator](wikipedia://en/467384) turns a function of the original position, $f(\mathbf{v})$, into a function of the final position, $f(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{\delta})$. In other words, $T_\mathbf{\delta}$ is defined such that $T_\mathbf{\delta} f(\mathbf{v}) = f(\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{\delta}).$ This [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) is more abstract than a function, since $T_\mathbf{\delta}$ defines a relationship between two functions, rather than the underlying vectors themselves. The translation operator can act on many kinds of functions, such as when the [translation operator acts on a wavefunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translation_operator_%28quantum_mechanics%29), which is studied in the field of quantum mechanics.

## As a group {#As_a_group}

The set of all translations forms the **translation group** $\mathbb{T}$, which is isomorphic to the space itself, and a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of [Euclidean group](wikipedia://en/652164) $E(n)$. The [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) of $E(n)$ by $\mathbb{T}$ is isomorphic to the group of rigid motions which fix a particular origin point, the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) $O(n)$:

$$E(n)/\mathbb{T}\cong O(n)$$

Because translation is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), the translation group is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974). There are an infinite number of possible translations, so the translation group is an [infinite group](wikipedia://en/4587030).

In the [theory of relativity](wikipedia://en/30001), due to the treatment of space and time as a single [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758), translations can also refer to changes in the [time coordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_time). For example, the [Galilean group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_group) and the [Poincaré group](wikipedia://en/230487) include translations with respect to time.

### Lattice groups {#Lattice_groups}

One kind of [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of the three-dimensional translation group are the [lattice groups](wikipedia://en/469246), which are [infinite groups](wikipedia://en/4587030), but unlike the translation groups, are [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042). That is, a finite [generating set](wikipedia://en/99945) generates the entire group.

## Matrix representation {#Matrix_representation}

A translation is an [affine transformation](wikipedia://en/38449) with *no* [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738). Matrix multiplications *always* have the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) as a fixed point. Nevertheless, there is a common [workaround](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Workaround) using [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316) to represent a translation of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) with [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280): Write the 3-dimensional vector $\mathbf{v}=(v_x, v_y, v_z)$ using 4 homogeneous coordinates as $\mathbf{v}=(v_x, v_y, v_z, 1)$.[^o11]

To translate an object by a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometry%29) $\mathbf{v}$, each homogeneous vector $\mathbf{p}$ (written in homogeneous coordinates) can be multiplied by this **translation matrix**:

$$T_{\mathbf{v}} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & v_x \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & v_y \\ 0 & 0 & 1 & v_z \\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix}$$

As shown below, the multiplication will give the expected result:

$$T_{\mathbf{v}} \mathbf{p} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 & v_x \\ 0 & 1 & 0 & v_y\\ 0 & 0 & 1 & v_z\\ 0 & 0 & 0 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} p_x \\ p_y \\ p_z \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} p_x + v_x \\ p_y + v_y \\ p_z + v_z \\ 1 \end{bmatrix} = \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{v}$$

The inverse of a translation matrix can be obtained by reversing the direction of the vector (in other words, it is an [M-matrix](wikipedia://en/7939066)):

$$T^{-1}_{\mathbf{v}} = T_{-\mathbf{v}} . \!$$

Similarly, the product of translation matrices is given by adding the vectors:

$$T_{\mathbf{v}}T_{\mathbf{w}} = T_{\mathbf{v}+\mathbf{w}} . \!$$

Because addition of vectors is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), multiplication of translation matrices is therefore also commutative (unlike multiplication of arbitrary matrices).

## Translation of axes {#Translation_of_axes}

While geometric translation is often viewed as an [active transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Active_transformation) that changes the position of a geometric object, a similar result can be achieved by a [passive transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passive_transformation) that moves the coordinate system itself but leaves the object fixed. The passive version of an active geometric translation is known as a *[translation of axes](wikipedia://en/47331006)*.

## Translational symmetry {#Translational_symmetry}

An object that looks the same before and after translation is said to have [translational symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Translational_symmetry). A common example is a [periodic function](wikipedia://en/76848), which is an [eigenfunction](wikipedia://en/228601) of a translation operator.

In translational symmetry, the figure "slides" along a line, remaining unchanged. In all translations, it is observed that the same element moves in a certain direction and always parallel to itself, that is, without ever rotating. In a [frieze](wikipedia://en/375503), there is a motif that repeats periodically, in a certain direction and always parallel to itself.

## Translations of a graph {#Translations_of_a_graph}

*Image: Translated graph of a function.png.* Compared to the graph $y = f(x)$, the graph $y = f(x\,-\,a)$ has been translated horizontally by $a$, while the graph $y = f(x)\,+\,b$ has been translated vertically by $b$.

The [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of a [real function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_function) $f$, the set of points $(x, f(x))$, is often pictured in the [real coordinate plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_coordinate_plane) with $x$ as the horizontal coordinate and $y = f(x)$ as the vertical coordinate.

Starting from the graph of $f$, a **horizontal translation** means [composing](wikipedia://en/195947) $f$ with a function $x \mapsto x - a$, for some constant number $a$, resulting in a graph consisting of points $(x, f(x - a))$. Each point $(x, y)$ of the original graph corresponds to the point $(x + a, y)$ in the new graph, which pictorially results in a horizontal shift.

A **vertical translation** means composing the function $y \mapsto y + b$ with $f$, for some constant $b$, resulting in a graph consisting of the points $\bigl(x, f(x) + b\bigr)$. Each point $(x, y)$ of the original graph corresponds to the point $(x, y + b)$ in the new graph, which pictorially results in a vertical shift.[^o15]

For example, taking the [quadratic function](wikipedia://en/187240) $y = x^2$, whose graph is a [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) with vertex at $(0, 0)$, a horizontal translation 5 units to the right would be the new function $y = (x - 5)^2 = x^2 - 10x + 25$ whose vertex has coordinates $(5, 0)$. A vertical translation 3 units upward would be the new function $y = x^2 + 3$ whose vertex has coordinates $(0, 3)$.

The [antiderivatives](wikipedia://en/2823) of a function all differ from each other by a [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939) and are therefore vertical translates of each other.[^o16]

## Applications {#Applications}

For describing [vehicle dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_dynamics) (or movement of any [rigid body](wikipedia://en/455769)), including [ship dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_motions) and [aircraft dynamics](wikipedia://en/9023486), it is common to use a mechanical model consisting of six [degrees of freedom](wikipedia://en/2321587), which includes translations along three reference axes (as well as rotations about those three axes). These translations are often called [*surge*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_motions#Surge), [*sway*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_motions#Sway), and [*heave*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ship_motions#Heave).

:::
