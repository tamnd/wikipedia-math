# Standardized moment

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Standardized moment"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), a **standardized moment** of a [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) is a [moment](wikipedia://en/368684) (often a higher degree [central moment](wikipedia://en/19983)) that is normalized, typically by a power of the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590), rendering the moment [scale invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariant). The [shape](wikipedia://en/9330700) of different probability distributions can be compared using standardized moments.[^o2]

:::

## Standard normalization {#Standard_normalization}

Let $X$ be a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) with a probability distribution $P$ and mean value $\mu = \operatorname{E}[X]$ (i.e. the first [raw moment or moment about zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raw_moments)), the operator $E$ denoting the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) of $X$. Then the **standardized moment** of degree $k$ is $\alpha_k = \mu_k/\sigma^k$,[^o4] that is, the ratio of the $k$-th [moment about the mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moment_about_the_mean)

$$\mu_k = \operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^k \right] = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} {\left(x - \mu\right)}^k f(x)\,dx,$$

to the $k$-th power of the [standard deviation](wikipedia://en/27590),

$$\sigma^k = \mu_2^{k/2} = \operatorname{E}\!{\left[ {\left(X - \mu\right)}^2 \right]}^{k/2}.$$

The power of $k$ is because moments scale as $x^k$, meaning that $\mu_k(\lambda X) = \lambda^k \mu_k(X):$ they are [homogeneous functions](wikipedia://en/622844) of degree $k$, thus the standardized moment is [scale invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scale_invariant). This can also be understood as being because moments have dimension; in the above ratio defining standardized moments, the dimensions cancel, so they are [dimensionless numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_number).

The first four standardized moments can be written as:

::: {#o5 .table cue=table}

| Degree *k* |  | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | $\alpha_1 = \frac{\mu_1}{\sigma^1}= \frac{\operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^1 \right]}{\left( \operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^2 \right]\right)^{1/2}} = \frac{\mu - \mu}{\sqrt{ \operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^2 \right]}} = 0$ | The first standardized moment is zero, because the first moment about the mean is always zero. |
| 2 | $\alpha_2 = \frac{\mu_2}{\sigma^2} = \frac{\operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^2 \right]}{\left( \operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^2 \right]\right)^{2/2}} = 1$ | The second standardized moment is one, because the second moment about the mean is equal to the [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) $\sigma^{2}$. |
| 3 | $\alpha_3 = \frac{\mu_3}{\sigma^3} = \frac{\operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^3 \right]}{\left( \operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^2 \right]\right)^{3/2}}$ | The third standardized moment is a measure of [skewness](wikipedia://en/28212). |
| 4 | $\alpha_4 = \frac{\mu_4}{\sigma^4} = \frac{\operatorname{E} \left[ ( X - \mu )^4 \right]}{\left( \operatorname{E} \left[ (X - \mu)^2 \right]\right)^{4/2}}$ | The fourth standardized moment refers to the [kurtosis](wikipedia://en/16848). |

:::

For skewness and kurtosis, alternative definitions exist, which are based on the third and fourth [cumulant](wikipedia://en/359684) respectively.

## Other normalizations {#Other_normalizations}

Another scale invariant, dimensionless measure for characteristics of a distribution is the [coefficient of variation](wikipedia://en/1012687), $\sigma / \mu$. However, this is not a standardized moment, firstly because it is a reciprocal, and secondly because $\mu$ is the first moment about zero (the mean), not the first moment about the mean (which is zero).

See [Normalization (statistics)](wikipedia://en/2978513) for further normalizing ratios.
