# Richard Whately

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Richard Whately"}

**Richard Whately** (1 February 1787 – 8 October 1863) was an English academic, rhetorician, logician, philosopher, economist, and theologian who also served as a reforming [Church of Ireland Archbishop of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Dublin_%28Church_of_Ireland%29). He was a leading [Broad Churchman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Broad_Church), a prolific and combative author over a wide range of topics, a flamboyant character, and one of the first reviewers to recognise the talents of [Jane Austen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Austen).[^o4][^o5][^o6]

:::

## Early life and education {#Early_life_and_education}

Whately was born in London, the son of the Rev. Dr. [Joseph Whately](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Whately) (1730–1797). He was educated at a private school near [Bristol](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bristol), and at [Oriel College, Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriel_College,_Oxford), from 1805. He obtained a [B.A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts). in 1808, with double second-class honours, and the prize for the English essay in 1810; in 1811, he was elected Fellow of Oriel, and in 1814 took [holy orders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holy_orders). After graduation he acted as a private tutor, in particular to [Nassau William Senior](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nassau_William_Senior) who became a close friend, and to [Samuel Hinds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_Hinds_%28bishop%29).[^o6][^o8]

## Career {#Career}

### University of Oxford {#University_of_Oxford}

In 1825, Whately was appointed principal of [St. Alban Hall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Alban_Hall,_Oxford) at the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), a position obtained for him by his mentor [Edward Copleston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_Copleston), who wanted to raise the notoriously low academic standards at the Hall, which was also a target for expansion by Oriel.[^o6]

Whately returned to the [University of Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Oxford), where he had seen the social impact of unemployment on the city and region.[^o11]

A reformer, Whately was initially on friendly terms with [John Henry Newman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Henry_Newman). They fell out over [Robert Peel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Robert_Peel)'s candidacy for the [Oxford University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford_University) seat in Parliament.[^o12]

In 1829, Whately was elected as [Drummond Professor of Political Economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drummond_Professor_of_Political_Economy) at Oxford in succession to Nassau William Senior. His tenure of office was cut short by his appointment to the [archbishopric of Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Archbishop_of_Dublin_%28Church_of_Ireland%29) in 1831. He published only one course of *Introductory Lectures* in two editions (1831 & 1832).

### Archbishop of Dublin {#Archbishop_of_Dublin}

Whately's appointment by [Lord Grey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Grey,_2nd_Earl_Grey) to the see of Dublin came as a political surprise. The aged [Henry Bathurst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Bathurst_%28bishop%29) had turned the post down. The new Whig administration found Whately, who was known at [Holland House](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Holland_House,_London) and effective in a parliamentary committee appearance speaking on [tithes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tithe), an acceptable option. Behind the scenes [Thomas Hyde Villiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Hyde_Villiers) had lobbied [Denis Le Marchant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denis_Le_Marchant) on his behalf, with the Brougham Whigs.[^o15] The appointment was challenged in the [House of Lords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Lords), but without success.[^o13]

In Ireland, Whately's bluntness and his lack of a conciliatory manner caused opposition from his own clergy, and from the beginning he gave offence by supporting state endowment of the [Catholic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church) clergy. He enforced strict discipline in his diocese; and he published a statement of his views on Sabbath (*Thoughts on the Sabbath*, 1832). He lived in Redesdale House in [Kilmacud](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilmacud), just outside Dublin, where he could garden. He was concerned to reform the [Church of Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_Ireland) and the [Irish Poor Laws](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_Poor_Laws).[^o13] He considered [tithe commutation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tithe_commutation) essential for the Church.[^o16]

### Irish national education 1831 to 1853 {#Irish_national_education_1831_to_1853}

In 1831, Whately attempted to establish a national and non-sectarian system of education in Ireland, on the basis of common instruction for Protestants and Catholics alike in literary and moral subjects, religious instruction being taken apart.

In 1841, Catholic archbishops [William Crolly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Crolly) and [John MacHale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_MacHale) debated whether to continue the system, with the more moderate Crolly supporting Whately's gaining papal permission to go on, given some safeguards.[^o18] In 1852, the scheme broke down due to the opposition of the new ultramontanist Catholic archbishop of Dublin, [Paul Cullen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Cullen_%28cardinal%29), who would later become the first Irish prelate named Cardinal. Whately withdrew from the Education Board the following year.

During the famine years of 1846 and 1847, the archbishop and his family tried to alleviate the miseries of the people.[^o13] On 27 March 1848, Whately became a member of the [Canterbury Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canterbury_Association).[^o19] He was elected a Foreign Honorary Member of the [American Academy of Arts and Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Academy_of_Arts_and_Sciences) in 1855.[^o20]

### Personal life {#Personal_life}

After his marriage to writer [Elizabeth Whately](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elizabeth_Whately) ( Pope) in 1821, Whately lived in [Oxford](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oxford). He gave up his college fellowship, which could not then be held by married men, and began tutoring and writing.[^o22]

An uncle, [William Plumer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Plumer_%281736–1822%29), presented him with a living in [Halesworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halesworth) in Suffolk, and Whately moved there. His daughters were writer [Jane Whately](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jane_Whately) and missionary [Mary Louisa Whately](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mary_Louisa_Whately).[^o23] One of his nephews was Canon [William Pope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Pope_%28priest%29).[^o24]

### Death {#Death}

*Image: Dublin St. Patrick's Cathedral South Transept West Aisle Monument Dedicated to Archbishop Richard Whately II 2012 09 26.jpg.* The monument dedicated to Whately in [St. Patrick's Cathedral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St_Patrick's_Cathedral,_Dublin) in [Dublin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin), sculpted by [Thomas Farrell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Farrell_%28sculptor%29)[^o26]

Beginning in 1856, Whately began experiencing symptoms of decline, including [paralysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paralysis) of his left side, but he continued his public duties.[^o42]

In the summer of 1863, Whately was prostrated by an [ulcer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ulcer_%28dermatology%29) in his leg; after several months of acute suffering, he died on 8 October 1863.[^o13]

## Works {#Works}

Whately was a prolific writer, a successful expositor and Protestant apologist in works that ran to many editions and translations. His *Elements of Logic* (1826) was drawn from an article "Logic" in the *[Encyclopædia Metropolitana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Encyclopædia_Metropolitana)*.[^o28] The companion article on "Rhetoric" provided *Elements of Rhetoric* (1828).[^o13] In these two works Whately introduced [erotetic logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erotetic_logic).[^o29]

In 1825 Whately published a series of *Essays on Some of the Peculiarities of the Christian Religion*, followed in 1828 by a second series *On Some of the Difficulties in the Writings of St Paul*, and in 1830 by a third *On the Errors of Romanism Traced to Their Origin in Human Nature*. In 1837 he wrote his handbook of *Christian Evidences*, which was translated during his lifetime into more than a dozen languages.[^o13] In the Irish context, the *Christian Evidences* was adapted to a form acceptable to Catholic beliefs, with the help of [James Carlile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Carlile).[^o30]

## Character {#Character}

[Humphrey Lloyd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Humphrey_Lloyd_%28physicist%29) told [Caroline Fox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caroline_Fox) that Whately's eccentric behaviour and [body language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_language) was exacerbated in Dublin by a sycophantic circle of friends.[^o35] He was a great talker, a wit, and loved [punning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pun). In Oxford his white hat, rough white coat, and huge white dog earned for him the sobriquet of the White Bear, and he exhibited the exploits of his climbing dog in [Christ Church Meadow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christ_Church_Meadow,_Oxford).[^o13][^o36]

## Views {#Views}

A member of the loose group called the [Oriel Noetics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oriel_Noetics), Whately supported religious liberty, civil rights, and freedom of speech for dissenters, Roman Catholics, Jews, and even atheists. He took the line that the [civil disabilities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Civil_disabilities) imposed on non-Anglicans made the state only nominally Christian, and supported [disestablishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disestablishment).[^o38] He was a follower of Edward Copleston, regarded as the founder of the Noetics taken as [apologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apologist) for the orthodoxy of the [Church of England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church_of_England).[^o6]

A devout Christian, Whately took a practical view of Christianity. He disagreed with the [Evangelical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Evangelical) party and generally favoured a more intellectual approach to religion. He also disagreed with the later Tractarian emphasis on ritual and church authority.[^o13] Instead, he emphasised careful reading and understanding of the Bible.

His cardinal principle was that of [Chillingworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Chillingworth) —‘the [Bible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bible), and the Bible alone, is the religion of [protestants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism);’ and his [exegesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exegesis) was directed to determine the general tenor of the scriptures to the exclusion of [dogmas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dogma) based on isolated texts. There is no reason to question his reception of the central doctrines of the [faith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Faith_in_Christianity), though he shrank from theorising or even attempting to formulate them with precision. On [election](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Election_%28Christianity%29) he held, broadly speaking, the [Arminian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arminianism_in_the_Church_of_England) view, and his antipathy to [Calvinism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Calvinism) was intense. He dwelt more on the life than on the [death of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death_of_Christ), the necessity of which he denied.[^n1]

Whately took a view of [political economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_economy) as an essentially logical subject. It proved influential in Oxford. The Noetics were reformers but largely centrist in politics, rather than strong Whigs or Tories.[^o40] One of Whately's initial acts on going to Dublin was to endow a [chair of political economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Whately_Chair_of_Political_Economy) in [Trinity College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinity_College,_Dublin). Its first holder was [Mountifort Longfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountifort_Longfield).[^o41] Later, in 1846, he founded the [Dublin Statistical Society](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dublin_Statistical_Society) with William Neilson Hancock.

Whately's view of political economy, and that common to the early holders of the Trinity college professorship, addressed it as a type of [natural theology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_theology).[^o43] He belonged to the group of supporters of [Thomas Malthus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Malthus) that included [Thomas Chalmers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Chalmers), some others of the Noetics, [Richard Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Jones_%28economist%29) and [William Whewell](wikipedia://en/205488) from Cambridge.[^o44]

He saw no inconsistency between science and [Christian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity) belief, which differed from the view of other Christian critics of Malthus.[^o45] He differed also from Jones and Whewell, expressing the view that the [inductive method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inductive_method) was of less use for political economy than the [deductive method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_method), properly applied.[^o46]

In periodicals, Whately addressed other public questions, including the topic of [transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transportation) and the "secondary punishments" on those who had been transported; his pamphlet on this topic influenced the politicians [Lord John Russell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lord_John_Russell) and [Henry George Grey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Grey,_3rd_Earl_Grey).[^o47]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

Whately was an important figure in the revival of [Aristotelian logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_logic) in the early nineteenth century. The *Elements of Logic* gave an impetus to the study of logic in Britain,[^o13] and in the United States of America, logician [Charles Sanders Peirce](wikipedia://en/6117) (1839–1914) wrote that his lifelong fascination with logic began when he read Whately's *Elements* as a 12-year-old boy.[^o49]

Whately's view of rhetoric as essentially a method for persuasion became an orthodoxy, challenged in mid-century by [Henry Noble Day](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Noble_Day).[^o50] *Elements of Rhetoric* is still cited, for thought about [presumption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Presumption), [burden of proof](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philosophic_burden_of_proof), and [testimony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Testimony).[^o51][^o52]

Irish historian [William Edward Hartpole Lecky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Edward_Hartpole_Lecky) thought Whately’s importance and influence greater than his later historical repute would indicate, and that Whately’s unsystematic, aphoristic style of writing might explain history’s relative forgetfulness of him:

> He had a singular felicity of illustration, and especially of metaphor, and a rare power of throwing his thoughts into terse and pithy sentences; but his many books, though full of original thinking and in a high degree suggestive to other writers, had always a certain fragmentary and occasional character, which prevented them from taking a place in standard literature. He was conscious of it himself, and was accustomed to say that it was the mission of his life to make up cartridges for others to fire.[^o53]

In 1864, Jane Whately, his daughter, published *Miscellaneous Remains* from his commonplace book and in 1866 his *Life and Correspondence* in two volumes. *The Anecdotal Memoirs of Archbishop Whately*, by [William John Fitzpatrick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_John_Fitzpatrick), was published in 1864.[^o13]
