# Gradian

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Gradian"}

In [trigonometry](wikipedia://en/18717261), the **gradian** – also known as the **gon** (grc), **grad**, or **grade**[^o3] – is a [unit of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_of_measurement) of an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196), defined as one-hundredth of the [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956); in other words, 100 gradians is equal to 90 degrees.[^o4][^o5][^o6] It is equivalent to $\tfrac{1}{400}$ of a [turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28geometry%29),[^o7] $\tfrac{9}{10}$ of a [degree](wikipedia://en/1195294), or $\tfrac{\pi}{200}$ of a [radian](wikipedia://en/26003). Measuring angles in gradians (gons) is said to employ the *centesimal* system of angular measurement, initiated as part of [metrication](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrication) and [decimalisation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimalisation) efforts.[^o8][^o9][^o10]

:::

In continental [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe), the French word ***centigrade***, also known as *centesimal minute of arc*, was in use for one hundredth of a grade; similarly, the *centesimal second of arc* was defined as one hundredth of a centesimal arc-minute, analogous to [decimal time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_time) and the [sexagesimal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sexagesimal_degrees) [minutes and seconds of arc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minutes_and_seconds_of_arc).[^o11] The chance of confusion was one reason for the adoption of the term *[Celsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius)* to replace *centigrade* as the name of the temperature scale.[^o12][^o13]

Gradians (gons) are principally used in [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying) (especially in Europe),[^o14][^o9][^o15] and to a lesser extent in [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining)[^o16] and [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology).[^o17][^o18]

The gon (gradian) is a legally recognised unit of measurement in the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union)[^o19] and in [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland).[^o20] However, this unit is not part of the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI).[^o31][^o19]

## History and name {#History_and_name}

The unit originated in [France](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/France) in connection with the [French Revolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Revolution) as the grade, along with the [metric system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_system), hence it is occasionally referred to as a *metric degree*. Due to confusion with the existing term *grad(e)* in some northern European countries (meaning a standard degree, $\tfrac{1}{360}$ of a turn), the name *gon* was later adopted, first in those regions, and later as the international standard. In France, it was also called grade nouveau. In [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language), the unit was formerly also called Neugrad (new degree) (whereas the standard degree was referred to as Altgrad (old degree)), likewise nygrad in [Danish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danish_language), [Swedish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swedish_language) and [Norwegian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norwegian_language) (also *gradian*), and nýgráða in [Icelandic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Icelandic_language).

Although attempts at a general introduction were made, the unit was only adopted in some countries, and for specialised areas such as [surveying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying),[^o14][^o9][^o15] [mining](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mining)[^o16] and [geology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geology).[^o17][^o18] Today, the degree, $\tfrac{1}{360}$ of a [turn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28geometry%29), or the mathematically more convenient [radian](wikipedia://en/26003), $\tfrac{1}{2\pi}$ of a turn (used in the [SI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI) system of units) is generally used instead.

In the 1970s–1990s, most [scientific calculators](wikipedia://en/439711) offered the gon (gradian), as well as radians and degrees, for their [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367).[^o22] In the 2010s, some scientific calculators lack support for gradians.[^o23]

### Symbol {#Symbol}

The international standard symbol for this unit is "gon" (see [ISO 31-1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_31-1), Annex B). Other symbols used in the past include "gr", "grd", and "g", the last sometimes written as a superscript, similarly to a degree sign: 50<sup>g</sup> = 45°. A [metric prefix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix) is sometimes used, as in "dgon", "cgon", "mgon", denoting respectively 0.1 gon, 0.01 gon, 0.001 gon. Centesimal arc-minutes and centesimal arc-seconds were also denoted with superscripts <sup>c</sup> and <sup>cc</sup>, respectively.

## Advantages and disadvantages {#Advantages_and_disadvantages}

Each quadrant is assigned a range of 100 gon, which eases recognition of the four quadrants, as well as arithmetic involving perpendicular or opposite angles.

{|

\|- |align="right"| 0° ||align="center"| = ||align="right"| 0 gradians |- |align="right"| 90° ||align="center"| = ||align="right"| 100 gradians |- |align="right"| 180° ||align="center"| = ||align="right"| 200 gradians |- |align="right"| 270° ||align="center"| = ||align="right"| 300 gradians |- |align="right"| 360° ||align="center"| = ||align="right"| 400 gradians |}

One advantage of this unit is that right angles to a given angle are easily determined. If one is sighting down a compass course of 117 gon, the direction to one's left is 17 gon, to one's right 217 gon, and behind one 317 gon. A disadvantage is that the common angles of 30° and 60° in geometry must be expressed in fractions (as $33\tfrac{1}{3}$ gon and $66\tfrac{2}{3}$ gon respectively).

## Relation to the metre {#Relation_to_the_metre}

*Image: Kilometre definition.svg.* An early definition of the [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre) was one ten-millionth of the distance from the [North Pole](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Pole) to the [equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator), measured along a [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29) through [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989).

In the eighteenth century, the [metre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre) was defined as the 10-millionth part of a [quarter meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quarter_meridian). Thus, 1 gon corresponds to an [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) along the Earth's surface of approximately 100 kilometres; 1 centigon to 1 kilometre; 10 microgons to 1 metre.[^o29] (The [metre has been redefined](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metre#History_of_definition) with increasing precision since then.)

## Relation to the SI system of units {#Relation_to_the_SI_system_of_units}

The gradian is *not* part of the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units) (SI). The EU directive on the units of measurement[^o19] notes that the gradian "does not appear in the lists drawn up by the [CGPM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Conference_on_Weights_and_Measures), [CIPM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Conference_on_Weights_and_Measures#CIPM) or [BIPM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Bureau_of_Weights_and_Measures)." The most recent, 9th edition of the [SI Brochure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units#SI_Brochure) does not mention the gradian at all. The previous edition mentioned it only in the following footnote: The gon (or grad, where grad is an alternative name for the gon) is an alternative unit of plane angle to the degree, defined as (π/200) rad. Thus there are 100 gon in a right angle. The potential value of the gon in navigation is that because the distance from the pole to the equator of the Earth is approximately 10000 km, 1 km on the surface of the Earth subtends an angle of one centigon at the centre of the Earth. However the gon is rarely used.
