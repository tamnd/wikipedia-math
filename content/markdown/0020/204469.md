# Gibbs–Helmholtz equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gibbs–Helmholtz equation"}

The **Gibbs–Helmholtz equation** is a [thermodynamic](wikipedia://en/29952) [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) used to calculate changes in the [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) of a system as a function of [temperature](wikipedia://en/20647050). It was originally presented in an 1882 paper entitled "[Die Thermodynamik chemischer Vorgänge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamik_chemischer_Vorgänge)" by [Hermann von Helmholtz](wikipedia://en/53311). It describes how the Gibbs free energy, which was presented originally by [Josiah Willard Gibbs](wikipedia://en/37332), varies with temperature.[^o2] It was derived by [Helmholtz](wikipedia://en/53311) first, and Gibbs derived it only 6 years later.[^o3] The attribution to Gibbs goes back to [Wilhelm Ostwald](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilhelm_Ostwald), who first translated [Gibbs' monograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Equilibrium_of_Heterogeneous_Substances) into German and promoted it in Europe.[^o4][^o5]

:::

where *H* is the [enthalpy](wikipedia://en/10274), *T* the [absolute temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolute_temperature) and *G* the [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) of the system, all at constant [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) *p*. The equation states that the change in the *G/T* ratio at constant pressure as a result of an [infinitesimally](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitesimally) small change in temperature is a factor *H/T*<sup>2</sup>.

## Chemical reactions and work {#Chemical_reactions_and_work}

The typical applications of this equation are to [chemical reactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_reaction). The equation reads:[^o11]

$$\left( \frac{\partial ( \Delta G^\ominus/T ) } {\partial T} \right)_p = - \frac {\Delta H^\ominus} {T^2}$$

with Δ*G* as the change in Gibbs energy due to reaction, and Δ*H* as the [enthalpy of reaction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enthalpy_of_reaction) (often, but not necessarily, assumed to be independent of temperature). The o denotes the use of [standard states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_state), and particularly the choice of a particular standard pressure (1 bar), to calculate Δ*G* and Δ*H*.

Integrating with respect to *T* (again *p* is constant) yields:

$$\frac{\Delta G^\ominus(T_2)}{T_2} - \frac{\Delta G^\ominus(T_1)}{T_1} = \Delta H^\ominus \left(\frac{1}{T_2} - \frac{1}{T_1}\right)$$

This equation quickly enables the calculation of the Gibbs free energy change for a chemical reaction at any temperature *T*<sub>2</sub> with knowledge of just the [standard Gibbs free energy change of formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_Gibbs_free_energy_change_of_formation) and the [standard enthalpy change of formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_enthalpy_change_of_formation) for the individual components.

Also, using the reaction isotherm equation,[^o12] that is

$$\frac{\Delta G^\ominus}{T} = -R \ln K$$

which relates the Gibbs energy to a chemical [equilibrium constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilibrium_constant), the [van 't Hoff equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Van_'t_Hoff_equation) can be derived.[^o13]

Since the change in a system's Gibbs energy is equal to the maximum amount of non-expansion work that the system can do in a process, the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation may be used to estimate how much non-expansion work can be done by a chemical process as a function of temperature.[^o14] For example, the capacity of rechargeable electric batteries can be estimated as a function of temperature using the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation.[^o15]

## Derivation {#Derivation}

### Background {#Background}

The definition of the Gibbs function is

$$H = G + ST$$

where $H$ is the enthalpy defined by:

$$H = U + pV$$

Taking [differentials](wikipedia://en/23997203) of each definition to find $dH$ and $dG$, then using the [fundamental thermodynamic relation](wikipedia://en/5529757) (always true for [reversible](wikipedia://en/416681) or [irreversible](wikipedia://en/636094) [processes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermodynamic_process)):

$$dU = T\,dS - p\,dV$$

where $S$ is the [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891), $V$ is [volume](wikipedia://en/32498), (minus sign due to reversibility, in which $dU = 0$: work other than pressure-volume may be done and is equal to $-pV$) leads to the "reversed" form of the initial fundamental relation into a new master equation:

$$dG = - S\,dT + V\,dp$$

This is the [Gibbs free energy](wikipedia://en/238181) for a closed system. The Gibbs–Helmholtz equation can be derived by this second master equation, and the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) for [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives).[^o6]

::: {#o18 .proof cue=math-proof name="Derivation"}

**Derivation.**

Starting from the equation

$$dG = - S\,dT + V\,dp$$

for the differential of *G*, and remembering

$$H = G + T\,S ,$$

one computes the differential of the ratio $G/T$ by applying the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) of [differentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiation_%28mathematics%29) in the version for differentials:

$$\begin{aligned} d\left(\frac{G}{T}\right) &= \frac{T\, dG - G\, dT}{T^2} = \frac{T\, (-S \,dT + V \, dp) - G\, dT}{T^2} \\ &= \frac{-T\, S \,dT -G\,dT + T\, V \, dp}{T^2} = \frac{-(G + T\,S)\,dT + T\, V \, dp}{T^2} \\ &= \frac{-H \,dT + T\,V\,dp}{T^2} \end{aligned}\,\!$$

Therefore,

$$d\left(\frac{G}{T}\right) = -\frac{H}{T^2}\, dT + \frac{V}{T}\, dp \,\!$$

A comparison with the general expression for a total differential

$$d\left(\frac{G}{T}\right) = \left( \frac{\partial(G/T)}{\partial T} \right)_p \, dT + \left( \frac{\partial(G/T)}{\partial p} \right)_T \, dp$$

gives the change of $G/T$ with respect to $T$ at constant [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure) (i.e. when $dp = 0$), the Gibbs–Helmholtz equation:

$$\left( \frac{\partial(G/T)}{\partial T} \right)_p = -\frac{H}{T^2}$$

:::
