# List of numbers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="List of numbers"}

This is a list of notable [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690) and articles about them. The list does not contain all numbers in existence as most of the [number sets](wikipedia://en/26691) are infinite. Numbers may be included in the list based on their mathematical, historical or cultural notability, but all numbers have qualities that could arguably make them notable. Even the smallest "uninteresting" number is paradoxically interesting for that very property. This is known as the [interesting number paradox](wikipedia://en/506692).

:::

The definition of what is classed as a number is rather diffuse and based on historical distinctions. For example, the pair of numbers (3,4) is commonly regarded as a number when it is in the form of a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) (3+4i), but not when it is in the form of a [vector](wikipedia://en/23218956) (3,4). This list will also be categorized with the standard convention of [types of numbers](wikipedia://en/29528690).

This list focuses on numbers as [mathematical objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_objects) and is *not* a list of [numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_%28linguistics%29), which are linguistic devices: nouns, adjectives, or adverbs that *designate* numbers. The distinction is drawn between the *number* five (an [abstract object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_object) equal to 2+3), and the *numeral* five (the [noun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Noun) referring to the number).

## Natural numbers {#Natural_numbers}

Natural numbers are a subset of the integers and are of historical and pedagogical value as they can be used for [counting](wikipedia://en/275768) and often have ethno-cultural significance (see below). Beyond this, natural numbers are widely used as a building block for other number systems including the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024#Formal_construction) and [real numbers](wikipedia://en/296666). Natural numbers are those used for [counting](wikipedia://en/275768) (as in "there are *six* coins on the table") and [ordering](wikipedia://en/30330) (as in "this is the *third* largest city in the country"). In common language, words used for counting are "[cardinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_number_%28linguistics%29)" and words used for ordering are "[ordinal numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinal_number_%28linguistics%29)". Defined by the [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005), the natural numbers form an infinitely large set. Often referred to as "the naturals", the natural numbers are usually symbolised by a boldface $\mathbf{N}$ (or [blackboard bold](wikipedia://en/40276) $\mathbb{\mathbb{N}}$, Unicode 2115)*.*

The inclusion of [0](wikipedia://en/34513) in the set of natural numbers is ambiguous and subject to individual definitions. In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) and [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), 0 is typically considered a natural number. In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), it usually is not. The ambiguity can be solved with the terms "non-negative integers", which includes 0, and "positive integers", which does not.

Natural numbers may be used as [cardinal numbers](wikipedia://en/6173), which may go by [various names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_%28linguistics%29). Natural numbers may also be used as [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932).

## Integers {#Integers}

The integers are a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of numbers commonly encountered in [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). There are many [subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subsets) of the integers, including the [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers), [prime numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_numbers), [perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_numbers), etc. Many integers are notable for their mathematical properties. Integers are usually symbolised by a boldface $\mathbf{Z}$ (or [blackboard bold](wikipedia://en/40276) $\mathbb{\mathbb{Z}}$, Unicode 2124); this became the symbol for the integers based on the German word for "numbers" (*Zahlen).*

Notable integers include [−1](wikipedia://en/449568), the additive inverse of unity, and [0](wikipedia://en/34513), the [additive identity](wikipedia://en/4178225).

As with the natural numbers, the integers may also have cultural or practical significance. For instance, [−40](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/−40_%28number%29) is the equal point in the [Fahrenheit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fahrenheit) and [Celsius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celsius) scales.

### SI prefixes {#SI_prefixes}

One important use of integers is in [orders of magnitude](wikipedia://en/260914). A [power of 10](wikipedia://en/1161104) is a number 10<sup>*k*</sup>, where *k* is an integer. For instance, with *k* = 0, 1, 2, 3, ..., the appropriate powers of ten are 1, 10, 100, 1000, ... Powers of ten can also be fractional: for instance, *k* = -3 gives 1/1000, or 0.001. This is used in [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715), real numbers are written in the form *m* × 10<sup>*n*</sup>. The number 394,000 is written in this form as 3.94 × 10<sup>5</sup>.

Integers are used as [prefixes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_prefix) in the [SI system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SI_system). A **metric prefix** is a [unit prefix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_prefix) that precedes a basic unit of measure to indicate a [multiple](wikipedia://en/3912709) or [fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) of the unit. Each prefix has a unique symbol that is prepended to the unit symbol. The prefix *[kilo-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilo-)*, for example, may be added to *gram* to indicate *multiplication* by one thousand: one kilogram is equal to one thousand grams. The prefix *[milli-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milli-)*, likewise, may be added to *metre* to indicate *division* by one thousand; one millimetre is equal to one thousandth of a metre.

## Rational numbers {#Rational_numbers}

A rational number is any number that can be expressed as the [quotient](wikipedia://en/332640) or [fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) $p/q$ of two [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), a [numerator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerator) $p$ and a non-zero [denominator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denominator) $q$.[^o104] Since $q$ may be equal to 1, every integer is trivially a rational number. The [set](wikipedia://en/26691) of all rational numbers, often referred to as "the rationals", the field of rationals or the field of rational numbers is usually denoted by a boldface $\mathbf{Q}$ (or [blackboard bold](wikipedia://en/40276) $\mathbb{Q}$, Unicode 211A);[^o105] it was thus denoted in 1895 by [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) after *quoziente*, Italian for "[quotient](wikipedia://en/332640)".

Rational numbers such as 0.12 can be represented in [infinitely](wikipedia://en/20110824) many ways, e.g. *zero-point-one-two* (0.12), *three twenty-fifths* ($\tfrac{3}{25}$), *nine seventy-fifths* ($\tfrac{9}{75}$), etc. This can be mitigated by representing rational numbers in a canonical form as an irreducible fraction.

## Real numbers {#Real_numbers}

[Real numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) are least upper bounds of sets of rational numbers that are bounded above, or greatest lower bounds of sets of rational numbers that are bounded below, or limits of convergent sequences of rational numbers. Real numbers that are not rational numbers are called [irrational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrational_numbers). The real numbers are categorised as algebraic numbers (which are the root of a polynomial with rational coefficients) or transcendental numbers, which are not; all rational numbers are algebraic.

## Numbers without specific values {#Numbers_without_specific_values}

Many languages have words expressing [indefinite and fictitious numbers](wikipedia://en/538913)—inexact terms of indefinite size, used for comic effect, for exaggeration, as [placeholder names](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Placeholder_name), or when precision is unnecessary or undesirable. One technical term for such words is "non-numerical vague quantifier".[^o266] Such words designed to indicate large quantities can be called "indefinite hyperbolic numerals".[^o267]
