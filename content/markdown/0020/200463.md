# Transitive relation

::: {#o1 .statement cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Transitive relation"}

**Transitive relation.**

A relation $R$ on a set $X$ is transitive if, for all elements $a$, $b$, $c$ in $X$, whenever $R$ relates $a$ to $b$ and $b$ to $c$, then $R$ also relates $a$ to $c$.

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Transitive relation"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931) $R$ on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $X$ is **transitive** if, for all elements $a$, $b$, $c$ in $X$, whenever $R$ relates $a$ to $b$ and $b$ to $c$, then $R$ also relates $a$ to $c$.

:::

Every [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order) and every [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) is transitive. For example, less than and [equality](wikipedia://en/90446) among [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) are both transitive: If $a < b$ and $b < c$ then $a < c$; and if $x = y$ and $y = z$ then $x = z$.

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A [homogeneous relation](wikipedia://en/12987179) $R$ on the set $X$ is a *transitive relation* if,[^o5]

for all $a, b, c \in X$, if $a R b$ and $b R c$, then $a R c$.

Or in terms of [first-order logic](wikipedia://en/10983):

$$\forall a,b,c \in X: (aRb \wedge bRc) \Rightarrow aRc,$$

where $a R b$ is the [infix notation](wikipedia://en/199014) for $(a, b) \in R$.

:::

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

As a non-mathematical example, the relation "is an ancestor of" is transitive. For example, if Amy is an ancestor of Becky, and Becky is an ancestor of Carrie, then Amy is also an ancestor of Carrie.

On the other hand, "is the birth mother of" is not a transitive relation, because if Alice is the birth mother of Brenda, and Brenda is the birth mother of Claire, then it does not follow that Alice is the birth mother of Claire. In fact, this relation is [antitransitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitransitive): Alice can *never* be the birth mother of Claire.

Non-transitive, non-antitransitive relations include sports fixtures (playoff schedules), 'knows' and 'talks to'.

The examples "is greater than", "is at least as great as", and "is equal to" ([equality](wikipedia://en/90446)) are transitive relations on various sets. As are the set of real numbers or the set of natural numbers:

whenever *x* > *y* and *y* > *z*, then also *x* > *z*

whenever *x* ≥ *y* and *y* ≥ *z*, then also *x* ≥ *z*

whenever *x* = *y* and *y* = *z*, then also *x* = *z*.

More examples of transitive relations:

- "is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of" (set inclusion, a relation on sets)
- "divides" ([divisibility](wikipedia://en/49492), a relation on natural numbers)
- "implies" ([implication](wikipedia://en/658808), symbolized by "⇒", a relation on [propositions](wikipedia://en/81094))

Examples of non-transitive relations:

- "is the [successor](wikipedia://en/325637) of" (a relation on natural numbers)
- "is a member of the set" (symbolized as "∈")[^o8]
- "is [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) to" (a relation on lines in [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417))

The [empty relation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Empty_relation) on any set $X$ is transitive[^o9] because there are no elements $a,b,c \in X$ such that $aRb$ and $bRc$, and hence the transitivity condition is [vacuously true](wikipedia://en/51672). A relation $R$ containing only one [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362) is also transitive: if the ordered pair is of the form $(x, x)$ for some $x \in X$ the only such elements $a,b,c \in X$ are $a=b=c=x$, and indeed in this case $aRc$, while if the ordered pair is not of the form $(x, x)$ then there are no such elements $a,b,c \in X$ and hence $R$ is vacuously transitive.

Vacuous transitivity is transitivity when in a relation there are no ordered pairs of the form (*a*,*b*) and (*b*,*c*).

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Closure properties {#Closure_properties}

- The [converse](wikipedia://en/3941020) (inverse) of a transitive relation is always transitive. For instance, knowing that "is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of" is transitive and "is a [superset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Superset) of" is its converse, one can conclude that the latter is transitive as well.
- The intersection of two transitive relations is always transitive.[^o13] For instance, knowing that "was born before" and "has the same first name as" are transitive, one can conclude that "was born before and also has the same first name as" is also transitive.
- The union of two transitive relations need not be transitive. For instance, "was born before or has the same first name as" is not a transitive relation, since e.g. [Herbert Hoover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Hoover) is related to [Franklin D. Roosevelt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_D._Roosevelt), who is in turn related to [Franklin Pierce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Franklin_Pierce), while Hoover is not related to Franklin Pierce.
- The complement of a transitive relation need not be transitive.[^o14] For instance, while "equal to" is transitive, "not equal to" is only transitive on sets with at most one element.

### Other properties {#Other_properties}

A transitive relation is [asymmetric](wikipedia://en/1229368) if and only if it is [irreflexive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irreflexive_relation).[^o16]

A transitive relation need not be [reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458). When it is, it is called a [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582). For example, on set *X* = {1,2,3}:

- *R* = {(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (1,3), (3,2)} is reflexive, but not transitive, as the pair (1,2) is absent,
- *R* = {(1,1), (2,2), (3,3), (1,3)} is reflexive as well as transitive, so it is a preorder,
- *R* = {(1,1), (2,2), (3,3)} is reflexive as well as transitive, another preorder,
- *R* = {(1,2), (2,3), (1,3)} is transitive, but not reflexive.

As a counter example, the relation $<$ on the real numbers is transitive, but not reflexive.

:::

## Transitive extensions and transitive closure {#Transitive_extensions_and_transitive_closure}

Let $R$ be a binary relation on set $X$. The *transitive extension* of $R$, denoted $R_{1}$, is the smallest binary relation on $X$ such that $R_{1}$ contains $R$, and if $(a, b) \in R$ and $(b, c) \in R$ then $(a, c) \in R_{1}$.[^o18] For example, suppose $X$ is a set of towns, some of which are connected by roads. Let $R$ be the relation on towns where $(A, B) \in R$ if there is a road directly linking town $A$ and town $B$. This relation need not be transitive. The transitive extension of this relation can be defined by $(A, C) \in R_{1}$ if you can travel between towns $A$ and $C$ by using at most two roads.

If a relation is transitive then its transitive extension is itself, that is, if $R$ is a transitive relation then $R_{1} = R$.

The transitive extension of $R_{1}$ would be denoted by $R_{2}$, and continuing in this way, in general, the transitive extension of $R_{i}$ would be $R_{i + 1}$. The *transitive closure* of $R$, denoted by $R*$ or $R^{\infty}$ is the set union of $R$, $R_{1}$, $R_{2}$, ... .[^o19]

The transitive closure of a relation is a transitive relation.[^o19]

The relation "is the birth parent of" on a set of people is not a transitive relation. However, in biology the need often arises to consider birth parenthood over an arbitrary number of generations: the relation "is a birth ancestor of" *is* a transitive relation and it is the transitive closure of the relation "is the birth parent of".

For the example of towns and roads above, $(A, C) \in R*$ provided you can travel between towns $A$ and $C$ using any number of roads.

## Relation types that require transitivity {#Relation_types_that_require_transitivity}

- [Preorder](wikipedia://en/23582) – a [reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458) and transitive relation
- [Partial order](wikipedia://en/23572) – an [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/1176) preorder
- [Total preorder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_preorder) – a [connected](wikipedia://en/57495962) (formerly called total) preorder
- [Equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) – a [symmetric](wikipedia://en/200459) preorder
- [Strict weak ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strict_weak_ordering) – a strict partial order in which incomparability is an equivalence relation
- [Total ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_ordering) – a connected (total), antisymmetric, and transitive relation

## Counting transitive relations {#Counting_transitive_relations}

No general formula that counts the number of transitive relations on a finite set is known.[^o22] However, there is a formula for finding the number of relations that are simultaneously reflexive, symmetric, and transitive – in other words, [equivalence relations](wikipedia://en/9259) – , those that are symmetric and transitive, those that are symmetric, transitive, and antisymmetric, and those that are total, transitive, and antisymmetric. Pfeiffer[^o23] has made some progress in this direction, expressing relations with combinations of these properties in terms of each other, but still calculating any one is difficult. See also Brinkmann and McKay (2005)[^o24] and Mala (2022).[^o25]

Since the reflexivization of any transitive relation is a [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582), the number of transitive relations on an *n*-element set is at most the 2<sup>*n*</sup>-fold of the number of preorders, thus it is asymptotically $2^{(1/4+o(1))n^2}$ by results of Kleitman and Rothschild.[^o26]

## Related properties {#Related_properties}

*Image: Rock-paper-scissors.svg.* The [Rock–paper–scissors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock–paper–scissors) game is based on an intransitive and antitransitive relation "*x* beats *y*".

A relation *R* is called *[intransitive](wikipedia://en/614040)* if it is not transitive, that is, if *xRy* and *yRz*, but not *xRz*, for some *x*, *y*, *z*. In contrast, a relation *R* is called *[antitransitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitransitive)* if *xRy* and *yRz* always implies that *xRz* does not hold. For example, the relation defined by *xRy* if *xy* is an [even number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Even_number) is intransitive,[^o28] but not antitransitive.[^o29] The relation defined by *xRy* if *x* is even and *y* is [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) is both transitive and antitransitive.[^o30] The relation defined by *xRy* if *x* is the [successor](wikipedia://en/325637) number of *y* is both intransitive[^o31] and antitransitive.[^o32] Unexpected examples of intransitivity arise in situations such as political questions or group preferences.[^o33]

Generalized to stochastic versions (*[stochastic transitivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_transitivity)*), the study of transitivity finds applications of in [decision theory](wikipedia://en/446216), [psychometrics](wikipedia://en/24982) and [utility models](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Utilitarianism).[^o34]

A *[quasitransitive relation](wikipedia://en/6770393)* is another generalization;[^o14] it is required to be transitive only on its non-symmetric part. Such relations are used in [social choice theory](wikipedia://en/1745670) or [microeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microeconomics).[^o35]

::: {#o36 .statement kind=statement/proposition cue=inline-head name="Proposition"}

**Proposition:** If *R* is a [univalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Univalent_relation), then R;R<sup>T</sup> is transitive.

proof: Suppose $x R;R^T y R;R^T z.$ Then there are *a* and *b* such that $x R a R^T y R b R^T z .$ Since *R* is univalent, *yRb* and *aR*<sup>T</sup>*y* imply *a*=*b*. Therefore *x*R*a*R<sup>T</sup>*z*, hence *x*R;R<sup>T</sup>*z* and R;R<sup>T</sup> is transitive.

:::

::: {#o37 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=inline-head name="Corollary"}

**Corollary**: If *R* is univalent, then R;R<sup>T</sup> is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on the domain of *R*.

proof: R;R<sup>T</sup> is symmetric and reflexive on its domain. With univalence of *R*, the transitive requirement for equivalence is fulfilled.

:::
