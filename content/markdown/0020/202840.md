# Adjugate matrix

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Adjugate matrix"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the **adjugate** or **classical adjoint** $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ of a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) $\mathbf{A}$ is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of its [cofactor matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_matrix).[^o2][^o3] It is occasionally known as **adjunct matrix**,[^o4][^o5] or "adjoint",[^o6] though that normally refers to a different concept, the [adjoint operator](wikipedia://en/667175) which for a real matrix is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844).

:::

The product of a matrix with its adjugate gives a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) (entries not on the main diagonal are zero) whose diagonal entries are the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the original matrix:

$$\mathbf{A} \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \det(\mathbf{A}) \mathbf{I},$$

where $\mathbf{I}$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718) of the same size as $\mathbf{A}$. Consequently, the multiplicative inverse of an [invertible matrix](wikipedia://en/217122) can be found by dividing its adjugate by its determinant.

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The **adjugate** of $\mathbf{A}$ is the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of the [cofactor matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_matrix) $\mathbf{C}$ of $\mathbf{A}$,

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{C}^\mathsf{T}.$$

In more detail, suppose $R$ is a ([unital](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_algebra)) [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) and $\mathbf{A}$ is an $n \times n$ matrix with entries from $R$. The $(i, j)$-*[minor](wikipedia://en/262107)* of $\mathbf{A}$, denoted $M_{ij}$, is the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of the $(n - 1) \times (n - 1)$ matrix that results from deleting row $i$ and column $j$ of $\mathbf{A}$. The [cofactor matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_%28linear_algebra%29#Inverse_of_a_matrix) of $\mathbf{A}$ is the $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{C}$ whose $(i, j)$ entry is the $(i, j)$ *[cofactor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cofactor_%28linear_algebra%29)* of $\mathbf{A}$, which is the $(i, j)$-minor times a sign factor:

$$\mathbf{C} = \left((-1)^{i+j} M_{ij}\right)_{1 \le i, j \le n}.$$

The adjugate of $\mathbf{A}$ is the transpose of $\mathbf{C}$, that is, the $n \times n$ matrix whose $(i, j)$ entry is the $(j,i)$ cofactor of $\mathbf{A}$,

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{C}^\mathsf{T} = \left((-1)^{i+j} {M}_{ji}\right)_{1 \le i, j \le n}.$$

### Important consequence {#Important_consequence}

The adjugate is defined so that the product of $\mathbf{A}$ with its adjugate yields a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) whose diagonal entries are the determinant $\det(\mathbf{A})$. That is,

$$\mathbf{A} \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) \mathbf{A} = \det(\mathbf{A}) \mathbf{I},$$

where $\mathbf{I}$ is the $n \times n$ [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718). This is a consequence of the [Laplace expansion](wikipedia://en/3506553) of the determinant.

The above formula implies one of the fundamental results in matrix algebra, that $\mathbf{A}$ is [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\det(\mathbf{A})$ is an [invertible element](wikipedia://en/289666) of $R$. When this holds, the equation above yields

$$\begin{aligned} \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) &= \det(\mathbf{A}) \mathbf{A}^{-1}, \\ \mathbf{A}^{-1} &= \det(\mathbf{A})^{-1} \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}). \end{aligned}$$

## Examples {#Examples}

### 1 × 1 generic matrix {#1_×_1_generic_matrix}

Since the determinant of a 0 × 0 matrix is 1, the adjugate of any 1 × 1 matrix ([complex](wikipedia://en/5826) scalar) is $\mathbf{I} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 \end{bmatrix}$. Observe that $\mathbf{A} \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{A} = (\det \mathbf{A}) \mathbf {I}.$

### 2 × 2 generic matrix {#2_×_2_generic_matrix}

The adjugate of the 2 × 2 matrix

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix}$$

is

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \begin{bmatrix} d & -b \\ -c & a \end{bmatrix}.$$

By direct computation,

$$\mathbf{A} \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \begin{bmatrix} ad - bc & 0 \\ 0 & ad - bc \end{bmatrix} = (\det \mathbf{A})\mathbf{I}.$$

In this case, it is also true that $\det$($\operatorname{adj}$(**A**)) = $\det$(**A**) and hence that $\operatorname{adj}$($\operatorname{adj}$(**A**)) = **A**.

### 3 × 3 generic matrix {#3_×_3_generic_matrix}

Consider a 3 × 3 matrix

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} a_{1} & a_{2} & a_{3} \\ b_{1} & b_{2} & b_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} & c_{3} \end{bmatrix}.$$

Its cofactor matrix is

$$\mathbf{C} = \begin{bmatrix} +\begin{vmatrix} b_{2} & b_{3} \\ c_{2} & c_{3} \end{vmatrix} & -\begin{vmatrix} b_{1} & b_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{3} \end{vmatrix} & +\begin{vmatrix} b_{1} & b_{2} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} \end{vmatrix} \\ \\ -\begin{vmatrix} a_{2} & a_{3} \\ c_{2} & c_{3} \end{vmatrix} & +\begin{vmatrix} a_{1} & a_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{3} \end{vmatrix} & -\begin{vmatrix} a_{1} & a_{2} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} \end{vmatrix} \\ \\ +\begin{vmatrix} a_{2} & a_{3} \\ b_{2} & b_{3} \end{vmatrix} & -\begin{vmatrix} a_{1} & a_{3} \\ b_{1} & b_{3} \end{vmatrix} & +\begin{vmatrix} a_{1} & a_{2} \\ b_{1} & b_{2} \end{vmatrix} \end{bmatrix},$$

where

$$\begin{vmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{vmatrix} = \det\!\begin{bmatrix} a & b \\ c & d \end{bmatrix} .$$

Its adjugate is the transpose of its cofactor matrix,

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{C}^\mathsf{T} = \begin{bmatrix} +\begin{vmatrix} b_{2} & b_{3} \\ c_{2} & c_{3} \end{vmatrix} & -\begin{vmatrix} a_{2} & a_{3} \\ c_{2} & c_{3} \end{vmatrix} & +\begin{vmatrix} a_{2} & a_{3} \\ b_{2} & b_{3} \end{vmatrix} \\ & & \\ -\begin{vmatrix} b_{1} & b_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{3} \end{vmatrix} & +\begin{vmatrix} a_{1} & a_{3} \\ c_{1} & c_{3} \end{vmatrix} & -\begin{vmatrix} a_{1} & a_{3} \\ b_{1} & b_{3} \end{vmatrix} \\ & & \\ +\begin{vmatrix} b_{1} & b_{2} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} \end{vmatrix} & -\begin{vmatrix} a_{1} & a_{2} \\ c_{1} & c_{2} \end{vmatrix} & +\begin{vmatrix} a_{1} & a_{2} \\ b_{1} & b_{2} \end{vmatrix} \end{bmatrix}.$$

### 3 × 3 numeric matrix {#3_×_3_numeric_matrix}

As a specific example, we have

$$\operatorname{adj}\!\begin{bmatrix} -3 & 2 & -5 \\ -1 & 0 & -2 \\ 3 & -4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} -8 & 18 & -4 \\ -5 & 12 & -1 \\ 4 & -6 & 2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

It is easy to check the adjugate is the [inverse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_matrix) times the determinant, $-6$.

The $-1$ in the second row, third column of the adjugate was computed as follows. The (2,3) entry of the adjugate is the (3,2) cofactor of **A**. This cofactor is computed using the [submatrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submatrix) obtained by deleting the third row and second column of the original matrix **A**,

$$\begin{bmatrix} -3 & -5 \\ -1 & -2 \end{bmatrix}.$$

The (3,2) cofactor is a sign times the determinant of this submatrix:

$$(-1)^{3+2}\operatorname{det}\!\begin{bmatrix}-3&-5\\-1&-2\end{bmatrix} = -(-3 \cdot -2 - -5 \cdot -1) = -1,$$

and this is the (2,3) entry of the adjugate.

## Properties {#Properties}

For any $n \times n$ matrix $\mathbf{A}$, elementary computations show that adjugates have the following properties:

- $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{I}) = \mathbf{I}$, where $\mathbf{I}$ is the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).
- $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{0}$, where $\mathbf{0}$ is the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888), except that if $n=1$ then $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{0}) = \mathbf{I}$.
- $\operatorname{adj}(c \mathbf{A}) = c^{n - 1}\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ for any scalar $c$.
- $$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}^\mathsf{T}) = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})^\mathsf{T}.$$
- $$\det(\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})) = (\det \mathbf{A})^{n-1}.$$
- If $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible, then $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = (\det \mathbf{A}) \mathbf{A}^{-1}$. It follows that:
  - $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ is invertible with inverse $(\det \mathbf{A})^{-1}\mathbf{A}$.
  - $$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}^{-1}) = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})^{-1}.$$
- $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ is entrywise [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000) in $\mathbf{A}$. In particular, over the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or complex numbers, the adjugate is a [smooth function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) of the entries of $\mathbf{A}$.

Over the complex numbers,

- $\operatorname{adj}(\overline\mathbf{A}) = \overline{\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})}$, where the bar denotes [complex conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_conjugation).
- $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}^*) = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})^*$, where the asterisk denotes [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548).

Suppose that $\mathbf{B}$ is another $n \times n$ matrix. Then

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{AB}) = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}).$$

This can be [proved](wikipedia://en/82285) in three ways. One way, valid for any commutative ring, is a direct computation using the [Cauchy–Binet formula](wikipedia://en/357354). The second way, valid for the real or complex numbers, is to first observe that for invertible matrices $\mathbf{A}$ and $\mathbf{B}$,

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = (\det \mathbf{B})\mathbf{B}^{-1}(\det \mathbf{A})\mathbf{A}^{-1} = (\det \mathbf{AB})(\mathbf{AB})^{-1} = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{AB}).$$

Because every non-invertible matrix is the limit of invertible matrices, [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122) of the adjugate then implies that the formula remains true when one of $\mathbf{A}$ or $\mathbf{B}$ is not invertible.

A [corollary](wikipedia://en/168865) of the previous formula is that, for any non-negative [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $k$,

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}^k) = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})^k.$$

If $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible, then the above formula also holds for negative $k$.

From the identity

$$(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})\mathbf{B} = \det(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B}),$$

we deduce

$$\mathbf{A}\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{B}\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B})\mathbf{A}.$$

Suppose that $\mathbf{A}$ [commutes](wikipedia://en/25264990) with $\mathbf{B}$. Multiplying the identity $\mathbf{AB} = \mathbf{BA}$ on the left and right by $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ proves that

$$\det(\mathbf{A})\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{B} = \det(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{B}\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}).$$

If $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible, this implies that $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ also commutes with $\mathbf{B}$. Over the real or complex numbers, continuity implies that $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ commutes with $\mathbf{B}$ even when $\mathbf{A}$ is not invertible.

Finally, there is a more general proof than the second proof, which only requires that an *n* × *n* matrix has entries over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) with at least 2*n* + 1 elements (e.g. a 5 × 5 matrix over the integers [modulo](wikipedia://en/20087) 11). $\det(\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{I})$ is a polynomial in *t* with [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) at most *n*, so it has at most *n* [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial). Note that the *ij*th entry of $\operatorname{adj}((\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{I})(\mathbf{B}))$ is a polynomial of at most order *n*, and likewise for $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{I})\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{B})$. These two polynomials at the *ij*th entry agree on at least *n* + 1 points, as we have at least *n* + 1 elements of the field where $\mathbf{A}+t\mathbf{I}$ is invertible, and we have proven the identity for invertible matrices. Polynomials of degree *n* which agree on *n* + 1 points must be identical (subtract them from each other and you have *n* + 1 roots for a polynomial of degree at most *n* – a contradiction unless their difference is identically zero). As the two polynomials are identical, they take the same value for every value of *t*. Thus, they take the same value when *t* = 0.

Using the above properties and other elementary computations, it is straightforward to show that if $\mathbf{A}$ has one of the following properties, then $\operatorname{adj}\mathbf{A}$ does as well:

- [upper triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular_matrix),
- [lower triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lower_triangular_matrix),
- [diagonal](wikipedia://en/174080),
- [orthogonal](wikipedia://en/105620),
- [unitary](wikipedia://en/129222),
- [symmetric](wikipedia://en/126474),
- [Hermitian](wikipedia://en/189682),
- [normal](wikipedia://en/170366).

If $\mathbf{A}$ is [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055), then $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ is skew-symmetric for even *n* and symmetric for odd *n*. Similarly, if $\mathbf{A}$ is [skew-Hermitian](wikipedia://en/509788), then $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ is skew-Hermitian for even *n* and Hermitian for odd *n*.

If $\mathbf{A}$ is invertible, then, as noted above, there is a formula for $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ in terms of the determinant and inverse of $\mathbf{A}$. When $\mathbf{A}$ is not invertible, the adjugate satisfies different but closely related formulas.

- If $\operatorname{rk}(\mathbf{A}) \le n - 2$, then $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{0}$.
- If $\operatorname{rk}(\mathbf{A}) = n - 1$, then $\operatorname{rk}(\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})) = 1$. (Some minor is non-zero, so $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ is non-zero and hence has [rank](wikipedia://en/26561) at least one; the identity $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{0}$ implies that the [dimension](wikipedia://en/38267) of the [nullspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nullspace) of $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ is at least $n - 1$, so its rank is at most one.) It follows that $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \alpha\mathbf{xy}^{T}$, where $\alpha$ is a scalar and $\mathbf{x}$ and $\mathbf{y}$ are vectors such that $\mathbf{Ax} = \mathbf{0}$ and $\mathbf{A}^{T} \mathbf{y} = \mathbf{0}$.

### Column substitution and Cramer's rule {#Column_substitution_and_Cramer's_rule}

Partition $\mathbf{A}$ into [column vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_vector):

$$\mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix}\mathbf{a}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_n\end{bmatrix}.$$

Let $\mathbf{b}$ be a column vector of size $n$. Fix $1 \le i \le n$ and consider the matrix formed by replacing column $i$ of $\mathbf{A}$ by $\mathbf{b}$:

$$(\mathbf{A} \stackrel{i}{\leftarrow} \mathbf{b})\ \stackrel{\text{def}}{=}\ \begin{bmatrix} \mathbf{a}_1 & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_{i-1} & \mathbf{b} & \mathbf{a}_{i+1} & \cdots & \mathbf{a}_n \end{bmatrix}.$$

Laplace expand the determinant of this matrix along column $i$. The result is entry $i$ of the product $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}$. Collecting these determinants for the different possible $i$ yields an equality of column vectors

$$\left(\det(\mathbf{A} \stackrel{i}{\leftarrow} \mathbf{b})\right)_{i=1}^n = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}.$$

This formula has the following concrete consequence. Consider the [linear system of equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_system_of_equations)

$$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}.$$

Assume that $\mathbf{A}$ is [non-singular](wikipedia://en/245528). Multiplying this system on the left by $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ and dividing by the determinant yields

$$\mathbf{x} = \frac{\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})\mathbf{b}}{\det \mathbf{A}}.$$

Applying the previous formula to this situation yields **Cramer's rule**,

$$x_i = \frac{\det(\mathbf{A} \stackrel{i}{\leftarrow} \mathbf{b})}{\det \mathbf{A}},$$

where $x_{i}$ is the $i$th entry of $\mathbf{x}$.

### Characteristic polynomial {#Characteristic_polynomial}

Let the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of $\mathbf{A}$ be

$$p(s) = \det(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \sum_{i=0}^n p_i s^i \in R[s].$$

The first [divided difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divided_difference) of $p$ is a [symmetric polynomial](wikipedia://en/1440207) of degree $n - 1$,

$$\Delta p(s, t) = \frac{p(s) - p(t)}{s - t} = \sum_{0 \le j + k < n} p_{j+k+1} s^j t^k \in R[s, t].$$

Multiply $s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}$ by its adjugate. Since $p(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{0}$ by the [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547), some elementary manipulations reveal

$$\operatorname{adj}(s\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A}) = \Delta p(s\mathbf{I}, \mathbf{A}).$$

In particular, the [resolvent](wikipedia://en/3378540) of $\mathbf{A}$ is defined to be

$$R(z; \mathbf{A}) = (z\mathbf{I} - \mathbf{A})^{-1},$$

and by the above formula, this is equal to

$$R(z; \mathbf{A}) = \frac{\Delta p(z\mathbf{I}, \mathbf{A})}{p(z)}.$$

### Jacobi's formula {#Jacobi's_formula}

The adjugate also appears in [Jacobi's formula](wikipedia://en/2350347) for the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) of the determinant. If $\mathbf{A}(t)$ is [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable), then

$$\frac{d(\det \mathbf{A})}{dt}(t) = \operatorname{tr}\left(\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}(t)) \mathbf{A}'(t)\right).$$

It follows that the [total derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_derivative) of the determinant is the transpose of the adjugate:

$$d(\det \mathbf{A})_{\mathbf{A}_0} = \operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}_0)^{\mathsf{T}}.$$

:::

::: {#o23 .statement cue=named-heading name="Cayley–Hamilton formula"}

### Cayley–Hamilton formula {#Cayley–Hamilton_formula}

Let $p_{\mathbf{A}}(t)$ be the characteristic polynomial of $\mathbf{A}$. The [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](wikipedia://en/173547) states that

$$p_{\mathbf{A}}(\mathbf{A}) = \mathbf{0}.$$

Separating the constant term and multiplying the equation by $\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})$ gives an expression for the adjugate that depends only on $\mathbf{A}$ and the coefficients of $p_{\mathbf{A}}(t)$. These coefficients can be explicitly represented in terms of [traces](wikipedia://en/43270) of powers of $\mathbf{A}$ using complete exponential [Bell polynomials](wikipedia://en/572498). The resulting formula is

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}) = \sum_{s=0}^{n-1} \mathbf{A}^{s} \sum_{k_1, k_2, \ldots, k_{n-1}} \prod_{\ell=1}^{n-1} \frac{(-1)^{k_\ell+1}}{\ell^{k_\ell}k_{\ell}!}\operatorname{tr}(\mathbf{A}^\ell)^{k_\ell},$$

where $n$ is the dimension of $\mathbf{A}$, and the sum is taken over $s$ and all sequences of $k_{l} \ge 0$ satisfying the linear [Diophantine equation](wikipedia://en/9109)

$$s+\sum_{\ell=1}^{n-1}\ell k_\ell = n - 1.$$

For the 2 × 2 case, this gives

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})=\mathbf{I}_2(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}) - \mathbf{A}.$$

For the 3 × 3 case, this gives

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})=\frac{1}{2}\mathbf{I}_3\!\left( (\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A})^2-\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}^2\right) - \mathbf{A}(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}) + \mathbf{A}^2 .$$

For the 4 × 4 case, this gives

$$\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})= \frac{1}{6}\mathbf{I}_4\!\left( (\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A})^3 - 3\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}^2 + 2\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}^{3} \right) - \frac{1}{2}\mathbf{A}\!\left( (\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A})^2 - \operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}^2\right) + \mathbf{A}^2(\operatorname{tr}\mathbf{A}) - \mathbf{A}^3.$$

The same formula follows directly from the terminating step of the [Faddeev–LeVerrier algorithm](wikipedia://en/49598229), which efficiently determines the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) of $\mathbf{A}$.

In general, adjugate matrix of arbitrary dimension N matrix can be computed by Einstein's convention.

$$(\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}))_{i_N}^{j_N} = \frac{1}{(N-1)!} \epsilon_{i_1 i_2 \ldots i_N} \epsilon^{j_1 j_2 \ldots j_N} A_{j_1}^{i_1} A_{j_2}^{i_2} \ldots A_{j_{N-1}}^{i_{N-1}}$$

## Relation to exterior algebras {#Relation_to_exterior_algebras}

The adjugate can be viewed in abstract terms using [exterior algebras](wikipedia://en/221537). Let $V$ be an $n$-dimensional [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). The [exterior product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_product) defines a bilinear pairing

$$V \times \wedge^{n-1} V \to \wedge^n V.$$

Abstractly, $\wedge^n V$ is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to $\mathbf{R}$, and under any such isomorphism the exterior product is a [perfect pairing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_pairing). That is, it yields an isomorphism

$$\phi \colon V\ \xrightarrow{\cong}\ \operatorname{Hom}(\wedge^{n-1} V, \wedge^n V).$$

This isomorphism sends each $\mathbf{v} \in V$ to the map $\phi_{\mathbf{v}}$ defined by

$$\phi_\mathbf{v}(\alpha) = \mathbf{v} \wedge \alpha.$$

Suppose that $T : V \to V$ is a [linear transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation). [Pullback](wikipedia://en/9413508) by the $(n - 1)$th exterior power of $T$ induces a morphism of $\operatorname{Hom}$ spaces. The **adjugate** of $T$ is the composite

$$V\ \xrightarrow{\phi}\ \operatorname{Hom}(\wedge^{n-1} V, \wedge^n V)\ \xrightarrow{(\wedge^{n-1} T)^*}\ \operatorname{Hom}(\wedge^{n-1} V, \wedge^n V)\ \xrightarrow{\phi^{-1}}\ V.$$

If $V = \mathbf{R}^{n}$ is endowed with its [canonical basis](wikipedia://en/1028158) $\mathbf{e}_{1}, ..., \mathbf{e}_{n}$, and if the matrix of $T$ in this [basis](wikipedia://en/18420) is $\mathbf{A}$, then the adjugate of $T$ is the adjugate of $\mathbf{A}$. To see why, give $\wedge^{n-1} \mathbf{R}^n$ the basis

$$\{\mathbf{e}_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \hat\mathbf{e}_k \wedge \dots \wedge \mathbf{e}_n\}_{k=1}^n.$$

Fix a basis vector $\mathbf{e}_{i}$ of $\mathbf{R}^{n}$. The image of $\mathbf{e}_{i}$ under $\phi$ is determined by where it sends basis vectors:

$$\phi_{\mathbf{e}_i}(\mathbf{e}_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \hat\mathbf{e}_k \wedge \dots \wedge \mathbf{e}_n) = \begin{cases} (-1)^{i-1} \mathbf{e}_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \mathbf{e}_n, &\text{if}\ k = i, \\ 0 &\text{otherwise.} \end{cases}$$

On basis vectors, the $(n - 1)$st exterior power of $T$ is

$$\mathbf{e}_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \hat\mathbf{e}_j \wedge \dots \wedge \mathbf{e}_n \mapsto \sum_{k=1}^n (\det A_{jk}) \mathbf{e}_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \hat\mathbf{e}_k \wedge \dots \wedge \mathbf{e}_n.$$

Each of these terms maps to zero under $\phi_{\mathbf{e}_i}$ except the $k = i$ term. Therefore, the pullback of $\phi_{\mathbf{e}_i}$ is the linear transformation for which

$$\mathbf{e}_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \hat\mathbf{e}_j \wedge \dots \wedge \mathbf{e}_n \mapsto (-1)^{i-1} (\det A_{ji}) \mathbf{e}_1 \wedge \dots \wedge \mathbf{e}_n.$$

That is, it equals

$$\sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{i+j} (\det A_{ji})\phi_{\mathbf{e}_j}.$$

Applying the inverse of $\phi$ shows that the adjugate of $T$ is the linear transformation for which

$$\mathbf{e}_i \mapsto \sum_{j=1}^n (-1)^{i+j}(\det A_{ji})\mathbf{e}_j.$$

Consequently, its matrix representation is the adjugate of $\mathbf{A}$.

If $V$ is endowed with an [inner product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inner_product) and a volume form, then the map $\varphi$ can be decomposed further. In this case, $\varphi$ can be understood as the composite of the [Hodge star operator](wikipedia://en/310914) and dualization. Specifically, if $\omega$ is the volume form, then it, together with the inner product, determines an isomorphism

$$\omega^\vee \colon \wedge^n V \to \mathbf{R}.$$

This induces an isomorphism

$$\operatorname{Hom}(\wedge^{n-1} \mathbf{R}^n, \wedge^n \mathbf{R}^n) \cong \wedge^{n-1} (\mathbf{R}^n)^\vee.$$

A vector $\mathbf{v}$ in $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ corresponds to the linear functional

$$(\alpha \mapsto \omega^\vee(\mathbf{v} \wedge \alpha)) \in \wedge^{n-1} (\mathbf{R}^n)^\vee.$$

By the definition of the Hodge star operator, this linear functional is dual to $*\mathbf{v}$. That is, $\omega^{\lor}\circ \varphi$ equals $\mathbf{v} \mapsto *\mathbf{v}^{\lor}$.

## Higher adjugates {#Higher_adjugates}

Let $\mathbf{A}$ be an $n \times n$ matrix, and fix $r \ge 0$. The **$r$th higher adjugate** of $\mathbf{A}$ is an $\binom{n}{r} \!\times\! \binom{n}{r}$ matrix, denoted $\operatorname{adj}_{r} \mathbf{A}$, whose entries are indexed by size $r$ [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) $I$ and $J$ of $\{1, ..., m\}$ . Let $I^{c}$ and $J^{c}$ denote the [complements](wikipedia://en/54347) of $I$ and $J$, respectively. Also let $\mathbf{A}_{I^c, J^c}$ denote the submatrix of $\mathbf{A}$ containing those rows and columns whose indices are in $I^{c}$ and $J^{c}$, respectively. Then the $(I, J)$ entry of $\operatorname{adj}_{r} \mathbf{A}$ is

$$(-1)^{\sigma(I) + \sigma(J)}\det \mathbf{A}_{J^c, I^c},$$

where $\sigma(I)$ and $\sigma(J)$ are the sum of the elements of $I$ and $J$, respectively.

Basic properties of higher adjugates include :

- $$\operatorname{adj}_{0}(\mathbf{A}) = \det \mathbf{A}.$$
- $$\operatorname{adj}_{1}(\mathbf{A}) = \operatorname{adj} \mathbf{A}.$$
- $$\operatorname{adj}_{n}(\mathbf{A}) = 1.$$
- $$\operatorname{adj}_{r}(\mathbf{BA}) = \operatorname{adj}_{r}(\mathbf{A}) \operatorname{adj}_{r}(\mathbf{B}).$$
- $\operatorname{adj}_r(\mathbf{A})C_r(\mathbf{A}) = C_r(\mathbf{A})\operatorname{adj}_r(\mathbf{A}) = (\det \mathbf{A})I_{\binom{n}{r}}$, where $C_{r}(\mathbf{A})$ denotes the $r$th [compound matrix](wikipedia://en/36846770).

Higher adjugates may be defined in abstract algebraic terms in a similar fashion to the usual adjugate, substituting $\wedge^r V$ and $\wedge^{n-r} V$ for $V$ and $\wedge^{n-1} V$, respectively.

## Iterated adjugates {#Iterated_adjugates}

[Iteratively](wikipedia://en/1448702) taking the adjugate of an invertible matrix **A** $k$ times yields

$$\overbrace{\operatorname{adj}\dotsm\operatorname{adj}}^k(\mathbf{A})=\det(\mathbf{A})^{\frac{(n-1)^k-(-1)^k}n}\mathbf{A}^{(-1)^k},$$

$$\det(\overbrace{\operatorname{adj}\dotsm\operatorname{adj}}^k(\mathbf{A}))=\det(\mathbf{A})^{(n-1)^k}.$$

For example,

$$\operatorname{adj}(\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A})) = \det(\mathbf{A})^{n - 2} \mathbf{A}.$$

$$\det(\operatorname{adj}(\operatorname{adj}(\mathbf{A}))) = \det(\mathbf{A})^{(n - 1)^2}.$$
