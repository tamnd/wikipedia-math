# Algebra tile

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Algebra tile"}

**Algebra tiles**, also known as **Algetiles**, or **Variable Blocks**, are [mathematical manipulatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_manipulatives) that allow students to better understand ways of algebraic thinking and the concepts of [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923). These tiles have proven to provide concrete models for [elementary school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_school), [middle school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_school), [high school](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/High_school), and college-level introductory [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) [students](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Students). They have also been used to prepare [prison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prison) inmates for their [General Educational Development](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Educational_Development) (GED) tests.[^o2] **Algebra tiles** allow both an algebraic and geometric approach to algebraic concepts. They give [students](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Students) another way to solve algebraic problems other than just abstract manipulation.[^o2] The [National Council of Teachers of Mathematics](wikipedia://en/1561691) ([NCTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCTM)) recommends a decreased emphasis on the memorization of the rules of [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) and the symbol manipulation of [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923) in their *Curriculum and Evaluation Standards for Mathematics*. According to the [NCTM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NCTM) 1989 standards "\[r\]elating models to one another builds a better understanding of each".[^o3]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

### Solving linear equations using addition {#Solving_linear_equations_using_addition}

The [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570) $x-8=6$ can be modeled with one positive $x$ tile and eight negative unit tiles on the left side of a piece of paper and six positive unit tiles on the right side. To maintain equality of the sides, each action must be performed on both sides.[^o2] For example, eight positive unit tiles can be added to both sides.[^o2] Zero pairs of unit tiles are removed from the left side, leaving one positive $x$ tile. The right side has 14 positive unit tiles, so $x=14$.

### Solving linear equations using subtraction {#Solving_linear_equations_using_subtraction}

The equation $x+7=10$ can be modeled with one positive $x$ tile and seven positive unit tiles on the left side and 10 positive unit tiles on the right side. Rather than adding the same number of tiles to both sides, the same number of tiles can be subtracted from both sides. For example, seven positive unit tiles can be removed from both sides. This leaves one positive $x$ tile on the left side and three positive unit tiles on the right side, so $x=3$.[^o2]

### Multiplying polynomials {#Multiplying_polynomials}

When using algebra tiles to multiply a [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) by a [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416), the student must first set up a rectangle where the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) of the rectangle is the one [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416) and then the [width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Width) of the rectangle is the other [monomial](wikipedia://en/357416), similar to when one multiplies [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) using algebra tiles. Once the sides of the rectangle are represented by the algebra tiles, one would then try to figure out which algebra tiles would fill in the rectangle. For instance, if one had x×x, the only algebra tile that would complete the rectangle would be x<sup>2</sup>, which is the answer.

[Multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) of [binomials](wikipedia://en/542399) is similar to [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) of [monomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomials) when using the algebra tiles . Multiplication of [binomials](wikipedia://en/542399) can also be thought of as creating a rectangle where the [factors](wikipedia://en/15491) are the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) and [width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Width).[^o3] As with the [monomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomials), one would set up the sides of the rectangle to be the [factors](wikipedia://en/15491) and then fill in the rectangle with the algebra tiles.[^o3] This method of using algebra tiles to multiply [polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomials) is known as the area model[^o9] and it can also be applied to multiplying [monomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monomials) and [binomials](wikipedia://en/542399) with each other. An example of multiplying [binomials](wikipedia://en/542399) is (2x+1)×(x+2) and the first step the student would take is set up two positive x tiles and one positive unit tile to represent the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) of a rectangle and then one would take one positive x tile and two positive unit tiles to represent the [width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Width). These two lines of tiles would create a space that looks like a rectangle which can be filled in with certain tiles. In the case of this example the rectangle would be composed of two positive x<sup>2</sup> tiles, five positive x tiles, and two positive unit tiles. So the solution is 2x<sup>2</sup>+5x+2.

### Factoring {#Factoring}

*Image: Algebra tile factoring.jpg.* Algebra tile model of $x^2+3x+2$

In order to factor using algebra tiles, one has to start out with a set of tiles that the student combines into a rectangle, this may require the use of adding zero pairs in order to make the rectangular shape. An example would be where one is given one positive x<sup>2</sup> tile, three positive x tiles, and two positive unit tiles. The student forms the rectangle by having the x<sup>2</sup> tile in the upper right corner, then one has two x tiles on the right side of the x<sup>2</sup> tile, one x tile underneath the x<sup>2</sup> tile, and two unit tiles are in the bottom right corner. By placing the algebra tiles to the sides of this rectangle we can determine that we need one positive x tile and one positive unit tile for the [length](wikipedia://en/18542) and then one positive x tile and two positive unit tiles for the [width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Width). This means that the two [factors](wikipedia://en/15491) are $x+1$ and $x+2$.[^o2] In a sense this is the reverse of the procedure for multiplying [polynomials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomials).

## Educational significance and limitations {#Educational_significance_and_limitations}

Algebra tiles are designed to support the "Concrete-Representational-Abstract" (CRA) instructional sequence, allowing students to physically manipulate objects (concrete) before drawing diagrams (representational) and finally using symbolic notation (abstract).

### Conceptual benefits {#Conceptual_benefits}

Proponents argue that the tiles provide a geometric interpretation of algebraic concepts, particularly for the [distributive property](wikipedia://en/103118) and factoring. By linking algebraic expressions to the [area of a rectangle](wikipedia://en/1209), students can visualize why $(x+1)^2$ results in a trinomial ($x^2 + 2x + 1$) rather than the common error $x^2 + 1$. The use of distinct colors for positive and negative values also reinforces the concept of [zero pairs](wikipedia://en/228312) in integer operations.

### Limitations and criticism {#Limitations_and_criticism}

Despite their utility, mathematics educators have noted several limitations in the model:

- **Variable assignment:** A common conceptual hurdle is that students may attempt to measure the "x" tile with the unit tiles. If the "x" tile is manufactured to be exactly 5 units long, students may erroneously conclude that $x = 5$ always. To mitigate this, standard algebra tiles are typically designed with lengths that are not integer multiples of the unit side (e.g., 5.25 cm).
- **Dimensionality:** The model effectively represents linear (1D) and quadratic (2D) expressions but becomes difficult to manage for cubic expressions (which require 3D blocks) and impossible for polynomials of the 4th degree or higher, as they cannot be represented in physical space.
- **Negative area:** While tiles can represent negative terms via color, the geometric concept of "negative area" is physically impossible, which can confuse students trying to reconcile the physical object with real-world geometry rules.
