# Finite ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Finite ring"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), more specifically [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a **finite ring** is a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) that has a finite number of elements. Every [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) is an example of a finite ring, and the additive part of every finite ring is an example of an [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707), but the concept of finite rings in their own right has a more recent history.

:::

Although rings have more structure than groups do, the theory of finite rings is simpler than that of finite groups. For instance, the [classification of finite simple groups](wikipedia://en/7445) was one of the major breakthroughs of 20th century mathematics, its proof spanning thousands of journal pages. On the other hand, it has been known since 1907 that any finite [simple ring](wikipedia://en/443387) is isomorphic to the ring $\mathrm{M}_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$ – the *n*-by-*n* matrices over a finite field of order *q* (as a consequence of Wedderburn's theorems, described below).

The number of rings (meaning [rngs](wikipedia://en/3405004)) with *m* elements, for *m* a natural number, is listed under in the [On-Line Encyclopedia of Integer Sequences](wikipedia://en/500004) (OEIS). For the number of rings with multiplicative identity (*refer to [variations on terminology on the page ring](wikipedia://en/48404#Variations_on_terminology)*), it is listed under . The number of rings/rngs are non-trivial problems in mathematics; both problems are listed as "hard" sequences in OEIS.

## Finite field {#Finite_field}

The theory of [finite fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_fields) is perhaps the most important aspect of finite ring theory due to its intimate connections with [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) and [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527). An important, but fairly old aspect of the theory is the classification of finite fields:[^o3]

- The order (number of elements of) a finite field can be expressed in the form *p*<sup>*n*</sup>, where *p* is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) called the [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) of the field, and *n* is a positive integer.
- For every prime number *p* and positive integer *n*, there exists a finite field with *p*<sup>*n*</sup> elements.
- Any two finite fields with the same order are [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic).

Despite the classification, finite fields are still an active area of research, including recent results on the [Kakeya conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kakeya_conjecture) and open problems regarding the size of smallest [primitive roots](wikipedia://en/186864) (in number theory).

A finite field *F* may be used to build a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of dimension *n* over *F*. The [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) *A* of *n*-by-*n* matrices with elements from *F* is used in [Galois geometry](wikipedia://en/25197704), with the [projective linear group](wikipedia://en/382667) serving as the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of *A*.

## Wedderburn's theorems {#Wedderburn's_theorems}

[Wedderburn's little theorem](wikipedia://en/7132002) asserts that any finite [division ring](wikipedia://en/9067) is necessarily commutative:

If every nonzero element *r* of a finite ring *R* has a multiplicative inverse, then *R* is commutative (and therefore a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615)).

[Nathan Jacobson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nathan_Jacobson) later discovered yet another condition that guarantees commutativity of a ring: if for every element *r* of *R* there exists an integer *n* > 1 such that *r* <sup>*n*</sup> = *r*, then *R* is commutative.[^o6] More general conditions that imply commutativity of a ring are also known.[^o7]

Yet another theorem by Wedderburn has, as its consequence, a result demonstrating that the theory of finite [simple rings](wikipedia://en/443387) is relatively straightforward in nature. More specifically, any finite simple ring is isomorphic to the ring $\mathrm{M}_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$, the *n*-by-*n* matrices over a finite field of order *q*. This follows from two theorems of [Joseph Wedderburn](wikipedia://en/2969494) established in 1905 and 1907 (one of which is Wedderburn's little theorem).

## Enumeration {#Enumeration}

In 1964 [David Singmaster](wikipedia://en/1323490) proposed the following problem in the [American Mathematical Monthly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Mathematical_Monthly): "(1) What is the order of the smallest non-trivial ring with identity which is not a field? Find two such rings with this minimal order. Are there more? (2) How many rings of order four are there?" One can find the solution by D.M. Bloom in a two-page proof[^o10] that there are eleven rings (meaning [rngs](wikipedia://en/3405004)) of order 4, four of which have a multiplicative identity. Indeed, four-element rings introduce the complexity of the subject. There are three rings over the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) C<sub>4</sub> and eight rings over the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928). There is an interesting display of the discriminatory tools ([nilpotents](wikipedia://en/252235), [zero-divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero-divisor), [idempotents](wikipedia://en/361940), and left- and right-identities) in Gregory Dresden's lecture notes.[^o11]

The occurrence of *[non-commutativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutativity)* in finite rings was described in (Eldridge 1968) in two theorems: If the order *m* of a finite ring with 1 has a [cube-free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cube-free) factorization, then it is [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346). And if a [non-commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_ring) finite ring with 1 has the order of a prime cubed, then the ring is isomorphic to the upper triangular 2 × 2 matrix ring over the Galois field of the prime. The study of rings of order the cube of a prime was further developed in (Raghavendran 1969) and (Gilmer Mott 1973). Next Flor and Wessenbauer (1975) made improvements on the cube-of-a-prime case. Definitive work on the isomorphism classes came with (Antipkin Elizarov 1982) proving that for *p* > 2, the number of classes is 3*p* + 50.

There are earlier references in the topic of finite rings, such as Robert Ballieu[^o16] and Scorza.[^o17]

These are a few of the facts that are known about the number of finite rings (not necessarily with 1) of a given order (suppose *p* and *q* represent distinct prime numbers):

- There are two finite rings of order *p*.
- There are four finite rings of order *pq*.
- There are eleven finite rings of order *p*<sup>2</sup>.
- There are twenty-two finite rings of order *p*<sup>2</sup>*q*.
- There are fifty-two finite rings of order eight.
- There are 3*p* + 50 finite rings of order *p*<sup>3</sup>, *p* > 2.

The number of rings (meaning [rngs](wikipedia://en/3405004)) with *n* elements are (with *a*(0) = 1)
