# Heinrich August Rothe

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Heinrich August Rothe"}

**Heinrich August Rothe** (1773–1842) was a German mathematician, a professor of mathematics at [Erlangen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Erlangen-Nuremberg). He was a student of [Carl Hindenburg](wikipedia://en/545047) and a member of Hindenburg's school of [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170).[^o2][^o3]

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Rothe was born in 1773 in [Dresden](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dresden), and in 1793 became a docent at the [University of Leipzig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Leipzig). He became an extraordinary professor at Leipzig in 1796, and in 1804 he moved to Erlangen as a full professor, taking over the chair formerly held by [Karl Christian von Langsdorf](wikipedia://en/15083433). He died in 1842, and his position at Erlangen was in turn taken by Johann Wilhelm Pfaff, the brother of the more famous mathematician [Johann Friedrich Pfaff](wikipedia://en/1669324).[^o5][^o6]

## Research {#Research}

The [Rothe–Hagen identity](wikipedia://en/5786821), a [summation](wikipedia://en/246160) formula for [binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/4668), appeared in Rothe's 1793 thesis. It is named for him and for the later work of [Johann Georg Hagen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Georg_Hagen).[^o8] The same thesis also included a formula for computing the [Taylor series](wikipedia://en/30448) of an [inverse function](wikipedia://en/14907) from the Taylor series for the function itself, related to the [Lagrange inversion theorem](wikipedia://en/94158).[^o9]

In the study of [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027), Rothe was the first to define the inverse of a permutation, in 1800. He developed a technique for visualizing permutations now known as a [Rothe diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rothe_diagram), a square table that has a dot in each cell (*i*,*j*) for which the permutation maps position *i* to position *j* and a cross in each cell (*i*,*j*) for which there is a dot later in row *i* and another dot later in column *j*. Using Rothe diagrams, he showed that the number of [inversions](wikipedia://en/21473801) in a permutation is the same as in its inverse, for the inverse permutation has as its diagram the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of the original diagram, and the inversions of both permutations are marked by the crosses. Rothe used this fact to show that the [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) of a [matrix](wikipedia://en/20556859) is the same as the determinant of the transpose: if one expands a determinant as a [polynomial](wikipedia://en/23000), each term corresponds to a permutation, and the sign of the term is determined by the [parity](wikipedia://en/143135) of its number of inversions. Since each term of the determinant of the transpose corresponds to a term of the original matrix with the inverse permutation and the same number of inversions, it has the same sign, and so the two determinants are also the same.[^o10]

In his 1800 work on permutations, Rothe also was the first to consider permutations that are [involutions](wikipedia://en/293450); that is, they are their own inverse, or equivalently they have symmetric Rothe diagrams. He found the [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806)

$$T(n) = T(n-1) + (n-1)T(n-2)$$

for [counting](wikipedia://en/3925533) these permutations, which also counts the number of [Young tableaux](wikipedia://en/683368), and which has as its solution the [telephone numbers](wikipedia://en/35258497)

1, 2, 4, 10, 26, 76, 232, 764, 2620, 9496, ... .[^o11]

Rothe was also the first to formulate the [*q*-binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/2233526#The_q-binomial_theorem), a [*q*-analog](wikipedia://en/1619396) of the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677), in an 1811 publication.[^o12][^o13]
