# Huntington–Hill method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Huntington–Hill method"}

The **Huntington–Hill method**, sometimes called **method of equal proportions**, is a [highest averages method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_averages_method) for assigning seats in a [legislature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legislature) to [political parties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Political_parties) or [states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/State_%28administrative_division%29).[^o2] Since 1941, this method has been used to apportion the 435 seats in the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) [House of Representatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_House_of_Representatives) following the completion of each decennial [census](wikipedia://en/57070).[^o3][^o4]

:::

The method minimizes the [relative difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relative_difference) in the number of constituents represented by each legislator. In other words, the method selects the allocation such that no transfer of a seat from one state to another can reduce the percent error in representation for both states.[^o2]

## Apportionment method {#Apportionment_method}

In this method, as a first step, each of the 50 states is given its one guaranteed seat in the House of Representatives, leaving 385 seats to be assigned. The remaining seats are allocated one at a time, to the state with the highest *average district population*, to bring its district population down. However, it is not clear if the average should be calculated *before* or *after* allocating an additional seat, and the two procedures give different results. Huntington-Hill uses a [continuity correction](wikipedia://en/572382) as a compromise, given by taking the [geometric mean](wikipedia://en/13046) of both divisors, i.e.:[^o6]

$$A_{n} = \frac{P}{\sqrt{n(n+1)}}$$

where *P* is the population of the state, and *n* is the number of seats it currently holds before the possible allocation of the next seat. Each time a state is assigned a seat, its *n* is incremented by 1, causing its priority value to be reduced.

Consider the reapportionment following the 2020 U.S. census. After every state is given one seat:

1. The 51st allocated seat goes to the state with the largest *A*<sub>1</sub> priority value, which in this instance is the largest-population state, California, with the value 39,576,757/√(1(1+1)) = 27,984,993 ≈ 28.0 M. Its *A*<sub>2</sub> priority value is then 39,576,757/√(2(2+1)) ≈ 16.2 M.
1. The 52nd seat goes to Texas, the 2nd largest state, because its *A*<sub>1</sub> ≈ 20.6 M is larger than the *A<sub>n</sub>* of any other state.
1. The 53rd seat goes back to California, because its *A*<sub>2</sub> ≈ 16.2 M is again larger than the *A<sub>n</sub>* of any other state; it now has three seats.
1. The 54th seat goes to Florida, the 3rd largest state, because its *A*<sub>1</sub> ≈ 15.3 M is the largest at this point.
1. The 55th seat is New York’s, the 4th largest state, because its *A*<sub>1</sub> ≈ 14.3 M is now the largest.
1. The 56th seat goes again to Texas for three seats, as its *A*<sub>2</sub> ≈ 11.9 M is the largest.
1. The 57th seat is then California’s for four seats, as its *A*<sub>3</sub> ≈ 11.4 M is the largest.
1. The 58th seat then goes to Pennsylvania, the 5th largest state, with its *A*<sub>1</sub> ≈ 9.2 M being the largest.

## Division by zero {#Division_by_zero}

Unlike the [D'Hondt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/D'Hondt_method) and [Sainte-Laguë](wikipedia://en/277659) systems, which allow the allocation of seats by calculating successive quotients right away, the Huntington–Hill system requires each party or state have at least one seat to avoid a [division by zero](wikipedia://en/185663) error.[^o6] In the U.S. House of Representatives, this is ensured by guaranteeing each state at least one seat;[^o6] in [party-list representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Party-list_proportional_representation), small parties would likely be eliminated using some [electoral threshold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electoral_threshold), or the first divisor can be modified.

## Example {#Example}

Consider an example to distribute 8 seats between three parties A, B, C having respectively 100,000, 80,000 and 30,000 votes.

Each eligible party is assigned one seat. With all the initial seats assigned, the remaining five seats are distributed by a priority number calculated as follows. Each eligible party's (Parties A, B, and C) total votes is divided by $\sqrt{2 1} \approx 1.41$, then by approximately 2.45, 3.46, 4.47, 5.48, 6.48, 7.48, and 8.49. The 5 highest entries, marked with asterisks, range from **70,711** down to **28,868**. For each, the corresponding party gets another seat.
