# PCP theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="PCP theorem"}

In [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543), the **PCP theorem** (also known as the **PCP characterization theorem**) states that every [decision problem](wikipedia://en/8336) in the [NP](wikipedia://en/21562) [complexity class](wikipedia://en/502426) has [probabilistically checkable proofs](wikipedia://en/504509) ([proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) that can be checked by a [randomized algorithm](wikipedia://en/495383)) of constant [query complexity](wikipedia://en/6511) and logarithmic randomness complexity (uses a logarithmic number of random bits).

:::

The PCP theorem says that for some universal constant $K$, for every $n$, any mathematical proof for a statement of length $n$ can be rewritten as a different proof of length $\operatorname{poly}(n)$ that is formally verifiable with 99% accuracy by a [randomized algorithm](wikipedia://en/495383) that inspects only $K$ letters of that proof.

The PCP theorem is the cornerstone of the theory of computational [hardness of approximation](wikipedia://en/20677277), which investigates the inherent difficulty in designing efficient [approximation algorithms](wikipedia://en/563105) for various [optimization problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_problems). It has been described by [Ingo Wegener](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ingo_Wegener) as "the most important result in complexity theory since [Cook's theorem](wikipedia://en/663047)"[^o2] and by [Oded Goldreich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oded_Goldreich) as "a culmination of a sequence of impressive works \[…\] rich in innovative ideas".[^o3]

::: {#o5 .statement cue=heading name="Formal statement"}

## Formal statement {#Formal_statement}

The PCP theorem states that

$$\mathsf{NP}=\mathsf{PCP}[O(\log n),O(1)]$$

where $\mathsf{NP}$ is the [complexity class](wikipedia://en/502426) of [problems solvable in nondeterministic polynomial time](wikipedia://en/21562) and where $\mathsf{PCP}[r(n),q(n)]$ is the class of problems for which a [probabilistically checkable proof](wikipedia://en/504509) of a solution can be given, such that the proof can be checked in polynomial time using $r(n)$ bits of randomness and by reading $q(n)$ bits of the proof, correct proofs are always accepted, and incorrect proofs are rejected with probability at least $\tfrac12$. The variable $n$ is the length in bits of the description of a problem instance. Note further that the verification algorithm is *non-adaptive*: the choice of bits of the proof to check depend only on the random bits and the description of the problem instance, not the actual bits of the proof.

:::

## PCP and hardness of approximation {#PCP_and_hardness_of_approximation}

An alternative formulation of the PCP theorem states that the maximum fraction of satisfiable constraints of a certain [constraint satisfaction problem](wikipedia://en/211652) is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard) to approximate within some constant factor.[^o7]

Formally, for some constants $q$ and $\alpha<1$, the following [promise problem](wikipedia://en/1471429) $(L_{\mathrm{yes}},L_{\mathrm{no}})$ is an NP-hard decision problem:

- $L_{\mathrm{yes}}=\{\Phi:$ all constraints in $\Phi$ are simultaneously satisfiable$\}$
- $L_{\mathrm{no}}=\{\Phi:$ every assignment satisfies fewer than an $\alpha$ fraction of $\Phi$'s constraints$\}$

where $\Phi$ is a [constraint satisfaction problem](wikipedia://en/211652) (CSP) over a Boolean alphabet with at most $q$ variables per constraint.

The connection to the class $\mathsf{PCP}$ mentioned above can be seen by noticing that checking a constant number of bits $q$ in a proof can be seen as evaluating a constraint in $q$ Boolean variables on those bits of the proof. Since the verification algorithm uses $O(\log n)$ bits of randomness, it can be represented as a CSP as described above with $\operatorname{poly}(n)$ constraints. The first formulation of the PCP theorem then guarantees the promise condition with $\alpha=\tfrac12$: if the NP problem's answer is yes, then every constraint (which corresponds to a particular value for the random bits) has a satisfying assignment (an acceptable proof); otherwise, any proof should be rejected with probability at least $\tfrac12$, which means any assignment must satisfy fewer than $\tfrac12$ of the constraints (which means it will be accepted with probability lower than $\tfrac12$). Therefore, an algorithm for the promise problem would be able to solve the underlying NP problem, and hence the promise problem must be NP-hard.

As a consequence of this theorem, it can be shown that the solutions to many natural optimization problems including maximum [boolean formula satisfiability](wikipedia://en/4715),[^o8] [maximum independent set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maximum_independent_set) in graphs,[^o9] and the [shortest vector problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_problems) for [lattices](wikipedia://en/469246)[^o10] cannot be approximated efficiently unless $\mathsf{P}=\mathsf{NP}$. This can be done by reducing the problem of approximating a solution to such problems to a promise problem of the above form. These results are sometimes also called PCP theorems because they can be viewed as [probabilistically checkable proofs](wikipedia://en/504509) for NP with some additional structure.

## Proof {#Proof}

A proof of a weaker result, $\mathsf{NP} \subseteq \mathsf{PCP}[n^3, 1]$ is given in one of the lectures of Dexter Kozen.[^o13]

## History {#History}

The PCP theorem is the culmination of a long line of work on [interactive proofs](wikipedia://en/160255) and probabilistically checkable proofs. The first theorem relating standard proofs and probabilistically checkable proofs is the statement that $\mathsf{NEXP}\subseteq\mathsf{PCP}[\operatorname{poly}(n),\operatorname{poly}(n)]$, proved by (Babai Fortnow Lund 1990).

### Origin of the initials {#Origin_of_the_initials}

The notation $\mathsf{PCP}_{c(n),s(n)}[r(n),q(n)]$ is explained at [probabilistically checkable proof](wikipedia://en/504509#Definition). The notation is that of a function that returns a certain complexity class. See the explanation mentioned above.

The name of this theorem (the "PCP theorem") probably comes either from **"PCP"** meaning "[probabilistically checkable proof](wikipedia://en/504509)", or from the notation mentioned above (or both).

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="First theorem [in 1990]"}

### First theorem \[in 1990\] {#First_theorem_[in_1990]}

Subsequently, the methods used in this work were extended by Babai, [Lance Fortnow](wikipedia://en/25303350), Levin, and Szegedy in 1991 (Babai Fortnow Levin Szegedy 1991), Feige, Goldwasser, Lund, Safra, and Szegedy (1991), and Arora and Safra in 1992 (Arora Safra 1992) to yield a proof of the PCP theorem by Arora, Lund, Motwani, Sudan, and Szegedy in 1998 (Arora Lund Motwani Sudan 1998).

The 2001 [Gödel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel_Prize) was awarded to [Sanjeev Arora](wikipedia://en/21513331), [Uriel Feige](wikipedia://en/24734804), [Shafi Goldwasser](wikipedia://en/842178), [Carsten Lund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carsten_Lund), [László Lovász](wikipedia://en/1623938), [Rajeev Motwani](wikipedia://en/16989288), [Shmuel Safra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shmuel_Safra), [Madhu Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madhu_Sudan), and [Mario Szegedy](wikipedia://en/4291061) for work on the PCP theorem and its connection to hardness of approximation.

In 2005 [Irit Dinur](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irit_Dinur) discovered a significantly simpler proof of the PCP theorem, using [expander graphs](wikipedia://en/9313).[^o22] She received the 2019 [Gödel Prize](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gödel_Prize) for this. [^o23]

In 2025 the PCP theorem was proven to hold even when limited to perfectly [zero knowledge](wikipedia://en/450541) proofs.[^o24]

:::

## Quantum analogs {#Quantum_analogs}

### Nonlocal games version {#Nonlocal_games_version}

A version of the PCP theorem for [quantum nonlocal games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_refereed_game) would state that it is computationally hard to approximate the quantum value of a quantum nonlocal game. In 2012, Thomas Vidick and Tsuyoshi Ito published a result[^o27] that showed a "strong limitation on the ability of entangled provers to collude in a multiplayer game". This could be a step toward proving the quantum analogue of the PCP theorem, since when the result[^o27] was reported in the media,[^o28][^o29] professor [Dorit Aharonov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dorit_Aharonov) called it "the quantum analogue of an earlier paper on multiprover interactive proofs" that "basically led to the PCP theorem".[^o29]

In 2018, Thomas Vidick and Anand Natarajan proved[^o30] a games variant of quantum PCP theorem under randomized reduction. It states that $\mathsf{QMA}\subseteq\mathsf{MIP}^*[\log n,1,\tfrac12]$, where $\mathsf{MIP}^*[f(n),c,s]$ is a complexity class of multi-prover quantum interactive proofs systems with $f(n)$-bit classical communications, and the completeness is $c$ and the soundness is $s$.

### Hamiltonian version {#Hamiltonian_version}

A version of the PCP theorem for quantum local Hamiltonians would state that it is computationally hard to approximate the ground energy of a quantum local Hamiltonian. The work of Natarajan and Vidick[^o30] also showed that a quantum Hamiltonian version of the PCP theorem, namely the existence of local Hamiltonian problem with constant promise gap $c-s$ which are [QMA](wikipedia://en/23545978)-hard, implies a quantum nonlocal games version of the PCP theorem.

The [NLTS conjecture](wikipedia://en/71478579) was an unresolved obstacle and precursor to a quantum Hamiltonian analog of the PCP theorem.[^o32] The conjecture was proven in 2022 by [Anurag Anshu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anurag_Anshu), [Nikolas Breuckmann](wikipedia://en/75619888), and [Chinmay Nirkhe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chinmay_Nirkhe) using a construction of a Hamiltonian based on quantum [CSS codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CSS_code).[^o33] However, the ground states of such Hamiltonians are given explicitly by the code states of the corresponding CSS code, and thus are not computationally hard enough for a general proof of a quantum PCP theorem.
