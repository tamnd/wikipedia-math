# Branched covering

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Branched covering"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **branched covering** is a map that is almost a [covering map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Covering_map), except on a small set.

:::

## In topology {#In_topology}

In topology, a map is a *branched covering* if it is a covering map everywhere except for a [nowhere dense set](wikipedia://en/48634) known as the branch set. Examples include the map from a [wedge of circles](wikipedia://en/3526954) to a single circle, where the map is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) on each circle.

## In algebraic geometry {#In_algebraic_geometry}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), the term **branched covering** is used to describe [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) $f$ from an [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) $V$ to another one $W$, the two [dimensions](wikipedia://en/253251) being the same, and the typical fibre of $f$ being of dimension 0.

In that case, there will be an open set $W'$ of $W$ (for the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744)) that is [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) in $W$, such that the restriction of $f$ to $W'$ (from $V' = f^{-1}(W')$ to $W'$, that is) is **[unramified](wikipedia://en/384644)**. Depending on the context, we can take this as [local homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/248582) for the [strong topology](wikipedia://en/589726), over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), or as an [étale morphism](wikipedia://en/3022771) in general (under some slightly stronger hypotheses, on [flatness](wikipedia://en/492616) and [separability](wikipedia://en/363340)). Generically, then, such a morphism resembles a [covering space](wikipedia://en/250323) in the topological sense. For example, if $V$ and $W$ are both compact [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), we require only that $f$ is holomorphic and not constant, and then there is a finite set of points $P$ of $W$, outside of which we do find an honest covering

$$V' \to W'.$$

### Ramification locus {#Ramification_locus}

The set of exceptional points on $W$ is called the **ramification locus** (i.e. this is the complement of the largest possible open set $W'$). In general [monodromy](wikipedia://en/251250) occurs according to the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of $W'$ acting on the sheets of the covering (this topological picture can be made precise also in the case of a general base field).

An **unramified covering** then is the occurrence of an empty ramification locus.

### Kummer extensions {#Kummer_extensions}

Branched coverings are easily constructed as [Kummer extensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kummer_extension), i.e. as [algebraic extension](wikipedia://en/2125) of the [function field](wikipedia://en/2783843). The [hyperelliptic curves](wikipedia://en/499361) are prototypic examples.

::: {#o7 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

### Elliptic curve {#Elliptic_curve}

Morphisms of curves provide many examples of ramified coverings. For example, let $C$ be the [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225) of equation

$$y^2 - x(x-1)(x-2)=0.$$

The projection of $C$ onto the $x$-axis is a ramified cover with ramification locus given by

$$x(x-1)(x-2)=0.$$

This is because for these three values of $x$ the fiber is the double point $y^2=0,$ while for any other value of $x$, the fiber consists of two distinct points (over an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018)).

This projection induces an [algebraic extension](wikipedia://en/2125) of degree two of the [function fields](wikipedia://en/2783843): Also, if we take the fraction fields of the underlying commutative rings, we get the morphism

$$\mathbb{C}(x) \to \mathbb{C}(x)[y]/(y^2 - x(x-1)(x-2))$$

Hence this projection is a degree 2 branched covering. This can be homogenized to construct a degree 2 branched covering of the corresponding projective elliptic curve to the projective line.

### Plane algebraic curve {#Plane_algebraic_curve}

The previous example may be generalized to any [algebraic plane curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_plane_curve) in the following way. Let $C$ be a plane curve defined by the equation $f(x,y) = 0$, where $f$ is a [separable](wikipedia://en/362400) and [irreducible](wikipedia://en/188725) polynomial in two indeterminates. If $n$ is the degree of $f$ in $y$, then the fiber consists of $n$ distinct points, except for a finite number of values of $x$. Thus, this projection is a branched covering of degree $n$.

The exceptional values of $x$ are the roots of the coefficient of $y^n$ in $f$, and the roots of the [discriminant](wikipedia://en/55607) of $f$ with respect to $y$.

Over a root $r$ of the discriminant, there is at least a ramified point, which is either a [critical point](wikipedia://en/1951424) or a [singular point](wikipedia://en/981915). If $r$ is also a root of the coefficient of $y^n$ in $f$, then this ramified point is "[at infinity](wikipedia://en/403139)".

Over a root $s$ of the coefficient of $y^n$ in $f$, the curve $C$ has an infinite branch, and the fiber at $s$ has less than $n$ points. However, if one extends the projection to the [projective completions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_completion) of $C$ and the $x$-axis, and if $s$ is not a root of the discriminant, the projection becomes a covering over a neighbourhood of $s$.

The fact that this projection is a branched covering of degree $n$ may also be seen by considering the [function fields](wikipedia://en/2783843). In fact, this projection corresponds to the [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) of degree $n$

$$\mathbb C(x) \to \mathbb C(x)[y]/f(x,y).$$

### Varying Ramifications {#Varying_Ramifications}

We can also generalize branched coverings of the line with varying ramification degrees. Consider a polynomial of the form

$$f(x,y) = g(x)$$

as we choose different points $x=\alpha$, the fibers given by the vanishing locus of $f(\alpha,y) - g(\alpha)$ vary. At any point where the multiplicity of one of the linear terms in the factorization of $f(\alpha,y) - g(\alpha)$ increases by one, there is a ramification.

:::

## Scheme Theoretic Examples {#Scheme_Theoretic_Examples}

### Elliptic Curves {#Elliptic_Curves}

Morphisms of curves provide many examples of ramified coverings of schemes. For example, the morphism from an affine elliptic curve to a line

$$\text{Spec}\left( {\mathbb{C}[x,y]}/{(y^2 - x(x-1)(x-2)} \right) \to \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[x])$$

is a ramified cover with ramification locus given by

$$X = \text{Spec}\left({\mathbb{C}[x]}/{(x(x-1)(x-2))} \right)$$

This is because at any point of $X$ in $\mathbb{A}^1$ the fiber is the scheme

$$\text{Spec}\left({\mathbb{C}[y]}/{(y^2)} \right)$$

Also, if we take the fraction fields of the underlying commutative rings, we get the [field homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_homomorphism)

$$\mathbb{C}(x) \to {\mathbb{C}(x)[y]}/{(y^2 - x(x-1)(x-2))},$$

which is an [algebraic extension](wikipedia://en/2125) of degree two; hence we got a degree 2 branched covering of an elliptic curve to the affine line. This can be homogenized to construct a morphism of a projective elliptic curve to $\mathbb{P}^1$.

### Hyperelliptic curve {#Hyperelliptic_curve}

A [hyperelliptic curve](wikipedia://en/499361) provides a generalization of the above degree $2$ cover of the affine line, by considering the affine scheme defined over $\mathbb C$ by a polynomial of the form

$y^2 - \prod(x-a_i)$ where $a_i \neq a_j$ for $i\neq j$

### Higher Degree Coverings of the Affine Line {#Higher_Degree_Coverings_of_the_Affine_Line}

We can generalize the previous example by taking the morphism

$$\text{Spec}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x,y]}{(f(y) - g(x))} \right) \to \text{Spec}(\mathbb{C}[x])$$

where $g(x)$ has no repeated roots. Then the ramification locus is given by

$$X = \text{Spec}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x]}{(f(x))} \right)$$

where the fibers are given by

$$\text{Spec}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[y]}{(f(y))} \right)$$

Then, we get an induced morphism of fraction fields

$$\mathbb{C}(x) \to \frac{\mathbb{C}(x)[y]}{(f(y) - g(x))}$$

There is an $\mathbb{C}(x)$-module isomorphism of the target with

$$\mathbb{C}(x)\oplus\mathbb{C}(x)\cdot y \oplus \cdots \oplus \mathbb{C}(x)\cdot y^{\text{deg}(f(y))}$$

Hence the cover is of degree $\text{deg}(f)$.

### Superelliptic Curves {#Superelliptic_Curves}

[Superelliptic curves](wikipedia://en/35809693) are a generalization of hyperelliptic curves and a specialization of the previous family of examples since they are given by affine schemes $X/\mathbb{C}$ from polynomials of the form

$y^k - f(x)$ where $k>2$ and $f(x)$ has no repeated roots.

### Ramified Coverings of Projective Space {#Ramified_Coverings_of_Projective_Space}

Another useful class of examples come from ramified coverings of projective space. Given a homogeneous polynomial $f \in \mathbb{C}[x_0,\ldots,x_n]$ we can construct a ramified covering of $\mathbb{P}^n$ with ramification locus

$$\text{Proj}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x_0,\ldots,x_n]}{f(x)} \right)$$

by considering the morphism of projective schemes

$$\text{Proj}\left( \frac{\mathbb{C}[x_0,\ldots,x_n][y]}{y^{\text{deg}(f)} - f(x)} \right) \to \mathbb{P}^n$$

Again, this will be a covering of degree $\text{deg}(f)$.

::: {#o18 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

Branched coverings $C \to X$ come with a symmetry group of transformations $G$. Since the symmetry group has stabilizers at the points of the ramification locus, branched coverings can be used to construct examples of orbifolds, or [Deligne–Mumford stacks](wikipedia://en/13185596).

:::
