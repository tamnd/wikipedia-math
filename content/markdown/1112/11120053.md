# Weyl–Brauer matrices

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Weyl–Brauer matrices"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in the theory of [spinors](wikipedia://en/29276), the **Weyl–Brauer matrices** are an explicit realization of a [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) as a [matrix algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_algebra) of $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor} \times 2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ matrices. They generalize the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868) to $n$ dimensions, and are a specific construction of [higher-dimensional gamma matrices](wikipedia://en/25322570). They are named for [Richard Brauer](wikipedia://en/605134) and [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544),[^o2] and were one of the earliest systematic constructions of [spinors](wikipedia://en/29276) from a [representation theoretic](wikipedia://en/19378200) standpoint.

:::

The matrices are formed by taking [tensor products](wikipedia://en/30844) of the [Pauli matrices](wikipedia://en/24868), and the space of spinors in $n$ dimensions may then be realized as the column vectors of size $2^{\lfloor n/2\rfloor}$ on which the Weyl–Brauer matrices act.

::: {#o4 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

## Construction {#Construction}

Suppose that *V* = **R**<sup>n</sup> is a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of dimension *n*. There is a sharp contrast in the construction of the Weyl–Brauer matrices depending on whether the dimension *n* is even or odd.

Let $n$ = 2$k$ (or 2$k$+1) and suppose that the Euclidean [quadratic form](wikipedia://en/251478) on $V$ is given by

$$q_1^2+\dots+q_k^2+p_1^2+\dots+p_k^2 ~~ (+p_n^2)~,$$

where (*p*<sub>i</sub>, *q*<sub>i</sub>) are the standard coordinates on **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.

Define matrices **1**, **1'**, *P*, and *Q* by

$$\begin{matrix} {\mathbf 1}=\sigma_0=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\0&1\end{matrix}\right),& {\mathbf 1}'=\sigma_3=\left(\begin{matrix}1&0\\0&-1\end{matrix}\right),\\ P=\sigma_1=\left(\begin{matrix}0&1\\1&0\end{matrix}\right),& Q=-\sigma_2=\left(\begin{matrix}0&i\\-i&0\end{matrix}\right) \end{matrix}.$$

In even or in odd dimensionality, this quantization procedure amounts to replacing the ordinary *p*, *q* coordinates with non-commutative coordinates constructed from *P*, *Q* in a suitable fashion.

### Even case {#Even_case}

In the case when *n* = 2*k* is even, let

$$P_i = {\mathbf 1}'\otimes\dots\otimes{\mathbf 1}'\otimes P \otimes {\mathbf 1}\otimes\dots\otimes{\mathbf 1}$$

$$Q_i = {\mathbf 1}'\otimes\dots\otimes{\mathbf 1}'\otimes Q \otimes {\mathbf 1}\otimes\dots\otimes{\mathbf 1}$$

for *i* = 1,2,...,*k* (where the *P* or *Q* is considered to occupy the *i*-th position). The operation $\otimes$ is the [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844) of matrices. It is no longer important to distinguish between the *P*s and *Q*s, so we shall simply refer to them all with the symbol *P*, and regard the index on *P*<sub>i</sub> as ranging from *i* = 1 to *i* = 2*k*. For instance, the following properties hold:

$P_i^2 = 1, i=1,2,...,2k$, and $P_iP_j=-P_jP_i$ for all unequal pairs *i* and *j*. (**Clifford relations**.)

Thus the algebra generated by the *P*<sub>i</sub> is the [Clifford algebra](wikipedia://en/45305) of euclidean *n*-space.

Let *A* denote the algebra generated by these matrices. By counting dimensions, *A* is a complete 2<sup>*k*</sup>×2<sup>*k*</sup> matrix algebra over the complex numbers. As a matrix algebra, therefore, it acts on 2<sup>*k*</sup>-dimensional column vectors (with complex entries). These column vectors are the **spinors**.

We now turn to the action of the [orthogonal group](wikipedia://en/173954) on the spinors. Consider the application of an orthogonal transformation to the coordinates, which in turn acts upon the *P*<sub>i</sub> via

$$P_i\mapsto R(P)_i = \sum_j R_{ij}P_j.$$

That is, $R\in SO(n)$. Since the *P*<sub>i</sub> generate *A*, the action of this transformation extends to all of *A* and produces an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160) of *A*. From elementary [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), any such automorphism must be given by a [change of basis](wikipedia://en/1179451). Hence there is a matrix *S*, depending on *R*, such that

$R(P)_i = S(R)P_iS(R)^{-1}$ (1).

In particular, *S*(*R*) will act on column vectors (spinors). By decomposing rotations into products of reflections, one can write down a formula for *S*(*R*) in much the same way as in the case of three dimensions.

There is more than one matrix *S*(*R*) which produces the action in (1). The ambiguity defines *S*(*R*) up to a nonevanescent scalar factor *c*. Since *S*(*R*) and *cS*(*R*) define the same transformation (1), the action of the orthogonal group on spinors is not single-valued, but instead descends to an action on the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) associated to the space of spinors. This multiple-valued action can be sharpened by normalizing the constant *c* in such a way that (det *S*(*R*))<sup>2</sup> = 1. In order to do this, however, it is necessary to discuss how the space of spinors (column vectors) may be identified with its dual (row vectors).

In order to identify spinors with their duals, let *C* be the matrix defined by

$$C=P\otimes Q\otimes P\otimes\dots\otimes Q.$$

Then conjugation by *C* converts a *P*<sub>i</sub> matrix to its transpose: <sup>t</sup>*P*<sub>i</sub> = *C P*<sub>i</sub> *C*<sup>−1</sup>. Under the action of a rotation,

$$\hbox{ }^tP_i\rightarrow \,^tS(R)^{-1}\,^tP_i\,^tS(R) = (CS(R)C^{-1})\,^tP_i(CS(R)C^{-1})^{-1}$$

whence *C* *S*(*R*) *C*<sup>−1</sup> = α <sup>t</sup>*S*(*R*)<sup>−1</sup> for some scalar α. The scalar factor α can be made to equal one by rescaling *S*(*R*). Under these circumstances, (det *S*(*R*))<sup>2</sup> = 1, as required.

In physics, the matrix *C* is conventionally interpreted as [charge conjugation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charge_conjugation).

#### Weyl spinors {#Weyl_spinors}

Let *U* be the element of the algebra *A* defined by

$U={\mathbf 1}'\otimes\dots\otimes{\mathbf 1}'$, (*k* factors).

Then *U* is preserved under rotations, so in particular its [eigenspace decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenspace) (which necessarily corresponds to the eigenvalues +1 and -1, occurring in equal numbers) is also stabilized by rotations. As a consequence, each spinor admits a decomposition into eigenvectors under *U*:

ξ = ξ<sup>\+</sup> + ξ<sup>−</sup>

into a *right-handed Weyl spinor* ξ<sup>\+</sup> and a *left-handed Weyl spinor* ξ<sup>−</sup>. Because rotations preserve the eigenspaces of *U*, the rotations themselves act diagonally as matrices *S*(*R*)<sup>\+</sup>, *S*(*R*)<sup>−</sup> via

(*S*(*R*)ξ)<sup>\+</sup> = *S*<sup>\+</sup>(*R*) ξ<sup>\+</sup>, and

(*S*(*R*)ξ)<sup>−</sup> = *S*<sup>−</sup>(*R*) ξ<sup>−</sup>.

This decomposition is not, however, stable under [improper rotations](wikipedia://en/244324) (e.g., reflections in a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862)). A reflection in a hyperplane has the effect of interchanging the two eigenspaces. Thus there are two irreducible spin representations in even dimensions given by the left-handed and right-handed Weyl spinors, each of which has dimension 2<sup>k-1</sup>. However, there is only one irreducible **pin representation** (see below) owing to the non-invariance of the above eigenspace decomposition under improper rotations, and that has dimension 2<sup>k</sup>.

#### Odd case {#Odd_case}

In the quantization for an odd number 2*k*+1 of dimensions, the matrices *P*<sub>i</sub> may be introduced as above for *i* = 1,2,...,2*k*, and the following matrix may be adjoined to the system:

$P_n = {\mathbf 1}'\otimes\dots\otimes{\mathbf 1}'$, (*k* factors),

so that the Clifford relations still hold. This adjunction has no effect on the algebra *A* of matrices generated by the *P*<sub>i</sub>, since in either case *A* is still a complete matrix algebra of the same dimension. Thus *A*, which is a complete 2<sup>*k*</sup>×2<sup>*k*</sup> matrix algebra, is not the Clifford algebra, which is an algebra of dimension 2×2<sup>*k*</sup>×2<sup>*k*</sup>. Rather *A* is the quotient of the Clifford algebra by a certain ideal.

Nevertheless, one can show that if *R* is a proper rotation (an orthogonal transformation of determinant one), then the rotation among the coordinates

$$R(P)_i = \sum_j R_{ij}P_j$$

is again an automorphism of *A*, and so induces a change of basis

$$R(P)_i = S(R)P_iS(R)^{-1}$$

exactly as in the even-dimensional case. The projective representation *S*(*R*) may again be normalized so that (det *S*(*R*))<sup>2</sup> = 1. It may further be extended to general orthogonal transformations by setting *S*(*R*) = -*S*(-*R*) in case det *R* = -1 (i.e., if *R* is a reversal).

In the case of odd dimensions it is not possible to split a spinor into a pair of Weyl spinors, and spinors form an irreducible representation of the [spin group](wikipedia://en/411231). As in the even case, it is possible to identify spinors with their duals, but for one caveat. The identification of the space of spinors with its [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) is invariant under *proper* rotations, and so the two spaces are spinorially equivalent. However, if *improper* rotations are also taken into consideration, then the spin space and its dual are not isomorphic. Thus, while there is only one [spin representation](wikipedia://en/10160091) in odd dimensions, there are a pair of inequivalent **pin representations**. This fact is not evident from the Weyl's quantization approach, however, and is more easily seen by considering the representations of the full Clifford algebra.
