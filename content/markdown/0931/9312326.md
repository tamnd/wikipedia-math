# Gabriel graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gabriel graph"}

*Image: Gabriel graph.svg.* The Gabriel graph of 100 random points

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [computational geometry](wikipedia://en/176927), the **Gabriel graph** of a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $S$ of points in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) expresses one notion of proximity or nearness of those points. Formally, it is the [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) $G$ with vertex set $S$ in which any two distinct points $p \in S$ and $q \in S$ are adjacent precisely when the closed [disc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disc_%28mathematics%29) having $pq$ as a [diameter](wikipedia://en/8007) contains no other points. Another way of expressing the same adjacency criterion is that $p$ and $q$ should be the two closest given points to their [midpoint](wikipedia://en/437979), with no other given point being as close. Gabriel graphs naturally generalize to higher dimensions, with the empty disks replaced by empty closed [balls](wikipedia://en/160556). Gabriel graphs are named after [K. Ruben Gabriel](wikipedia://en/43203588), who introduced them in a paper with [Robert R. Sokal](wikipedia://en/15415012) in 1969.

:::

## Percolation {#Percolation}

For Gabriel graphs of infinite random point sets, the finite site [percolation threshold](wikipedia://en/11790568) gives the fraction of points needed to support connectivity: if a random subset of fewer vertices than the threshold is given, the remaining graph will almost surely have only finite connected components, while if the size of the random subset is more than the threshold, then the remaining graph will almost surely have an infinite component (as well as finite components). This threshold was proved to exist by (Bertin Billiot Drouilhet 2002), and more precise values of both site and bond thresholds have been given by Norrenbrock.

## Related geometric graphs {#Related_geometric_graphs}

The Gabriel graph is a [subgraph](wikipedia://en/325802#Subgraphs) of the [Delaunay triangulation](wikipedia://en/8864). It can be found in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time) if the Delaunay triangulation is given.

The Gabriel graph contains, as subgraphs, the [Euclidean minimum spanning tree](wikipedia://en/1040597), the [relative neighborhood graph](wikipedia://en/13322903), and the [nearest neighbor graph](wikipedia://en/12061759).

It is an instance of a [beta-skeleton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beta-skeleton). Like beta-skeletons, and unlike Delaunay triangulations, it is not a [geometric spanner](wikipedia://en/11844294): for some point sets, distances within the Gabriel graph can be much larger than the Euclidean distances between points.
