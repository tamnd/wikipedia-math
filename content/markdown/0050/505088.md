# Perfect field

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Perfect field"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384), a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) $K$ is **perfect** if any one of the following equivalent conditions holds:

:::

- Every [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) over $K$ has no [multiple roots](wikipedia://en/19211790#Multiplicity_of_a_root_of_a_polynomial) in any [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $L/K$.
- Every [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) over $K$ has non-zero [formal derivative](wikipedia://en/3832917).
- Every [irreducible polynomial](wikipedia://en/188725) over $K$ is [separable](wikipedia://en/362400).
- Every [finite extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_extension) of $K$ is [separable](wikipedia://en/363340).
- Every [algebraic extension](wikipedia://en/2125) of $K$ is separable.
- Either $K$ has [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 0, or, when $K$ has characteristic $p>0$, every element of $K$ is a $p$-th [power](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_%28mathematics%29).
- Either $K$ has [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) 0, or, when $K$ has characteristic $p>0$, the [Frobenius endomorphism](wikipedia://en/1088270) $x\mapsto x^p$ is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160).
- The [separable closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_closure) of $K$ is [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed).
- Every [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749) commutative $K$-[algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_%28ring_theory%29) $A$ is a [separable algebra](wikipedia://en/13205367); i.e., $A \otimes_k L$ is [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749) for every [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $L/K$.

Otherwise, $K$ is called **imperfect**.

In particular, all fields of characteristic zero and all [finite fields](wikipedia://en/11615) are perfect.

Perfect fields are significant because [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) over these fields becomes simpler, since the general Galois assumption of field extensions being separable is automatically satisfied over these fields (see third condition above).

Another important property of perfect fields is that they admit [Witt vectors](wikipedia://en/5573710).

More generally, a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) of characteristic $p$ ($p$ a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666)) is called **perfect** if the [Frobenius endomorphism](wikipedia://en/1088270) is an [automorphism](wikipedia://en/1160).[^o2] When restricted to [integral domains](wikipedia://en/15462), this is equivalent to the above condition "every element of $K$ is a $p$-th power".

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Examples of perfect fields are:

- every field of characteristic zero, so $\mathbb{Q}$ and its finite extensions, as well as $\mathbb{R}$ and $\mathbb{C}$;[^o5]
- every [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) $\mathbb{F}_q$;[^o6]
- every [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018);
- the union of a set of perfect fields [totally ordered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered) by extension;
- fields algebraic over a perfect field.

Most fields that are encountered in practice are perfect. The imperfect case arises mainly in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) in characteristic $p>0$. Every imperfect field is necessarily [transcendental](wikipedia://en/11634#Algebraic_and_transcendental_elements) over its [prime subfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_subfield) (the minimal subfield), because the latter is perfect.

An example of an imperfect field is the field $\mathbb{F}_p(t)$ of rational polynomials in the unknown $t$. This can be seen from the fact that the Frobenius endomorphism sends $x \mapsto x^p$ and therefore is not surjective. Equivalently, one can show that the polynomial $f(x)=x^p-t$, which is an element of $(\mathbb{F}_p(t))[x]$, is irreducible but inseparable.

Imperfect fields cause technical difficulties because irreducible polynomials can become reducible in the algebraic closure of the base field. For example,[^o7] consider $f(x,y) = x^p + ay^p \in k[x,y]$ for $k$ an imperfect field of characteristic $p$ and $a$ not a $p$-th power in $k$. Then in its algebraic closure $k^{\operatorname{alg}}[x,y]$, the following equality holds:

$$f(x,y) = (x + b y)^p ,$$

where $b^p=a$ and such a $b$ exists in this algebraic closure. Geometrically, this means that $f$ does not define an [affine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_curve) [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) in $k[x,y]$.

:::

## Field extension over a perfect field {#Field_extension_over_a_perfect_field}

Any [finitely generated field extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_generated_field_extension) $K$ over a perfect field $k$ is separably generated, i.e. admits a separating [transcendence base](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transcendence_basis), that is, a transcendence base $\Gamma$ such that $K$ is separably algebraic over $k(\Gamma)$.[^o9]

## Perfect closure and perfection {#Perfect_closure_and_perfection}

Every field can be embedded in a perfect field: in characteristic $p$, a field $k$ adjoined with all $p^r$-th roots ($r\geq 1$) is perfect; it is called the **perfect closure** of $k$ and usually denoted by $k^{p^{-\infty}}$. For example, $\mathbb{F}_q(t)$ embeds into $\mathbb{F}_q(t,t^{1/p},t^{1/p^2},\ldots)$.

The perfect closure can be used in a test for separability. More precisely, a commutative $k$-algebra $A$ is separable if and only if $A \otimes_k k^{p^{-\infty}}$ is reduced.[^o12]

The perfect closure can be defined by a [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245): the perfect closure of a ring $A$ of characteristic $p$ is a perfect ring $A_p$ of characteristic $p$ together with a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) $u:A\to A_p$ such that for any other perfect ring $B$ of characteristic $p$ with a homomorphism $v:A\to B$, there is a unique homomorphism $f:A_p\to B$ such that $v$ factors through $u$ (i.e. $v=fu$). The perfect closure always exists; the proof involves "adjoining $p$-th roots of elements of $A$", similar to the case of fields.[^o13]

The **perfection** of a ring $A$ of characteristic $p$ is the [dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_%28mathematics%29) notion (though this term is sometimes used for the perfect closure). In other words, the perfection $R(A)$ of $A$ is a perfect ring of characteristic $p$ together with a map $\theta:R(A)\to A$ such that for any perfect ring $B$ of characteristic $p$ equipped with a map $\phi:B\to A$, there is a unique map $f:B\to R(A)$ such that $\phi$ factors through $\theta$ (i.e. $\phi=\theta f$). The perfection of $A$ may be constructed as follows. Consider the [projective system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_system)

$$\cdots\rightarrow A\rightarrow A\rightarrow A\rightarrow\cdots$$

where the transition maps are the Frobenius endomorphism. The [inverse limit](wikipedia://en/15109) of this system is $R(A)$ and consists of sequences $(x_0,x_1,\dots)$ of elements of $A$ such that $x_{i+1}^p=x_i$ for all $i$. The map $\theta:R(A)\to A$ sends $(x_i)$ to $x_0$.[^o14]
