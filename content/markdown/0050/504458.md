# Conditional probability distribution

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conditional probability distribution"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the conditional probability distribution is a probability distribution that describes the probability of an outcome given the occurrence of a particular event. Given two [jointly distributed](wikipedia://en/879637) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) $X$ and $Y$, the **conditional probability distribution** of $Y$ given $X$ is the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of $Y$ when $X$ is known to be a particular value; in some cases the conditional probabilities may be expressed as functions containing the unspecified value $x$ of $X$ as a parameter. When both $X$ and $Y$ are [categorical variables](wikipedia://en/821165), a [conditional probability table](wikipedia://en/41394453) is typically used to represent the conditional probability. The conditional distribution contrasts with the [marginal distribution](wikipedia://en/506077) of a random variable, which is its distribution without reference to the value of the other variable.

:::

If the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X$ is a [continuous distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_distribution), then its [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) is known as the **conditional density function**.[^n1] The properties of a conditional distribution, such as the [moments](wikipedia://en/368684), are often referred to by corresponding names such as the [conditional mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conditional_mean) and [conditional variance](wikipedia://en/11391180).

More generally, one can refer to the conditional distribution of a subset of a set of more than two variables; this conditional distribution is contingent on the values of all the remaining variables, and if more than one variable is included in the subset then this conditional distribution is the conditional [joint distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_distribution) of the included variables.

The relation with the probability distribution of $X$ given $Y$ is:

$$P(Y=y \mid X=x) P(X=x) = P(\{X=x\} \cap \{Y=y\}) = P(X=x \mid Y=y)P(Y=y).$$

### Example {#Example}

Consider the roll of a fair die and let $X=1$ if the number is even (i.e., 2, 4, or 6) and $X=0$ otherwise. Furthermore, let $Y=1$ if the number is prime (i.e., 2, 3, or 5) and $Y=0$ otherwise.

::: {#o7 .table cue=table}

| D | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 6 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| X | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 | 1 |
| Y | 0 | 1 | 1 | 0 | 1 | 0 |

:::

Then the unconditional probability that $X=1$ is 3/6 = 1/2 (since there are six possible rolls of the die, of which three are even), whereas the probability that $X=1$ conditional on $Y=1$ is 1/3 (since there are three possible [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) rolls—2, 3, and 5—of which one is even).

:::

where $f_{X,Y}(x,y)$ gives the [joint density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_distribution) of $X$ and $Y$, while $f_X(x)$ gives the [marginal density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_density) for $X$. Also in this case it is necessary that $f_X(x)>0$.

The relation with the probability distribution of $X$ given $Y$ is given by:

$$f_{Y\mid X}(y \mid x)f_X(x) = f_{X,Y}(x, y) = f_{X|Y}(x \mid y)f_Y(y).$$

The concept of the conditional distribution of a continuous random variable is not as intuitive as it might seem: [Borel's paradox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel's_paradox) shows that conditional probability density functions need not be invariant under coordinate transformations.

## Relation to independence {#Relation_to_independence}

Random variables $X$, $Y$ are [independent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_independence) [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) the conditional distribution of $Y$ given $X$ is, for all possible realizations of $X$, equal to the unconditional distribution of $Y$. For discrete random variables this means $P(Y=y|X=x) = P(Y=y)$ for all possible $y$ and $x$ with $P(X=x)>0$. For continuous random variables $X$ and $Y$, having a [joint density function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joint_density_function), it means $f_Y(y|X=x) = f_Y(y)$ for all possible $y$ and $x$ with $f_X(x)>0$.

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

Seen as a function of $y$ for given $x$, $P(Y=y|X=x)$ is a probability mass function and so the sum over all $y$ (or integral if it is a conditional probability density) is 1. Seen as a function of $x$ for given $y$, it is a [likelihood function](wikipedia://en/44968), so that the sum (or integral) over all $x$ need not be 1.

Additionally, a marginal of a joint distribution can be expressed as the expectation of the corresponding conditional distribution. For instance, $p_X(x) = E_{Y}[p_{X|Y}(x \ |\ Y)]$.

:::

## Measure-theoretic formulation {#Measure-theoretic_formulation}

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, P)$ be a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325), $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$ a $\sigma$-field in $\mathcal{F}$. Given $A\in \mathcal{F}$, the [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746) implies that there is[^n3] a $\mathcal{G}$-measurable random variable $P(A\mid\mathcal{G}):\Omega\to \mathbb{R}$, called the [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134), such that

$$\int_G P(A\mid\mathcal{G})(\omega) dP(\omega)=P(A\cap G)$$

for every $G\in \mathcal{G}$, and such a random variable is uniquely defined up to sets of probability zero. A conditional probability is called [**regular**](wikipedia://en/18963326) if $\operatorname{P}(\cdot\mid\mathcal{G})(\omega)$ is a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) on $(\Omega, \mathcal{F})$ for all $\omega \in \Omega$ a.e.

Special cases:

- For the trivial sigma algebra $\mathcal G= \{\emptyset,\Omega\}$, the conditional probability is the constant function $\operatorname{P}\!\left( A\mid \{\emptyset,\Omega\} \right) = \operatorname{P}(A).$
- If $A\in \mathcal{G}$, then $\operatorname{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})=1_A$, the indicator function (defined below).

Let $X : \Omega \to E$ be a $(E, \mathcal{E})$-valued random variable. For each $B \in \mathcal{E}$, define

$$\mu_{X \, | \, \mathcal{G}} (B \, |\, \mathcal{G}) = \mathrm{P} (X^{-1}(B) \, | \, \mathcal{G}).$$

For any $\omega \in \Omega$, the function $\mu_{X \, | \mathcal{G}}(\cdot \, | \mathcal{G}) (\omega) : \mathcal{E} \to \mathbb{R}$ is called the **conditional probability distribution** of $X$ given $\mathcal{G}$. If it is a probability measure on $(E, \mathcal{E})$, then it is called [**regular**](wikipedia://en/18963326).

For a real-valued random variable (with respect to the Borel $\sigma$-field $\mathcal{R}^1$ on $\mathbb{R}$), every conditional probability distribution is regular.[^n4] In this case,$E[X \mid \mathcal{G}] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x \, \mu_{X \mid \mathcal{G}}(d x, \cdot)$ [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908).

### Relation to conditional expectation {#Relation_to_conditional_expectation}

For any event $A \in \mathcal{F}$, define the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790):

$$\mathbf{1}_A (\omega) = \begin{cases} 1 \; &\text{if } \omega \in A, \\ 0 \; &\text{if } \omega \notin A, \end{cases}$$

which is a random variable. Note that the expectation of this random variable is equal to the probability of *A* itself:

$$\operatorname{E}(\mathbf{1}_A) = \operatorname{P}(A). \;$$

Given a $\sigma$-field $\mathcal{G} \subseteq \mathcal{F}$, the conditional probability $\operatorname{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G})$ is a version of the [conditional expectation](wikipedia://en/435099) of the indicator function for $A$:

$$\operatorname{P}(A\mid\mathcal{G}) = \operatorname{E}(\mathbf{1}_A\mid\mathcal{G}) \;$$

An expectation of a random variable with respect to a regular conditional probability is equal to its conditional expectation.

### Interpretation of conditioning on a Sigma Field {#Interpretation_of_conditioning_on_a_Sigma_Field}

Consider the probability space $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ and a sub-sigma field $\mathcal{A} \subset \mathcal{F}$. The sub-sigma field $\mathcal{A}$ can be loosely interpreted as containing a subset of the information in $\mathcal{F}$. For example, we might think of $\mathbb{P}(B|\mathcal{A})$ as the probability of the event $B$ given the information in $\mathcal{A}$.

Also recall that an event $B$ is independent of a sub-sigma field $\mathcal{A}$ if $\mathbb{P}(B | A) = \mathbb{P}(B)$ for all $A \in \mathcal{A}$. It is incorrect to conclude in general that the information in $\mathcal{A}$ does not tell us anything about the probability of event $B$ occurring. This can be shown with a counter-example:

Consider a probability space on the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324), $\Omega = [0, 1]$. Let $\mathcal{G}$ be the sigma-field of all countable sets and sets whose complement is countable. So each set in $\mathcal{G}$ has measure $0$ or $1$ and so is independent of each event in $\mathcal{F}$. However, notice that $\mathcal{G}$ also contains all the singleton events in $\mathcal{F}$ (those sets which contain only a single $\omega \in \Omega$). So knowing which of the events in $\mathcal{G}$ occurred is equivalent to knowing exactly which $\omega \in \Omega$ occurred! So in one sense, $\mathcal{G}$ contains no information about $\mathcal{F}$ (it is independent of it), and in another sense it contains all the information in $\mathcal{F}$.[^n5]
