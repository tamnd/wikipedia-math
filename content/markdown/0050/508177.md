# Circle group

*Image: Circle-group.svg.* Multiplication on the circle group is equivalent to addition of angles.

Other

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Circle group"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **circle group**, denoted by $\mathbb T$ or $S^1$, is the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) of all [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) with [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991#Complex_numbers) 1, that is, the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) in the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) or simply the **unit complex numbers**[^n1]

:::

$$\mathbb T = \{ z \in \mathbb{C} : |z| = 1 \}.$$

The circle group plays a fundamental role in many areas of mathematics.

When a given unit complex number $u$ multiplies the other points of the circle, the effect is to rotate them through an angle determined by $u$. In this way, the circle group becomes the [group of symmetries](wikipedia://en/28186) of the [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) which preserve its orientation (do not flip it). [Composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of two rotations is the ordinary [multiplication of complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826#Multiplication). Multiplication is [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative), $z_1z_2 = z_2z_1$, making the circle group commutative (an [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974)); correspondingly several rotations of the plane can be composed in any order with the same result. Rotations can alternately be parametrized by the [angle measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_measure) $\theta$, which is related to $z$ by the [complex exponential function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_exponential_function):

$$\theta \mapsto z = e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i\sin\theta.$$

The circle group is sometimes denoted by $U(1)$, which is the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) of $1\times 1$ complex matrices. It is structurally the same as (i.e., [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to) the group of 2-dimensional [rotation matrices](wikipedia://en/856005), i.e., the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) $\mathrm{SO}(2)$. The angle measure gives a [periodic](wikipedia://en/76848) parameterization of the circle group, so the group is often treated as a periodic interval $[0,2\pi]$ with the endpoints glued together, and the group operation as addition [modulo](wikipedia://en/1352428) $2\pi$: the usual addition of angles. The notation $\mathbb T$ for the circle group stems from the fact that a circle is a [1-dimensional torus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/N-torus). More generally, $\mathbb T^n$ (the [direct product](wikipedia://en/3706015) of $\mathbb T$ with itself $n$ times) is geometrically an $n$-torus. The notation $S^1$ stands for [1-dimensional sphere](wikipedia://en/39782).

The circle group has applications throughout mathematics, especially in advanced mathematics. It is the group underlying classical [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038). It is [dual](wikipedia://en/366136) to the additive group of the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563). It also has applications throughout [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) and [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416). It is the group underlying [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism). Electromagnetic theory can be formulated as a theory of [bundles](wikipedia://en/361366) [associated](wikipedia://en/398874) to the circle group, which are primary objects of study in [homotopy theory](wikipedia://en/61994831) and [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801).

## Elementary introduction {#Elementary_introduction}

Elements of the circle group can be thought of as representing [rotations of the Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/73820317) about a particular point, those [geometric transformations](wikipedia://en/43194879) which keep that point fixed while preserving the distance between arbitrary points as well as the [orientation](wikipedia://en/187446) of shapes.[^n2] The group operation then corresponds to [composition](wikipedia://en/195947) of rotations, that is, the combination of two rotations resulting from applying one rotation and then the other.

If a point $p$ with [rectangular coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectangular_coordinates) $(x_p, y_p)$ in the [Euclidean plane](wikipedia://en/15575410) is represented by a complex number $z_p = x_p + y_pi$ (see [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628)), then [multiplication](wikipedia://en/5826#Multiplication) by a unit complex number $z_R$ results in the complex number $z_R\cdot z_p$ which represents the point $R(p)$, the result of rotating $p$ around the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) $(0, 0)$ by some rotation $R$. Each unit complex number corresponds to a different rotation, and vice versa. So the unit complex number $z_R$ can be used to represent the corresponding rotation $R$. If two different rotations $R_1$ and $R_2$ are represented by the unit complex numbers $z_1$ and $z_2$, then their composition $R_2\circ R_1$ is represented by the product of the complex numbers $z_2 \cdot z_1$.

Another common way of representing a rotation is by an [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) measured in [degrees](wikipedia://en/1195294) or [radians](wikipedia://en/26003), often denoted by the Greek letter $\theta$. Conventionally, a positive angle is considered to represent a [counterclockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterclockwise) rotation. Two angles which differ by $360^\circ$ or $2\pi$ radians represent the same rotation, so it is common to pick the representative angle for the rotation to be between $0^\circ$ and $360^\circ$ (or $[0, 2\pi)$ radians) or, more symmetrically, between $-180^\circ$ and $+180^\circ$ (or $(-\pi, +\pi]$ radians). Two rotations represented by angle measure can be composed simply by adding them together; sometimes the new angle falls outside the designated [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) for representative angles – we have looped around the circle – and we can then add or subtract a multiple of $360^\circ$ ($2\pi$ radians) to return to the interval.

A rotation of a point in the complex plane by an angle $\theta$ about the origin (measured in radians) corresponds to multiplication by the complex number $e^{\theta i} = \cos \theta + i\sin \theta$, where $e^z$ is the [complex exponential function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_exponential_function) and $\sin$ and $\cos$ are [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367)). Adding angles corresponds to multiplying unit complex numbers:

$$e^{(\theta_1 + \theta_2) i} = e^{\theta_1 i} e^{\theta_2 i}.$$

In some contexts, instead of using degrees in the interval $[0, 360)$ or radians in the interval $[0, 2\pi)$, angles are represented by [fractions of a full turn](wikipedia://en/855329) in the interval $[0, 1)$. This version makes arithmetic convenient because an angle which has fallen outside of the interval can be brought back inside it by taking its [fractional part](wikipedia://en/501532): for a positive number, this amounts to throwing away any digits before the decimal point. For example, to compose a $0.75$ turn rotation and a $0.5$ turn rotation, add the two numbers, $0.75 + 0.5 = 1.25$, and then throw away the leading $1$, resulting in a $0.25$ turn rotation.

The [periodic interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_interval) $[0, 1)$ can be considered to be the [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) of the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) [modulo](wikipedia://en/1352566) the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), symbolically written $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$; this is the group of [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260) of real numbers, with any two real numbers which differ by an integer considered to be equivalent. Each equivalence class includes one real number in the finite interval $[0, 1)$, which can be used to represent it. As a group under addition, $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$ is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the circle group $\mathbb T$ of unit complex numbers under multiplication, meaning the two groups are structurally the same.

## Isomorphisms and conventions {#Isomorphisms_and_conventions}

The circle group shows up in a variety of forms in mathematics. We list some of the more common forms here. Specifically, we show that

$$\mathbb T \cong \mbox{U}(1) \cong \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z} \cong \mathrm{SO}(2),$$

where the slash ($~\!/~\!$) denotes [group quotient](wikipedia://en/11526) and $\cong$ the existence of an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) between the groups.

### Unitary group U(1) {#Unitary_group_U(1)}

A $1\times1$ matrix has $1$ row and $1$ column, and only a single entry. The product of two $1\times1$ [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) is the matrix whose single entry is the product of the entries of each of the matrices:

$$\begin{bmatrix}z_1\end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix}z_2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix}z_1z_2\end{bmatrix}.$$

Therefore any group of $1\times1$ matrices under [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) can be trivially related to the group of corresponding entries under multiplication. A [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222) is a matrix with complex entries for which multiplication by the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) results in the [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718); for a $1\times1$ matrix, an equivalent condition is that the single entry has unit absolute value, making it an element of the circle group. This makes the circle group canonically isomorphic to the first [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $\mathrm{U}(1)$, the group of $1\times1$ unitary matrices under matrix multiplication. That is,

$$\mathbb T \cong \mbox{U}(1).$$

It is routine to conflate a $1\times1$ matrix with its single entry, considering elements of $\mathrm{U}(1)$ to be unit complex numbers directly. The symbol $\mathrm{U}(1)$ can be used as an abbreviation for the multiplicative group of unit complex numbers.

### Periodic interval under addition {#Periodic_interval_under_addition}

The [exponential function](wikipedia://en/9678) gives rise to a map $\exp\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb T$ from the additive real numbers $\mathbb{R}$ to the circle group $\mathbb T$, elements of which can be separated into real and imaginary components using [Euler's formula](wikipedia://en/9613),

$$\theta \mapsto e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i \sin \theta,$$

where the real number $\theta$ corresponds to an angle (argument) of a unit complex number as measured counterclockwise from the positive real axis. The exponential function converts sums in the domain to products in the image. In particular, for any pair of real numbers $\theta_1$ and $\theta_2$,

$$e^{i(\theta_1+\theta_2)} = e^{i\theta_1} e^{i\theta_2}.$$

This property makes $\exp\colon \mathbb{R} \to \mathbb T$ a [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396). While the map is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), it is not [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective) and therefore not an isomorphism. The [kernel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kernel_%28group_theory%29) of this map (the elements which map to the complex number $1$) is the set of all [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) multiples of $2\pi$. By the [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_isomorphism_theorem) we then have that

$$\mathbb T \cong \mathbb{R}~\!/~\!2\pi\mathbb{Z}.$$

After rescaling we can also say that $\mathbb T$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$.

### Special orthogonal group SO(2) {#Special_orthogonal_group_SO(2)}

Rotations of the [Euclidean vector](wikipedia://en/32533) plane about the origin are examples of [linear transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_transformation), which can be represented as $2\times 2$ matrices with real entries, specifically as [rotation matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_matrices) (also called [special orthogonal matrices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_matrices)), those matrices $Q$ which yield the identity matrix when multiplied by their transpose, $QQ^\mathsf{T} = Q^\mathsf{T} Q = I$, and whose [determinant](wikipedia://en/8468) is 1. Such a matrix always takes the form

$$Q = \begin{bmatrix} x & -y \\ y & \phantom{-}x \\ \end{bmatrix},$$

with $\operatorname{det}(Q) = x^2 + y^2 = 1$. The matrix $Q$ can be uniquely identified with the unit complex number $z = x + iy$. If the matrix represents a rotation by angle $\theta$, then $z = e^{i\theta}$, $x = \cos\theta$, and $y = \sin\theta$, so $Q$ can be written as

$$Q = \begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta & -\sin \theta \\ \sin \theta & \phantom{-}\cos \theta \\ \end{bmatrix}.$$

The [squared modulus](wikipedia://en/991#Complex_numbers) of the complex number, $|z|^2 = \bar{z}z = x^2 + y^2$, corresponds to the determinant and the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918), $\bar{z} = x - iy$, corresponds to the [transpose](wikipedia://en/173844) of the matrix. Matrix multiplication of such matrices is consistent with addition of corresponding angles or multiplication of corresponding complex numbers (see [Angle sum identities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_sum_identities)).

$$\begin{aligned} &\begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta_1 & -\sin\theta_1 \\ \sin \theta_1 & \phantom{-}\cos\theta_1 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} \cos \theta_2 & -\sin\theta_2 \\ \sin \theta_2 & \phantom{-}\cos\theta_2 \end{bmatrix} \\[3pt] &\quad= \begin{bmatrix} \cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 & -\cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2 \\ \cos\theta_1\sin\theta_2 + \sin\theta_1\cos\theta_2 & \phantom{-}\cos\theta_1\cos\theta_2 - \sin\theta_1\sin\theta_2 \end{bmatrix} \\[3pt] &\quad= \begin{bmatrix} \cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) & -\sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \\ \sin(\theta_1 + \theta_2) & \phantom{-}\cos(\theta_1 + \theta_2) \end{bmatrix}. \end{aligned}$$

Therefore the circle group is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group) $\mathrm{SO}(2)$, that is,

$$\mathbb T \cong \mathrm{SO}(2).$$

This isomorphism has the geometric interpretation that multiplication by a unit complex number is a proper rotation in the complex (and real) plane, and every such rotation is of this form.

::: {#o10 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Conventions"}

### Conventions {#Conventions}

While the symbol $\mathbb T$ and the name "circle group" often refer to the unit complex numbers under multiplication, it is common to give this name and symbol to one of the other isomorphic groups, depending on convenience.[^n3][^n4] The three common definitions of the circle group are:[^n5]

- $$\mathbb T = \{ z\in\mathbb{C} : |z| = 1\}$$
- $$\mathbb T = \mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Z}$$
- $\mathbb T = \mathbb{R}/2\pi\mathbb{Z}$[^n6]

In the first, the group operation is written multiplicatively, while in the latter two the operation is written additively.

:::

## Topological, measurable, and analytic structure {#Topological,_measurable,_and_analytic_structure}

The circle group is more than just an abstract algebraic object. It has a [natural topology](wikipedia://en/11934923) when regarded as a [subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subspace_%28topology%29) of the complex plane. It is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) with (at least) two natural notions of distance between elements: the absolute value of the difference between two unit complex numbers (the length of the [chord](wikipedia://en/297947) between points in the complex plane), or the absolute value of the difference between nearest representative angles (the [arc length](wikipedia://en/5578920#length) of the shortest [arc](wikipedia://en/5578920) between points on the complex unit circle). The [open balls](wikipedia://en/160556#In_general_metric_spaces) are circular arcs. Since multiplication and inversion are [continuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) on $\mathbb{C}^\times$, the circle group has the structure of a [topological group](wikipedia://en/42315). Moreover, since the unit circle is a [closed subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_subset) of the complex plane, the circle group is a closed subgroup of $\mathbb{C}^\times$ (itself regarded as a topological group). It is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), connected, but not [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected). Its [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) is the additive group of integers, with each integer corresponding to the number of times a closed path loops around the circle.

### Integration and Haar measure {#Integration_and_Haar_measure}

Integration in the circle group can be defined in a canonical invariant way. For a continuous function $f$, the integral takes the form:

- For the convention $\mathbb T=U(1)$,

$$\int_{U(1)} f(z) d\mu(z) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(e^{i\theta})\,d\theta.$$

- For the convention $\mathbb T=\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z$,[^n7]

$$\int_{\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z} f(\theta) d\mu(\theta) = \frac1{2\pi}\int_0^{2\pi}f(\theta)\,d\theta.$$

- For the convention $\mathbb T=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$,[^n8][^o19]

$$\int_{\mathbb R/\mathbb Z} f(x) d\mu(x) = \int_0^1f(x)\,dx.$$

Here, $d\theta$ and $dx$ refer to the usual [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on the real line. In each case, this is a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437), meaning that the integral of the constant function $1$ over the whole group is equal to 1. This integral is not arbitrary, but is the unique ([Borel](wikipedia://en/4322)) probability measure that is invariant under constant change of variables $z\to e^{iC}z$ for $U(1)$ (or $\theta\to\theta + C$ for $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ or $\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z$). The asserted uniqueness is a special case of [Haar's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Haar's_theorem), and the measure is known as the (normalized) [Haar measure](wikipedia://en/40645).

### Invariant metrics {#Invariant_metrics}

*Image: Copo de nieve de Koch 2.png.* Lipschitz embedding of a snowflake metric on the circle group into the Euclidean plane, the [Koch snowflake](wikipedia://en/46959)

The chordal and [length metrics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_metric_space) already defined give a pair of invariant metrics on the circle group. They are invariant in the sense that the distance between two points is unchanged if the points are rotated through the same angle. These metrics are equivalent, in the sense that there is a positive constant $C$ with

$$\frac{1}{C}d_{\text{chord}}(x,y)\le d_{\text{length}}(x,y) \le C d_{\text{chord}}(x,y)$$

for every pair of points $x,y\in\mathbb T$. The arclength metric is the only invariant [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) on the circle, and in fact the only invariant [length metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Length_metric_space),[^o21] up to a constant normalization.

Other invariant distance functions defining an invariant [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) structure on the circle group can also be defined. In general, an invariant metric is determined by specifying the distance from a point $1$ to each point $x\in\mathbb T$, $\lambda(x) = d(1,x)$. If $\lambda:\mathbb T\to[0,\infty)$ is continuous, satisfies $\lambda(1)=0$, $\lambda(x)>0$ for $x\ne1$, $\lambda(x)=\lambda(x^{-1})$, and $\lambda(xy)\leq\lambda(x)+\lambda(y)$, then $d(x,y)=\lambda(x^{-1}y)$ defines a compatible invariant metric on $\mathbb T$.

An example is $d(x,y) = d_{\text{chord}}(x,y)^\alpha$ where $0 < \alpha \le 1$, called a *snowflake metric*. It is not equivalent to the chord (or length) metric unless $\alpha=1$. With this metric, the [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294) of $\mathbb T$ is $1/\alpha$, and so gives the group the structure of a [fractal curve](wikipedia://en/4972605). The snowflake interpolates between the usual length metric $\alpha=1$, which is one-dimensional, and metrics that have unbounded Hausdorff dimension. The snowflake metric with $\alpha=\log(3)/\log(4)$ can be embedded in the plane into the [Koch snowflake](wikipedia://en/46959) plane curve, whose chordal metric is equivalent.[^o22]

Other invariant metrics on the circle are used in areas of [data analysis](wikipedia://en/2720954) and [machine learning](wikipedia://en/233488), and exhibit similar metric-measure properties as the snowflake metrics. One family derives from the study of [diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) on the circle, or equivalently is given by the [Sobolev distance](wikipedia://en/611964) between [Dirac delta functions](wikipedia://en/37021) on the circle group:[^o23]

$$d_s(x,y)^2 = \|\delta_x-\delta_y\|_{H^{-s}}^2 = \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z\setminus\{0\}} |n|^{-2s}|x^n-y^n|^2,\quad s > 1/2.$$

### Manifold and Lie group structure {#Manifold_and_Lie_group_structure}

The circle is a 1-dimensional real [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), and multiplication and inversion are [real-analytic maps](wikipedia://en/61478) on the circle. This gives the circle group the structure of a [one-parameter group](wikipedia://en/754851), an instance of a [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945). In fact, [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) isomorphism, it is the unique 1-dimensional [compact](wikipedia://en/6042), [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) Lie group. Moreover, every $n$-dimensional compact, connected, abelian Lie group is isomorphic to $\mathbb T^n$.

Every compact Lie group $\mathrm{G}$ of positive dimension contains a non-trivial [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136) as a subgroup, and therefore a subgroup isomorphic to the circle group. This means that, thinking in terms of [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741), a compact symmetry group acting *continuously* can be expected to have one-parameter circle subgroups acting; the consequences in physical systems are seen, for example, at [rotational invariance](wikipedia://en/424202) and [spontaneous symmetry breaking](wikipedia://en/297466).

## Subgroups {#Subgroups}

The circle group has many [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011), but its only proper [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) subgroups consist of [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950): For each integer $n > 0$, there are $n$ distinct $n$th roots of unity, which form a [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order $n$, which is the unique subgroup of that order.

All other subgroups of the circle group have infinitely many elements and are [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29). Some examples are:

- The set of *all* roots of unity. This is the group $\{e^{2\pi i q}\mid q\in\mathbb Q\}$, where $\mathbb Q$ is the set of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024). It is dense in the same way that the rational numbers are dense in the reals.
- A different dense subgroup is the [group of rational points on the unit circle](wikipedia://en/29508075), that is pairs $(x,y)$ of rational numbers satisfying $x^2+y^2=1$. This is because the rational numbers are mapped to rational points of the circle under [stereographic projection](wikipedia://en/143431), the same parametrization that produces primitive [Pythagorean triples](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagorean_triples).
- When $p$ is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), the subgroup of all roots of unity whose order is a power of $p$ is also dense. Equivalently, under the isomorphism $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z\cong\mathbb T$, this is the subgroup consisting of rationals whose denominator is a power of $p$. This subgroup is called the [Prüfer $p$-group](wikipedia://en/4885031).
- For an [irrational number](wikipedia://en/20647689) $a$ in $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$, the multiples of $a$, $\{na|n\in\mathbb Z\}$ is a dense subgroup which is discussed further below.

### Endomorphisms and covering maps {#Endomorphisms_and_covering_maps}

The continuous group [endomorphisms](wikipedia://en/9569) of the circle group (written multiplicatively) are the power maps

$$z\mapsto z^n,\qquad n\in\mathbb Z.$$

For $n\ne 0$, this map is an $|n|$-fold [covering homomorphism](wikipedia://en/250323) whose kernel is the group of $|n|$th roots of unity. In particular, the [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) of $\mathbb T$ as a topological group is

$$\operatorname{Aut}(\mathbb T)\cong\{\pm 1\},$$

corresponding to $z\mapsto z$ and $z\mapsto z^{-1}$.

## Representations {#Representations}

The finite-dimensional continuous [representations](wikipedia://en/12695) of the circle group are easy to describe. It follows from [Schur's lemma](wikipedia://en/521696) that the [irreducible](wikipedia://en/385982) finite-dimensional continuous [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) representations of an abelian compact group are all 1-dimensional. Since the circle group is compact, any continuous representation

$$\rho\colon \mathbb T \to \mathrm{GL}(1, \mathbb{C}) \cong \mathbb{C}^\times$$

must take values in $\mbox{U}(1) \cong \mathbb T$. Therefore, the irreducible finite-dimensional continuous complex representations of the circle group are just the continuous [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/12396) from the circle group to itself.

For each integer $n$ we can define a representation $\phi_n$ of the circle group by $\phi_n(z) = z^n$. These representations are all inequivalent. The representation $\phi_{-n}$ is [conjugate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conjugate_representation) to $\phi_{n}$:

$$\phi_{-n} = \overline{\phi_n}.$$

These representations are just the continuous [characters](wikipedia://en/293418) of the circle group. The continuous [character group](wikipedia://en/1384568) of $\mathbb T$, also called its [Pontryagin dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontryagin_dual), is an [infinite cyclic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_cyclic_group) generated by $\phi_1$:

$$\operatorname{Hom}_{\mathrm{cts}}(\mathbb T, \mathbb T) \cong \mathbb{Z}.$$

The finite-dimensional continuous irreducible [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) representations of the circle group are the [trivial representation](wikipedia://en/450573) (which is 1-dimensional) and the representations

$$\rho_n\bigl(e^{i\theta}\bigr) = \begin{bmatrix} \cos n\theta & -\sin n\theta \\ \sin n\theta & \cos n\theta \end{bmatrix}, \quad n \in \mathbb{Z}^+ ,$$

taking values in $\mathrm{SO}(2)$. Here we only have positive integers $n$, since the representation $\rho_{-n}$ is equivalent to $\rho_n$. After complexification, $\rho_n$ decomposes as the direct sum of the two complex characters $\phi_n$ and $\phi_{-n}$.

The representations of the circle group play a role in constructing the representations of all [compact Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_Lie_group).[^o29] Every compact Lie group has a [maximal torus](wikipedia://en/360136), which is the product of $n$ copies of the circle group. The number $n$ is called the rank of the Lie group. The representations of the torus determine the [weight lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weight_lattice), and the representations of the full group can be found by [highest weight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Highest_weight) theory.

:::

## Fourier analysis {#Fourier_analysis}

### Fourier series {#Fourier_series}

The characters of $\mathbb T$ are the fundamental building blocks of [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038). The characters form an [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) for $L^2(\mathbb T)$ with respect to normalized Haar measure. Thus any square-integrable function on $\mathbb T$ has an expansion

$$f=\sum_{n=-\infty}^{\infty} \widehat f(n)e_n$$

with convergence in $L^2$, where

$$\widehat f(n)=\int_{\mathbb T}\overline{e_n(t)}f(t)\,d\mu(t)$$

is its $n$th Fourier coefficient.

In the three common conventions for realizing the circle group, the characters are:

- For $U(1)$, they are $e_n(z)=z^n$.
- For $\mathbb R/2\pi\mathbb Z$, they are $e_n(\theta)=e^{in\theta}$.
- For $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$, they are $e_n(x)=e^{2\pi i n x}$.

These conventions place the factor $2\pi$ in different parts of the notation. The first is the unit-circle or [Laurent convention](wikipedia://en/61213) (and $2\pi$ is the usual arc length of the circle). When the series is regarded as a Laurent series in a complex variable $z$, it is closely related to the bilateral [z-transform](wikipedia://en/171589). The second is the [angular frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_frequency) convention, using a coordinate of period $2\pi$. The third is the ordinary frequency convention using a coordinate of period $1$, with $2\pi$ appearing in the character.

### Heat equation and diffusion {#Heat_equation_and_diffusion}

[Diffusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffusion) is an invariant process on the circle that governs the statistics of [motion of Brownian particles](wikipedia://en/4436) and the [flow of heat](wikipedia://en/166415) in a one-dimensional periodic domain. Diffusion is governed by the standard translation-invariant [Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laplacian), which on $\mathbb T=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$ is $\Delta=d^2/dx^2$, and the heat equation is

$$\frac{\partial u}{\partial t} = \frac{\partial^2u}{\partial x^2}.$$

This equation can be solved for initial data the character $e^{2\pi i n x}$, giving the diagonal evolution

$$e^{t\Delta}e^{2\pi i n x}=e^{-4\pi^2n^2t}e^{2\pi i n x}.$$

Thus the [heat kernel](wikipedia://en/3904336) on the circle is the Fourier series

$$p_t(x)=\sum_{n\in\mathbb Z}e^{-4\pi^2n^2t}e^{2\pi i n x} =1+2\sum_{n=1}^{\infty}e^{-4\pi^2n^2t}\cos(2\pi n x).$$

Equivalently, by [Poisson summation](wikipedia://en/502042),

$$p_t(x)=\frac{1}{\sqrt{4\pi t}}\sum_{k\in\mathbb Z} \exp\!\left(-\frac{(x+k)^2}{4t}\right).$$

This is a periodized [Gaussian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gaussian) and may be expressed in terms of a [Jacobi theta function](wikipedia://en/449745). The solution of the heat equation with initial data $f$ is [convolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convolution_%28mathematics%29) with this kernel, $u(t,x)=(p_t*f)(x)$.

An analogous method, essentially replacing $t$ by $it$, can be used to study the [Schrödinger equation](wikipedia://en/59874).

### Use in central extensions {#Use_in_central_extensions}

The circle group serves an important role in [projective representations](wikipedia://en/348860) of other groups. A projective [unitary representation](wikipedia://en/292788) is a continuous group homomorphism from a group $G$ to the [projective unitary group](wikipedia://en/4402359) of a [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $H$, $\rho\colon G\to PU(H)$. Such a representation can be locally lifted to a function $\tilde \rho\colon G\to U(H)$ that makes a choice of [phase factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_factor), such that $\tilde \rho(g)\tilde \rho(h)=\omega(g,h)\tilde\rho(gh)$. The obstruction $\omega$ is a [2-cocycle](wikipedia://en/2870898) on $G$ with values in the circle group. In this setting, the circle group is used to construct a group $\widetilde G$ for which the representation becomes linear rather than projective, by taking the [central extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_extension_%28mathematics%29)

$$1\to\mathbb T\to \widetilde G\to G\to 1$$

associated with the cocycle $\omega$.[^o34] This construction underlies the [Heisenberg group](wikipedia://en/448518), where phase factors give the central extension appearing in the [canonical commutation relations](wikipedia://en/706295) and the [Stone–von Neumann theorem](wikipedia://en/744569).[^o35] In [conformal field theory](wikipedia://en/364774), one version of the [Virasoro group](wikipedia://en/64053581) is the universal central extension of the oriented [diffeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/8564) of the circle by the circle group.

## Ergodic theory {#Ergodic_theory}

The circle group is a fundamental starting point in the study of [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986) and [dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_systems). For each $a\in\mathbb T=\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$, consider the operation of translation by $a$ on the circle group $\mathbb T$, $R_a\colon \mathbb T\to\mathbb T$, $R_a(x) = a+x$. Several natural questions are:

- What structure does the orbit of a point have? For instance, is it [dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dense_%28topology%29) in the group? Is the orbit of a point [equidistributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equidistributed)?
- What is the effect of averaging functions on the circle over many applications of the operator $R_a$?

The answers turn on whether $a$ is rational or irrational, that is whether $a\in\mathbb Q/\mathbb Z$ or not.

If $a$ is rational with (least) denominator $n$, then one has $R_a^n=1$, and so the orbits are periodic. If not, then the orbit is dense, and in fact equidistributed.

Similarly, the semigroup of translations $R_a,R_a^2,\dots$ is [minimal](wikipedia://en/16379713) and acts [ergodically](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ergodic) if and only if $a$ is irrational.[^n8] It is [uniquely ergodic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniquely_ergodic) in that case.[^o38] [One version](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birkhoff_ergodic_theorem) of the ergodicity states that, given any Haar-integrable function $f$ on $\mathbb T$, the average value of $f$ over the whole circle is equal to the average of the function computed over the countable family translates $f(R_ax),f(R_a^2x),\dots$, for almost every $x$ More formally,[^n8]

$$\frac1N\sum_{n=1}^N f(R_a^nx) \to \int_{\mathbb T} f(x)\,dx$$

for [almost every](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_every) $x$, and also in $L^1(\mathbb T)$.

## Line bundles and gauge theory {#Line_bundles_and_gauge_theory}

The circle group appears naturally in the theory of [Hermitian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_vector_bundle) complex [line bundles](wikipedia://en/361366) over [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470) and [algebraic varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) such as [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181). If $L\to X$ is a complex line bundle with a Hermitian metric, the unit vectors in the fibers from a [principal $U(1)$-bundle](wikipedia://en/310950). Conversely, a principal $U(1)$-bundle determines an associated complex line bundle.

For suitable spaces, the isomorphism classes of principal $U(1)$-bundles are classified by the [first Chern class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_Chern_class), $c_1\in H^2(X;\mathbb Z)$. Equivalently, the [classifying space](wikipedia://en/923556) of $U(1)$, $BU(1)$, is an infinite complex [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbb{CP}^\infty$ of rays in a separable complex [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932), whose second [cohomology group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohomology_group) is generated by the first Chern class.[^o41][^o42]

In [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/23912155), $U(1)$ is the structure group of [electromagnetism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetism). A [connection](wikipedia://en/308815) on a principal $U(1)$-bundle may be represented locally by an imaginary-valued one-form $A$, which corresponds to the [electromagnetic potential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electromagnetic_potential), whose [curvature two-form](wikipedia://en/489440) $F=dA$ represents the field.[^o43]

## Pontryagin duality and Banach algebras {#Pontryagin_duality_and_Banach_algebras}

The [Pontryagin dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontryagin_dual) of the circle group is naturally isomorphic to the additive group of integers. The character corresponding to $n\in\mathbb Z$ is the homomorphism $\chi_n\colon \mathbb T\to\mathbb T$ given by $\chi_n(z)=z^n$. Conversely, the Pontryagin dual of the discrete group $\mathbb Z$ is naturally isomorphic to $\mathbb T$, because every character of the integers has the form $n\to z^n$ for some unit complex number $z$.

These dualities are reflected in the associated [commutative Banach algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_Banach_algebra). The [group algebra](wikipedia://en/381010) $L^1(\mathbb T)$, with convolution, has [Gelfand transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gelfand_transform) given by the Fourier transform, and its characters are indexed by $\mathbb Z$; equivalently its space of [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164) is $\mathbb Z$. In the opposite direction, the group algebra $\ell^1(\mathbb Z)$ has maximal ideal space naturally homeomorphic to $\mathbb T$. Its Gelfand transform maps a summable sequence $(a_n)_{n\in\mathbb Z}$ to the function $\textstyle z\mapsto \sum_{n\in\mathbb Z} a_n z^n$.[^n9] The image is the [Wiener algebra](wikipedia://en/27489616) $A(\mathbb T)$, the algebra of continuous functions on the circle with absolutely convergent Fourier series.[^o46] This is a proper subalgebra, under pointwise multiplication, of the Banach algebra $C(\mathbb T)$.

The same duality appears for group [$C^*$-algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cstar_algebra). The group $C^*$-algebra of the discrete group $\mathbb Z$ is the universal unital $C^*$-algebra generated by one unitary element, and is naturally isomorphic to $C(\mathbb T)$; the generator corresponds to the coordinate function $z\mapsto z$. In the opposite direction, the group $C^*$-algebra of the circle group, formed using convolution on $\mathbb T$, is identified by the Fourier transform with $c_0(\mathbb Z)$, the space of bi-infinite sequences of complex numbers that tend to zero at $\pm\infty$.[^n10] The statement encodes the [Riemann-Lebesgue lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann-Lebesgue_lemma) for Fourier series.

The circle group also appears as the basic building block of [solenoids](wikipedia://en/2217712). If $n_1,n_2,\ldots$ are integers with $|n_i|>1$, the [inverse limit](wikipedia://en/15109) $\vphantom{\Bigg|}\mathbb T \xleftarrow{z~\!\mapsto z^{n_1}} \mathbb T\xleftarrow{z~\!\mapsto z^{n_2}} \cdots$ is a compact connected abelian group. Its Pontryagin dual is the [direct limit](wikipedia://en/211566) $\vphantom{\bigg|}\mathbb Z \xrightarrow{\!\times n_1\,} \mathbb Z\xrightarrow{\!\times n_2\,} \cdots$, which may be identified with a rank-one subgroup of $\mathbb Q$.[^o48]p. 403–404 In this way solenoids are one-dimensional compact connected abelian groups obtained from the circle by inverse limits of covering homomorphisms.

Every Pontryagin dual of a torsion-free rank one discrete abelian group is either a circle or a solenoid.[^o48]p. 407–409 Here a torsion-free abelian group $A$ has rank one if $A\otimes_{\mathbb Z}\mathbb Q\cong\mathbb Q$; equivalently, $A$ is isomorphic to a nonzero subgroup of $\mathbb Q$. The cyclic rank-one group $\mathbb Z$ has dual $\mathbb T$; noncyclic rank-one subgroups of $\mathbb Q$ give the one-dimensional solenoids.

## Abstract group structure {#Abstract_group_structure}

The circle group $\mathbb T$ can be regarded as an abstract group without regard for its topology, or equivalently as a (non-compact) topological group equipped with the [discrete topology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_topology).

The circle group is a [divisible group](wikipedia://en/472147). Its [torsion subgroup](wikipedia://en/144052) is given by the set of all $n$-th [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity) for all $n$ and is isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$. The [structure theorem](wikipedia://en/472147#Structure_theorem_of_divisible_groups) for divisible groups and the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) together tell us that $\mathbb T$ is isomorphic, as an abstract group, to the [direct sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_sum_of_abelian_groups) of $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$ with a number of copies of $\mathbb{Q}$.[^n11]

The number of copies of $\mathbb{Q}$ must be $\mathfrak c$ (the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901)) in order for the cardinality of the direct sum to be correct. But the direct sum of $\mathfrak c$ copies of $\mathbb{Q}$ is isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$, as $\mathbb{R}$ is a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of dimension $\mathfrak c$ over $\mathbb{Q}$. Thus, in this sense,

$$\mathbb T \cong \mathbb{R} \oplus (\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}).$$

The isomorphism of abstract groups

$$\mathbb{C}^\times \cong \mathbb{R} \oplus (\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z})$$

can be proved in the same way, since $\mathbb{C}^\times$ is also a divisible abelian group whose torsion subgroup is the same as the torsion subgroup of $\mathbb T$.

## Algebraic group structure {#Algebraic_group_structure}

The circle group can also be viewed as an [algebraic torus](wikipedia://en/504697). The [field norm](wikipedia://en/450555) from $\mathbb C$ to $\mathbb R$ is

$$N_{\mathbb C/\mathbb R}(z)=z\bar z=|z|^2,$$

and the circle group is the [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of the norm homomorphism

$$N_{\mathbb C/\mathbb R}\colon \mathbb C^\times\to\mathbb R^\times.$$

Equivalently,

$$\mathbb T=\{z\in\mathbb C^\times:z\bar z=1\}.$$

In [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) terminology, this is the group of real points of the [norm-one torus](wikipedia://en/504697) associated with the [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) $\mathbb C/\mathbb R$.[^o52]

## Non-standard analysis {#Non-standard_analysis}

In [non-standard analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-standard_analysis), the most natural replacement of the circle group is the [hyperfinite](wikipedia://en/25317960) [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327)

$$C_N={}^*\mathbb Z/N{}^*\mathbb Z,$$

where $N$ is an infinite [hyperinteger](wikipedia://en/19327282), that is a number that is greater than every standard natural number. The [standard part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_part) maps $C_N$ to the circle group $\mathbb R/\mathbb Z$. Moreover, it carries a natural normalized [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786) $\mu_N(A) = |A|/N$, whose associated [Loeb measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loeb_measure) (the [pushforward measure](wikipedia://en/9412979) under the standard part) gives the usual Haar measure on the circle group. The topology on the standard part is recovered by imposing an invariant metric on $C_N$.[^o54]

The group $C_N$ is the natural setting for non-standard Fourier analysis. One reason is that, being a cyclic group, it is isomorphic to its dual group (internally). Non-standard Fourier series are ordinary (hyper-)finite sums, whose standard part recovers traditional Fourier series on the circle group.

There is an embedding of $C_N$ into the non-standard circle ${}^*S^1$, but its image is a proper subgroup. The dual group of ${}^*S^1$ is the full group of non-standard integers ${}^*\mathbb Z$, which is not hyperfinite, and so the Fourier theory on the full non-standard circle group is less finite.
