# Telescoping series

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Telescoping series"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **[telescoping](wikipedia://en/83409961) series** is a [series](wikipedia://en/15287) whose general term $t_n$ is of the form $t_n=a_n-a_{n-1}$, i.e. the difference of two consecutive terms of a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) $(a_n)$. As a consequence the partial sums of the series only consists of two terms of $(a_n)$ after cancellation.[^o2][^o3]

:::

The cancellation technique, with part of each term cancelling with part of the next term, is known as the **method of differences**.

An early statement of the formula for the sum or partial sums of a telescoping series can be found in a 1644 work by [Evangelista Torricelli](wikipedia://en/210413), *De dimensione parabolae*.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

*Image: Telescoping Series.png.* A telescoping series of powers. Note in the [summation sign](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Summation_sign), $\sum$, the index *n* goes from 1 to *m*. There is no relationship between *n* and *m* beyond the fact that both are [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers).

Telescoping [sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_%28mathematics%29) are finite sums in which pairs of consecutive terms partly cancel each other, leaving only parts of the initial and final terms.[^o2][^o7] Let $a_n$ be the elements of a sequence of numbers. Then

$$\sum_{n=1}^N \left(a_n - a_{n-1}\right) = a_N - a_0.$$

If $a_n$ converges to a limit $L$, the telescoping [series](wikipedia://en/15287) gives:

$$\sum_{n=1}^\infty \left(a_n - a_{n-1}\right) = L-a_0.$$

Every series is a telescoping series of its own partial sums.[^o8]

:::

::: {#o10 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The product of a finite [geometric series](wikipedia://en/12630) with initial term $a$ and common ratio $r$ by the factor $(1 - r)$ yields a telescoping sum:[^o11] $$(1 - r) \sum^n_{k=0} ar^k = \sum^n_{k=0} \left(ar^k - ar^{k+1}\right) = a - a r^{n+1}$$ When $|r| < 1$, this allows for a direct calculation of its limit as $n \rightarrow \infty$ and implies:[^o12] $$\sum^\infty_{k=0} ar^k = \frac{a}{1 - r}.$$
- The series $$\sum_{n=1}^\infty\frac{1}{n(n+1)}$$ is the series of [reciprocals](wikipedia://en/229940) of [pronic numbers](wikipedia://en/652733), and it is recognizable as a telescoping series once rewritten in [partial fraction](wikipedia://en/247288) form[^o2] $$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{1}{n(n+1)} & = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1} \right) \\ & = \lim_{N\to\infty} \sum_{n=1}^N \left( \frac{1}{n} - \frac{1}{n+1} \right) \\ & = \lim_{N\to\infty} \left\lbrack {\left(1 - \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left(\frac{1}{2} - \frac{1}{3}\right) + \cdots + \left(\frac{1}{N} - \frac{1}{N+1}\right) } \right\rbrack \\ & = \lim_{N\to\infty} \left\lbrack { 1 + \left( - \frac{1}{2} + \frac{1}{2}\right) + \left( - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{3}\right) + \cdots + \left( - \frac{1}{N} + \frac{1}{N}\right) - \frac{1}{N+1} } \right\rbrack \\ & = \lim_{N\to\infty} \left\lbrack { 1 - \frac{1}{N+1} } \right\rbrack = 1. \end{aligned}$$
- Let *k* be a positive integer. Then $$\sum^\infty_{n=1} {\frac{1}{n(n+k)}} = \frac{H_k}{k}$$ where *H*<sub>*k*</sub> is the *k*th [harmonic number](wikipedia://en/214729).
- Let *k* and *m* with *k* $\neq$ *m* be positive integers. Then $$\sum^\infty_{n=1} {\frac{1}{(n+k)(n+k+1)\dots(n+m-1)(n+m)}} = \frac{1}{m-k} \cdot \frac{k!}{m!}$$ where $!$ denotes the [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) operation.
- Many [trigonometric functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trigonometric_function) also admit representation as differences, which may reveal telescopic canceling between the consecutive terms. Using the [angle addition identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_addition_identity) for a product of sines, $$\begin{aligned} \sum_{n=1}^N \sin\left(n\right) & {} = \sum_{n=1}^N \frac{1}{2} \csc\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \left(2\sin\left(\frac{1}{2}\right)\sin\left(n\right)\right) \\ & {} =\frac{1}{2} \csc\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \sum_{n=1}^N \left(\cos\left(\frac{2n-1}{2}\right) -\cos\left(\frac{2n+1}{2}\right)\right) \\ & {} =\frac{1}{2} \csc\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) \left(\cos\left(\frac{1}{2}\right) -\cos\left(\frac{2N+1}{2}\right)\right), \end{aligned}$$ which does not converge as $N \rightarrow \infty.$

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), a [Poisson process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poisson_process) is a stochastic process of which the simplest case involves "occurrences" at random times, the waiting time until the next occurrence having a [memoryless](wikipedia://en/772441) [exponential distribution](wikipedia://en/45906), and the number of "occurrences" in any time interval having a [Poisson distribution](wikipedia://en/23009144) whose expected value is proportional to the length of the time interval. Let *X*<sub>*t*</sub> be the number of "occurrences" before time *t*, and let *T*<sub>*x*</sub> be the waiting time until the *x*th "occurrence". We seek the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of the [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) *T*<sub>*x*</sub>. We use the [probability mass function](wikipedia://en/154725) for the Poisson distribution, which tells us that

$$\Pr(X_t = x) = \frac{(\lambda t)^x e^{-\lambda t}}{x!},$$

where λ is the average number of occurrences in any time interval of length 1. Observe that the event {*X*<sub>*t*</sub> ≥ x} is the same as the event {*T*<sub>*x*</sub> ≤ *t*}, and thus they have the same probability. Intuitively, if something occurs at least $x$ times before time $t$, we have to wait at most $t$ for the $x$th occurrence. The density function we seek is therefore

$$\begin{aligned} f(t) & = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Pr(T_x \le t) \\ & = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\Pr(X_t \ge x) \\ & = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}(1 - \Pr(X_t \le x-1)) \\ & = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left( 1 - \sum_{u=0}^{x-1} \Pr(X_t = u)\right) \\ & = \frac{\mathrm{d}}{\mathrm{d}t}\left( 1 - \sum_{u=0}^{x-1} \frac{(\lambda t)^u e^{-\lambda t}}{u!} \right) \\ & = \lambda e^{-\lambda t} - e^{-\lambda t} \sum_{u=1}^{x-1} \left( \frac{\lambda^ut^{u-1}}{(u-1)!} - \frac{\lambda^{u+1} t^u}{u!} \right) \end{aligned}$$

The sum telescopes, leaving

$$f(t) = \frac{\lambda^x t^{x-1} e^{-\lambda t}}{(x-1)!}.$$

For other applications, see:

- [Proof that the sum of the reciprocals of the primes diverges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_that_the_sum_of_the_reciprocals_of_the_primes_diverges), where one of the proofs uses a telescoping sum;
- [Fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696), a continuous analog of telescoping series;
- [Order statistic](wikipedia://en/160986), where a telescoping sum occurs in the derivation of a probability density function;
- [Lefschetz fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/582530), where a telescoping sum arises in [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801);
- [Homology theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homology_theory), concept in algebraic topology;
- [Eilenberg–Mazur swindle](wikipedia://en/14463498), where a telescoping sum of knots occurs;
- [Faddeev–LeVerrier algorithm](wikipedia://en/49598229).

:::

## Related concepts {#Related_concepts}

A *telescoping product* is a finite [product](wikipedia://en/24977) (or the partial product of an infinite product) that can be canceled by the method of quotients to be eventually only a finite number of factors.[^o16][^o17] It is the finite products in which consecutive terms cancel denominator with numerator, leaving only the initial and final terms. Let $a_n$ be a sequence of numbers. Then,

$$\prod_{n=1}^N \frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n} = \frac{a_0}{a_N}.$$

If $a_n$ converges to 1, the resulting product gives:

$$\prod_{n=1}^\infty \frac{a_{n-1}}{a_n} = a_0$$

For example, the infinite product[^o16]

$$\prod_{n=2}^{\infty} \left(1-\frac{1}{n^2} \right)$$

simplifies as

$$\begin{aligned} \prod_{n=2}^{\infty} \left(1-\frac{1}{n^2} \right) &=\prod_{n=2}^{\infty}\frac{(n-1)(n+1)}{n^2} \\ &=\lim_{N\to\infty} \prod_{n=2}^{N}\frac{n-1}{n} \times \prod_{n=2}^{N}\frac{n+1}{n} \\ &= \lim_{N\to\infty} \left\lbrack {\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{2}{3} \times \frac{3}{4} \times \cdots \times \frac{N-1}{N}} \right\rbrack \times \left\lbrack {\frac{3}{2} \times \frac{4}{3} \times \frac{5}{4} \times \cdots \times \frac{N}{N-1} \times \frac{N+1}{N}} \right\rbrack \\ &= \lim_{N\to\infty} \left\lbrack \frac{1}{2} \right\rbrack \times \left\lbrack \frac{N+1}{N} \right\rbrack \\ &= \frac{1}{2}\times \lim_{N\to\infty} \left\lbrack \frac{N+1}{N} \right\rbrack \\ &=\frac{1}{2}. \end{aligned}$$
