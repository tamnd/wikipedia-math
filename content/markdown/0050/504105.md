# Minkowski addition

*Image: Сумма Минковского.svg.* The red figure is the Minkowski sum of blue and green figures.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Minkowski addition"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **sumset** of two [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) $A$ and $B$ of an (additive) [abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) is formed by adding each element of $A$ to each element of $B$:

$$A + B = \{a+b\mid a\in A,\ b\in B\}.$$

:::

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), the **Minkowski sum** of two subsets $A$ and $B$ of a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is the set of the points whose [position vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Position_vector) form the sumset of the position vectors of $A$ and $B$. The Minkowski sum depends on the choice of an [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) in the Euclidean space. As a change of origin amounts to [translate](wikipedia://en/204682) the Minkowski sum, the Minkowski sum is defined [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a translation, and its shape and orientation are well defined.

The **Minkowski difference** (also *Minkowski subtraction*, *Minkowski decomposition*, or *geometric difference*)[^o2] is the corresponding inverse, where $(A - B)$ produces a set that could be summed with *B* to recover *A*. This is defined as the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of the Minkowski sum of the complement of *A* with the reflection of *B* about the origin.[^o3]

$$\begin{aligned} -B &= \{\mathbf{-b}\,|\,\mathbf{b}\in B\}\\ A - B &= (A^\complement + (-B))^\complement \end{aligned}$$

This definition allows a symmetrical relationship between the Minkowski sum and difference. Note that alternately taking the sum and difference with *B* is not necessarily equivalent. The sum can fill gaps which the difference may not re-open, and the difference can erase small islands which the sum cannot recreate from nothing.

$$\begin{aligned} (A - B) + B &\subseteq A\\ (A + B) - B &\supseteq A\\ A - B &= (A^\complement + (-B))^\complement\\ A + B &= (A^\complement - (-B))^\complement\\ \end{aligned}$$

In 2D [image processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Image_processing) the Minkowski sum and difference are known as [dilation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dilation_%28morphology%29) and [erosion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erosion_%28morphology%29).

An alternative definition of the Minkowski difference is sometimes used for computing intersection of convex shapes.[^o4] This is not equivalent to the previous definition, and is not an inverse of the sum operation. Instead it replaces the vector addition of the Minkowski sum with a [vector subtraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subtraction). If the two convex shapes intersect, the resulting set will contain the origin.

$$A - B = \{\mathbf{a}-\mathbf{b}\,|\,\mathbf{a}\in A,\ \mathbf{b}\in B\} = A + (-B)$$

The concept is named for [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537).

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

*Image: Minkowski-sumex4.svg.* Minkowski sum *A* + *B*

For example, if we have two sets *A* and *B*, each consisting of three position vectors (informally, three points), representing the [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) of two [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) in $\mathbb{R}^2$, with coordinates

$$A = \{(1,0), (0,1), (0,-1)\}$$

and

$$B = \{(0,0), (1,1), (1,-1)\}$$

then their Minkowski sum is

$$A + B = \{(1,0), (2,1), (2,-1), (0,1), (1,2), (1,0), (0,-1), (1,0), (1,-2)\},$$

which comprises the vertices of a hexagon and its center.

For Minkowski addition, the zero set, $\{ 0 \},$ containing only the [zero vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_vector), 0, is an [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962): for every subset *S* of a vector space,

$$S + \{0\} = S.$$

The [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) is important in Minkowski addition, because the empty set annihilates every other subset: for every subset *S* of a vector space, its sum with the empty set is empty:

$$S + \emptyset = \emptyset.$$

For another example, consider the Minkowski sums of open or closed balls in the field $\mathbb{K},$ which is either the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$ or [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$. If $B_r:= \{ s \in \mathbb{K}: |s| \leq r \}$ is the closed ball of radius $r \in [0, \infty]$ centered at $0$ in $\mathbb{K}$ then for any $r, s \in [0, \infty]$, $B_r + B_s = B_{r+s}$ and also $c B_r = B_{|c|r}$ will hold for any scalar $c \in \mathbb{K}$ such that the product $|c|r$ is defined (which happens when $c \neq 0$ or $r \neq \infty$). If $r$, $s$, and $c$ are all non-zero then the same equalities would still hold had $B_r$ been defined to be the open ball, rather than the closed ball, centered at 0 (the non-zero assumption is needed because the open ball of radius 0 is the empty set). The Minkowski sum of a closed ball and an open ball is an open ball. More generally, the Minkowski sum of an [open subset](wikipedia://en/39358) with any other set will be an open subset.

If $G = \{ (x, 1/x) : 0 \neq x \in \mathbb{R} \}$ is the [graph](wikipedia://en/87352) of $f(x) = \frac{1}{x}$ and if and $Y = \{ 0 \} \times \mathbb{R}$ is the $y$-axis in $X = \mathbb{R}^2$ then the Minkowski sum of these two [closed subsets](wikipedia://en/47279) of the plane is the [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $G + Y = \{ (x, y) \in \mathbb{R}^2 : x \neq 0 \} = \mathbb{R}^2 \setminus Y$ consisting of everything other than the $y$-axis. This shows that the Minkowski sum of two [closed sets](wikipedia://en/47279) is not necessarily a closed set. However, the Minkowski sum of two closed subsets will be a closed subset if at least one of these sets is also a [compact subset](wikipedia://en/6042).

:::

## Convex hulls of Minkowski sums {#Convex_hulls_of_Minkowski_sums}

Minkowski addition behaves well with respect to the operation of taking [convex hulls](wikipedia://en/40634), as shown by the following proposition:

For all non-empty subsets $S_1$ and $S_2$ of a real vector space, the convex hull of their Minkowski sum is the Minkowski sum of their convex hulls:

$$\operatorname{Conv}(S_1 + S_2) = \operatorname{Conv}(S_1) + \operatorname{Conv}(S_2).$$

This result holds more generally for any finite collection of non-empty sets:

$$\operatorname{Conv}\left(\sum{S_n}\right) = \sum\operatorname{Conv}(S_n).$$

In mathematical terminology, the [operations](wikipedia://en/4140245) of Minkowski summation and of forming [convex hulls](wikipedia://en/40634) are [commuting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutativity) operations.[^o8][^o9]

If $S$ is a convex set then $\mu S + \lambda S$ is also a convex set; furthermore

$$\mu S + \lambda S = (\mu + \lambda)S$$

for every $\mu,\lambda \geq 0$. Conversely, if this "[distributive property](wikipedia://en/103118)" holds for all non-negative real numbers, $\mu$ and $\lambda$, then the set is convex.[^o10]

*Image: Minkowskisum.svg.* An example of a non-convex set such that $A + A \neq 2 A.$

The figure to the right shows an example of a non-convex set for which $2 A \subsetneq A + A.$

An example in one dimension is: $B = [1, 2] \cup [4, 5].$ It can be easily calculated that $2 B = [2, 4] \cup [8, 10]$ but $B + B = [2, 4] \cup [5, 7] \cup [8, 10],$ hence again $2 B \subsetneq B + B.$

Minkowski sums act linearly on the perimeter of two-dimensional convex bodies: the perimeter of the sum equals the sum of perimeters. Additionally, if $K$ is (the interior of) a [curve of constant width](wikipedia://en/580238), then the Minkowski sum of $K$ and of its 180° rotation is a disk. These two facts can be combined to give a short proof of [Barbier's theorem](wikipedia://en/580264) on the perimeter of curves of constant width.[^o11]

## Applications {#Applications}

Minkowski addition plays a central role in [mathematical morphology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_morphology). It arises in the [brush-and-stroke paradigm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brush-and-stroke_paradigm) of [2D computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2D_computer_graphics) (with various uses, notably by [Donald E. Knuth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Donald_E._Knuth) in [Metafont](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metafont)), and as the [solid sweep](wikipedia://en/14236540) operation of [3D computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_computer_graphics). It has also been shown to be closely connected to the [Earth mover's distance](wikipedia://en/11915675), and by extension, [optimal transport](wikipedia://en/6631661).[^o14]

### Motion planning {#Motion_planning}

Minkowski sums are used in [motion planning](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motion_planning) of an object among obstacles. They are used for the computation of the [configuration space](wikipedia://en/473596), which is the set of all admissible positions of the object. In the simple model of translational motion of an object in the plane, where the position of an object may be uniquely specified by the position of a fixed point of this object, the configuration space are the Minkowski sum of the set of obstacles and the movable object placed at the origin and rotated 180 degrees.

### Numerical control (NC) machining {#Numerical_control_(NC)_machining}

In [numerical control](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_control) machining, the programming of the NC tool exploits the fact that the Minkowski sum of the [cutting piece](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cutting_piece) with its trajectory gives the shape of the cut in the material.

### 3D solid modeling {#3D_solid_modeling}

In [OpenSCAD](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenSCAD) Minkowski sums are used to outline a shape with another shape creating a composite of both shapes.

### Aggregation theory {#Aggregation_theory}

Minkowski sums are also frequently used in aggregation theory when individual objects to be aggregated are characterized via sets.[^o19][^o20]

### Collision detection {#Collision_detection}

Minkowski sums, specifically Minkowski differences, are often used alongside [GJK algorithms](wikipedia://en/30861099) to compute [collision detection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collision_detection) for convex hulls in [physics engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_engines).

:::

## Algorithms for computing Minkowski sums {#Algorithms_for_computing_Minkowski_sums}

*Image: Shapley–Folkman lemma.svg.* Minkowski addition and convex hulls. The sixteen dark-red points (on the right) form the Minkowski sum of the four non-convex sets (on the left), each of which consists of a pair of red points. Their convex hulls (shaded pink) contain plus-signs (+): The right plus-sign is the sum of the left plus-signs.

### Planar case {#Planar_case}

#### Two convex polygons in the plane {#Two_convex_polygons_in_the_plane}

For two [convex polygons](wikipedia://en/660019) $P$ and $Q$ in the plane with $m$ and $n$ vertices, their Minkowski sum is a convex polygon with at most $m$ + $n$ vertices and may be computed in time O($m$ + $n$) by a very simple procedure, which may be informally described as follows. Assume that the edges of a polygon are given and the direction, say, counterclockwise, along the polygon boundary. Then it is easily seen that these edges of the convex polygon are ordered by [polar angle](wikipedia://en/25120). Let us [merge the ordered sequences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Merge_algorithm) of the directed edges from $P$ and $Q$ into a single ordered sequence $S$. Imagine that these edges are solid [arrows](wikipedia://en/51513) which can be moved freely while keeping them parallel to their original direction. Assemble these arrows in the order of the sequence $S$ by attaching the tail of the next arrow to the head of the previous arrow. It turns out that the resulting [polygonal chain](wikipedia://en/12056032) will in fact be a convex polygon which is the Minkowski sum of $P$ and $Q$.

#### Other {#Other}

If one polygon is convex and another one is not, the complexity of their Minkowski sum is O(*nm*). If both of them are nonconvex, their Minkowski sum complexity is O((*mn*)<sup>2</sup>).

## Essential Minkowski sum {#Essential_Minkowski_sum}

There is also a notion of the **essential Minkowski sum** +<sub>e</sub> of two subsets of Euclidean space. The usual Minkowski sum can be written as

$$A + B = \left\{ z \in \mathbb{R}^{n} \,|\, A \cap (z - B) \neq \emptyset \right\}.$$

Thus, the **essential Minkowski sum** is defined by

$$A +_{\mathrm{e}} B = \left\{ z \in \mathbb{R}^{n} \,|\, \mu \left[A \cap (z - B)\right] > 0 \right\},$$

where *μ* denotes the *n*-dimensional [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198). The reason for the term "essential" is the following property of [indicator functions](wikipedia://en/240790): while

$$1_{A \,+\, B} (z) = \sup_{x \,\in\, \mathbb{R}^{n}} 1_{A} (x) 1_{B} (z - x),$$

it can be seen that

$$1_{A \,+_{\mathrm{e}}\, B} (z) = \mathop{\mathrm{ess\,sup}}_{x \,\in\, \mathbb{R}^{n}} 1_{A} (x) 1_{B} (z - x),$$

where "ess sup" denotes the [essential supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_supremum).

## *L<sup>p</sup>* Minkowski sum {#Lp_Minkowski_sum}

For *K* and *L* compact convex subsets in $\mathbb{R}^n$, the Minkowski sum can be described by the [support function](wikipedia://en/9377661) of the convex sets:

$$h_{K+L} = h_K + h_L.$$

For *p* ≥ 1, Firey[^o28] defined the ***L*<sup>*p*</sup> Minkowski sum** *K* +${}_{p}$ *L* of compact convex sets *K* and *L* in $\mathbb{R}^n$ containing the origin as

$$h_{K +_p L}^p = h_K^p + h_L^p.$$

By the [Minkowski inequality](wikipedia://en/192022), the function *h${}_{K+_{p}L}$* is again positive homogeneous and convex and hence the support function of a compact convex set. This definition is fundamental in the *L*<sup>*p*</sup> Brunn-Minkowski theory.
