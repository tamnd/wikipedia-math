# Berry–Esseen theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Berry–Esseen theorem"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406) states that, under certain circumstances, the [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543) of the scaled [mean of a random sample](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean) [converges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convergence_in_distribution) to a [normal distribution](wikipedia://en/21462) as the sample size increases to infinity. Under stronger assumptions, the **Berry–Esseen theorem**, or **Berry–Esseen inequality**, gives a more quantitative result, because it also specifies the rate at which this convergence takes place by giving a bound on the maximal error of [approximation](wikipedia://en/1092713) between the normal distribution and the true distribution of the scaled sample mean. The approximation is measured by the [Kolmogorov–Smirnov distance](wikipedia://en/16714#Kolmogorov–Smirnov_statistic). In the case of [independent samples](wikipedia://en/27593), the convergence rate is $n^{-1/2}$, where $n$ is the sample size, and the constant is estimated in terms of the [third](wikipedia://en/28212) absolute [normalized moment](wikipedia://en/368684). It is also possible to give non-uniform bounds which become more strict for more extreme events.

:::

## Statement of the theorem {#Statement_of_the_theorem}

Statements of the theorem vary, as it was independently discovered by two [mathematicians](wikipedia://en/18902), [Andrew C. Berry](wikipedia://en/59125571) (in 1941) and [Carl-Gustav Esseen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl-Gustav_Esseen) (1942), who then, along with other authors, refined it repeatedly over subsequent decades.

### Identically distributed summands {#Identically_distributed_summands}

One version, sacrificing generality somewhat for the sake of clarity, is the following:

There exists a positive [constant](wikipedia://en/24758132) *C* such that if *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., are [i.i.d. random variables](wikipedia://en/30876902) with [E](wikipedia://en/9653)(*X*<sub>1</sub>) = 0, E(*X*<sub>1</sub><sup>2</sup>) = *σ*<sup>2</sup> > 0, and E(|*X*<sub>1</sub>|<sup>3</sup>) = *ρ* \< ∞,[^o5] and if we define

$$Y_n = {X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n \over n}$$

the [sample mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sample_mean), with *F*<sub>*n*</sub> the [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of

$${Y_n \sqrt{n} \over {\sigma}},$$

and Φ the cumulative distribution function of the [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution), then for all *x* and *n*,

$$\left|F_n(x) - \Phi(x)\right| \le {C \rho \over \sigma^3\sqrt{n}}.\ \ \ \ (1)$$

*Image: BerryEsseenTheoremCDFGraphExample.png.* Illustration of the difference in cumulative distribution functions alluded to in the theorem.

That is: given a sequence of [independent and identically distributed random variables](wikipedia://en/30876902), each having [mean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mean) zero and positive [variance](wikipedia://en/32344), if additionally the third absolute [moment](wikipedia://en/368684) is finite, then the [cumulative distribution functions](wikipedia://en/5793) of the [standardized](wikipedia://en/221722) sample mean and the standard normal distribution differ (vertically, on a graph) by no more than the specified amount. Note that the [approximation error](wikipedia://en/640422) for all *n* (and hence the limiting [rate of convergence](wikipedia://en/999701) for indefinite *n* sufficiently large) is bounded by the [order](wikipedia://en/44578) of *n*<sup>−1/2</sup>.

Calculated upper bounds on the constant *C* have decreased markedly over the years, from the original value of 7.59 by Esseen in 1942.[^o6] The estimate *C* \< 0.4748 follows from the inequality

$$\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}\left|F_n(x) - \Phi(x)\right| \le {0.33554 (\rho+0.415\sigma^3)\over \sigma^3\sqrt{n}},$$

since *σ*<sup>3</sup> ≤ *ρ* and 0.33554 · 1.415 \< 0.4748. However, if *ρ* ≥ 1.286*σ*<sup>3</sup>, then the estimate

$$\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}\left|F_n(x) - \Phi(x)\right| \le {0.3328 (\rho+0.429\sigma^3)\over \sigma^3\sqrt{n}},$$

is even tighter.[^n1]

(Esseen 1956) proved that the constant also satisfies the lower bound

$$C\geq\frac{\sqrt{10}+3}{6\sqrt{2\pi}} \approx 0.40973 \approx \frac{1}{\sqrt{2\pi}} + 0.01079 .$$

### Non-identically distributed summands {#Non-identically_distributed_summands}

Let *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., be independent random variables with [E](wikipedia://en/9653)(*X*<sub>*i*</sub>) = 0, E(*X*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>2</sup>) = *σ*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>2</sup> > 0, and E(|*X*<sub>*i*</sub>|<sup>3</sup>) = *ρ*<sub>*i*</sub> \< ∞. Also, let

$$S_n = {X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n \over \sqrt{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2+\cdots+\sigma_n^2} }$$

be the normalized *n*-th partial sum. Denote *F*<sub>*n*</sub> the [cdf](wikipedia://en/5793) of *S*<sub>*n*</sub>, and Φ the cdf of the [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution). For the sake of convenience denote

$$\vec{\sigma}=(\sigma_1,\ldots,\sigma_n),\ \vec{\rho}=(\rho_1,\ldots,\rho_n).$$

In 1941, [Andrew C. Berry](wikipedia://en/59125571) proved that for all *n* there exists an absolute constant *C*<sub>1</sub> such that

$$\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}\left|F_n(x) - \Phi(x)\right| \le C_1\cdot\psi_1,\ \ \ \ (2)$$

where

$$\psi_1=\psi_1\big(\vec{\sigma},\vec{\rho}\big)=\Big({\textstyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^n\sigma_i^2}\Big)^{-1/2}\cdot\max_{1\le i\le n}\frac{\rho_i}{\sigma_i^2}.$$

Independently, in 1942, [Carl-Gustav Esseen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl-Gustav_Esseen) proved that for all *n* there exists an absolute constant *C*<sub>0</sub> such that

$$\sup_{x\in\mathbb R}\left|F_n(x) - \Phi(x)\right| \le C_0\cdot\psi_0, \ \ \ \ (3)$$

where

$$\psi_0=\psi_0\big(\vec{\sigma},\vec{\rho}\big)=\Big({\textstyle\sum\limits_{i=1}^n\sigma_i^2}\Big)^{-3/2}\cdot\sum\limits_{i=1}^n\rho_i.$$

It is easy to make sure that ψ<sub>0</sub>≤ψ<sub>1</sub>. Due to this circumstance inequality (3) is conventionally called the Berry–Esseen inequality, and the quantity ψ<sub>0</sub> is called the Lyapunov fraction of the third order. Moreover, in the case where the summands *X*<sub>1</sub>, ..., *X*<sub>*n*</sub> have identical distributions

$$\psi_0=\psi_1=\frac{\rho_1}{\sigma_1^3\sqrt{n}},$$

and thus the bounds stated by inequalities (1), (2) and (3) coincide apart from the constant.

Regarding *C*<sub>0</sub>, obviously, the lower bound established by (Esseen 1956) remains valid:

$$C_0\geq\frac{\sqrt{10}+3}{6\sqrt{2\pi}} = 0.4097\ldots.$$

The lower bound is exactly reached only for certain Bernoulli distributions (see (Esseen 1956) for their explicit expressions).

The upper bounds for *C*<sub>0</sub> were subsequently lowered from Esseen's original estimate 7.59 to 0.5600.[^o12]

### Sum of a random number of random variables {#Sum_of_a_random_number_of_random_variables}

Berry–Esseen theorems exist for the sum of a random number of random variables. The following is Theorem 1 from Korolev (1989), substituting in the constants from Remark 3.[^o14] It is only a portion of the results that they established:

Let $\{X_i\}$ be independent, identically distributed random variables with $E(X_i) = \mu$, $\operatorname{Var}(X_i) = \sigma^2$, $E|X_i - \mu|^3 = \kappa^3$. Let $N$ be a non-negative integer-valued random variable, independent from $\{X_i\}$. Let $S_N = X_1 + \cdots + X_N$, and define

$$\Delta = \sup_x \left| P\left( \frac{S_N - E(S_N)}{\sqrt{\operatorname{Var}(S_N)}} \leq z \right) - \Phi(z) \right|.$$

Then

$$\Delta \leq 3.8696\frac{\kappa^3}{\sqrt{E(N)}\sigma^3} + 1.0395\frac{E|N - E(N)|}{E(N)} + 0.2420\frac{\mu^2 \operatorname{Var}(N)}{\sigma^2 E(N)}.$$

### Multidimensional version {#Multidimensional_version}

As with the [multidimensional central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406#Multidimensional_CLT), there is a multidimensional version of the Berry–Esseen theorem.[^o16][^o17]

Let $X_1,\dots,X_n$ be independent $\mathbb R^d$-valued random vectors each having mean zero. Write $S_n = \sum_{i=1}^n X_i$ and assume $\Sigma_n = \operatorname{Cov}[S_n]$ is invertible. Let $Z_n\sim\operatorname{N}(0,{\Sigma_n})$ be a $d$-dimensional Gaussian with the same mean and [covariance matrix](wikipedia://en/191752) as $S_n$. Then for all convex sets $U\subseteq\mathbb R^d$,

$$\big|\Pr[S_n\in U]-\Pr[Z_n\in U]\,\big| \le C d^{1/4} \gamma_n,$$

where $C$ is a universal constant and $\gamma_n=\sum_{i=1}^n \operatorname{E}\big[\|\Sigma_n^{-1/2}X_i\|_2^3\big]$ (the third power of the [L<sup>2</sup> norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L2_norm)).

The dependency on $d^{1/4}$ is conjectured to be optimal, but might not be.[^o17]

:::

## Non-uniform bounds {#Non-uniform_bounds}

The bounds given above consider the maximal difference between the cdf's. They are 'uniform' in that they do not depend on $x$ and quantify the uniform convergence $F_n \to \Phi$. However, because $F_n(x) - \Phi(x)$ goes to zero for large $x$ by general properties of cdf's, these uniform bounds will be overestimating the difference for such arguments. This is despite the uniform bounds being sharp in general. It is therefore desirable to obtain upper bounds which depend on $x$ and in this way become smaller for large $x$.

One such result going back to Esseen that was since improved multiple times is the following.

As above, let *X*<sub>1</sub>, *X*<sub>2</sub>, ..., be independent random variables with [E](wikipedia://en/9653)(*X*<sub>*i*</sub>) = 0, E(*X*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>2</sup>) = *σ*<sub>*i*</sub><sup>2</sup> > 0, and E(|*X*<sub>*i*</sub>|<sup>3</sup>) = *ρ*<sub>*i*</sub> \< ∞. Also, let $\sigma^2 = \sum_{i=1}^{n} \sigma_i^2$ and

$$S_n = {X_1 + X_2 + \cdots + X_n \over \sigma}$$

be the normalized *n*-th partial sum. Denote *F*<sub>*n*</sub> the [cdf](wikipedia://en/5793) of *S*<sub>*n*</sub>, and Φ the cdf of the [standard normal distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal_distribution). Then

$$|F_n(x) - \Phi(x)| \leq \frac{C_3}{\sigma^{3} + |x|^3} \cdot \sum_{i = 1}^n \rho_i,$$

where $C_3$ is a universal constant.

The constant $C_3$ may be taken as 114.667.[^o19] Moreover, if the $X_i$ are identically distributed, it can be taken as $C + 8(1+\mathrm{e})$, where $C$ is the constant from the first theorem above, and hence 30.2211 works.[^o20]
