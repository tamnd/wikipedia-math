# Solomon Kullback

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Solomon Kullback"}

**Solomon Kullback** (April 3, 1907 – August 5, 1994) was an American [cryptanalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalyst) and [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902), who was one of the first three employees hired by [William F. Friedman](wikipedia://en/175115) at the [US Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Army)'s [Signal Intelligence Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_Intelligence_Service) (SIS) in the 1930s, along with [Frank Rowlett](wikipedia://en/477018) and [Abraham Sinkov](wikipedia://en/477032). He went on to a long and distinguished career at SIS and its eventual successor, the [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) (NSA). Kullback was the Chief Scientist at the NSA until his retirement in 1962, whereupon he took a position at the [George Washington University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_University).

:::

The [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) is named after Kullback and [Richard Leibler](wikipedia://en/4869853).

## Life and career {#Life_and_career}

Kullback was born to Jewish parents in [Brooklyn, New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brooklyn,_New_York). His father Nathan had been born in Vilna, Russian Empire, (now [Vilnius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vilnius), [Lithuania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lithuania)) and had immigrated[^o4] to the US as a young man circa 1905, and became a naturalized American in 1911.[^o5] Kullback attended [Boys High School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boys_High_School_%28Brooklyn%29) in Brooklyn. He then went to [City College of New York](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/City_College_of_New_York), graduating with a [BA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bachelor_of_Arts) in 1927 and an [MA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Master_of_Arts) in math in 1929.[^o10] He completed a [doctorate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doctor_of_Philosophy) in math from [George Washington University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_University) in 1934. His intention had been to teach, and he returned to Boy's High School to do so, but found it not to his taste; he discovered his real interest was using mathematics, not teaching it.

At the suggestion of [Abraham Sinkov](wikipedia://en/477032), who showed him a [Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_federal_civil_service) flyer for "junior mathematicians" at US\$2,000 per year, he took the examination. Both passed, and were assigned to [Washington, D.C.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Washington,_D.C.) as junior cryptanalysts.

Upon arrival in Washington, Kullback was assigned to [William F. Friedman](wikipedia://en/175115). Friedman had begun an intensive program of training in cryptology for his new civilian employees. For several summers running, the SIS cryptanalysts attended training camps at [Fort Meade](wikipedia://en/129313) until they received commissions as reserve officers in the Army. Kullback and Sinkov took Friedman's admonitions on education seriously and spent the next several years attending night classes; both received their doctorates in mathematics. Afterward, Kullback rediscovered a love of teaching; he began offering evening classes in mathematics at [George Washington University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Washington_University) from 1939.

Once they had completed the training, the three were put to the work for which they had actually been hired, compilations of [cipher](wikipedia://en/5244) or [code](wikipedia://en/838989) material for the U.S. Army. Another task was to test commercial cipher devices which vendors wished to sell to the U.S. government.

Kullback worked in partnership with [Frank Rowlett](wikipedia://en/477018) against [RED cipher machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RED_%28cipher_machine%29) messages. Almost overnight, they unravelled the keying system and then the machine pattern – with nothing but the intercepted messages in hand. Using the talents of linguist John Hurt to translate text, SIS started issuing current intelligence to military decision-makers.

In May 1942, five months after attack on [Pearl Harbor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pearl_Harbor), Kullback, by then a Major, was sent to Britain.[^o6] He learned at [Bletchley Park](wikipedia://en/4037) that the British were producing intelligence of high quality by exploiting the [Enigma machine](wikipedia://en/9256). He also cooperated with the British in the solution of more conventional German codebook-based systems. Shortly after his return to the States, Kullback moved into the Japanese section as its chief.

When the [National Security Agency](wikipedia://en/21939) (NSA) was formed in 1952, Rowlett became chief of cryptanalysis. The primary problem facing research and development in the post-war period was development of high-speed processing equipment. Kullback supervised a team of about 60 people, including such innovative thinkers in automated data processing development as [Leo Rosen](wikipedia://en/1409555) and Sam Snyder. His staff pioneered new forms of input and memory, such as [magnetic tape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnetic_tape) and [drum memory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drum_memory), and compilers to make machines truly "multi-purpose". Kullback gave priority to using computers to generate [communications security](wikipedia://en/40922) (COMSEC) materials.

Kullback's book *Information Theory and Statistics* was published by [John Wiley & Sons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wiley_&_Sons) in 1959. The book was republished, with additions and corrections, by [Dover Publications](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dover_Publications) in 1968.

## Retirement {#Retirement}

Solomon Kullback retired from NSA in 1962, and focused on his teaching at George Washington University and publishing new papers. In 1963 he was elected as a [Fellow of the American Statistical Association](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fellow_of_the_American_Statistical_Association).[^o8] He reached the rank of [colonel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colonel), and was inducted into the [Military Intelligence Hall of Fame](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Military_Intelligence_Hall_of_Fame).

Kullback is remembered by his colleagues at NSA as straightforward; one described him as "totally guileless, you always knew where you stood with him." One former NSA senior recalled him as a man of unlimited energy and enthusiasm and a man whose judgment was usually "sound and right."
