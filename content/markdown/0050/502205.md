# Diophantine geometry

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Diophantine geometry"}

branches In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Diophantine geometry** is the study of [Diophantine equations](wikipedia://en/9109) (the search for integer solutions of polynomial equations) by means of powerful methods in [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997). The extensive development of algebraic geometry in the 20<sup>th</sup> century produced powerful tools to study these equations.[^n1] Diophantine geometry is part of the broader field of [arithmetic geometry](wikipedia://en/1973177).

:::

Four [theorems](wikipedia://en/30977) of fundamental importance in Diophantine geometry are:[^n2]

- [Mordell–Weil theorem](wikipedia://en/23087727)
- [Roth's theorem](wikipedia://en/787853)
- [Siegel's theorem](wikipedia://en/3989092)
- [Faltings' theorem](wikipedia://en/172199)

Another major theorem is [Mazur's torsion theorem](wikipedia://en/35173610). More modern examples include the [André–Oort conjecture](wikipedia://en/32262980), the [Bogomolov conjecture](wikipedia://en/18992933) and also the [uniform Mordell conjecture](wikipedia://en/66851658).

## Background {#Background}

[Serge Lang](wikipedia://en/872991) published a book *Diophantine Geometry* in 1962,[^n3] which introduced the term "Diophantine geometry"[^n1] and led to disagreement over the most promising framework for the solution of Diophantine equations.

The traditional arrangement of material on Diophantine equations was by [degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) and number of [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109), as in [Mordell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Mordell)'s *Diophantine Equations* (1969). The [Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert)–[Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690) result from 1890 reducing the Diophantine geometry of curves of genus 0 to degrees 1 and 2 ([conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673)) occurs in Chapter 17, as does [Mordell's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell's_conjecture). [Siegel's theorem on integral points](wikipedia://en/3989092) occurs in Chapter 28. [Mordell's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mordell's_theorem) on the finite generation of the group of [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200) on an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225) is in Chapter 16, and integer points on the [Mordell curve](wikipedia://en/3132697) in Chapter 26.

In a negative review of Lang's book, Mordell wrote:

In recent times, powerful new geometric ideas and methods have been developed by means of which important new arithmetical theorems and related results have been found and proved and some of these are not easily proved otherwise. Further, there has been a tendency to clothe the old results, their extensions, and proofs in the new geometrical language. Sometimes, however, the full implications of results are best described in a geometrical setting. Lang has these aspects very much in mind in this book, and seems to miss no opportunity for geometric presentation. This accounts for his title "Diophantine Geometry."[^o7]

He notes that the content of the book is largely versions of the [Mordell–Weil theorem](wikipedia://en/23087727), [Thue–Siegel–Roth theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thue–Siegel–Roth_theorem), and Siegel's theorem, with a treatment of [Hilbert's irreducibility theorem](wikipedia://en/1951153) and applications in the style of Siegel. Leaving aside issues of generality, and a completely different style, the major mathematical difference between the two books is that Lang used [abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_varieties) and offered a proof of Siegel's theorem, while Mordell noted that the proof "is of a very advanced character" (p. 263).

Despite a bad press initially, Lang's conception has been widely accepted, and a 2006 tribute called the book "visionary".[^o8] A larger field sometimes called [arithmetic of abelian varieties](wikipedia://en/384577) now includes Diophantine geometry along with [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695), [complex multiplication](wikipedia://en/537183), [local zeta-functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local_zeta-function) and [L-functions](wikipedia://en/245283). [Paul Vojta](wikipedia://en/1802312) wrote:

While others at the time shared this viewpoint (e.g., [Weil](wikipedia://en/2019), [Tate](wikipedia://en/245933), [Serre](wikipedia://en/205483)), it is easy to forget that others did not, as Mordell's review of *Diophantine Geometry* attests.[^o10]

## Approaches {#Approaches}

A single equation defines a [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/525101), and [simultaneous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simultaneous_equations) Diophantine equations give rise to a general [algebraic variety](wikipedia://en/248808) *V* over *K*; the typical question is about the nature of the set *V*(*K*) of points on *V* with co-ordinates in *K*, and by means of [height functions](wikipedia://en/3141855), quantitative questions about the "size" of these solutions may be posed, as well as the qualitative issues of whether any points exist, and if so whether there are an infinite number. Given the geometric approach, the consideration of [homogeneous equations](wikipedia://en/1698977) and [homogeneous co-ordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_co-ordinates) is fundamental, for the same reasons that [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) is the dominant approach in algebraic geometry. Rational number solutions therefore are the primary consideration; but integral solutions (i.e., [integer lattice points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_lattice_point)) can be treated in the same way as an [affine variety](wikipedia://en/320468) may be considered inside a projective variety that has extra [points at infinity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Points_at_infinity).

The general approach of Diophantine geometry is illustrated by [Faltings' theorem](wikipedia://en/172199) (a conjecture of [L. J. Mordell](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L._J._Mordell)) stating that an [algebraic curve](wikipedia://en/253260) *C* of [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus_%28curve%29) *g* > 1 over the rational numbers has only finitely many [rational points](wikipedia://en/2960200). The first result of this kind may have been the theorem of Hilbert and Hurwitz dealing with the case *g* = 0. The theory consists both of theorems and many conjectures and open questions.
