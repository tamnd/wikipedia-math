## 1-polytopes {#1-polytopes}

::: {#o14 .table cue=table}

There is only one polytope of rank 1 (1-polytope), the closed [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) bounded by its two endpoints. Every realization of this 1-polytope is regular. It has the [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) { },[^n2][^n3] or a [Coxeter diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_diagram) with a single ringed node, . [Norman Johnson](wikipedia://en/715527) calls it a *dion*[^o17] and gives it the Schläfli symbol { }.

Although trivial as a polytope, it appears as the [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034) of polygons and other higher dimensional polytopes.[^n4] It is used in the definition of [uniform prisms](wikipedia://en/283007) like Schläfli symbol { }×{p}, or Coxeter diagram as a [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of a line segment and a regular polygon.[^n5]

## 2-polytopes (polygons) {#2-polytopes_(polygons)}

The polytopes of rank 2 (2-polytopes) are called [polygons](wikipedia://en/23621). Regular polygons are [equilateral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equilateral) and [cyclic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon). A $p$-gonal regular polygon is represented by [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) {p}.

Many sources only consider [convex polygons](wikipedia://en/660019), but [star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303), like the [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544), when considered, can also be regular. They use the same vertices as the convex forms, but connect in an alternate connectivity which passes around the circle more than once to be completed.

#### Spherical {#Spherical}

The regular [digon](wikipedia://en/5442728) {2} can be considered to be a [degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) regular polygon. It can be realized non-degenerately in some non-Euclidean spaces, such as on the surface of a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859) or [torus](wikipedia://en/74800). For example, digon can be realised non-degenerately as a [spherical lune](wikipedia://en/23024648). A [monogon](wikipedia://en/5483767) {1} could also be realised on the sphere as a single point with a great circle through it.[^o25] However, a monogon is not a valid [abstract polytope](wikipedia://en/1211056) because its single edge is incident to only one vertex rather than two.

### Stars {#Stars}

There exist infinitely many regular star polytopes in two dimensions, whose Schläfli symbols consist of rational numbers $\{n/m\}$. They are called [star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303) and share the same [vertex arrangements](wikipedia://en/14308644) of the convex regular polygons.

In general, for any natural number $n$, there are regular $n$-pointed stars with Schläfli symbols $\{n/m\}$ for all $m$ such that $m < n/2$ (strictly speaking $\{n/m\} = \{n/(n - m)\}$) and $m$ and $n$ are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime) (as such, all stellations of a polygon with a prime number of sides will be regular stars). Symbols where $m$ and $n$ are not coprime may be used to represent compound polygons.

### Skew polygons {#Skew_polygons}

In addition to the planar regular polygons there are infinitely many [regular skew polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon). Skew polygons can be created via the blending operation.

The blend of two polygons $P$ and $Q$, written $P\#Q$, can be constructed as follows:

1. take the cartesian product of their vertices $V_{P} \times V_{Q}$.
1. add edges $(p_{0} \times q_{0}, p_{1} \times q_{1})$ where $(p_{0}, p_{1})$ is an edge of $P$ and $(q_{0}, q_{1})$ is an edge of $Q$.
1. select an arbitrary connected component of the result.

Alternatively, the blend is the polygon $\langle \rho_{0}\sigma_{0}, \rho_{1}\sigma_{1}\rangle$ where $\rho$ and $\sigma$ are the generating mirrors of $P$ and $Q$ placed in orthogonal subspaces.[^n6] The blending operation is commutative, associative and idempotent.

Every regular skew polygon can be expressed as the blend of a unique set of planar polygons.[^n6] If $P$ and $Q$ share no factors then $\operatorname{Dim}(P\#Q) = \operatorname{Dim}(P) + \operatorname{Dim}(Q)$.

#### In 3 space {#In_3_space}

The regular finite polygons in 3 dimensions are exactly the blends of the planar polygons (dimension 2) with the digon (dimension 1). They have vertices corresponding to a prism ($\{n/m\}\#$ where $n$ is odd) or an antiprism ($\{n/m\}\#$ where $n$ is even). All polygons in 3 space have an even number of vertices and edges.

Several of these appear as the Petrie polygons of regular polyhedra.

#### In 4 space {#In_4_space}

The regular finite polygons in 4 dimensions are exactly the polygons formed as a blend of two distinct planar polygons. They have vertices lying on a [Clifford torus](wikipedia://en/7950934) and related by a [Clifford displacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clifford_displacement). Unlike 3-dimensional polygons, skew polygons on double rotations can include an odd-number of sides.

## 3-polytopes (polyhedra) {#3-polytopes_(polyhedra)}

Polytopes of dimension 3 are called [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra):

A regular polyhedron with [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) $\{p, q\}$, Coxeter diagrams , has a regular face type $\{p\}$, and regular [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) $\{q\}$.

A [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) (of a polyhedron) is a polygon, seen by connecting those vertices which are one edge away from a given vertex. For [regular polyhedra](wikipedia://en/333299), this vertex figure is always a regular (and planar) polygon.

Existence of a regular polyhedron $\{p, q\}$ is constrained by an inequality, related to the vertex figure's [angle defect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Defect_%28geometry%29):

$$\begin{aligned} & \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} > \frac{1}{2} : \text{Polyhedron (existing in Euclidean 3-space)} \\[6pt] & \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} = \frac{1}{2} : \text{Euclidean plane tiling} \\[6pt] & \frac{1}{p} + \frac{1}{q} < \frac{1}{2} : \text{Hyperbolic plane tiling} \end{aligned}$$

By enumerating the [permutations](wikipedia://en/44027), we find five convex forms, four star forms and three plane tilings, all with polygons $\{p\}$ and $\{q\}$ limited to: {3}, {4}, {5}, {5/2}, and {6}.

Beyond Euclidean space, there is an infinite set of regular hyperbolic tilings.

### Skew polyhedra {#Skew_polyhedra}

[Regular skew polyhedra](wikipedia://en/19193037) are generalizations to the set of [regular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/333299) which include the possibility of nonplanar [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719).

For 4-dimensional skew polyhedra, Coxeter offered a modified [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) {l,m|n} for these figures, with {l,m} implying the [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719), *m* l-gons around a vertex, and $n$-gonal holes. Their vertex figures are [skew polygons](wikipedia://en/6545461), zig-zagging between two planes.

The regular skew polyhedra, represented by {l,m|n}, follow this equation:

$$2 \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{l}\right) \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{m}\right) = \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)$$

## 4-polytopes (polychora) {#4-polytopes_(polychora)}

Regular [4-polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-polytopes) with [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) $\{p,q,r\}$ have cells of type $\{p,q\}$, faces of type $\{p\}$, edge figures $\{r\}$, and vertex figures $\{q,r\}$.

::: {#o59 .entry cue=list-entry name="Vertex figure"}

- A [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) (of a 4-polytope) is a polyhedron, seen by the arrangement of neighboring vertices around a given vertex. For regular 4-polytopes, this vertex figure is a regular polyhedron.

:::

::: {#o60 .entry cue=list-entry name="edge figure"}

- An [edge figure](wikipedia://en/1005719#Edge_figure) is a polygon, seen by the arrangement of faces around an edge. For regular 4-polytopes, this edge figure will always be a regular polygon.

:::

The existence of a regular 4-polytope $\{p,q,r\}$ is constrained by the existence of the regular polyhedra $\{p,q\}, \{q,r\}$. A suggested name for 4-polytopes is "polychoron".[^o61]

Each will exist in a space dependent upon this expression:

$$\sin \left ( \frac{\pi}{p} \right ) \sin \left(\frac{\pi}{r}\right) - \cos\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)$$

$> 0$ : Hyperspherical 3-space honeycomb or 4-polytope

$= 0$ : Euclidean 3-space honeycomb

$< 0$ : Hyperbolic 3-space honeycomb

These constraints allow for 21 forms: 6 are convex, 10 are nonconvex, *one* is a Euclidean 3-space honeycomb, and 4 are hyperbolic honeycombs.

### Skew 4-polytopes {#Skew_4-polytopes}

In addition to the 16 planar 4-polytopes above there are 18 finite skew polytopes.[^n1] One of these is obtained as the [Petrie dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrie_dual) of the tesseract, and the other 17 can be formed by applying the kappa operation to the planar polytopes and the Petrie dual of the tesseract.

## Dimension 5 and higher {#Dimension_5_and_higher}

5-polytopes can be given the symbol $\{p,q,r,s\}$ where $\{p,q,r\}$ is the 4-face type, $\{p,q\}$ is the cell type, $\{p\}$ is the face type, and $\{s\}$ is the face figure, $\{r,s\}$ is the edge figure, and $\{q,r,s\}$ is the vertex figure.

A [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) (of a 5-polytope) is a 4-polytope, seen by the arrangement of neighboring vertices to each vertex.

An [edge figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_figure) (of a 5-polytope) is a polyhedron, seen by the arrangement of faces around each edge.

A [face figure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_figure) (of a 5-polytope) is a polygon, seen by the arrangement of cells around each face.

A regular 5-polytope $\{p,q,r,s\}$ exists only if $\{p,q,r\}$ and $\{q,r,s\}$ are regular 4-polytopes.

The space it fits in is based on the expression:

$$\frac{\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi}{q}\right)}{\sin^2\left(\frac{\pi}{p}\right)} + \frac{\cos^2\left(\frac{\pi}{r}\right)}{\sin^2\left(\frac{\pi}{s}\right)}$$

$< 1$ : Spherical 4-space tessellation or 5-space polytope

$= 1$ : Euclidean 4-space tessellation

$> 1$ : hyperbolic 4-space tessellation

Enumeration of these constraints produce 3 convex polytopes, no star polytopes, 3 tessellations of Euclidean 4-space, and *5* tessellations of paracompact hyperbolic 4-space. The only non-convex regular polytopes for ranks 5 and higher are skews.

There are also improper cases where some numbers in the Schläfli symbol are 2. For example, {p,q,r,...2} is an improper regular spherical polytope whenever {p,q,r...} is a regular spherical polytope, and {2,...p,q,r} is an improper regular spherical polytope whenever {...p,q,r} is a regular spherical polytope. Such polytopes may also be used as facets, yielding forms such as {p,q,...2...y,z}.

### Star polytopes {#Star_polytopes}

There are no regular star polytopes of rank 5 or higher, with the exception of degenerate polytopes created by the star product of lower rank star polytopes, [e.g.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exempli_gratia) hosotopes and ditopes.

## Regular projective polytopes {#Regular_projective_polytopes}

A projective regular $(n+1)$-polytope exists when an original regular $n$-spherical tessellation, {p,q,...}, is [centrally symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centrally_symmetric). Such a polytope is named hemi-{p,q,...}, and contain half as many elements. Coxeter gives a symbol {p,q,...}/2, while McMullen writes {p,q,...}<sub>h/2</sub> with *h* as the [coxeter number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_number).[^n9]

Even-sided [regular polygons](wikipedia://en/333306) have hemi-*2n*-gon projective polygons, {2p}/2.

There are 4 regular [projective polyhedra](wikipedia://en/26952055) related to 4 of 5 [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905).

The hemi-cube and hemi-octahedron generalize as hemi-[$n$-cubes](wikipedia://en/39783) and hemi-$n$-[orthoplexes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthoplex) to any rank.

## Apeirotopes {#Apeirotopes}

An **[apeirotope](wikipedia://en/12768648)** or **infinite polytope** is a [polytope](wikipedia://en/23471) which has infinitely many [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647). An $n$-apeirotope is an infinite $n$-polytope: a 2-apeirotope or apeirogon is an infinite polygon, a 3-apeirotope or apeirohedron is an infinite polyhedron, etc.

There are two main geometric classes of apeirotope:[^o158]

::: {#o159 .entry cue=list-entry name="honeycombs"}

- Regular [honeycombs](wikipedia://en/6008654) in $n$ dimensions, which completely fill an $n$-dimensional space.

:::

::: {#o160 .entry cue=list-entry name="Skew apeirotope"}

- Regular [skew apeirotopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_apeirotope), comprising an $n$-dimensional manifold in a higher space.

:::

#### Hyperbolic tilings {#Hyperbolic_tilings}

Tessellations of [hyperbolic 2-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lobachevski_plane) are *[hyperbolic tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_tiling)*. There are infinitely many regular tilings in H<sup>2</sup>. As stated above, every positive integer pair {*p*,*q*} such that 1/*p* + 1/*q* \< 1/2 gives a hyperbolic tiling. In fact, for the general [Schwarz triangle](wikipedia://en/2069325) (*p*, *q*, *r*) the same holds true for 1/*p* + 1/*q* + 1/*r* \< 1.

There are a number of different ways to display the hyperbolic plane, including the [Poincaré disk model](wikipedia://en/30871836) which maps the plane into a circle, as shown below. It should be recognized that all of the polygon faces in the tilings below are equal-sized and only appear to get smaller near the edges due to the projection applied, very similar to the effect of a camera [fisheye lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fisheye_lens).

There are infinitely many flat regular 3-apeirotopes (apeirohedra) as regular tilings of the hyperbolic plane, of the form {p,q}, with p+q\<pq/2.

#### Hyperbolic star-tilings {#Hyperbolic_star-tilings}

There are 2 infinite forms of hyperbolic tilings whose [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) or [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719) are star polygons: {*m*/2, *m*} and their duals {*m*, *m*/2} with *m* = 7, 9, 11, ...[^n10] The {*m*/2, *m*} tilings are [stellations](wikipedia://en/180253) of the {*m*, 3} tilings while the {*m*, *m*/2} dual tilings are [facetings](wikipedia://en/8613537) of the {3, *m*} tilings and [greatenings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greatening_%28geometry%29) of the {*m*, 3} tilings.

The other two Kepler–Poinsot polyhedra (the [great stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029878) and [great icosahedron](wikipedia://en/3029898)) do not have regular hyperbolic tiling analogues. Noncompact solutions exist as [Lorentzian Coxeter groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorentzian_Coxeter_group), and can be visualized with open domains in hyperbolic space (the fundamental tetrahedron having ultra-ideal vertices). All honeycombs with hyperbolic cells or vertex figures and do not have 2 in their Schläfli symbol are noncompact.

There are no regular compact or paracompact hyperbolic star-honeycombs in H<sup>3</sup>: all forms with a regular star polyhedron as cell, vertex figure or both end up being spherical.

Ideal vertices now appear when the vertex figure is a Euclidean tiling, becoming inscribable in a horosphere rather than a sphere. They are dual to ideal cells (Euclidean tilings rather than finite polyhedra). As the last number in the Schläfli symbol rises further, the vertex figure becomes hyperbolic, and vertices become ultra-ideal (so the edges do not meet within hyperbolic space). In honeycombs {p, q, ∞} the edges intersect the Poincaré ball only in one ideal point; the rest of the edge has become ultra-ideal. Continuing further would lead to edges that are completely ultra-ideal, both for the honeycomb and for the fundamental simplex (though still infinitely many {p, q} would meet at such edges). In general, when the last number of the Schläfli symbol becomes ∞, faces of codimension two intersect the Poincaré hyperball only in one ideal point.[^o179]

## Abstract polytopes {#Abstract_polytopes}

The [abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056) arose out of an attempt to study polytopes apart from the geometrical space they are embedded in. They include the tessellations of spherical, Euclidean and hyperbolic space, and of other [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470). There are infinitely many of every rank greater than 1. See [this atlas](http://www.abstract-polytopes.com/atlas/) for a sample. Some notable examples of abstract regular polytopes that do not appear elsewhere in this list are the [11-cell](wikipedia://en/948617), {3,5,3}, and the [57-cell](wikipedia://en/948622), {5,3,5}, which have regular projective polyhedra as cells and vertex figures.

The elements of an abstract polyhedron are its body (the maximal element), its faces, edges, vertices and the *null polytope* or empty set. These abstract elements can be mapped into ordinary space or *realised* as geometrical figures. Some abstract polyhedra have well-formed or *faithful* realisations, others do not. A *flag* is a connected set of elements of each rank - for a polyhedron that is the body, a face, an edge of the face, a vertex of the edge, and the null polytope. An abstract polytope is said to be *regular* if its combinatorial symmetries are transitive on its flags - that is to say, that any flag can be mapped onto any other under a symmetry of the polyhedron. Abstract regular polytopes remain an active area of research.

Five such regular abstract polyhedra, which cannot be realised faithfully and symmetrically, were identified by [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) in his book *[Regular Polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_Polytopes)* (1977) and again by [J. M. Wills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._Wills) in his paper "The combinatorially regular polyhedra of index 2" (1987).[^o279] They are all topologically equivalent to [toroids](wikipedia://en/14973218). Their construction, by arranging *n* faces around each vertex, can be repeated indefinitely as tilings of the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291#Models_of_the_hyperbolic_plane).
