# Fractional part

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fractional part"}

*Image: Parte\_fraccionaria.png.* Graph of the fractional part of real numbers

The **fractional part** or **decimal part**[^o2] of a non‐negative [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x$ is the excess beyond that number's [integer part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_part). The latter is defined as the largest integer not greater than $x$, called *[floor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function)* of $x$ or $\lfloor x\rfloor$. Then, the fractional part can be formulated as a [difference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_%28mathematics%29):

$$\operatorname{frac} (x)=x - \lfloor x \rfloor,\; x > 0.$$

:::

The fractional part of [logarithms](wikipedia://en/17860),[^o3] specifically, is also known as the [**mantissa**](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mantissa_%28logarithm%29); by contrast with the mantissa, the integral part of a logarithm is called its *characteristic*.[^o4][^o5] The word *mantissa* was introduced by [Henry Briggs](wikipedia://en/210399).[^o6]

For a [positive number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) written in a conventional [positional numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positional_numeral_system) (such as [binary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_numeral_system) or [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214)), its fractional part hence corresponds to the digits appearing after the [radix point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radix_point), such as the [decimal point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_point) in English. The result is a real number in the half-open [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) \[0, 1).

## For negative numbers {#For_negative_numbers}

However, in case of negative numbers, there are various conflicting ways to extend the fractional part function to them: It is either defined in the same way as for positive numbers, i.e., by $\operatorname{frac} (x)=x-\lfloor x \rfloor$ (Graham Knuth Patashnik 1992),[^o9] or as the part of the number to the right of the radix point $\operatorname{frac} (x)=|x|-\lfloor |x| \rfloor$ (Daintith 2004),[^o11] or by the [odd function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_function):[^o12]

$$\operatorname{frac} (x)=\begin{cases} x - \lfloor x \rfloor & x \ge 0 \\ x - \lceil x \rceil & x < 0 \end{cases}$$

with $\lceil x \rceil$ as the smallest integer not less than $x$, also called the [ceiling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ceiling_function) of $x$. By consequence, we may get, for example, three different values for the fractional part of just one $x$: let it be −1.3, its fractional part will be 0.7 according to the first definition, 0.3 according to the second definition, and −0.3 according to the third definition, whose result can also be obtained in a straightforward way by

$$\operatorname{frac} (x)= x - \lfloor |x| \rfloor \cdot \operatorname{sgn}(x).$$

The $x - \lfloor x \rfloor$ and the "odd function" definitions permit for unique decomposition of any real number $x$ to the [sum](wikipedia://en/61338) of its integer and fractional parts, where "integer part" refers to $\lfloor x \rfloor$ or $\lfloor |x| \rfloor \cdot \operatorname{sgn}(x)$ respectively. These two definitions of fractional-part function also provide [idempotence](wikipedia://en/14972).

The fractional part defined via difference from [⌊ ⌋](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function) is usually denoted by [curly braces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curly_brace):

$$\{ x \} := x-\lfloor x \rfloor.$$

## Relation to continued fractions {#Relation_to_continued_fractions}

Every real number can be essentially uniquely represented as a [simple continued fraction](wikipedia://en/46802), namely as the sum of its integer part and the [reciprocal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reciprocal_%28mathematics%29) of its fractional part which is written as the sum of *its* integer part and the reciprocal of *its* fractional part, and so on.
