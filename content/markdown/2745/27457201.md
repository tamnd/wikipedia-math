# Geodetic coordinates

Geographic coordinate system

*Image: Geodetic coordinates.svg.* Geodetic coordinates $P(\text{ɸ},\lambda,h)$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Geodetic coordinates"}

**Geodetic coordinates** are a type of [curvilinear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvilinear_coordinate_system) [orthogonal coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_coordinate_system) used in [geodesy](wikipedia://en/12608) based on a *[reference ellipsoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reference_ellipsoid)*. They include **geodetic latitude** (north/south) $\phi$, *[longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude)* (east/west) $\lambda$, and **ellipsoidal height** $h$ (also known as **geodetic height**[^o2]). The triad is also known as **Earth ellipsoidal coordinates**[^o3] (not to be confused with *[ellipsoidal-harmonic coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipsoidal-harmonic_coordinates)*).

:::

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

Longitude measures the rotational [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between the zero meridian and the measured point. By convention for the Earth, Moon and Sun, it is expressed in degrees ranging from −180° to +180°. For other bodies a range of 0° to 360° is used. For this purpose, it is necessary to identify a *zero [meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_%28geography%29)*, which for Earth is usually the [Prime Meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_Meridian). For other bodies a fixed surface feature is usually referenced, which for Mars is the meridian passing through the crater [Airy-0](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Airy-0). It is possible for many different coordinate systems to be defined upon the same reference ellipsoid.

Geodetic latitude measures how close to the poles or equator a point is along a meridian, and is represented as an angle from −90° to +90°, where 0° is the equator. The *geodetic latitude* is the angle between the equatorial plane and a line that is [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_normal) to the reference ellipsoid. Depending on the flattening, it may be slightly different from the *[geocentric latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_latitude)*, which is the angle between the equatorial plane and a line from the center of the ellipsoid. For non-Earth bodies the terms *[planetographic latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetographic_latitude)* and *[planetocentric latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetocentric_latitude)* are used instead.

Ellipsoidal height (or ellipsoidal [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude)), also known as geodetic height (or geodetic altitude), is the distance between the point of interest and the ellipsoid surface, evaluated along the [ellipsoidal normal vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ellipsoidal_normal_vector); it is defined as a [signed distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signed_distance) such that points inside the ellipsoid have negative height.

:::

## Geodetic vs. geocentric coordinates {#Geodetic_vs._geocentric_coordinates}

*Image: Geocentric coords 03.svg.* The definition of geodetic latitude ($\phi$) and geocentric latitude ($\theta$)

Geodetic latitude and *[geocentric latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_latitude)* have different definitions. Geodetic latitude is defined as the angle between the [equatorial](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equator) plane and the [surface normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_normal) at a point on the ellipsoid, whereas geocentric latitude is defined as the angle between the equatorial plane and a radial line connecting the centre of the ellipsoid to a point on the surface (see figure). When used without qualification, the term latitude refers to geodetic latitude. For example, the latitude used in [geographic coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate_system) is geodetic latitude. The standard notation for geodetic latitude is $\varphi$. There is no standard notation for geocentric latitude; examples include $\theta$, $\psi$, $\varphi'$.

Similarly, geodetic altitude is defined as the height above the ellipsoid surface, normal to the ellipsoid; whereas *[geocentric altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_altitude)* is defined as the distance to the reference ellipsoid along a radial line to the geocenter. When used without qualification, as in aviation, the term [altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Altitude) refers to geodetic altitude (possibly with further refinements, such as in [orthometric heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthometric_height)). Geocentric altitude is typically used in [orbital mechanics](wikipedia://en/182146) (see [orbital altitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbital_altitude)).

If the impact of Earth's [equatorial bulge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_bulge) is not significant for a given application (e.g., [interplanetary spaceflight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interplanetary_spaceflight)), the [Earth ellipsoid](wikipedia://en/16112368) may be simplified as a [spherical Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spherical_Earth), in which case the geocentric and geodetic latitudes are equal and the latitude-dependent geocentric radius simplifies to a global mean [Earth's radius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth's_radius) (see also: [spherical coordinate system](wikipedia://en/29181)).

## Conversion {#Conversion}

Given geodetic coordinates, one can compute the *[geocentric Cartesian coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_Cartesian_coordinates)* of the point as follows:[^o8]

$$\begin{aligned} X &= \big( N + h\big)\cos{\phi}\cos{\lambda} \\ Y &= \big( N + h\big)\cos{\phi}\sin{\lambda} \\ Z &= \left( \frac{b^2}{a^2} N + h\right)\sin{\phi} \end{aligned}$$

where $a$ and $b$ are the equatorial radius ([semi-major axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-major_axis)) and the polar radius ([semi-minor axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semi-minor_axis)), respectively. $N$ is the *[prime vertical radius of curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_vertical_radius_of_curvature)*, function of latitude $\phi$:

$$N = \frac{a^2}{\sqrt{a^2\cos^2\phi + b^2\sin^2 \phi }},$$

In contrast, extracting $\phi$, $\lambda$ and $h$ from the rectangular coordinates usually requires [iteration](wikipedia://en/15237) as $\phi$ and $h$ are mutually involved through $N$:[^o9][^o10]

$$\lambda = \operatorname{atan2}(Y,X).$$

$$h=\frac{p}{\cos\phi} - N,$$

$$\phi = \arctan\left( (Z / p)/(1 - e^2 N / (N + h)) \right).$$

where $p = \sqrt{X^2 + Y^2}$. More sophisticated methods are [available](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geographic_coordinate_conversion#From_ECEF_to_geodetic_coordinates).
