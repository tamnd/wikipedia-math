# Fold-and-cut theorem

*Image: FoldedKoch.gif.* Creating a [Koch snowflake](wikipedia://en/46959) curve by the fold-and-cut method

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fold-and-cut theorem"}

The **fold-and-cut theorem** states that any shape with straight sides can be cut from a single (idealized) sheet of paper by folding it flat and making a single straight complete cut.[^o2] Such shapes include polygons, which may be concave, shapes with holes, and collections of such shapes (i.e. the regions need not be [connected](wikipedia://en/6233)).

:::

The corresponding problem that the theorem solves is known as the **fold-and-cut problem**, which asks what shapes can be obtained by the so-called fold-and-cut method. A particular instance of the problem, which asks how a particular shape can be obtained by the fold-and-cut method, is known as *a* fold-and-cut problem.

## History {#History}

The earliest known description of a fold-and-cut problem appears in *Wakoku Chiyekurabe* (Mathematical Contests), a book that was published in 1721 by Kan Chu Sen in Japan.[^o4]

*Image: origamiheaven\_singlecutpentagram.jpg.* A method to fold a sheet of paper to make a regular pentagram with a single cut

An 1873 article in *Harper's New Monthly Magazine* describes how [Betsy Ross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Betsy_Ross) may have proposed that stars on the American flag have five points, because such a shape can easily be obtained by the fold-and-cut method.[^o5]

In the 20th century, several magicians published books containing examples of fold-and-cut problems, including Will Blyth,[^o6] [Harry Houdini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harry_Houdini),[^o7] and Gerald Loe (1955).[^o8]

Inspired by Loe, [Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) wrote about the fold-and-cut problems in *[Scientific American](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_American)* in 1960. Examples mentioned by Gardner include separating the red squares from the black squares of a [checkerboard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Checkerboard) with one cut, and "an old paper-cutting stunt, of unknown origin" in which one cut splits a piece of paper into both a [Latin cross](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latin_cross) and a set of smaller pieces that can be rearranged to spell the word "hell". Foreshadowing work on the general fold-and-cut theorem, he writes that "more complicated designs present formidable problems".[^o9]

The first proof of the fold-and-cut theorem, solving the problem, was published in 1999 by [Erik Demaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erik_Demaine), [Martin Demaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Demaine), and [Anna Lubiw](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anna_Lubiw) and was solved using straight skeleton method.[^o10][^o11]

*Image: FoldedAntiKoch.gif.* Creating an anti-Koch snowflake curve by the fold-and-cut method

## Solutions {#Solutions}

There are two general methods known for solving instances of the fold-and-cut problem, based on [straight skeletons](wikipedia://en/6757195) and on [circle packing](wikipedia://en/16879665) respectively.
