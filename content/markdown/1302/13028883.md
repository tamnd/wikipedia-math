# Micromort

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Micromort"}

A **micromort** (from [micro-](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Micro-) and mortality) is a unit of [risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk) defined as a one-in-a-million chance of [death](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Death). Micromorts are usually assigned per event, or per unit of time a person is exposed to something.[^o2][^o3] Micromorts can be used to measure the riskiness of various day-to-day activities. A **microprobability** is a one-in-a million chance of some event; thus, a micromort is the microprobability of mortality from an event, or the health impacts of an event. The micromort concept was introduced by [Ronald A. Howard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ronald_A._Howard) who pioneered the modern practice of [decision analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decision_analysis).[^o4]

:::

Micromorts for future activities can only be rough assessments, as specific circumstances will always have an impact. However, past historical rates of events can be used to provide a rough estimate.

A related and somewhat opposing unit is the [microlife](wikipedia://en/37987755), which measures statistical improvements to life expectancy from an activity—one example being regular [aerobic exercise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerobic_exercise).

## Value of a micromort {#Value_of_a_micromort}

### Willingness to pay {#Willingness_to_pay}

An application of micromorts is measuring the value that humans place on risk. For example, a person can consider the amount of money they would be [willing to pay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willingness_to_pay) to avoid a one-in-a-million chance of death (or conversely, the amount of money [they would receive to accept](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Willingness_to_accept) a one-in-a-million chance of death). When offered this situation, people claim a high number. However, when looking at their day-to-day actions (e.g., how much they are willing to pay for safety features on cars), a typical value for a micromort is around \$50 (in 2009).[^o49][^o50] This is not to say the \$50 valuation should be taken to mean that a human life (1 million micromorts) is valued at \$50,000,000. Rather, people are [less inclined to spend money after a certain point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marginal_utility) to increase their safety. This means that analyzing risk using the micromort is more useful when using small risks, not necessarily large ones.[^o50]

### Value of a statistical life {#Value_of_a_statistical_life}

Government agencies use a nominal [Value of a Statistical Life](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_of_a_Statistical_Life) (VSL) – or [Value for Preventing a Fatality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Value_for_Preventing_a_Fatality) (VPF) – to evaluate the cost-effectiveness of expenditure on safeguards. For example, in the UK, the VSL is £1 million [GBP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GBP) in 1997 value (equivalent to £GBP million in UKUK).[^o52] Since road improvements have the effect of lowering the risk of large numbers of people by a small amount, the [UK Department for Transport](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UK_Department_for_Transport) essentially prices a reduction of 1 micromort at £1.60. The [US Department of Transportation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States_Department_of_Transportation) uses a VSL of US\$6.2 million, pricing a micromort at US\$6.20.[^o53]

## Chronic risks {#Chronic_risks}

Micromorts are best used to measure the size of *acute* risks, i.e. immediate deaths. Risks from lifestyle, exposure to [air pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_pollution), and so on are *chronic* risks, in that they do not kill straight away, but reduce life expectancy. Ron Howard included such risks in his original 1979 work,[^o55] for example, an additional one micromort from:

- Drinking 0.5 liter of [wine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wine) (cirrhosis of the liver)[^o55]
- [Smoking](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smoking) 1.4 cigarettes (cancer, heart disease)[^o55]
- Spending 1 hour in a coal mine (black lung disease)[^o55]
- Spending 3 hours in a coal mine (accident)[^o55]
- Living 2 days in New York or Boston in 1979 (air pollution)[^o55]
- Living 2 months with a smoker (cancer, heart disease)[^o55]
- Drinking Miami water for 1 year (cancer from chloroform)[^o55]
- Eating 100 charcoal-broiled steaks (cancer from benzopyrene)[^o55]
- Traveling 6000 miles (10,000 km) by jet (cancer due to increased background radiation)[^o56]

Chronic risks are typically measured in terms of individual life expectancy, rather than death rates. Therefore, chronic risks are better measured with a related unit, the [microlife](wikipedia://en/37987755).
