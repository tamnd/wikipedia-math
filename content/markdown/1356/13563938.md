# Universal parabolic constant

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Universal parabolic constant"}

*Image: Parabolic constant illustration v4.svg.* The universal parabolic constant is the red length divided by the green length.

The **universal parabolic constant** is a [mathematical constant](wikipedia://en/48779551).

:::

It is defined as the ratio, for any [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231), of the [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401) of the parabolic segment formed by the [latus rectum](wikipedia://en/23231#Latus_rectum,_semilatus_rectum,_and_polar_coordinates) to the focal parameter. The focal parameter is twice the [focal length](wikipedia://en/23231#Focal_length). The ratio is denoted *P*.[^o2][^o3][^o4] In the diagram, the latus rectum is pictured in blue, the parabolic segment that it forms in red and the focal parameter in green. (The [focus](wikipedia://en/1032607) of the parabola is the point *F* and the [directrix](wikipedia://en/19008673#Eccentricity.2C_focus_and_directrix) is the line *L*.)

The value of *P* is[^o5]

$$P = \ln(1 + \sqrt2) + \sqrt2 = 2.29558714939\dots$$

. The [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) and parabola are unique among conic sections in that they have a universal constant. The analogous ratios for [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277) and [hyperbolas](wikipedia://en/14052) depend on their [eccentricities](wikipedia://en/1239472). This means that all circles are [similar](wikipedia://en/39377) and all parabolas are similar, whereas ellipses and hyperbolas are not.

## Derivation {#Derivation}

Take $y = \frac{x^2}{4f}$ as the equation of the parabola. The focal parameter is $p=2f$ and the [semilatus rectum](wikipedia://en/23231#Latus_rectum,_semilatus_rectum,_and_polar_coordinates) is $\ell=2f$.

$$\begin{aligned} P & := \frac{1}{p}\int_{-\ell}^\ell \sqrt{1+\left(y'(x)\right)^2}\, dx \\ & = \frac{1}{2f}\int_{-2f}^{2f}\sqrt{1+\frac{x^2}{4f^2}}\, dx \\ & = \int_{-1}^{1}\sqrt{1+t^2}\, dt & (x = 2 f t) \\ & = \operatorname{arsinh}(1) + \sqrt{2}\\ & = \ln(1+\sqrt{2}) + \sqrt{2}. \end{aligned}$$

## Properties {#Properties}

*P* is a [transcendental number](wikipedia://en/30325).

**Proof**. Suppose that *P* is [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158). Then $\!\ P - \sqrt2 = \ln(1 + \sqrt2)$ must also be algebraic. However, by the [Lindemann–Weierstrass theorem](wikipedia://en/348976), $\!\ e^{\ln(1+ \sqrt2)} = 1 + \sqrt2$ would be transcendental, which is not the case. Hence *P* is transcendental.

Since *P* is transcendental, it is also [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689).

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

The average distance from a point randomly selected in the [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301) to its center is[^o11]

$$d_\text{avg} = {P \over 6}.$$

**Proof**.

$$\begin{aligned} d_\text{avg} & := 8\int_{0}^{1 \over 2}\int_{0}^{x}\sqrt{x^2+y^2}\, dy\, dx \\ & = 8\int_{0}^{1 \over 2}{1 \over 2}x^2(\ln(1 + \sqrt2) + \sqrt2)\, dx \\ & = 4P\int_{0}^{1 \over 2}x^2\, dx \\ & = {P \over 6} \end{aligned}$$

There is also an interesting geometrical reason why this constant appears in unit squares. The average distance between a center of a unit square and a point on the square's boundary is ${P \over 4}$. If we uniformly sample every point on the perimeter of the square, take line segments (drawn from the center) corresponding to each point, add them together by joining each [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) next to the other, scaling them down, the curve obtained is a parabola.[^o12]
