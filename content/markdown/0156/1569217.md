# Veronese surface

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Veronese surface"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Veronese surface** is an [algebraic surface](wikipedia://en/648311) in five-dimensional [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135), and is realized by the **Veronese embedding**, the embedding of the [projective plane](wikipedia://en/24350) given by the complete [linear system of conics](wikipedia://en/16154807). It is named after [Giuseppe Veronese](wikipedia://en/1617753) (1854–1917). Its generalization to higher dimension is known as the **Veronese variety**.

:::

The surface admits an embedding in the four-dimensional projective space defined by the projection from a general point in the five-dimensional space. Its general projection to three-dimensional projective space is called a [Steiner surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steiner_surface).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The Veronese surface is the image of the mapping

$$\nu:\mathbb{P}^2\to \mathbb{P}^5$$

given by

$$\nu: [x:y:z] \mapsto [x^2:y^2:z^2:yz:xz:xy]$$

where $[x:\cdots]$ denotes [homogeneous coordinates](wikipedia://en/243316). The map $\nu$ is known as the **Veronese embedding.**

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

The Veronese surface arises naturally in the study of [conics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conic). A conic is a degree 2 plane curve, thus defined by an equation:

$$Ax^2 + Bxy + Cy^2 +Dxz + Eyz + Fz^2 = 0.$$

The pairing between coefficients $(A, B, C, D, E, F)$ and variables $(x,y,z)$ is linear in coefficients and quadratic in the variables; the Veronese map makes it linear in the coefficients and linear in the monomials. Thus for a fixed point $[x:y:z],$ the condition that a conic contains the point is a [linear equation](wikipedia://en/17570) in the coefficients, which formalizes the statement that "passing through a point imposes a linear condition on conics".

## Veronese map {#Veronese_map}

The Veronese map or Veronese variety generalizes this idea to mappings of general degree *d* in *n*+1 variables. That is, the Veronese map of degree *d* is the map

$$\nu_d\colon \mathbb{P}^n \to \mathbb{P}^m$$

with *m* given by the [multiset coefficient](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiset_coefficient), or more familiarly the [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668), as:

$$m= \left(\!\!{n + 1 \choose d}\!\!\right) - 1 = {n+d \choose d} - 1.$$

The map sends $[x_0:\ldots:x_n]$ to all possible [monomials](wikipedia://en/357416) of [total degree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Total_degree) *d* (of which there are $m+1$); we have $n+1$ since there are $n+1$ variables $x_0, \ldots, x_n$ to choose from; and we subtract $1$ since the projective space $\mathbb{P}^m$ has $m+1$ coordinates. The second equality shows that for fixed source dimension *n,* the target dimension is a polynomial in *d* of degree *n* and leading coefficient $1/n!.$

For low degree, $d=0$ is the trivial constant map to $\mathbf{P}^0,$ and $d=1$ is the identity map on $\mathbf{P}^n,$ so *d* is generally taken to be 2 or more.

One may define the Veronese map in a coordinate-free way, as

$$\nu_d: \mathbb{P}(V) \ni [v] \mapsto [v^d] \in \mathbb{P}(\operatorname{Sym}^d V)$$

where *V* is any [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) of finite dimension, and $\operatorname{Sym}^d V$ are its [symmetric powers](wikipedia://en/57047243) of degree *d*. This is homogeneous of degree *d* under scalar multiplication on *V*, and therefore passes to a mapping on the underlying [projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135).

If the vector space *V* is defined over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *K* which does not have [characteristic zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_zero), then the definition must be altered to be understood as a mapping to the dual space of polynomials on *V*. This is because for fields with finite characteristic *p*, the *p*th powers of elements of *V* are not [rational normal curves](wikipedia://en/2380117), but are of course a line. (See, for example, [additive polynomial](wikipedia://en/1374699) for a treatment of polynomials over a field of finite characteristic.)

### Rational normal curve {#Rational_normal_curve}

For $n=1,$ the Veronese variety is known as the [rational normal curve](wikipedia://en/2380117), of which the lower-degree examples are familiar.

- For $n=1, d=1$ the Veronese map is simply the identity map on the projective line.
- For $n=1, d=2,$ the Veronese variety is the standard [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231) $[x^2:xy:y^2],$ in affine coordinates $(x,x^2).$
- For $n=1, d=3,$ the Veronese variety is the [twisted cubic](wikipedia://en/1547121), $[x^3:x^2y:xy^2:y^3],$ in affine coordinates $(x,x^2,x^3).$

## Biregular {#Biregular}

The image of a variety under the Veronese map is again a variety, rather than simply a [constructible set](wikipedia://en/23234121); furthermore, these are isomorphic in the sense that the inverse map exists and is [regular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_function) – the Veronese map is [biregular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biregular). More precisely, the images of [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744) are again open.
