# James Cooley

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="James Cooley"}

**James William Cooley** (September 18, 1926[^o3] – June 29, 2016[^o4]) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902). Cooley received a B.A. degree in 1949 from [Manhattan College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Manhattan_College), Bronx, New York, an M.A. degree in 1951 from [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University), New York City, and a Ph.D. degree in 1961 in [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969) from [Columbia University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Columbia_University). He was a [programmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programmer) on [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942)'s computer at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179), Princeton, New Jersey, from 1953 to 1956, where he notably programmed the [Blackman–Tukey transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackman–Tukey_transformation).[^o5]

:::

He worked on [quantum mechanical computations](wikipedia://en/25220) at the [Courant Institute](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Courant_Institute), New York University, from 1956 to 1962, when he joined the Research Staff at the [IBM Watson Research Center](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_Watson_Research_Center), Yorktown Heights, New York. Upon retirement from IBM in 1991, he joined the Department of [Electrical Engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electrical_Engineering) at the [University of Rhode Island](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Rhode_Island), Kingston, where he served on the faculty of the [computer engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_engineering) program.

His most significant contribution to the world of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing) is re-discovering the [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512),[^o5] which he co-developed with [John Tukey](wikipedia://en/415974) (see [Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm](wikipedia://en/352702)) while working for the research division of [IBM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM) in 1965.

The motivation for it was provided by Dr. [Richard L. Garwin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_L._Garwin) at IBM Watson Research who was concerned about verifying a [nuclear arms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_weapon) treaty with the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) for the [SALT talks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_Arms_Limitation_Talks). Garwin thought that if he had a very much faster [Fourier Transform](wikipedia://en/52247) he could plant sensors in the ground in [countries surrounding the Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Containment). He suggested to both Cooley and [Tukey](wikipedia://en/415974) how Fourier transforms could be programmed to be much faster. They did the work, the sensors were planted, and he was able to locate [nuclear explosions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nuclear_explosion) to within 15 kilometers of where they were occurring.

J. W. Cooley was a member of the *Digital Signal Processing Committee* of the [IEEE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Institute_of_Electrical_and_Electronics_Engineers), was elected a Fellow of IEEE for his work on the FFT, and received the [IEEE Centennial Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Centennial_Medal).[^o6] In 2002 he received the [IEEE Jack S. Kilby Signal Processing Medal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IEEE_Jack_S._Kilby_Signal_Processing_Medal).[^o7] He considerably contributed to the establishing of terminology in [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing).

Comput. **15**, 363–374. doi:10.1090/S0025-5718-1961-0129566-X This describes the so-called Numerov-Cooley method for numerically solving one-dimensional [Schrödinger equations](wikipedia://en/59874).
- James W. Cooley & John W. Tukey (1965): "[An algorithm for the machine calculation of complex Fourier series](https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/An-algorithm-for-the-machine-calculation-of-complex-Cooley-Tukey/0e6beb95b5150ce99b108acdefabf70ccd3fee30)", Math. Comput. **19**, 297–301.
