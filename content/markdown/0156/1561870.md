# Dallas Willard

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dallas Willard"}

**Dallas Albert Willard** (September 4, 1935 – May 8, 2013) was an American philosopher also known for his writings on [Christian spiritual formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_formation). Much of his work in philosophy was related to [phenomenology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenology_%28philosophy%29), particularly the work of [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl), many of whose writings he translated into English for the first time.[^o3]

:::

He was longtime Professor of Philosophy at [The University of Southern California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_University_of_Southern_California) in Los Angeles, teaching at the school from 1965 until his death in 2013 and serving as the department chair from 1982 to 1985.[^o4]

## Education {#Education}

Willard attended [William Jewell College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Jewell_College), and later graduated from [Tennessee Temple College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tennessee_Temple_University) in 1956 with a B.A. in psychology, and from [Baylor University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baylor_University) in 1957 with a B.A. in Philosophy and Religion. He went to graduate school at Baylor University and the [University of Wisconsin&ndash;Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin&ndash;Madison), earning a Ph.D. in philosophy from the University of Wisconsin with a minor in the History of Science, with the dissertation "*Meaning and Universals In Husserl's 'Logische Untersuchungen*'" in 1964.[^o6]

## Academic career {#Academic_career}

Willard spent five years teaching at the [University of Wisconsin&ndash;Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin&ndash;Madison), first as a research and teaching assistant (1960–63), then as an Advanced Knapp Fellow (63–64), and finally as an instructor in philosophy (64–65). He then moved to the [University of Southern California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Southern_California) (USC) in Los Angeles, where he taught as an assistant professor (1965–69), as an associate professor (69–84), and finally as a full professor (1984–2013). He spent a total of 48 years at USC.

He served as director of the School of Philosophy at USC from 1982 to 1985, as well as visiting appointments at [UCLA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/UCLA) (1969) and the University of Colorado (1984).[^o6]

His publications in philosophy are concerned primarily with [epistemology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epistemology), the philosophy of mind and of logic, and with the philosophy of [Edmund Husserl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edmund_Husserl), the founder of phenomenology. He translated many of Husserl's early writings from German into English, and is widely regarded as an international authority on the philosopher's works, which span from the topics of time-consciousness to [intentionality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intentionality) and intuition in [Cartesian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesianism) thought.

## Other work {#Other_work}

In addition to teaching and writing about philosophy, Willard gave lectures and wrote books about Christianity and Christian living. His book *The Divine Conspiracy* was *[Christianity Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_Today)*’s Book of the Year for 1999.[^o11] Another of his books, *[Renovation of the Heart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renovation_of_the_Heart)*, won *[Christianity Today](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christianity_Today)*’s 2003 Book Award for books on Spirituality and The Association of Logos Bookstores' 2003 Book Award for books on Christian Living.[^o12]

Willard believed passivity to be a widespread problem in the Church (loosely summed up in his phrase "Grace is not opposed to effort {which is action}, but to earning {which is attitude}").[^o13][^o14] He emphasized the importance of deliberately choosing to be a disciple of Jesus Christ (someone being with Jesus, learning to be like him).[^o13][^o14][^o15] An important outgrowth of the choice to be identified as a disciple of Jesus is the desire to learn about activities that aid spiritual transformation into the likeness of Christ.[^o16]

In this regard, being an apprentice of Jesus (someone being with Jesus, learning to be like him), involves learning about activities that might help one grow in the fruit of the spirit, namely love, joy, peace, patience, kindness, goodness, faithfulness, gentleness, and self-control (Galatians 5:22-23).[^o13][^o16][^o17] Such activities might include spiritual exercises practiced throughout the ages such as prayer, fellowship, service, study, simplicity, chastity, solitude, and fasting.[^o16][^o18] Willard explains the crucial role of engaging in spiritual exercises in his book *The Spirit of the Disciplines: Understanding How God Changes Lives*—a book that was written after *In Search of Guidance: Developing a Conversational Relationship with God*.

Willard has a recommended reading page on his website listing specific titles by [Thomas a Kempis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_a_Kempis), [William Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Law), [Frank Laubach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Laubach), [William Wilberforce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Wilberforce), [Richard Baxter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Baxter), [Charles Finney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Finney), [Jan Johnson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Johnson), [Dietrich Bonhoeffer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dietrich_Bonhoeffer), [Jeremy Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Taylor), [Richard Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Foster_%28theologian%29), [E. Stanley Jones](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._Stanley_Jones), [William Penn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Penn), [Brother Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brother_Lawrence), [Francis de Sales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_de_Sales), [James Gilchrist Lawson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Gilchrist_Lawson), and others.[^o19]

He was influenced by many, including [Jacques Maritain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacques_Maritain), [Aquinas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aquinas), [Augustine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Augustine_of_Hippo), [P.T. Forsyth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Taylor_Forsyth), [John Calvin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Calvin), [John Wesley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Wesley), [William Law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Law), [Andrew Murray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Murray_%28minister%29), [Richard Baxter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Baxter), [Teresa of Avila](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teresa_of_Avila), [Francis de Sales](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francis_de_Sales), [Brother Lawrence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brother_Lawrence), and the Rule of [St. Benedict](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Benedict).

He served on the boards of the [C.S. Lewis Foundation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C.S._Lewis) and of [Biola University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biola_University).[^o20] He also served on the board of the Templeton foundation, and on multiple evaluation committees for the Western Association of Schools and colleges.[^o21]

## Personal life {#Personal_life}

Willard was born in Buffalo, Missouri on September 4, 1935, the son of Albert Willard and Maymie Lindesmith. His mother died when he was two years old. He married Jane Lakes of Macon, Georgia, in 1955. He had two children, John and Rebecca, and a granddaughter, Larissa. His brother, L. Duane Willard, was also for many years a tenured professor of philosophy at the [University of Nebraska Omaha](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Nebraska_Omaha).

Willard struggled with balancing academic and family life. He once remarked, "I have not been a wise husband or father, and this has cost us dearly."[^o23] Nonetheless, his daughter commented that he was a “great example of unconditional fatherly love." His wife Jane, a marriage and family therapist, said "I certainly don't feel unloved, at least at this juncture … Always down deep in my formation was this thing before God of 'I cannot stand in his way.'” [^o23]

Willard died on May 8, 2013, after a short battle with cancer.[^o24]

## Impact {#Impact}

- In 2010, [Westmont College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Westmont_College) established the “Martin Institute for Christianity and Culture and the Dallas Willard Center for Spiritual Formation.” The institute is “dedicated to the intellectual Legacy of Willard”.[^o26]
- Willard was active in the establishment of the Christian [spiritual formation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spiritual_formation) ministry, [Renovare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renovaré).
- Willard had an impact on the vision and establishment of [The Apprentice Institute for Christian Spiritual Formation](https://apprenticeinstitute.org/), which intends to carry out Willard's vision of [Trinitarian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trinitarian) and [Christocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christocentrism) spiritual formation.
- [Dallas Willard Ministries](https://dwillard.org/) exists for the purpose of carrying out the vision and extension of Willard's worldview and approach to Christian [Discipleship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disciple_%28Christianity%29).
- [Dallas Willard Ministries](https://dwillard.org/) and [North Eastern Seminary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Northeastern_Seminary) partnered together to create the [Dallas Willard Ministries School of Kingdom Living](https://www.nes.edu/about/partners/dallas-willard-ministries-school-of-kingdom-living/) which also intends to extend and achieve the vision that Willard had for helping people to become [apprentices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disciple_%28Christianity%29) of Jesus.
- Willard is known for having closely worked with and influenced the work and writings of [Richard J. Foster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Foster_%28theologian%29).
- Willard is also noted for having heavily influenced the theology, praxis, and writings of James Bryan Smith, John Mark Comer, E. James Wilder, Hugh Halter, and Christian theologian and philosopher [J.P. Moreland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._P._Moreland) among others.
