# Transport-of-intensity equation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Transport-of-intensity equation"}

The **transport-of-intensity equation** (**TIE**) is a computational approach to [reconstruct the phase](wikipedia://en/11081803) of a [complex wave](wikipedia://en/33516) in [optical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_microscope) and [electron microscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electron_microscope).[^o2] It describes the internal relationship between the [intensity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensity_%28physics%29) and phase distribution of a wave.[^o3]

:::

The TIE was first proposed in 1983 by Michael Reed Teague.[^o4] Teague suggested to use the law of [conservation of energy](wikipedia://en/67088) to write a [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) for the transport of energy by an [optical field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_field). This equation, he stated, could be used as an approach to [phase recovery](wikipedia://en/11081803).[^o5]

Teague approximated the [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) of the wave propagating nominally in the z-direction by a [parabolic equation](wikipedia://en/23231) and then expressed it in terms of [irradiance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irradiance) and phase:

$$\frac{2\pi}{\lambda} \frac{\partial}{\partial z}I(x,y,z)= -\nabla_{x,y} \cdot [I(x,y,z)\nabla_{x,y}\Phi],$$

where $\lambda$ is the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125), $I(x,y,z)$ is the irradiance at point $(x,y,z)$, and $\Phi$ is the phase of the wave. If the intensity distribution of the wave and its spatial [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) can be measured experimentally, the equation becomes a linear equation that can be solved to obtain the phase distribution $\Phi$.[^o6]

For a phase sample with a constant intensity, the TIE simplifies to

$$\frac{d}{dz}I(z) = -\frac{\lambda}{2\pi} I(z) \nabla_{x,y}^2 \Phi.$$

It allows measuring the phase distribution of the sample by acquiring a defocused image, i.e. $I(x,y,z + \Delta z)$.

TIE-based approaches are applied in biomedical and technical applications, such as quantitative monitoring of cell growth in culture,[^o7] investigation of cellular dynamics and characterization of optical elements.[^o8] The TIE method is also applied for phase retrieval in transmission electron microscopy.[^o9]
