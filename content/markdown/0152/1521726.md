# Superquadrics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Superquadrics"}

*Image: Ex\_sq.png.* Some superquadrics.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **superquadrics** or **super-quadrics** (also **superquadratics**) are a family of [geometric shapes](wikipedia://en/18973446) defined by formulas that resemble those of [ellipsoids](wikipedia://en/145381) and other [quadrics](wikipedia://en/145570), except that the [squaring](wikipedia://en/659942) operations are replaced by arbitrary powers. They can be seen as the three-dimensional relatives of the [superellipses](wikipedia://en/52211). The term may refer to the solid object or to its [surface](wikipedia://en/4342970), depending on the context. The equations below specify the surface; the solid is specified by replacing the equality signs by less-than-or-equal signs.

:::

The superquadrics include many shapes that resemble [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285), [octahedra](wikipedia://en/80177635), [cylinders](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cylinder_%28geometry%29), [lozenges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lozenge_%28shape%29) and [spindles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spindle_torus), with rounded or sharp corners.[^o4] Because of their flexibility and relative simplicity, they are popular [geometric modeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_model) tools, especially in [computer graphics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_graphics). It becomes an important [geometric primitive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_primitive) widely used in [computer vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_vision),[^o6][^o7] robotics,[^o2] and physical simulation.[^o3]

Some authors, such as [Alan Barr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alan_H._Barr), define "superquadrics" as including both the [superellipsoids](wikipedia://en/19681409) and the [supertoroids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supertoroid).[^o4][^o5] In modern computer vision literature, superquadrics and [superellipsoids](wikipedia://en/19681409) are used interchangeably, since [superellipsoids](wikipedia://en/19681409) are the most representative and widely utilized shape among all the superquadrics.[^o6][^o7] Comprehensive coverage of geometrical properties of superquadrics and methods of their recovery from [range images](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Range_image) and [point clouds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_cloud) are covered in several computer vision literatures.[^o4][^o7][^o8][^o9]

## Formulas {#Formulas}

### Implicit equation {#Implicit_equation}

The surface of the basic superquadric is given by

$$\left|x\right|^r + \left|y\right|^s + \left|z\right|^t =1$$

where *r*, *s*, and *t* are positive real numbers that determine the main features of the superquadric. Namely:

- less than 1: a pointy octahedron modified to have [concave](wikipedia://en/605156) [faces](wikipedia://en/149926) and sharp [edges](wikipedia://en/12158034).
- exactly 1: a regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635).
- between 1 and 2: an octahedron modified to have convex faces, blunt edges and blunt corners.
- exactly 2: a sphere
- greater than 2: a cube modified to have rounded edges and corners.
- [infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinity_%28mathematics%29) (in the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602)): a cube

Each exponent can be varied independently to obtain combined shapes. For example, if *r*=*s*=2, and *t*=4, one obtains a [solid of revolution](wikipedia://en/329400) which resembles an ellipsoid with round cross-section but flattened ends. This formula is a special case of the superellipsoid's formula if (and only if) *r* = *s*.

If any exponent is allowed to be negative, the shape extends to infinity. Such shapes are sometimes called **super-hyperboloids**.

The basic shape above spans from -1 to +1 along each coordinate axis. The general superquadric is the result of [scaling](wikipedia://en/39377) this basic shape by different amounts *A*, *B*, *C* along each axis. Its general equation is

$$\left|\frac{x}{A}\right|^r + \left|\frac{y}{B}\right|^s + \left|\frac{z}{C}\right|^t = 1.$$

### Parametric description {#Parametric_description}

Parametric equations in terms of surface parameters *u* and *v* (equivalent to longitude and latitude if m equals 2) are

$$\begin{aligned} x(u,v) &{}= A g\left(v,\frac{2}{r}\right) g\left(u,\frac{2}{r}\right) \\ y(u,v) &{}= B g\left(v,\frac{2}{s}\right) f\left(u,\frac{2}{s}\right) \\ z(u,v) &{}= C f\left(v,\frac{2}{t}\right) \\ & -\frac{\pi}{2} \le v \le \frac{\pi}{2}, \quad -\pi \le u < \pi , \end{aligned}$$

where the [auxiliary functions](wikipedia://en/11826062) are

$$\begin{aligned} f(\omega,m) &{}= \operatorname{sgn}(\sin \omega) \left|\sin \omega \right|^m \\ g(\omega,m) &{}= \operatorname{sgn}(\cos \omega) \left|\cos \omega \right|^m \end{aligned}$$

and the [sign function](wikipedia://en/505449) sgn(*x*) is

$$\operatorname{sgn}(x) = \begin{cases} -1, & x < 0 \\ 0, & x = 0 \\ +1, & x > 0 . \end{cases}$$

### Spherical product {#Spherical_product}

Barr introduces the *spherical product* which given two plane curves produces a 3D surface. If

$$f(\mu)=\begin{pmatrix}f_1(\mu) \\ f_2(\mu)\end{pmatrix},\quad g(\nu)=\begin{pmatrix}g_1(\nu)\\g_2(\nu)\end{pmatrix}$$

are two plane curves then the spherical product is

$$h(\mu,\nu) = f(\mu)\otimes g(\nu) = \begin{pmatrix} f_1(\mu)\ g_1(\nu) \\ f_1(\mu)\ g_2(\nu) \\ f_2(\mu) \end{pmatrix}$$

This is similar to the typical parametric equation of a [sphere](wikipedia://en/27859):

$$\begin{aligned} x&=x_{0}+r\sin \theta \;\cos \varphi \\ y&=y_{0}+r\sin \theta \;\sin \varphi \qquad (0\leq \theta \leq \pi ,\;0\leq \varphi <2\pi )\\ z&=z_{0}+r\cos \theta \end{aligned}$$

which give rise to the name spherical product.

Barr uses the spherical product to define quadric surfaces, like [ellipsoids](wikipedia://en/145381), and [hyperboloids](wikipedia://en/145844) as well as the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), [superellipsoid](wikipedia://en/19681409), superquadric hyperboloids of one and two sheets, and supertoroids.[^o4]

## Plotting code {#Plotting_code}

The following [GNU Octave](wikipedia://en/48707) code generates a mesh approximation of a superquadric:

```matlab
function superquadric(epsilon, a)
  n = 50;
  etamax = pi / 2;
  etamin = -pi / 2;
  wmax = pi;
  wmin = -pi;
  deta = (etamax - etamin) / n;
  dw = (wmax - wmin) / n;
  [i, j] = meshgrid(1:n+1, 1:n+1)
  eta = etamin + (i - 1) * deta;
  w   = wmin + (j - 1) * dw;
  x = a(1) .* sign(cos(eta)) .* abs(cos(eta)).^epsilon(1) .* sign(cos(w)) .* abs(cos(w)).^epsilon(1);
  y = a(2) .* sign(cos(eta)) .* abs(cos(eta)).^epsilon(2) .* sign(sin(w)) .* abs(sin(w)).^epsilon(2);
  z = a(3) .* sign(sin(eta)) .* abs(sin(eta)).^epsilon(3);

  mesh(x, y, z);
end
```
