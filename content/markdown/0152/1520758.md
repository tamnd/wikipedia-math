# Null graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Null graph"}

In the [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) field of [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), the term "**null graph**" may refer either to the [order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_%28graph_theory%29)-[zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero) [graph](wikipedia://en/325806), or alternatively, to any edgeless graph (the latter is sometimes called an "empty graph").

:::

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Order-zero graph"}

## Order-zero graph {#Order-zero_graph}

The **order-zero graph**, $K_{0}$, is the unique graph having no [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) (hence its order is zero). It follows that $K_{0}$ also has no [edges](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29). Thus the null graph is a [regular graph](wikipedia://en/85821) of degree zero. Some authors exclude $K_{0}$ from consideration as a graph (either by definition, or more simply as a matter of convenience). Whether including $K_{0}$ as a valid graph is useful depends on context. On the positive side, $K_{0}$ follows naturally from the usual [set-theoretic](wikipedia://en/27553) definitions of a graph (it is the [ordered pair](wikipedia://en/22362) $(V, E)$ for which the vertex and edge sets, $V$ and $E$, are both [empty](wikipedia://en/9566)), in [proofs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_%28mathematics%29) it serves as a natural base case for [mathematical induction](wikipedia://en/18881), and similarly, in [recursively defined](wikipedia://en/1047605) [data structures](wikipedia://en/8519) $K_{0}$ is useful for defining the base case for recursion (by treating the **null [tree](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tree_%28data_structure%29)** as the [child](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_node_%28of_a_tree%29) of missing edges in any non-null [binary tree](wikipedia://en/4321), every non-null binary tree has *exactly* two children). On the negative side, including $K_{0}$ as a graph requires that many well-defined formulas for [graph properties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_properties) include exceptions for it (for example, either "counting all [strongly connected components](wikipedia://en/684680) of a graph" becomes "counting all *non-null* strongly connected components of a graph", or the definition of connected graphs has to be modified not to include $K_{0}$). To avoid the need for such exceptions, it is often assumed in literature that the term *graph* implies "graph with at least one vertex" unless context suggests otherwise.

In [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), the order-zero graph is, according to some definitions of "category of graphs," the [initial object](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Initial_object) in the category.

$K_{0}$ does fulfill ([vacuously](wikipedia://en/51672)) most of the same basic graph properties as does $K_{1}$ (the graph with one vertex and no edges). As some examples, $K_{0}$ is of [size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Size_%28graph_theory%29) zero, it is equal to its [complement graph](wikipedia://en/1175649) $\overline{K}_{0}$, a [forest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Forest_%28graph_theory%29), and a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314). It may be considered [undirected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph), [directed](wikipedia://en/19721986), or even both; when considered as directed, it is a [directed acyclic graph](wikipedia://en/204002). And it is both a [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) and an edgeless graph. However, definitions for each of these graph properties will vary depending on whether context allows for $K_{0}$.

:::

## Edgeless graph {#Edgeless_graph}

For each [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $n$, the **edgeless graph** (or empty graph) $\overline{K}_{n}$ of order $n$ is the graph with $n$ vertices and zero edges. An edgeless graph is occasionally referred to as a null graph in contexts where the order-zero graph is not permitted.[^o6][^o7]

It is a 0-[regular](wikipedia://en/85821) graph. The notation $\overline{K}_{n}$ arises from the fact that the $n$-vertex edgeless graph is the [complement](wikipedia://en/1175649) of the [complete graph](wikipedia://en/85816) $K_{n}$.
