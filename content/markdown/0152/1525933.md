# Rodrigues' rotation formula

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Rodrigues' rotation formula"}

In the theory of [three-dimensional rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Three-dimensional_rotation), **Rodrigues' rotation formula**, named after [Olinde Rodrigues](wikipedia://en/1003283), is an efficient algorithm for rotating a [vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28geometric%29) in space, given an [axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Axis_angle) and [angle of rotation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_rotation). By extension, this can be used to transform all three [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector) to compute a [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005) in $\operatorname{SO}(3)$, the group of all rotation matrices, from an [axis–angle representation](wikipedia://en/9136071). In terms of [Lie theory](wikipedia://en/1595681), this Rodrigues' formula provides an algorithm to compute the [exponential map](wikipedia://en/276548) from the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathbf{so}(3)$ to its [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) $\operatorname{SO}(3)$.

:::

This formula is variously credited to [Leonhard Euler](wikipedia://en/17902), [Olinde Rodrigues](wikipedia://en/1003283), or a combination of the two. A detailed historical analysis in 1989 concluded that the formula should be attributed to Euler, and recommended calling it "Euler's finite rotation formula."[^o2] This proposal has received notable support,[^o3] but some others have viewed the formula as just one of many variations of the [Euler–Rodrigues formula](wikipedia://en/32352950), thereby crediting both.[^o4]

::: {#o6 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

If $\mathbf{v}$ is a vector in $\mathbb{R}^{3}$ and $\mathbf{k}$ is a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) describing an axis of rotation about which $\mathbf{v}$ rotates by an angle $\theta$ according to the [right hand rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_hand_rule#Direction_associated_with_a_rotation), the Rodrigues formula for the rotated vector $\mathbf{v}_{\operatorname{rot}}$ is

The intuition of the above formula is that the first term scales the vector down, while the second skews it (via [vector addition](wikipedia://en/32533#Addition_and_subtraction)) toward the new rotational position. The third term re-adds the height (relative to $\textbf{k}$) that was lost by the first term.

An alternative statement is to write the axis vector as a [cross product](wikipedia://en/157092) $\mathbf{a} \times \mathbf{b}$ of any two nonzero vectors $\mathbf{a}$ and $\mathbf{b}$ which define the [plane of rotation](wikipedia://en/25935424), and the sense of the angle $\theta$ is measured away from $\mathbf{a}$ and towards $\mathbf{b}$. Letting $\alpha$ denote the angle between these vectors, the two angles $\theta$ and $\alpha$ are not necessarily equal, but they are measured in the same sense. Then the unit axis vector can be written

$$\mathbf{k} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}|} = \frac{\mathbf{a}\times\mathbf{b}}{|\mathbf{a}||\mathbf{b}|\sin\alpha}\,.$$

This form may be more useful when two vectors defining a plane are involved. An example in physics is the [Thomas precession](wikipedia://en/1868229) which includes the rotation given by Rodrigues' formula, in terms of two non-collinear boost velocities, and the axis of rotation is perpendicular to their plane.

:::

## Derivation {#Derivation}

*Image: Rodrigues-formula.svg.* Rodrigues' rotation formula rotates $\mathbf{v}$ by an angle $\theta$ around vector $k$ by decomposing it into its components parallel and perpendicular to $k$, and rotating only the perpendicular component.

*Image: Orthogonal decomposition unit vector rodrigues rotation formula.svg.* Vector geometry of Rodrigues' rotation formula, as well as the decomposition into parallel and perpendicular components.

Let $\mathbf{k}$ be a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) defining a rotation axis, and let $\mathbf{v}$ be any vector to rotate about $\mathbf{k}$ by angle $\theta$ ([right hand rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right_hand_rule), anticlockwise in the figure), producing the rotated vector $\mathbb{v}_{\text{rot}}$.

Using the [dot](wikipedia://en/157093) and [cross products](wikipedia://en/157092), the vector $\mathbf{v}$ can be decomposed into components parallel and perpendicular to the axis $\mathbf{k}$,

$$\mathbf{v} = \mathbf{v}_\parallel + \mathbf{v}_\perp \,,$$

where the component parallel to $\mathbf{k}$ is called the [vector projection](wikipedia://en/1508507) of $\mathbf{v}$ on $\mathbf{k}$,

$$\mathbf{v}_\parallel = (\mathbf{v} \cdot \mathbf{k}) \mathbf{k},$$

and the component perpendicular to $\mathbf{k}$ is called the [vector rejection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_rejection) of $\mathbf{v}$ from $\mathbf{k}$:

$$\mathbf{v}_{\perp} = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_{\parallel} = \mathbf{v} - (\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{v}) \mathbf{k} = - \mathbf{k}\times(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v}),$$

where the last equality follows from the [vector triple product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_triple_product) formula: $\mathbf{a}\times (\mathbf{b} \times \mathbf{c}) = (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{c})\mathbf{b} - (\mathbf{a} \cdot \mathbf{b})\mathbf{c}$. Finally, the vector $\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{v}_{\perp} = \mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{v}$ is a copy of $\mathbf{v}_{\perp}$ rotated 90° around $\mathbf{k}$. Thus the three vectors

$$\mathbf{k}\,,\ \mathbf{v}_{\perp}\,,\, \mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{v}$$

form a [right-handed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Right-hand_rule) [orthogonal basis](wikipedia://en/755400) of $\mathbb{R}^3$, with the last two vectors of equal length.

*Image: Rodrigues\_rotation\_formula\_animation.gif.* Animated geometric illustration of Rodrigues rotation formula: a vector is decomposed into components parallel and perpendicular to the rotation axis, and the perpendicular component is rotated in its plane.

Under the rotation, the component $\mathbf{v}_{\parallel}$ parallel to the axis will not change magnitude nor direction:

$$\mathbf{v}_{\parallel\mathrm{rot}} = \mathbf{v}_\parallel \,;$$

while the perpendicular component will retain its magnitude but rotate its direction in the perpendicular plane spanned by $\mathbf{v}_{\perp}$ and $\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{v}$, according to

$$\mathbf{v}_{\perp\mathrm{rot}} = \cos(\theta) \mathbf{v}_\perp + \sin(\theta) \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v}_\perp = \cos(\theta) \mathbf{v}_\perp + \sin(\theta) \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v} \,,$$

in analogy with the planar [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) $(r, \theta)$ in the [Cartesian basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_coordinates) $\mathbf{e}_{x}$, $\mathbf{e}_{y}$:

$$\mathbf{r} = r\cos(\theta) \mathbf{e}_x + r\sin(\theta) \mathbf{e}_y \,.$$

Now the full rotated vector is:

$$\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rot}} = \mathbf{v}_{\parallel\mathrm{rot}} + \mathbf{v}_{\perp\mathrm{rot}} = \mathbf{v}_\parallel + \cos(\theta) \, \mathbf{v}_\perp + \sin(\theta) \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v} .$$

Substituting $\mathbf{v}_{\perp} = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_{\|}$ or $\mathbf{v}_{\| } = \mathbf{v} - \mathbf{v}_{\perp}$ in the last expression gives respectively:

$$\mathbf{v}_{\text{rot}} = \cos(\theta) \, \mathbf{v} + \sin(\theta) \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v} + (1 - \cos\theta)(\mathbf{k} \cdot \mathbf{v})\mathbf{k}$$

$$\phantom{\mathbf{v}_{\text{rot}}} = \mathbf{v} + \sin(\theta) \mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v} + (1-\cos\theta)\mathbf{k}\times(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v}).$$

## Matrix notation {#Matrix_notation}

The linear transformation on $\mathbf{v}\in\mathbb{R}^3$ defined by the cross product $\mathbf{v} \mapsto \mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{v}$ is given in coordinates by representing $\mathbf{v}$ and $\mathbf{k} \times \mathbf{v}$ as [column matrices](wikipedia://en/149968):

$$\begin{bmatrix} (\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v})_x \\ (\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v})_y \\ (\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v})_z \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} k_y v_z - k_z v_y \\ k_z v_x - k_x v_z \\ k_x v_y - k_y v_x \end{bmatrix} = \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 0\ \, & -k_z & k_y \\ k_z & 0\ \, & -k_x \\ -k_y & k_x & 0\ \, \end{array}\right] \begin{bmatrix} v_x \\ v_y \\ v_z \end{bmatrix} \,.$$

That is, the [matrix of this linear transformation](wikipedia://en/692458) (with respect to standard coordinates) is the [cross-product matrix](wikipedia://en/157092#Conversion_to_matrix_multiplication):

$$\mathbf{K}= \left[\begin{array}{rrr} 0\ \, & -k_z & k_y \\ k_z & 0\ \, & -k_x \\ -k_y & k_x & 0\ \, \end{array}\right]\,.$$

That is to say,

$$\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v}=\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}, \qquad\qquad \mathbf{k}\times(\mathbf{k}\times\mathbf{v})=\mathbf{K}(\mathbf{K}\mathbf{v}) = \mathbf{K}^2\mathbf{v} \,.$$

The last formula in the previous section can therefore be written as:

$$\mathbf{v}_{\mathrm{rot}} = \mathbf{v} + (\sin\theta) \mathbf{K}\mathbf{v} + (1 - \cos\theta)\mathbf{K}^2\mathbf{v}\,.$$

Collecting terms allows the compact expression

$$\mathbf{v}_\mathrm{rot} = \mathbf{R}\mathbf{v}$$

where

is the [rotation matrix](wikipedia://en/856005) through an angle $\theta$ counterclockwise about the axis $\mathbf{k}$, and $\mathbf{I}$ the 3 × 3 [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).[^o11] This matrix $\mathbf{R}$ is an element of the rotation group $\operatorname{SO}(3)$ of $\mathbb{R}^3$, and $\mathbf{K}$ is an element of the [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ generating that Lie group (note that $\mathbf{K}$ is [skew-symmetric](wikipedia://en/174055), which characterizes $\mathfrak{so}(3)$).

In terms of the matrix exponential,

$$\mathbf{R} = \exp (\theta\mathbf{K})\,.$$

To see that the last identity holds, one notes that

$$\mathbf{R}(\theta) \mathbf{R}(\phi) = \mathbf{R} (\theta+\phi), \quad \mathbf{R}(0) = \mathbf{I}\,,$$

characteristic of a [one-parameter subgroup](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One-parameter_subgroup), i.e. exponential, and that the formulas match for [infinitesimal](wikipedia://en/160990) $\theta$.

For an alternative derivation based on this exponential relationship, see [exponential map from $\mathfrak{so}(3)$ to $\operatorname{SO}(3)$](wikipedia://en/9136071#Exponential_map_from_𝔰𝔬%283%29_to_SO%283%29). For the inverse mapping, see [log map from $\operatorname{SO}(3)$ to $\mathfrak{so}(3)$](wikipedia://en/9136071#Log_map_from_SO%283%29_to_𝔰𝔬%283%29).

The above result can be written in [index notation](wikipedia://en/320026) as follows. The elements of the matrix for an active rotation by an angle $\theta$ about an axis $\mathbf{k}$ are given by

$$R_{ij} = \cos\theta\, \delta_{ij} + (1 - \cos\theta) k_i k_j - \sin\theta\, \epsilon_{ijl} k_l.$$

Here, $i$, $j$, and $l$ label the Cartesian components $(x, y, z)$ or $(1, 2, 3)$, $\delta_{ij}$ and $\epsilon_{ijl}$ are the Kronecker and Levi-Civita symbols, and there is an implicit sum on repeated indices. More explicitly, its entries are given by:

$$\mathbf{R}= \left[\begin{array}{lll} c + (1 - c) k_x^2 & (1 - c) k_x k_y - s k_z & (1 - c) k_x k_z + s k_y \\ (1 - c) k_x k_y + s k_z & c + (1 - c) k_y^2 & (1 - c) k_y k_z - s k_x \\ (1 - c) k_x k_z -s k_y & (1 - c) k_y k_z + s k_x & c + (1 - c) k_z^2 \end{array}\right]\, ,$$

where $s = \sin\theta$ and $c = \cos\theta$.

The [Hodge dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_dual) of the rotation $\mathbf{R}$ is just $\mathbf{R}^* = -\sin(\theta)\mathbf{k}$ which enables the extraction of both the axis of rotation and the sine of the angle of the rotation from the rotation matrix itself, with the usual ambiguity,

$$\begin{aligned} \sin(\theta) &= \sigma \left|\mathbf{R}^*\right| \\[3pt] \mathbf{k} &= -\frac{\sigma\mathbf{R}^*}{\left|\mathbf{R}^*\right|} \end{aligned}$$

where $\sigma = \pm 1$. The above simple expression results from the fact that the Hodge duals of $\mathbf{I}$ and $\mathbf{K}^2$ are zero, and $\mathbf{K}^* = -\mathbf{k}$.
