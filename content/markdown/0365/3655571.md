# Eastern Arabic numerals

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Eastern Arabic numerals"}

*Image: The\_Brahmi\_numeral\_system\_and\_its\_descendants.png.* Evolution of Indian numerals into Arabic and Eastern Arabic numerals along with their adoption in [Europe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europe)

The **Eastern Arabic numerals**, also called **Indo-Arabic numerals**, or **Arabic–Indic numerals**, as designated by [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode), are the symbols used to represent [numerical digits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numerical_digits) in conjunction with the [Arabic script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_script) in the countries of the [Mashriq](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mashriq) (the east of the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world), including the [Arabian Peninsula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabian_Peninsula)), and the variants in other countries that use the [Persian numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_numerals) in [greater Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greater_Iran) and [Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asia).

:::

The early [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](wikipedia://en/3393371) used a variety of shapes.[^o2] It is unknown when the Western Arabic numeral shapes diverged from those of Eastern Arabic numerals. The Western digits 1, 2, 3, 4, 5 and 9 are clearly related, but the origins of 6, 7 and 8 are obscure.[^o3]

## Origin {#Origin}

The numeral system originates from an ancient [Indian numeral system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numerals), which was reintroduced during the [Islamic Golden Age](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_Golden_Age) in the book *On the Calculation with Hindic Numerals* written by the [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persians) mathematician and engineer [al-Khwarizmi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muhammad_ibn_Musa_al-Khwarizmi), whose name was [Latinized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Latinisation_of_names) as *Algoritmi*.[^o5]

## Other names {#Other_names}

These numbers are known as ʾarqām mašriqiyya (أَرْقَام مَشْرِقِيَّة) or ʾarqām hindiyya (أَرْقَام هِنْدِيَّة) in [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic). They are sometimes also called Indic numerals[^o7] or Arabic–Indic numerals[^o8] in English. However, that is sometimes discouraged as it can lead to confusion with [Devanagari numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_numerals), used in [Brahmic scripts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brahmic_scripts) of the [Indian subcontinent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_subcontinent).[^o9]

## Numerals {#Numerals}

*Image: clock-in-cairo-with-eastern-arabic-numerals.jpg.* Eastern Arabic numerals on a clock in the [Cairo Metro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cairo_Metro)

Each numeral in the Persian variant has a different [Unicode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicode) point even if it looks identical to the Eastern Arabic numeral counterpart.[^o11] However, the variants used with [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu) and other [languages of South Asia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Languages_of_South_Asia) are not encoded separately from the Persian variants.

In Arabic, negative signs are written to the right of magnitudes, e.g. −٣ (−3). In Persian, they are written to the left, e.g. ٣−.

*Image: Peugeot\_Pars\_Face.jpg.* Eastern Arabic letters and numerals on the [license plate of a car in Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle_registration_plates_of_Iran), which reads "82N712-11"

In-line fractions are written with the numerator on the left and the denominator on the right of the fraction slash, e.g. ٢/٧ ($\tfrac{2}{7}$).

The Arabic decimal separator ٫ (U+066B) or the comma , is used as the [decimal mark](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_mark), as in ٣٫١٤١٥٩٢٦٥٣٥٨ ([\3.14159265358](wikipedia://en/23601)).

The Arabic thousands separator ٬ (U+066C) or quote ' or Arabic comma ، (U+060C) may be used as a [thousands separator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decimal_mark#Digit_grouping), e.g. ١٬٠٠٠٬٠٠٠٬٠٠٠ (1,000,000,000).

## Contemporary use {#Contemporary_use}

Eastern Arabic numerals are predominantly used over Western Arabic numerals in many countries to the east of the [Arab world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arab_world), notably [Iran](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iran) and [Afghanistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Afghanistan).

Both Eastern and Western Arabic numerals are concurrently used (and are often employed alongside each other) across Arabic-speaking Asia, [Egypt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egypt) and [Sudan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sudan), although Western Arabic numerals are increasingly favoured in the region, particularly in [Saudi Arabia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saudi_Arabia). The [United Arab Emirates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Arab_Emirates) uses both Eastern and Western Arabic numerals.

In [Pakistan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pakistan), Western Arabic numerals are more extensively used digitally. Eastern numerals continue to see use in [Urdu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urdu) publications and newspapers, as well as signboards.

In the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb), only Western Arabic numerals are commonly used today. In medieval times, this region used a slightly different variation of the system (from which, via [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy), Western Arabic numerals derive).

The [Thaana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thaana) writing system used for the [Maldivian language](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dhivehi_language) adopted its first nine letters (*haa*, *shaviyani*, *noonu*, *raa*, *baa*, *ihaviyani*, *kaafu*, *alifu*, and *vaavu*) from Perso-Arabic numerals.[^o16]
