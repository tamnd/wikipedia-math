# Choquet theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Choquet theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Choquet theory**, named after [Gustave Choquet](wikipedia://en/17301193), is an area of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and [convex analysis](wikipedia://en/3237201) concerned with [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) which have [support](wikipedia://en/381013) on the [extreme points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extreme_points) of a [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) *C*. Roughly speaking, every [vector](wikipedia://en/32533) of *C* should appear as a weighted average of extreme points, a concept made more precise by generalizing the notion of weighted average from a [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) to an [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) taken over the set *E* of extreme points. Here *C* is a subset of a [real vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) *V*, and the main thrust of the theory is to treat the cases where *V* is an infinite-dimensional (locally convex Hausdorff) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) along lines similar to the finite-dimensional case. The main concerns of Gustave Choquet were in [potential theory](wikipedia://en/1431342). Choquet theory has become a general paradigm, particularly for treating [convex cones](wikipedia://en/3558266) as determined by their extreme [rays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Line_%28mathematics%29#Ray), and so for many different notions of *positivity* in mathematics.

:::

The two ends of a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860) determine the points in between: in vector terms the segment from *v* to *w* consists of the λ*v* + (1 − λ)*w* with 0 ≤ λ ≤ 1. The classical result of [Hermann Minkowski](wikipedia://en/193537) says that in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), a [bounded](wikipedia://en/48258), [closed](wikipedia://en/47279) [convex set](wikipedia://en/6292) *C* is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of its extreme point set *E*, so that any *c* in *C* is a (finite) [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) of points *e* of *E*. Here *E* may be a finite or an [infinite set](wikipedia://en/142679). In vector terms, by assigning non-negative weights *w*(*e*) to the *e* in *E*, [almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) 0, we can represent any *c* in *C* as

$$c = \sum_{e\in E} w(e) e\$$

with

$$\sum_{e\in E} w(e) = 1.\$$

In any case the *w*(*e*) give a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) supported on a finite subset of *E*. For any [affine function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_function) *f* on *C*, its value at the point *c* is

$$f (c) = \int f(e) d w(e).$$

In the infinite dimensional setting, one would like to make a similar statement.

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Choquet's theorem"}

## Choquet's theorem {#Choquet's_theorem}

**Choquet's theorem** states[^o4][^o5] that for a [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) convex [metrizable](wikipedia://en/19738) subset *C* of a [locally convex topological vector space](wikipedia://en/518844) *V*, given *c* in *C* there exists a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/45437) *w* on *C* supported on the set *E* of extreme points of *C* such that, for any affine function *f* on *C,*

$$f (c) = \int f(e) d w(e).$$

In practice *V* could be a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) but in many important cases *V* is not a Banach space and it is even possible that *V* itself is not metrizable, such as in the case of *V* having the [weak-\* topology](wikipedia://en/33662). The original [Krein–Milman theorem](wikipedia://en/3206654) follows from Choquet's result. Another corollary is the [Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem](wikipedia://en/38948504) for [states](wikipedia://en/454959) on the continuous functions on a metrizable compact Hausdorff space.

More generally, when *C* is not metrizable the **Choquet–Bishop–de Leeuw theorem**[^o4][^o5][^o6] gives a similar representation with a probability measure that vanishes on the [Baire subsets](wikipedia://en/3976202) of *C* which contain no extreme points.

In addition to the existence of a probability measure supported on the extreme boundary that represents a given point *c*, one might also consider the uniqueness of such measures. It is easy to see that uniqueness does not hold even in the finite dimensional setting. One can take, for counterexamples, the convex set to be a [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) or a ball in **R**<sup>3</sup>. Uniqueness does hold, however, when the convex set is a finite dimensional [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781). A finite dimensional simplex is a special case of a **Choquet simplex**. Any point in a Choquet simplex is represented by a unique probability measure on the extreme points.

:::
