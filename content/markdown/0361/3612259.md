# Hierarchy (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hierarchy"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **hierarchy** is a [set-theoretical](wikipedia://en/27553) object, consisting of a [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582) defined on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691). This is often referred to as an [ordered set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordered_set), though that is an ambiguous term that many authors reserve for [partially ordered sets](wikipedia://en/23572) or [totally ordered sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Totally_ordered_set). The term *pre-ordered set* is unambiguous, and is always synonymous with a mathematical hierarchy. The term *hierarchy* is used to stress a *[hierarchical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hierarchical)* relation among the elements.

:::

Sometimes, a set comes equipped with a natural hierarchical structure. For example, the set of [natural numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_numbers) **N** is equipped with a natural pre-order structure, where $n \le n'$ whenever we can find some other number $m$ so that $n + m = n'$. That is, $n'$ is bigger than $n$ only because we can get to $n'$ from $n$ *using* $m$. This idea can be applied to any [commutative monoid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_monoid). On the other hand, the set of integers **Z** requires a more sophisticated argument for its hierarchical structure, since we can always solve the equation $n + m = n'$ by writing $m = (n'-n)$.

Other natural hierarchies arise in [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), where the word refers to [partially ordered sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poset) whose elements are [classes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Class_%28programming%29) of objects of increasing [complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complexity). In that case, the preorder defining the hierarchy is the class-containment relation. [Containment hierarchies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Containment_hierarchy) are thus special cases of hierarchies.

## Related terminology {#Related_terminology}

Individual elements of a hierarchy are often called **levels** and a hierarchy is said to be infinite if it has infinitely many distinct levels but said to **collapse** if it has only finitely many distinct levels.

::: {#o4 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

In [theoretical computer science](wikipedia://en/323392), the [time hierarchy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_hierarchy) is a classification of [decision problems](wikipedia://en/8336) according to the amount of time required to solve them.

:::
