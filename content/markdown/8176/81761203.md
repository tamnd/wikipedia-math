# Game theory on networks

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Game theory on networks"}

**Game theory on networks** is a field that studies strategy in competing interest interactions among rational or adaptive players that are affected by the topology of networks.[^o12] Players' interactions are modeled by a [network](wikipedia://en/766409) (a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) with nodes for each player, plus additional data). This contains concepts from [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924), [nonlinear dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_dynamics), and [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) to analyze behavioral player-player phenomena like cooperation, and collective behavior as well as competition and percolation in networked systems.[^o11][^o15]

:::

This field has applications in areas such as [economics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Economics), [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), [biology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biology), and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), where players (nodes) interact through network connections (edges) instead of fully homogeneously mixed populations.[^o13]

## Overview {#Overview}

Typical models in game theory assume that all players interact with every other player in a *well-mixed population* that is homogeneous.[^o16] However, in *networked game theory,* nodes are limited to interact only through edges to other neighboring nodes.[^o12] In these networks, each node denotes a unique player while each edge denotes a path through which interactions are possible. These can be represented by payoff matrices that quantify utilities of different competing strategies.[^o14]

Furthermore, topological features (e.g. degree distribution, clustering, modularity, centrality) in networks can be studied in game theory settings, which may change the evolution, stability, and equilibria of strategies and therefore players.[^o15]

## Mathematical formulation {#Mathematical_formulation}

Consider a graph (or *network*) $G = (V, E)$ with $N = |V|$ nodes and with an adjacency matrix $A = [A_{ij}]$.[^o13] Each node $i \in V$ denotes a unique player with a strategy $s_i$ chosen from a set of strategies $S_i$. The payoff for node $i$ is:[^o16]

$$u_i(s_i, \mathbf{s}_{\mathcal{N}_i}) = \sum_{j \in \mathcal{N}_i} A_{ij} P(s_i, s_j)$$

where $P(s_i, s_j)$ is some payoff function pairwise between node $i$ each of its neighbors, $\mathcal{N}_i$.[^o12]

A Nash equilibrium of a network is a collection of strategies for each player $\mathbf{s}^* = (s_1^*, \dots, s_N^*)$ such that[^o16] $u_i(s_i^*, s_{-i}^*) \ge u_i(s_i, s_{-i}^*) \quad \forall i, s_i \in S_i.$

## Evolutionary dynamics {#Evolutionary_dynamics}

In evolutionary networked game theory, each node's strategy changes over time based on its payoff relative to its neighbors.[^o12] Let $x_i(t)$ be the probability that node $i$ uses strategy $s_i$. The replicator dynamics in this network are:[^o16]

$$\dot{x}_i = x_i(1 - x_i) [\Pi_i^{s_1} - \Pi_i^{s_2}],$$

$$\Pi_i^{s_1} = \sum_j A_{ij} P(s_1, s_j), \quad \Pi_i^{s_2} = \sum_j A_{ij} P(s_2, s_j).$$

These dynamics are the networked population version of the classical replicator equation for well-mixed populations.[^o11]

$$\dot{x} = x(1 - x) [\Pi_{s_1} - \Pi_{s_2}],$$

One often-used structure updating mechanism is the Fermi rule:[^o12]

$$\Pr(i \leftarrow j) = \frac{1}{1 + e^{-(\Pi_j - \Pi_i)/K}},$$

where $K$ controls the level of randomness in the imitation process, which is reminiscent of the [Boltzmann distribution](wikipedia://en/4107).[^o14] In this way, we can compare game theory dynamics to [statistical mechanics](wikipedia://en/28481) models.[^o15]

## Spectral and topological effects {#Spectral_and_topological_effects}

The [graph Laplacian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_Laplacian), $L = D - A$ (where $D$ is the degree matrix), can be used to determine specific characteristics of the node dynamics.[^o15] Linearizing the networked replicator dynamics around an equilibrium yields:[^o12] $\dot{\mathbf{x}} = -L W \mathbf{x},$

where $W$ logs the payoff gradients for local neighbors. The eigenvalues of $L$ (especially the algebraic connectivity $\lambda_2(L)$) can be used to calculate rates of convergence and the equilibrium stability.[^o13] Networks with a modular structure may exhibit slow strategy transition or extremely stable cooperative clusters, which is similar to phenomena observed in spin systems and synchronization.[^o15]

## Network formation games {#Network_formation_games}

For network formation games, players can decide to form or delete links in order to strategically maximize utility.[^o13] If creating a link creates a cost $c$ and yields benefit $b_{ij}$, a player's payoff can be written as:[^o13]

$$u_i(G) = \sum_j b_{ij} - c k_i,$$

where $k_i$ is the node's degree. A network $G^*$ is pairwise stable if:[^o13]

$$u_i(G^*) \ge u_i(G^* - ij) \quad \text{and} \quad u_i(G^* + ij) < u_i(G^*) \text{ or } u_j(G^* + ij) < u_j(G^*).$$

Models like these can explain the natural formation of social, economic, and communication networks as being the equilibrium outcomes of decentralized optimization.[^o13]

## Applications {#Applications}

Game theory in network science has applications in many fields.[^o14]

- economics - modeling competition and cooperation in trade networks[^o13]
- biology - modeling evolution of inter- or intra-species cooperation, and host–parasite interactions[^o11]
- computer science - distributed algorithms, routing, and cybersecurity[^o15]
- sociology - opinion dynamics, cultural evolution, and collective behavior[^o14]
- engineering - resource allocation in energy networks[^o15]

There are many current areas of research [^o14] that include the following. Multi-layer and temporal networks are games played on multiplex topologies.[^o15] Quantum game theory, which is the application of quantum information to strategic interactions on networks.[^o12] Learning and reinforcement dynamics which covers machine learning in evolutionary games.[^o14] Control and optimization, which means designing network structures to create desired equilibria[^o13]

Theoretical challenges include extending equilibrium concepts to non-stationary networks and developing scalable analytical approximations.[^o16] In nonlinear dynamics, it is also a large question of how to link microscopic dynamics to macroscopic observables.[^o15]
