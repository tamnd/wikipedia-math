# Galileo Galilei

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Galileo Galilei"}

**Galileo di Vincenzo Bonaiuti de' Galilei** (15 February 1564 – 8 January 1642), commonly referred to as **Galileo Galilei** or [mononymously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mononym) as **Galileo**, was an Italian [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer), [physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physicist), [engineer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineer) and [polymath](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polymath). He was born in [Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisa), then part of the [Duchy of Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Florence).[^o5] Galileo has been called the father of [observational astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observational_astronomy),[^o6] [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics),[^o7] the [scientific method](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scientific_method),[^o8] and [modern science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modern_science).[^o9]

:::

He studied [speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed) and [velocity](wikipedia://en/20491903), [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) and [free fall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_fall), the [principle of relativity](wikipedia://en/74327), [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia), [projectile motion](wikipedia://en/862717), and also worked in [applied science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_science) and technology, describing the properties of the [pendulum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) and "[hydrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic) balances". He was one of the earliest developers of the [thermoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoscope)[^o10] and the inventor of various [military compasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sector_%28instrument%29). With an improved [telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope) he built, he observed the stars of the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way), the [phases of Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phases_of_Venus), the [four largest satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_moons) of [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter), [Saturn's rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn's_rings), [lunar craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_craters), and [sunspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot). He also built an early [microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscope).

Galileo's championing of [Copernican heliocentrism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_heliocentrism) was met with opposition from within the [Catholic Church](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Catholic_Church) and from some astronomers. The matter was investigated by the [Roman Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Inquisition) in 1615, which concluded that his opinions contradicted accepted Biblical interpretations.[^n1][^n2][^n3]

Galileo defended his views in *[Dialogo sopra i due massimi sistemi del mondo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogo_sopra_i_due_massimi_sistemi_del_mondo)* (*Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, 1632), which appeared to dispute and satirize [Pope Urban VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_VIII), thus alienating both the Pope and the [Jesuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuits), who had both strongly supported Galileo until this point.[^n1] He was tried by the Inquisition, found "vehemently suspect of heresy", and forced to recant. He spent the rest of his life under house arrest.[^n4][^n5] During this time, he wrote *[Discorsi e Dimostrazioni Matematiche, intorno a due nuove scienze](wikipedia://en/268475)* (*Discourses and Mathematical Demonstrations Relating to Two New Sciences*, 1638) primarily about [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914) and the [strength of materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strength_of_materials).[^o17]

## Early life and family {#Early_life_and_family}

Galileo was born in [Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pisa) (then part of the [Duchy of Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Duchy_of_Florence)) on 15 February 1564,[^o19] the first of six children of [Vincenzo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Galilei), a leading [lutenist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lutenist), [composer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Composer), and [music theorist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music_theory), and [Giulia Ammannati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giulia_Ammannati), the daughter of a prominent merchant, who had married two years earlier in 1562, when he was 42 and she was 24. Vincenzo was part of the [Florentine Camerata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florentine_Camerata), which laid the groundwork for the new genre of [opera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opera). Galileo became an accomplished lutenist himself.[^n6]

Three of Galileo's five siblings survived infancy. The youngest, [Michelangelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelagnolo_Galilei) (or Michelagnolo), also became a lutenist and composer who added to Galileo's financial burdens for the rest of his life.[^n7] Michelangelo was unable to contribute his fair share of their father's promised dowries to their brothers-in-law, who later attempted to seek legal remedies for payments due. Michelangelo also occasionally had to borrow funds from Galileo to support his musical endeavours and excursions. These financial burdens may have contributed to Galileo's early desire to develop inventions that would bring him additional income.[^n8]

When Galileo Galilei was eight, his family moved to [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence,_Italy), but he was left under the care of Muzio Tedaldi for two years. When Galileo was ten, he left Pisa to join his family in Florence, where he came under the tutelage of Jacopo Borghini.[^o19] He was educated, particularly in [logic](wikipedia://en/46426065), from 1575 to 1578 in the [Vallombrosa Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vallombrosa_Abbey), about 30 km southeast of Florence.[^o23][^o24]

### Name {#Name}

Galileo tended to refer to himself by his first name. At the time, surnames were optional in Italy, and his first name had the same origin as his sometimes-family name, Galilei. Both his given and family name ultimately derived from an ancestor, [Galileo Bonaiuti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_Bonaiuti), an important physician, professor, and politician in Florence in the 15th century.[^n9] When he did refer to himself with more than one name, it was sometimes as Galileo Galilei Linceo, a reference to his being a member of the [Accademia dei Lincei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_dei_Lincei), an elite science organization founded in the [Papal States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Papal_States). It was common for mid-16th century Tuscan families to name the eldest son after the parents' surname.[^n10] Hence, Galileo Galilei was not necessarily named after his ancestor Galileo Bonaiuti.

The Italian male given name "Galileo" (and thence the surname "Galilei") derives from the Latin "Galilaeus", meaning "of [Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilee)".[^o28][^n9] This biblical name became the subject of a (supposed) pun. In 1614, during the [Galileo affair](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_affair), one of Galileo's opponents, the Dominican priest [Tommaso Caccini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommaso_Caccini), delivered against Galileo a controversial and influential [sermon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tommaso_Caccini#Sermon_at_Santa_Maria_Novella) quoting the Book of [Acts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acts_of_the_Apostles): "Ye men of [Galilee](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilee), why stand ye gazing up into heaven?".[^n11]

### Children {#Children}

*Image: A nun, traditionally identified as Suor Maria Celeste, daugh Wellcome L0031890.jpg.* Portrait believed to be of Galileo's elder daughter [Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Celeste), who was particularly devoted to her father

Though a pious Catholic,[^n12] Galileo fathered three children out of wedlock with [Marina Gamba](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marina_Gamba): daughters [Virginia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Celeste) (b. 1600) and Livia (1601), and a son [Vincenzo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Gamba) (1606).[^o33]

Due to their illegitimate birth, Galileo considered the girls unmarriageable, requiring expensive support or exorbitant dowries, similar to Galileo's previous financial problems with two of his sisters.[^n13] Their only respectable alternative was the religious life: they became lifelong nuns in the convent of San Matteo in [Arcetri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcetri).[^n14]

Virginia took the name Maria Celeste upon entering the convent. She died on 2 April 1634, and is buried with Galileo at the [Basilica of Santa Croce, Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_of_Santa_Croce,_Florence). Livia took the name Sister Arcangela and was ill most of her life. Vincenzo was later [legitimised](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legitimation) as Galileo's legal heir, and married Sestilia Bocchineri.[^o36]

## Career and first scientific contributions {#Career_and_first_scientific_contributions}

Although Galileo seriously considered the priesthood as a young man, at his father's urging he instead enrolled in 1580 at the [University of Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pisa) for a medical degree.[^n15] He was influenced by the lectures of [Girolamo Borro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girolamo_Borro), [Domingo de Soto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domingo_de_Soto) and [Francesco Buonamici](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Buonamici_%28philosopher%29) of Florence.[^o24] In 1581, when he was studying medicine, he noticed a swinging [chandelier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chandelier), which air currents shifted about to swing in larger and smaller arcs. To him, it seemed, by comparison with his heartbeat, that the chandelier took the same amount of time to swing back and forth, no matter how far it was swinging. When he returned home, he set up two [pendulums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum) of equal length and swung one with a large sweep and the other with a small sweep and found that they kept time together. It was not until the work of [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127), almost one hundred years later, that the [tautochrone](wikipedia://en/171878) nature of a swinging pendulum was used to create an accurate timepiece.[^o39] Up to this point, Galileo had deliberately been kept away from [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), since a physician earned a higher income than a mathematician. However, after accidentally attending a lecture on [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), he talked his reluctant father into letting him study mathematics and [natural philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_philosophy) instead of medicine.[^o39] He created a [thermoscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermoscope), a forerunner of the [thermometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermometer), and, in 1586, published a small book on the design of a [hydrostatic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrostatic) balance he had invented (which first brought him to the attention of the scholarly world). Galileo also studied *disegno*, a term encompassing fine art, and, in 1588, obtained the position of instructor in the [Accademia delle Arti del Disegno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_delle_Arti_del_Disegno) in Florence, teaching perspective and [chiaroscuro](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chiaroscuro). In the same year, upon invitation by the [Florentine Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florentine_Academy), he presented two lectures, *[On the Shape, Location, and Size of Dante's Inferno](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Shape,_Location,_and_Size_of_Dante's_Inferno)*, in an attempt to propose a rigorous cosmological model of [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante)'s *[Divine Comedy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Divine_Comedy)*.[^o40] Being inspired by the artistic tradition of the city and the works of the [Renaissance artists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance_art), Galileo acquired an [aesthetic mentality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aestheticism). While a young teacher at the Accademia, he began a lifelong friendship with the Florentine painter [Cigoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cigoli).[^o41][^o42]

In 1589, he was appointed to the chair of mathematics in Pisa. In 1591, his father died, and he was entrusted with the care of his younger brother [Michelagnolo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelagnolo_Galilei). In 1592, he moved to the [University of Padua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Padua) where he taught geometry, [mechanics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mechanics), and astronomy until 1610.[^n16] During this period, Galileo made significant discoveries in both pure [fundamental science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_science) as well as practical [applied science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Applied_science). His multiple interests included the study of [astrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrology), which at the time was a discipline tied to the studies of mathematics, astronomy and medicine.[^o44][^o45] Additionally, Galileo engaged in practical [hydraulic engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydraulic_engineering), obtaining a patent from the [Venetian Republic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Republic_of_Venice) for a horse-powered [water pump](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water_pumping) in 1594.[^o46]

### Astronomy {#Astronomy}

#### Kepler's supernova {#Kepler's_supernova}

[Tycho Brahe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tycho_Brahe) and others had observed the [supernova of 1572](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supernova_of_1572). Ottavio Brenzoni's letter of 15 January 1605 to Galileo brought the 1572 supernova and the less bright nova of 1601 to Galileo's notice. Galileo observed and discussed [Kepler's Supernova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kepler's_Supernova) in 1604. Since these new stars displayed no detectable [diurnal parallax](wikipedia://en/23253#Diurnal_parallax), Galileo concluded that they were distant stars, and, therefore, disproved the [Aristotelian](wikipedia://en/308) belief in the immutability of the heavens.[^o49]

#### Refracting telescope {#Refracting_telescope}

*Image: Galileo galilei, telescopi del 1609-10 ca..JPG.* "Cannocchiali" [telescopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telescope) at the [Museo Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_Galileo), Florence, suspected to be Galilean telescopes (top: 1610–1630; bottom: 1609–1640)[^o51]

Perhaps based only on descriptions of the first practical telescope which [Hans Lippershey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Lippershey) tried to patent in the Netherlands in 1608,[^n17] Galileo, in the following year, made a telescope with about 3× magnification. He later made improved versions with up to about 30× magnification.[^n18] With a [Galilean telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_telescope), the observer could see magnified, upright images on the Earth—it was what is commonly known as a terrestrial telescope or a spyglass. He could also use it to observe the sky; for a time he was one of those who could construct telescopes good enough for that purpose. On 25 August 1609, he demonstrated one of his early telescopes, with a magnification of about 8× or 9×, to [Venetian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice) lawmakers. Telescopes were also a profitable sideline for Galileo, who sold them to merchants who found them useful both at sea and as items of trade. He published his initial telescopic astronomical observations in March 1610 in a brief [treatise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise) titled *[Sidereus Nuncius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius)* (*Starry Messenger*).[^n19]

#### Moon {#Moon}

*Image: Sidereus, nuncius magna longeqve admirabilia spectacula... Wellcome L0072633.jpg.* An illustration of the Moon from *Sidereus Nuncius*, published in Venice, 1610

On 30 November 1609, Galileo aimed his telescope at the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon).[^n20] While not being the first person to observe the Moon through a telescope (English mathematician [Thomas Harriot](wikipedia://en/780445) had done so four months before but only saw a "strange spottednesse"),[^n21] Galileo was the first to deduce the cause of the uneven waning as light occlusion from lunar mountains and [craters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_crater). In his study, he also made topographical charts, estimating the heights of the mountains. The Moon was not what was long thought to have been a translucent and perfect sphere, as Aristotle claimed, and hardly the first "planet", an "eternal pearl to magnificently ascend into the heavenly empyrian", as put forth by [Dante](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dante). Galileo is sometimes credited with the discovery of the [lunar libration in latitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Libration#Lunar_libration) in 1632,[^o58] although Thomas Harriot or [William Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gilbert_%28astronomer%29) may have done so before.[^o59]

The painter Cigoli, a friend of Galileo, included a realistic depiction of the Moon in one of his paintings; he probably used his own telescope to make the observation.[^o41]

#### Jupiter's moons {#Jupiter's_moons}

On 7 January 1610, Galileo observed with his telescope what he described at the time as "three fixed stars, totally invisible by their smallness", all close to Jupiter, and lying on a straight line through it.[^n22] Observations on subsequent nights showed that the positions of these "stars" relative to Jupiter were changing in a way that would have been inexplicable if they had really been [fixed stars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fixed_stars). On 10 January, Galileo noted that one of them had disappeared, an observation which he attributed to its being hidden behind Jupiter. On 13 January he saw four distinct objects, and on 15 January he concluded that they were [orbiting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit) Jupiter: he had discovered [Jupiter's four largest moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moons_of_Jupiter).[^o62][^n23] This discovery provided evidence in favour of [Copernicus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernicus)'s [heliocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_model).[^o64][^o65] Galileo named the group of four the *Medicean stars*, in honour of his future patron, [Cosimo II de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosimo_II_de'_Medici,_Grand_Duke_of_Tuscany), and Cosimo's three brothers.[^n24] Later astronomers, however, renamed them *[Galilean satellites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_moons)* in honour of their discoverer. These satellites were independently discovered by [Simon Marius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simon_Marius) on 8 January 1610 and are now called [Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_%28moon%29), [Europa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_%28moon%29), [Ganymede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganymede_%28moon%29), and [Callisto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callisto_%28moon%29), the names given by Marius in his *Mundus Iovialis* published in 1614.[^o67]

*Image: Carte de France corrigée par ordre du Roy.jpg.* Map of France presented in 1684, showing the outline of an earlier map (light outline) compared to a new survey conducted using the moons of Jupiter as an accurate timing reference (heavier outline)

Galileo's observations of the satellites of Jupiter caused controversy in astronomy: a planet with smaller planets orbiting it did not conform to the principles of [Aristotelian cosmology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/On_the_Heavens), which held that all heavenly bodies should circle the Earth,[^n25][^n26] and many astronomers and philosophers initially refused to believe that Galileo could have discovered such a thing.[^n27][^n28] Compounding this problem, other astronomers had difficulty confirming Galileo's observations. When he demonstrated the telescope in Bologna, the attendees struggled to see the moons. One of them, [Martin Horky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_Horky), noted that some fixed stars, such as [Spica Virginis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spica), appeared double through the telescope. He took this as evidence that the instrument was deceptive when viewing the heavens, casting doubt on the existence of the moons.[^n29][^n30] [Christopher Clavius](wikipedia://en/633645)'s observatory in Rome confirmed the observations and, although unsure how to interpret them, gave Galileo a hero's welcome when he visited the next year.[^n31] Galileo continued to observe the satellites over the next eighteen months, and by mid-1611, he had obtained remarkably accurate estimates for their periodsa feat which [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) had believed impossible.[^n32][^n33]

Galileo saw a practical use for his discovery. Determining the east–west position of ships at sea required their clocks to be synchronized with clocks at the [prime meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_meridian). Solving this [longitude problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude_problem) had great importance to safe navigation and large prizes were established by Spain and later Holland for its solution. Since eclipses of the moons he discovered were relatively frequent and their times could be predicted with great accuracy, they could be used to set shipboard clocks and Galileo applied for the prizes. Observing the moons from a ship proved too difficult, but the method was used for land surveys, including the remapping of France.[^o77][^o78]

#### Phases of Venus {#Phases_of_Venus}

From September 1610, Galileo observed that [Venus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venus) exhibits [a full set of phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phases_of_Venus) similar to [that of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase). Copernicus's [heliocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heliocentric_model) of the [Solar System](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_System) predicted that all phases would be visible since the orbit of Venus around the [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun) would cause its illuminated hemisphere to face the Earth when it was on the opposite side of the Sun and to face away from the Earth when it was on the Earth-side of the Sun. In [Ptolemy's geocentric model](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric_model#Ptolemaic_model), it was impossible for any of the planets' orbits to intersect the spherical shell carrying the Sun. Traditionally, the orbit of Venus was placed entirely on the near side of the Sun, where it could exhibit only crescent and new phases. It was also possible to place it entirely on the far side of the Sun, where it could exhibit only gibbous and full phases. After Galileo's telescopic observations of the crescent, gibbous and full phases of Venus, the Ptolemaic model became untenable.[^o80] In the early 17th century, as a result of his discovery, the great majority of astronomers converted to one of the various geo-heliocentric planetary models, such as the [Tychonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tychonic_system), [Capellan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martianus_Capella) and Extended Capellan models, each either with or without a daily rotating Earth. These all explained the phases of Venus without the 'refutation' of full heliocentrism's prediction of stellar parallax.[^n34][^n35]

#### Saturn and Neptune {#Saturn_and_Neptune}

In 1610, Galileo also observed [Saturn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Saturn), and at first mistook its rings for planets,[^o84] thinking it was a three-bodied system. When he observed the planet later, Saturn's rings were directly oriented to Earth, causing him to think that two of the bodies had disappeared. The rings reappeared when he observed the planet in 1616, further confusing him.[^o85]

Galileo observed [Neptune](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neptune) in 1612. It appears in his notebooks as one of many unremarkable dim stars. He did not realise that it was a planet, but he did note its motion relative to the stars before losing track of it.[^n36]

#### Sunspots {#Sunspots}

Galileo made naked-eye and telescopic studies of [sunspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sunspot).[^o88] Their existence raised another difficulty with the unchanging perfection of the heavens as posited in orthodox Aristotelian celestial physics. An apparent annual variation in their trajectories, observed by [Francesco Sizzi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Sizzi) and others in 1612–1613,[^n37] also provided a powerful argument against both the Ptolemaic system and the geoheliocentric system of Tycho Brahe. A dispute over claimed priority in the discovery of sunspots, and in their interpretation, led Galileo to a long and bitter feud with the [Jesuit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jesuit) [Christoph Scheiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Scheiner). In the middle was [Mark Welser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Welser), to whom Scheiner had announced his discovery, and who asked Galileo for his opinion. Both of them were unaware of [Johannes Fabricius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johannes_Fabricius)' earlier observation and publication of sunspots.[^n38]

#### Milky Way and stars {#Milky_Way_and_stars}

Galileo observed the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way), previously believed to be [nebulous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nebula), and found it to be a multitude of stars packed so densely that they appeared from Earth to be clouds. He located many other stars too distant to be visible to the naked eye. He observed the double star [Mizar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mizar_%28star%29) in [Ursa Major](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursa_Major) in 1617.[^n39]

In the *Starry Messenger*, Galileo reported that stars appeared as mere blazes of light, essentially unaltered in appearance by the telescope, and contrasted them to planets, which the telescope revealed to be discs. But shortly thereafter, in his *[Letters on Sunspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_on_Sunspots)*, he reported that the telescope revealed the shapes of both stars and planets to be "quite round". From that point forward, he continued to report that telescopes showed the roundness of stars, and that stars seen through the telescope measured a few seconds of arc in diameter.[^n40][^n41] He also devised a method for measuring the apparent size of a star without a telescope. His method was to hang a thin rope in his line of sight to the star and measure the maximum distance from which it would wholly obscure the star. From his measurements of this distance and of the width of the rope, he could calculate the angle subtended by the star at his viewing point.[^n42][^n43][^n44]

In his *Dialogue*, he reported that he had found the apparent diameter of a star of [first magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stellar_magnitude) to be no more than 5 [arcseconds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcsecond), and that of one of sixth magnitude to be about <sup>5</sup>/<sub>6</sub> arcseconds. Like most astronomers of his day, Galileo did not recognise that the apparent sizes of stars that he measured were spurious, caused by diffraction and atmospheric distortion, and did not represent the true sizes of stars. However, Galileo's values were much smaller than previous estimates of the apparent sizes of the brightest stars, such as those made by Brahe, and enabled Galileo to counter anti-Copernican arguments such as those made by Tycho that these stars would have to be absurdly large for their annual [parallaxes](wikipedia://en/202661) to be undetectable.[^n45][^n46][^n47] Other astronomers such as Simon Marius, [Giovanni Battista Riccioli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Battista_Riccioli), and [Martinus Hortensius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martin_van_den_Hove) made similar measurements of stars, and Marius and Riccioli concluded the smaller sizes were not small enough to answer Tycho's argument.[^n48][^n49]

### Theory of tides {#Theory_of_tides}

*Image: Galileo Galilei, 1564-1642 RMG BHC2699.tiff.* Galileo Galilei, portrait by [Francesco Porcia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Porcia)

[Cardinal Bellarmine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_Bellarmine) had written in 1615 that the [Copernican system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Copernican_heliocentrism) could not be defended without "a true physical demonstration that the sun does not circle the earth but the earth circles the sun".[^n50] Galileo considered his theory of the [tides](wikipedia://en/30718) to provide such evidence.[^n51] This theory was so important to him that his *[Dialogo sopra i due massimi sistemi del mondo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dialogo_sopra_i_due_massimi_sistemi_del_mondo)* (*Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, 1632), was originally entitled the *Dialogue on the Ebb and Flow of the Sea*.[^n52] The reference to tides was removed from the title by order of the Inquisition.[^n53]

For Galileo, the tides were caused by the sloshing back and forth of water in the seas as a point on the Earth's surface sped up and slowed down because of the Earth's rotation on its axis and revolution around the Sun. He circulated his first account of the tides in 1616, addressed to [Cardinal Orsini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Orsini_%28cardinal%29).[^n54] His theory gave insight into the importance of the shapes of ocean basins in the size and timing of tides; it accounted, for instance, for the negligible tides halfway along the [Adriatic Sea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adriatic_Sea) compared to those at the ends.[^n55][^n56]

Galileo's theory, however, fails to explain the observed phenomena of tides. It implies only one high tide per day, and in his 1616 account, he claimed that this occurred in the Atlantic.[^n57] He attributed the two daily high tides seen at [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice) and other places to secondary causes, including the shape of the sea, its depth, and other factors.[^n58][^n59][^n60] However, tides occur twice-daily in the Atlantic and most seas. Upon learning this, Galileo put forth his theory in the *Dialogue* without referencing the Atlantic or other locations with once-daily tides, leaving the daily tides question unsolved.[^n61] He also dismissed the idea, [known from antiquity](wikipedia://en/30718#History) and by his contemporary Johannes Kepler, that the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon)[^n62] caused the tides, which is the basis of modern theories.

### Controversy over comets and *The Assayer* {#Controversy_over_comets_and_The_Assayer}

In 1619, Galileo became embroiled in a controversy with Father [Orazio Grassi](wikipedia://en/17093165), professor of mathematics at the Jesuit [Collegio Romano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collegio_Romano). It began as a dispute over the nature of comets, but by the time Galileo had published *[Il Saggiatore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Il_Saggiatore)* (*The Assayer*) in 1623, his last salvo in the dispute, it had become a much wider controversy over the very nature of science itself. The title page of the book describes Galileo as a philosopher and "Matematico Primario" of the Grand Duke of Tuscany.[^o118]

Because *The Assayer* contains such a wealth of Galileo's ideas on how science should be practised, it has been referred to as his scientific manifesto.[^n63][^n64] Early in 1619, Father Grassi had anonymously published a pamphlet, *An Astronomical Disputation on the Three Comets of the Year 1618*,[^n65] which discussed the nature of a comet that had appeared late in November of the previous year. Grassi concluded that the comet was a fiery body that had moved along a segment of a great circle at a constant distance from the earth,[^n66][^n67] and since it moved in the sky more slowly than the Moon, it must be farther away than the Moon.[^n68]

Grassi's arguments and conclusions were criticised in a subsequent article, *[Discourse on Comets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_on_Comets)*,[^n69] published under the name of one of Galileo's disciples, a Florentine lawyer named [Mario Guiducci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Guiducci), although it had been largely written by Galileo himself.[^n70] Galileo and Guiducci offered no definitive theory of their own on the nature of comets,[^n71][^n72] although they did present some tentative conjectures that are now known to be mistaken. (The correct approach to the study of comets had been proposed at the time by Tycho Brahe.) In its opening passage, Galileo and Guiducci's *Discourse* gratuitously insulted the Jesuit [Christoph Scheiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christoph_Scheiner),[^n73][^n74][^n75] and various uncomplimentary remarks about the professors of the [Collegio Romano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_College) were scattered throughout the work.[^n73] The Jesuits were offended,[^n73][^n72] and Grassi soon replied with a [polemical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polemical) tract of his own, *The Astronomical and Philosophical Balance*,[^n76] under the pseudonym Lothario Sarsio Sigensano,[^n77] purporting to be one of his own pupils.[^n78] *The Assayer* was Galileo's devastating reply to the *Astronomical Balance*.[^n69] It has been widely recognized as a masterpiece of polemical literature,[^n79][^n80] in which "Sarsi's" arguments are subjected to withering scorn.[^n81] It was greeted with wide acclaim and particularly pleased the new pope, [Urban VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_VIII), to whom it had been dedicated.[^n82] In Rome, in the previous decade, Barberini, the future Urban VIII, had come down on the side of Galileo and the [Lincean Academy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lincean_Academy).[^o143]

Galileo's dispute with Grassi permanently alienated many Jesuits,[^n83] and Galileo and his friends were convinced that they were responsible for bringing about his later condemnation,[^n84] although supporting evidence for this is not conclusive.[^n85][^n86]

### Controversy over heliocentrism {#Controversy_over_heliocentrism}

*Image: Galileo facing the Roman Inquisition.jpg.* [Cristiano Banti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cristiano_Banti)'s 1857 painting *Galileo facing the [Roman Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Inquisition)*

At the time of Galileo's conflict with the Church, Europe was convulsed by the [Wars of religion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_wars_of_religion) and the [Counter-Reformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counter-Reformation). The majority of educated people subscribed to the [Aristotelian](wikipedia://en/308) [geocentric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geocentric) view that the Earth is the [centre of the Universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_center_of_the_Universe) and the orbits of all heavenly bodies, or Tycho Brahe's new system blending geocentrism with heliocentrism.[^n87][^o150] Galileo's writings on heliocentrism faced both religious and scientific objections. Religious opposition arose from biblical passages implying the fixed nature of the Earth. Scientific opposition came from Brahe, who argued that heliocentrism would imply an annual stellar parallax, though none was observed at the time. Aristarchus and Copernicus had correctly postulated that parallax was negligible because the stars were so distant. However, Brahe countered that since stars [appear to have measurable angular size](wikipedia://en/513128), if the stars were that distant, they would have to be far larger than the Sun or even the orbit of the Earth.[^n88] It would not be until much later that astronomers realized the apparent magnitudes of stars were caused by an optical phenomenon called the [airy disk](wikipedia://en/513128), and were functions of their brightness rather than true physical size (see [the history of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnitude_%28astronomy%29#History)).[^n88]

Galileo defended heliocentrism based on [his astronomical observations of 1609](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereus_Nuncius). In 1611, the same year Galileo's telescopic discoveries were acknowledged by Jesuit members of the Collegio Romano, a commission of cardinals began investigating Galileo, inquiring if he had been involved in the trial of [Cesare Cremonini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cesare_Cremonini_%28philosopher%29), who had taught alongside Galileo at the University of Padua and had been charged for heresy. These inquiries marked the first time Galileo's name was mentioned by the Roman Inquisition.[^o153]

In December 1613, the Grand Duchess [Christina of Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christina_of_Lorraine) confronted one of Galileo's friends and followers, [Benedetto Castelli](wikipedia://en/855653), with biblical objections to the motion of the Earth. Prompted by this incident, Galileo wrote an eight page [letter to Castelli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_to_Benedetto_Castelli) in which he argued that heliocentrism was actually not contrary to biblical texts and that the Bible was an authority on faith and morals, not science. This letter was not published but circulated widely.[^n89] Two years later, Galileo wrote a [letter to Christina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_to_the_Grand_Duchess_Christina) that expanded his arguments to forty pages.[^n90]

*Image: Justus Sustermans - Portrait of Galileo Galilei (Uffizi).jpg.* *Portrait of Galilei* by [Justus Sustermans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Justus_Sustermans), 1635

By 1615, Galileo's writings on heliocentrism had been submitted to the [Roman Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_Inquisition) by Father [Niccolò Lorini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Lorini), who claimed that Galileo and his followers were attempting to reinterpret the Bible, which was seen as a violation of the [Council of Trent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Council_of_Trent) and looked dangerously like [Protestantism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protestantism).[^n91] Lorini specifically cited Galileo's letter to Castelli.[^n92] Galileo went to Rome to defend himself and his ideas. At the start of 1616, [Francesco Ingoli](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Francesco_Ingoli) initiated a debate with Galileo, sending him an essay disputing the Copernican system. Galileo later stated that he believed this essay to have been instrumental in the action against Copernicanism that followed.[^n93] Ingoli may have been commissioned by the Inquisition to write an expert opinion on the controversy, with the essay providing the basis for the Inquisition's actions.[^n94] The essay focused on eighteen physical and mathematical arguments against heliocentrism. It borrowed primarily from Tycho Brahe's arguments, notably that heliocentrism would require the stars as they appeared to be much larger than the Sun. The essay also included four theological arguments, but Ingoli suggested Galileo focus on the physical and mathematical arguments, and he did not mention Galileo's biblical ideas.[^n95]

In February 1616, an Inquisitorial commission declared heliocentrism to be "foolish and absurd in philosophy, and formally heretical since it explicitly contradicts in many places the sense of Holy Scripture". The Inquisition found that the idea of the Earth's movement "receives the same judgement in philosophy and... in regard to theological truth, it is at least erroneous in faith".[^o161] [Pope Paul V](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Paul_V) instructed Cardinal Bellarmine to deliver this finding to Galileo, and to order him to abandon heliocentrism. On 26 February, Galileo was called to Bellarmine's residence and ordered "to abandon completely... the opinion that the sun stands still at the centre of the world and the Earth moves, and henceforth not to hold, teach, or defend it in any way whatever, either orally or in writing."[^n96] The decree of the [Congregation of the Index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congregation_of_the_Index) banned Copernicus's *[De Revolutionibus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Revolutionibus)* and other heliocentric works until correction.[^n96]

For the next decade, Galileo stayed well away from the controversy. He revived his project of writing a book on the subject, encouraged by the election of Cardinal Maffeo [Barberini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barberini) as [Pope Urban VIII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Urban_VIII) in 1623. Barberini was a friend and admirer of Galileo and had opposed the admonition of Galileo in 1616. Galileo's resulting book, *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, was published in 1632, with formal authorization from the Inquisition and papal permission.[^o164]

Earlier, Pope Urban VIII had personally asked Galileo to give arguments for and against heliocentrism in the book and to be careful not to advocate heliocentrism. Whether unknowingly or deliberately, Simplicio, the defender of the Aristotelian geocentric view in *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, was often caught in his own errors and sometimes came across as a fool. Indeed, although Galileo states in the preface of his book that the character is named after a famous Aristotelian philosopher ([Simplicius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplicius_of_Cilicia) in Latin, "Simplicio" in Italian), the name "Simplicio" in Italian also has the connotation of "simpleton".[^n97][^n98]

This portrayal of Simplicio made *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* appear as a polemic against Aristotelian geocentrism in defence of the Copernican theory. Most historians agree Galileo had not intended to satirize and was genuinely surprised by the reaction to his book.

However, the Pope did not take the perceived public disrespect lightly, nor the Copernican advocacy.[^n99] [Dava Sobel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dava_Sobel) argues that prior to Galileo's 1633 trial and judgement for heresy, Pope Urban VIII had been accused of weakness in defending the church, and became preoccupied with court intrigue and problems of state, fearing even for his own life. In this context, Sobel argues that Urban felt betrayed by Galileo's *Dialogues,* and court insiders and enemies of Galileo exploited this sentiment.[^n100] Mario Livio places the Galileo affair in the context of modern science and politics, making a parallel with contemporary science denial.[^o169]

Galileo had alienated his most powerful supporter, the Pope, and was called to Rome to defend his writings[^o170] in September 1632. He finally arrived in February 1633 and was brought before inquisitor [Vincenzo Maculani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Maculani) to be [charged](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Criminal_charge). Throughout his trial, Galileo steadfastly maintained that since 1616 he had faithfully kept his promise not to hold any of the condemned opinions, and initially he denied even defending them. However, he was eventually persuaded to admit that, contrary to his declared intention, a reader of his *Dialogue* could well get the impression that it was a defence of Copernicanism. In view of Galileo's rather implausible denial that he had ever held Copernican ideas after 1616 or ever intended to defend them in the *Dialogue*, his final interrogation, in July 1633, concluded with the threat of [torture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Judicial_torture) if he did not tell the truth, but he maintained his denial despite the threat.[^n101][^n102][^n103]

The sentence of the Inquisition was delivered on 22 June. It was in three essential parts:

- Galileo was found "vehemently suspect of heresy" (though he was never formally charged with heresy, relieving him from corporal punishment),[^o174] for having held the opinions that the Sun lies motionless at the centre of the universe, that the Earth is not at its centre and moves, and that one may hold and defend an opinion as probable after it has been declared contrary to Holy Scripture. He was required to "[abjure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abjure), curse and detest" those opinions.[^n104][^n105][^n106][^n107]
- He was sentenced to formal imprisonment at the pleasure of the Inquisition.[^n108] On the following day, this was commuted to house arrest, under which he remained for the rest of his life.[^o180]
- His offending *Dialogue* was banned; and in an action not announced at the trial, publication of any of his works was forbidden, including any he might write in the future.[^n109][^n110]

*Image: E pur si muove.jpg.* Portrait, originally attributed to Murillo, of Galileo gazing at the words "E pur si muove" (*[And yet it moves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_pur_si_muove!)*; not legible in this image) scratched on the wall of his prison cell. The attribution and narrative surrounding the painting have since been contested.

According to popular legend, after recanting his theory that the Earth moved around the Sun, Galileo muttered the rebellious phrase "[And yet it moves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E_pur_si_muove!)". The earliest known written account of the legend dates to a century after his death. Supporting the legend was a claim that a 1640s painting by the Spanish painter [Bartolomé Esteban Murillo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bartolomé_Esteban_Murillo) or an artist of his school, in which the words were hidden until restoration work in 1911, depicts an imprisoned Galileo apparently gazing at the words "E pur si muove" written on the wall of his dungeon. Based on the painting, [Stillman Drake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stillman_Drake) wrote "there is no doubt now that the famous words were already attributed to Galileo before his death".[^n111] However, an intensive investigation by astrophysicist [Mario Livio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Livio) concludes that the supposed Murillo painting is most probably much more recent, a copy of an 1837 Flemish painting by Roman-Eugene Van Maldeghem.[^o184]

After a period with the friendly [Ascanio Piccolomini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascanio_II_Piccolomini) (Archbishop of [Siena](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siena)), Galileo was allowed to return to his villa at [Arcetri](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arcetri) near Florence in 1634, where he spent part of his life under house arrest. He was ordered to read the [Seven Penitential Psalms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_Penitential_Psalms) once a week for the next three years. However, his daughter Maria Celeste relieved him of the burden after securing [ecclesiastical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ecclesiastical) permission to take it upon herself.[^o185]

While under house arrest, Galileo dedicated his time to one of his finest works, *[Two New Sciences](wikipedia://en/268475)*, a major reason Albert Einstein called Galileo the "father of modern physics".[^o186] Here he summarised work he had done some forty years earlier, on the two sciences now called [kinematics](wikipedia://en/65914) and [strength of materials](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strength_of_materials). It was published in Holland to avoid Catholic censorship. Galileo went completely blind in 1638 and developed a painful [hernia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hernia) and [insomnia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Insomnia), and he was permitted to travel to Florence for medical advice.[^o17]

## Scientific contributions {#Scientific_contributions}

### Scientific methods {#Scientific_methods}

Galileo made original contributions to the science of motion through an innovative combination of experiments and mathematics.[^n112] More typical of science at the time were the qualitative studies of [William Gilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Gilbert_%28astronomer%29), on magnetism and electricity. Galileo's father, [Vincenzo Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Galilei), a [lutenist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lute) and music theorist, had performed experiments establishing perhaps the oldest known non-linear relation in physics: for a stretched string, the pitch varies as the square root of the tension.[^o190] These observations lay within the framework of the [Pythagorean](wikipedia://en/23275#Musical_theories_and_investigations) tradition of music, well known to instrument makers, which included the fact that subdividing a string by a whole number produces a harmonious scale. Thus, a limited amount of mathematics had long related to music and physical science, and young Galileo could see his own father's observations expand on that tradition.[^o191]

Galileo was one of the first modern thinkers to clearly state that the [laws of nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_law) are mathematical. In *[The Assayer](wikipedia://en/7079795#Science,_mathematics,_and_philosophy)*, he wrote "Philosophy is written in this grand book, the universe... It is written in the language of mathematics, and its characters are triangles, circles, and other geometric figures;...."[^n113] His mathematical analyses are a further development of a tradition employed by late [scholastic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scholasticism) natural philosophers, which Galileo learned when he studied philosophy.[^n114] His work marked another step towards the eventual separation of science from both philosophy and religion; a major development in human thought. He was often willing to change his views in accordance with observation.

In order to perform his experiments, Galileo had to set up standards of length and time, so that measurements made on different days and in different laboratories could be compared in a reproducible fashion. This provided a reliable foundation on which to confirm mathematical laws using [inductive reasoning](wikipedia://en/393736). Galileo showed a modern appreciation for the proper relationship between mathematics, theoretical physics, and experimental physics. He understood the [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231), both in terms of [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673) and in terms of the [ordinate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinate) (y) varying as the square of the [abscissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscissa) (x). Galileo further asserted that the parabola was the theoretically ideal [trajectory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trajectory) of a uniformly accelerated projectile in the absence of [air resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_resistance) or other disturbances. He conceded that there are limits to the validity of this theory, noting on theoretical grounds that a projectile trajectory of a size comparable to that of the Earth could not possibly be a parabola,[^n115][^n116][^n117] but he nevertheless maintained that for distances up to the range of the artillery of his day, the deviation of a projectile's trajectory from a parabola would be only very slight.[^n115][^n118][^n119]

### Astronomy {#Astronomy}

*Image: Galileo telescope replica.jpg.* A replica of the earliest surviving telescope attributed to Galileo Galilei, on display at the [Griffith Observatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Griffith_Observatory)

Using his [refracting telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refracting_telescope), Galileo observed in late 1609 that the surface of the Moon is not smooth.[^o41] Early the next year, he observed the four largest moons of Jupiter.[^n24] Later in 1610, he observed the phases of Venus as well as Saturn, though he thought the planet's rings were two other planets.[^o84] In 1612, he observed Neptune and noted its motion, but did not identify it as a planet.[^n36]

Galileo made studies of sunspots,[^o88] the Milky Way, and made various observations about stars, including how to measure their apparent size without a telescope.[^n42][^n43][^n44]

He coined the term [Aurora Borealis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora_Borealis) in 1619 from the Roman goddess of the dawn and the Greek name for the north wind, to describe lights in the northern and southern sky when particles from the solar wind energise the magnetosphere.[^o206]

### Engineering {#Engineering}

*Image: Peter Paul Rubens - Self-Portrait in a Circle of Friends at Mantua.jpg.* *[Self-Portrait in a Circle of Friends from Mantua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-Portrait_in_a_Circle_of_Friends_from_Mantua)* by [Rubens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Paul_Rubens), 1602–1606. Galilei is the third man on the left. The picture depicts the Aurora Borealis in the distance.

Galileo made a number of contributions to [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), as distinct from pure [physics](wikipedia://en/22939). Between 1595 and 1598, Galileo devised and improved a [geometric and military compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sector_%28instrument%29) suitable for use by [gunners](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artillery) and [surveyors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surveying). This expanded on earlier instruments designed by [Niccolò Tartaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Tartaglia) and [Guidobaldo del Monte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Guidobaldo_del_Monte). For gunners, it offered, in addition to a new and safer way of elevating [cannons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon) accurately, a way of quickly computing the charge of [gunpowder](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gunpowder) for [cannonballs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_shot) of different sizes and materials. As a geometric instrument, it enabled the construction of any regular [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621), computation of the area of any polygon or circular sector, and a variety of other calculations. Under Galileo's direction, instrument maker [Marc'Antonio Mazzoleni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc'Antonio_Mazzoleni) produced more than 100 of these compasses, which Galileo sold (along with an instruction manual he wrote) for 50 *lire* and offered a course of instruction in the use of the compasses for 120 *lire*.[^n120]

*Image: Galileo's geometrical and military compass in Putnam Gallery, 2009-11-24.jpg.* Galileo's [geometrical and military compass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sector_%28instrument%29), thought to have been made 1604 by his personal instrument-maker [Marc'Antonio Mazzoleni](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marc'Antonio_Mazzoleni)

In [1593](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_temperature_and_pressure_measurement_technology), Galileo constructed a [thermometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_thermometer), using the expansion and contraction of air in a bulb to move water in an attached tube.[^n121]

In 1609, Galileo was, along with Englishman [Thomas Harriot](wikipedia://en/780445) and others, among the first to use a refracting telescope as an instrument to observe stars, planets or moons. The name "telescope" was coined for Galileo's instrument by a Greek mathematician, [Giovanni Demisiani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Demisiani),[^n122][^n123] at a banquet held in 1611 by Prince [Federico Cesi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Federico_Cesi) to make Galileo a member of his [Accademia dei Lincei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_dei_Lincei).[^o213] In 1610, he used a telescope at close range to magnify the parts of insects.[^n124][^n125] By 1624, Galileo had used a compound [microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microscope). He gave one of these instruments to Cardinal Zollern in May of that year for presentation to the Duke of Bavaria,[^n126] and in September, he sent another to Prince Cesi.[^n127] The [Linceans](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_dei_Lincei) played a role again in naming the "microscope" a year later when fellow academy member [Giovanni Faber](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giovanni_Faber) coined the word for Galileo's invention from the Greek words *μικρόν* (*micron*) meaning "small", and *σκοπεῖν* (*skopein*) meaning "to look at". The word was meant to be analogous with "telescope".[^o218][^o219] Illustrations of insects made using one of Galileo's microscopes and published in 1625, appear to have been the first clear documentation of the [use of a compound microscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timeline_of_microscope_technology).[^n127]

*Image: Galileo Pendulum Clock.jpg.* The earliest known pendulum clock design, conceived by Galileo Galilei

In 1612, having determined the orbital periods of Jupiter's satellites, Galileo proposed that with sufficiently accurate knowledge of their orbits, one could use their positions as a universal clock, and this would make possible the determination of [longitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude). He worked on this problem from time to time during the remainder of his life, but the practical problems were severe. The method was first successfully applied by [Giovanni Domenico Cassini](wikipedia://en/5215428) in 1681 and was later used extensively for large land surveys; this method, for example, was used to survey France, and later by [Zebulon Pike](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zebulon_Pike) of the midwestern United States in 1806. For sea navigation, where delicate telescopic observations were more difficult, the longitude problem eventually required the development of a practical portable [marine chronometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marine_chronometer), such as that of [John Harrison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Harrison).[^o221] Late in his life, when totally blind, Galileo designed an [escapement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Escapement) mechanism for a pendulum clock (called [Galileo's escapement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo's_escapement)), although no clock using this was built until after the first fully operational pendulum clock was made by [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127) in the 1650s.

Galileo was invited on several occasions to advise on engineering schemes to alleviate river flooding. In 1630 Mario Guiducci was probably instrumental in ensuring that he was consulted on a [scheme by Bartolotti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mario_Guiducci#Hydrology_of_the_Bisenzio_River) to cut a new channel for the [Bisenzio River](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bisenzio_%28river%29) near Florence.[^o222]

An issue with simple [ball bearings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ball_bearing) is that the balls rub against each other, causing additional friction. This can be reduced by enclosing each individual ball within a cage. The captured, or caged, ball bearing was originally described by Galileo in the 17th century.[^o223]

### Physics {#Physics}

*Image: Tito Lessi - Galileo and Viviani.jpg.* *Galileo e [Viviani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Viviani)*, by [Tito Lessi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tito_Lessi), 1892

*Image: Pisa.Duomo.dome.Riminaldi01.jpg.* Dome of the [Cathedral of Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Pisa) with the "lamp of Galileo"

Galileo's theoretical and experimental work on the motions of bodies, along with the largely independent work of Kepler and [René Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525), was a precursor of the [classical mechanics](wikipedia://en/19555586) developed by [Sir Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627).

#### Pendulum {#Pendulum}

Galileo conducted several experiments with [pendulums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum). It is popularly believed (thanks to the biography by [Vincenzo Viviani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Viviani)) that these began by watching the swings of the bronze chandelier in the [Cathedral of Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Pisa), using his pulse as a timer. The first recorded interest in pendulums made by Galileo was in his posthumously published notes titled *[On Motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_motu_antiquiora)*,[^o226] but later experiments are described in his *Two New Sciences*. Galileo claimed that a simple pendulum is [isochronous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pendulum#Period_of_oscillation), i.e. that its swings always take the same amount of time, independently of the [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude). In fact, this is only approximately true,[^o227] as was discovered by [Christiaan Huygens](wikipedia://en/42127). Galileo also found that the square of the period varies directly with the length of the pendulum.

#### Sound frequency {#Sound_frequency}

Galileo is lesser known for, yet still credited with, being one of the first to understand sound frequency. By scraping a chisel at different speeds, he linked the pitch of the sound produced to the spacing of the chisel's skips, a measure of frequency.

#### Water pump {#Water_pump}

By the 17th century, water pump designs had improved to the point that they produced measurable vacuums, but this was not immediately understood. What was known was that suction pumps could not pull water beyond a certain height: 18 Florentine yards according to a measurement taken 1635, or about 34 ft.[^o230] This limit was a concern in irrigation projects, mine drainage, and decorative water fountains planned by the Duke of Tuscany, so the duke commissioned Galileo to investigate the problem. In his *Two New Sciences* (1638) Galileo suggested, incorrectly, that the column of water pulled up by a water pump would break of its own weight once reaching beyond 34 feet.[^o230]

#### Speed of light {#Speed_of_light}

In 1638, Galileo described an experimental method to measure the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light#Measurement) by arranging that two observers, each having lanterns equipped with shutters, observe each other's lanterns at some distance. The first observer opens the shutter of his lamp, and, the second, upon seeing the light, immediately opens the shutter of his own lantern. The time between the first observer's opening his shutter and seeing the light from the second observer's lamp indicates the time it takes light to travel back and forth between the two observers. Galileo reported that when he tried this at a distance of less than a mile, he was unable to determine whether or not the light appeared instantaneously.[^o232] Sometime between Galileo's death and 1667, the members of the Florentine *[Accademia del Cimento](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_del_Cimento)* repeated the experiment over a distance of about a mile and obtained a similarly inconclusive result.[^o233] The speed of light has since been determined to be far too fast to be measured by such methods.

#### Galilean invariance {#Galilean_invariance}

Galileo put forward [the basic principle of relativity](wikipedia://en/286788), that the laws of physics are the same in any system that is moving at a constant speed in a straight line, regardless of its particular speed or direction. In *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, Salviati gives the following [thought experiment](wikipedia://en/49535):

> Shut yourself up with some friend in the main cabin below the decks of some ship, and have with you there some flies, butterflies, and other small, flying animals. Have a large bowl of water with some fish in it; hang up a bottle that empties drop by drop into a narrow-mouthed vessel beneath it. With the ship standing still, observe carefully how the little animals fly with equal speed to all sides of the cabin. The fish swim indifferently in all directions; the drops fall into the vessel beneath; and in throwing something to your friend, you need throw it no more strongly in one direction than another, the distances being equal; jumping with your feet together, you pass equal spaces in every direction. When you have observed all these things carefully (though there is no doubt that when the ship is standing still, everything must happen this way), have the ship proceed with any speed you like, so long as the motion is uniform and not fluctuating this way and that. You will discover not the least change in all the effects named, nor could you tell from any of them whether the ship was moving or standing still.[^o235]

This principle provided the basic framework for Newton's laws of motion and is central to Einstein's [special theory of relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_theory_of_relativity).

#### Falling bodies {#Falling_bodies}

That unequal weights would fall with the same speed may have been proposed as early as 60BC by the Roman philosopher [Lucretius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lucretius).[^o237] Observations that similarly sized objects of different weights fall at the same speed are documented in sixth-century works by [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus), of which Galileo was aware.[^n128][^o239] In the 14th century, [Nicole Oresme](wikipedia://en/593659) had derived the time-squared law for uniformly accelerated change,[^n129][^n130] and in the 16th century, [Domingo de Soto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Domingo_de_Soto) had suggested that bodies falling through a homogeneous medium would be uniformly accelerated.[^n131] De Soto, however, did not anticipate many of the qualifications and refinements contained in Galileo's theory of falling bodies. He did not, for instance, recognise, as Galileo did, that a body would fall with a strictly uniform acceleration only in a vacuum, and that it would otherwise eventually reach a uniform terminal velocity.

##### Delft tower experiment {#Delft_tower_experiment}

In 1586, [Simon Stevin](wikipedia://en/72586) (commonly known as Stevinus) and [Jan Cornets de Groot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_Cornets_de_Groot) dropped lead balls from the [Nieuwe Kerk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nieuwe_Kerk_%28Delft%29) in the Dutch city of [Delft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Delft). The experiment established that objects of identical size, but different masses, fall at the same speed.[^o39][^o244] While the Delft tower experiment had been a success, it was not conducted with the same scientific rigour that later experiments were. Stevin was forced to rely on audio feedback (caused by the spheres impacting a wooden platform below) to deduce that the balls had fallen at the same speed. The experiment was given less credence than the more substantive work of Galileo Galilei and his famous Leaning Tower of Pisa thought experiment of 1589.

##### Leaning Tower of Pisa experiment {#Leaning_Tower_of_Pisa_experiment}

*Image: Apollo 15 feather and hammer drop.ogv.* During the [Apollo 15](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apollo_15) mission in 1971, astronaut [David Scott](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Scott) showed that Galileo was right: acceleration is the same for all bodies subject to gravity on the Moon, even for a hammer and a feather.

A biography by Galileo's pupil [Vincenzo Viviani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Viviani) stated that Galileo had [dropped balls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo's_Leaning_Tower_of_Pisa_experiment) of the same material, but different [masses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass), from the [Leaning Tower of Pisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leaning_Tower_of_Pisa) to demonstrate that their time of descent was independent of their mass.[^n132] This was contrary to what Aristotle had taught: that heavy objects fall faster than lighter ones, in direct proportion to weight.[^n133][^n134] While this story has been retold in popular accounts, there is no account by Galileo himself of such an experiment, and it is generally accepted by historians that it was at most a [thought experiment](wikipedia://en/49535) which did not actually take place.[^o249] An exception is Stillman Drake,[^n135] who argues that the experiment did take place, more or less as Viviani described it. However, most of Galileo's experiments with falling bodies were carried out using inclined planes where both the issues of timing and [air resistance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air_resistance) were much reduced.[^o251]

In Galileo’s *Two New Sciences* (1638), the character [Salviati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filippo_Salviati), widely regarded as Galileo's spokesman, held that "In a medium totally devoid of all resistance all bodies would fall with the same speed."[^o252] Salviati also held that this could be experimentally demonstrated by the comparison of pendulum motions in air with bobs of lead and of cork which had different weights but which were otherwise similar.

::: {#o254 .statement cue=named-heading name="Time-squared law"}

##### Time-squared law {#Time-squared_law}

Galileo proposed that a falling body would fall with a uniform acceleration, as long as the resistance of the medium through which it was falling remained negligible, or in the limiting case of its falling through a vacuum.[^n136][^n137] He also derived the correct kinematical law for the distance travelled during a uniform acceleration starting from rest—namely, that it is proportional to the square of the elapsed time (*d*∝*t*<sup>2</sup>).[^n131][^n138] Galileo expressed the time-squared law using geometrical constructions and mathematically precise words, adhering to the standards of the day. (It remained for others to re-express the law in algebraic terms.)

:::

#### Inertia {#Inertia}

Galileo also concluded that objects *retain their velocity* in the absence of any impediments to their motion,[^o260] thereby contradicting the generally accepted Aristotelian hypothesis that a body could only remain in so-called ["violent", "unnatural", or "forced" motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aristotelian_physics#Unnatural_motion) so long as an agent of change (the "mover") continued to act on it.[^n139] Philosophical ideas relating to [inertia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inertia) had been proposed by [John Philoponus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Philoponus) and [Jean Buridan](wikipedia://en/172466). Galileo stated:[^n140][^n141]

But the surface of the earth would be an instance of such a plane if all its unevenness could be removed.[^o264] This was incorporated into [Newton's laws of motion](wikipedia://en/55212) (first law), except for the direction of the motion: Newton's is straight, Galileo's is circular (for example, the planets' motion around the Sun, which according to him, and unlike Newton, takes place in absence of gravity). According to [Dijksterhuis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eduard_Jan_Dijksterhuis) Galileo's conception of inertia as a tendency to persevere in circular motion is closely related to his Copernican conviction.[^o265]

### Mathematics {#Mathematics}

While Galileo's application of mathematics to experimental physics was innovative, his mathematical methods were the standard ones of the day, including dozens of examples of an inverse proportion [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) method passed down from [Fibonacci](wikipedia://en/17949) and [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844). The analysis and proofs relied heavily on the [Eudoxian theory of proportion](wikipedia://en/48388#Mathematics), as set forth in the fifth book of [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s [*Elements*](wikipedia://en/244107). This theory had become available only a century before, thanks to accurate translations by [Tartaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Niccolò_Tartaglia) and others; but by the end of Galileo's life, it was being superseded by the algebraic methods of [Descartes](wikipedia://en/25525). The concept now named [Galileo's paradox](wikipedia://en/339195) was not original with him. His proposed solution, that [infinite numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_number) cannot be compared, is no longer considered useful.[^o267]

## Death {#Death}

*Image: Tomb of Galileo Galilei.JPG.* Tomb of Galileo, [Santa Croce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_di_Santa_Croce_di_Firenze), Florence

Galileo continued to receive visitors until his death on 8 January 1642, aged 77, following a fever and heart palpitations.[^o17][^o269] The Grand Duke of Tuscany, [Ferdinando II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinando_II_de'_Medici,_Grand_Duke_of_Tuscany), wished to bury him in the main body of the [Basilica of Santa Croce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basilica_di_Santa_Croce_di_Firenze), next to the tombs of his father and other ancestors, and to erect a marble mausoleum in his honour.[^n142][^n143]

*Image: Galileo's finger.jpg.* [Galileo's middle finger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo's_middle_finger) from his right hand

These plans were dropped, however, after Pope Urban VIII and his nephew, Cardinal Francesco Barberini, protested,[^n142][^n143][^n144] because Galileo had been condemned by the Catholic Church for "vehement suspicion of heresy".[^o275] He was instead buried in a small room next to the novices' chapel at the end of a corridor from the southern transept of the basilica to the sacristy.[^n142][^n145] He was reburied in the main body of the basilica in 1737 after a monument had been erected there in his honour;[^n146][^n147] during this move, three fingers and a tooth were removed from his remains.[^o280] [One of these fingers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo's_middle_finger) is currently on exhibition at the [Museo Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Museo_Galileo) in Florence, Italy.[^o281]

## Legacy {#Legacy}

### Later Church reassessments {#Later_Church_reassessments}

The Galileo affair was largely forgotten after Galileo's death, and the controversy subsided. The Inquisition's ban on reprinting Galileo's works was lifted in 1718 when permission was granted to publish an edition of his works (excluding the condemned *Dialogue*) in Florence.[^n148] In 1741, [Pope Benedict XIV](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XIV) authorised the publication of an edition of Galileo's complete scientific works[^n149] which included a mildly censored version of the *Dialogue*.[^n150][^n149] In 1758, the general prohibition against works advocating heliocentrism was removed from the [Index of prohibited books](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_Librorum_Prohibitorum). However, the specific ban on uncensored versions of the *Dialogue* and Copernicus's *De Revolutionibus* remained.[^n151][^n149] All traces of official opposition to heliocentrism by the church disappeared in 1835 when these works were finally dropped from the Index.[^n152][^n153]

Interest in the Galileo affair was revived in the early 19th century when Protestant polemicists used it (and other events such as the [Spanish Inquisition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spanish_Inquisition) and the [myth of the flat Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Myth_of_the_flat_Earth)) to attack Roman Catholicism.[^n1] Interest in it has waxed and waned ever since. In 1939, [Pope Pius XII](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Pius_XII), in his first speech to the [Pontifical Academy of Sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pontifical_Academy_of_Sciences), within a few months of his election to the papacy, described Galileo as being among the "most audacious heroes of research... not afraid of the stumbling blocks and the risks on the way, nor fearful of the funereal monuments".[^o293] His close advisor of 40 years, Professor Robert Leiber, wrote: "Pius XII was very careful not to close any doors (to science) prematurely. He was energetic on this point and regretted that in the case of Galileo."[^o294]

On 15 February 1990, in a speech delivered at the [Sapienza University of Rome](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sapienza_University_of_Rome),[^n154][^n155] Cardinal Ratzinger (later [Pope Benedict XVI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_Benedict_XVI)) cited some current views on the Galileo affair as forming what he called "a symptomatic case that permits us to see how deep the self-doubt of the modern age, of science and technology goes today".[^n156] Some of the views he cited were those of the philosopher [Paul Feyerabend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Feyerabend), whom he quoted as saying: "The Church at the time of Galileo kept much more closely to reason than did Galileo himself, and it took into consideration the ethical and social consequences of Galileo's teaching too. Its verdict against Galileo was rational and just and the revision of this verdict can be justified only on the grounds of what is politically opportune."[^n156] The Cardinal did not clearly indicate whether he agreed or disagreed with Feyerabend's assertions. He did, however, say: "It would be foolish to construct an impulsive apologetic on the basis of such views."[^n156]

On 31 October 1992, [Pope John Paul II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pope_John_Paul_II) acknowledged that the Inquisition had erred in condemning Galileo for asserting that the Earth revolves around the Sun. "John Paul said the theologians who condemned Galileo did not recognize the formal distinction between the Bible and its interpretation."[^o300]

In March 2008, the head of the Pontifical Academy of Sciences, [Nicola Cabibbo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicola_Cabibbo), announced a plan to honour Galileo by erecting a statue of him inside the Vatican walls.[^n157] In December of the same year, during events to mark the 400th anniversary of Galileo's earliest telescopic observations, Pope Benedict XVI praised his contributions to astronomy.[^o302] A month later, however, the head of the Pontifical Council for Culture, Gianfranco Ravasi, revealed that the plan to erect a statue of Galileo on the grounds of the Vatican had been suspended.[^n158]

### Impact on modern science {#Impact_on_modern_science}

*Image: Bertini fresco of Galileo Galilei and Doge of Venice.jpg.* Galileo showing the [Doge of Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doge_of_Venice) how to use the telescope (fresco by [Giuseppe Bertini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giuseppe_Bertini), 1858)

According to [Stephen Hawking](wikipedia://en/19376148), Galileo probably bears more of the responsibility for the birth of modern science than anybody else,[^n159] and [Albert Einstein](wikipedia://en/736) called him the father of modern science.[^n160][^o307] In a foreword to *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems*, Einstein wrote: "The [leitmotif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leitmotif) I recognize in Galileo's work is the passionate fight against any kind of dogma based on authority. Only experience and careful reflection are accepted by him as criteria of truth."[^o308]

Author John G. Simmons notes Galileo's place in the history of science:[^o309]But perhaps most significant, Galileo epitomized a new scientific outlook. By his rhetoric, supported by mathematical reasoning, and the force of his personality, Galileo helped to establish the Copernican model of the solar system as a revolution in science. The four large moons of [Jupiter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jupiter) ([Io](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Io_%28moon%29), [Europa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Europa_%28moon%29), [Ganymede](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ganymede_%28moon%29) and [Callisto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Callisto_%28moon%29)) Galileo discovered were christened the [Galilean moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_moons). Other scientific endeavours and principles are named after Galileo including the [Galileo spacecraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_%28spacecraft%29).[^o310]

In 1999, he was named the sixth greatest physicist of all time.[^o311]

### In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

Galileo has featured in literature, notably [Bertolt Brecht](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bertolt_Brecht)'s 1938 play *[Life of Galileo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Life_of_Galileo)*, which also received a [1975 film adaptation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galileo_%281975_film%29).[^o313] In 2009, partly because the year was the fourth centenary of Galileo's first recorded astronomical observations with the telescope, the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations) scheduled it to be the [International Year of Astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Year_of_Astronomy).[^o314]

A Broadway musical titled *Galileo,* based on the life of Galileo Galilei and his conflicts with the Church, will debut at the [Shubert Theatre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shubert_Theatre_%28Broadway%29) in New York City beginning November 10, 2026 starring [Raúl Esparza](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raúl_Esparza) as Galileo Galilei, [Jeremy Kushnier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jeremy_Kushnier) as Cardinal [Maffeo Barberini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maffeo_Barberini), and [Joy Woods](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joy_Woods) as [Virginia Galilei](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maria_Celeste). The production is directed by [Michael Mayer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Mayer_%28director%29), and features songs by Zoe Sarnak and [Michael Weiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Weiner_%28actor%29), and a book by [Danny Strong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Danny_Strong). ## Writings {#Writings}

*Image: Galileo Galilei01.jpg.* Statue outside the [Uffizi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uffizi), [Florence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Florence)

*Image: Statue of Galileo by Pio Fedi.jpg.* Statue of Galileo by [Pio Fedi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pio_Fedi) (1815–1892) inside the Lanyon Building of the [Queen's University Belfast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Queen's_University_Belfast). Sir [William Whitla](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Whitla) (Professor of [Materia Medica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Materia_Medica) 1890–1919) brought the statue back from Italy and donated it to the university.

Galileo's early works describing scientific instruments include the 1586 tract *La Billancetta* (*The Little Balance*) describing an accurate balance to weigh objects in air or water[^o317] and the 1606 manual *Le Operazioni del Compasso Geometrico et Militare* on the operation of a geometrical and military compass.[^o318]

His early works on dynamics, the science of motion and mechanics were his 1590 Pisan *[De Motu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Motu_Antiquiora)* (*On Motion*) and his 1600 Paduan *[Le Mecaniche](wikipedia://en/81302083)* (*Mechanics*). The former was based on Aristotelian–Archimedean fluid dynamics and held that the speed of gravitational fall in a fluid medium was proportional to the excess of a body's specific weight over that of the medium, whereby in a vacuum, bodies would fall at speeds in proportion to their specific weights. It also subscribed to the Philoponan [impetus dynamics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impetus_dynamics) in which impetus is self-dissipating and free-fall in a vacuum would have an essential terminal speed according to specific weight after an initial period of acceleration.[^n161]

Galileo's 1610 *Starry Messenger* was the first scientific treatise to be published based on observations made through a telescope. It reported his observations of:

- the [Galilean moons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galilean_moons)
- the roughness of the Moon's surface
- the existence of a large number of stars invisible to the naked eye, particularly those responsible for the appearance of the [Milky Way](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Milky_Way)
- differences between the appearances of the planets and those of the fixed stars—the former appearing as small discs, while the latter appeared as unmagnified points of light

Galileo published a description of sunspots, *[Letters on Sunspots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letters_on_Sunspots)* (1613), suggesting the Sun and heavens are changable.[^o320] The *Letters on Sunspots* also reported his 1610 telescopic observations of the full set of phases of Venus, and his discovery of the puzzling "appendages" of Saturn and their even more puzzling subsequent disappearance. In 1615, Galileo prepared a manuscript known as the "[Letter to the Grand Duchess Christina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_to_the_Grand_Duchess_Christina)" which was not published in printed form until 1636. This letter was a revised version of the *Letter to Castelli*, which was denounced to the Inquisition by Niccolò Lorini (as discussed previously).[^o321] In 1616, after the order by the Inquisition not to hold or defend the Copernican position, he wrote "[Discorso sul flusso e il reflusso del mare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discourse_on_the_Tides)" ("Discourse on the Tides") based on the Copernican earth, in the form of a private letter to [Cardinal Orsini](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alessandro_Orsini_%28cardinal%29).[^o322] In 1619, Galileo's pupil Mario Guiducci published a lecture written largely by Galileo, *Discorso Delle Comete* (*Discourse on the Comets*), arguing against the Jesuit interpretation of comets.[^o323]

In 1623, Galileo published *The Assayer*, which attacked theories based on Aristotle's authority and promoted experimentation and the mathematical formulation of scientific ideas. The book was highly successful; Pope Urban was "so charmed by it as to have it read aloud to him at table."[^n162] Following the success of *The Assayer*, Galileo published the *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* in 1632. Despite taking care to adhere to the Inquisition's 1616 instructions, the claims in the book favouring Copernican theory and a non-geocentric model of the solar system led to Galileo being tried and banned from publication. Despite the publication ban, Galileo published his *Two New Sciences* in 1638 in [Holland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/House_of_Elzevir), outside the jurisdiction of the Inquisition.[^n163]

[Steven Weinberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Steven_Weinberg) writes that "Galileo not only disobeyed the orders of the Roman Inquisition when he argued in his *Dialogo* that it is the Sun and not the Earth that is at rest, he wrote the *Dialogo* in Italian rather than in the Latin of scholars, using little mathematics, so that it could be read and understood by any literate Italian. His countrymen were not unappreciative; by the time the church had suppressed the book, it had sold out."[^o326] [Ian McEwan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ian_McEwan) included *Two New Sciences* in his canon of [science writing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_writing).[^o327]

### Personal library {#Personal_library}

In the last years of his life, Galileo Galilei kept a library of at least 598 volumes (560 of which have been identified) at [Villa Il Gioiello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Villa_Il_Gioiello), on the outskirts of Florence.[^o332] Under the restrictions of house arrest, he was forbidden to write or publish his ideas. However, he continued to receive visitors right up to his death and it was through them that he remained supplied with the latest scientific texts from Northern Europe.[^o333]

Galileo's will does not refer to his collection of books and manuscripts. An itemized inventory was only later produced after Galileo's death, when the majority of his possessions including his library passed to his son, Vincenzo Galilei Jr. On his death in 1649, the collection was inherited by his wife Sestilia Bocchineri.[^o333]

Galileo's books, personal papers and unedited manuscripts were then collected by [Vincenzo Viviani](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vincenzo_Viviani), his former assistant and student, with the intent of preserving his old teacher's works in published form. It was a project that never materialised and in his final will, Viviani bequeathed a significant portion of the collection to the [Hospital of Santa Maria Nuova](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hospital_of_Santa_Maria_Nuova) in Florence, where there already existed an extensive library. The value of Galileo's possessions was not realised, and duplicate copies were dispersed to other libraries, such as the [Biblioteca Comunale degli Intronati](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Biblioteca_Comunale_degli_Intronati), the public library in Sienna. In a later attempt to specialise the library's holdings, volumes unrelated to medicine were transferred to the Biblioteca Magliabechiana, an early foundation for what was to become the Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Firenze, the [National Central Library](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Central_Library_%28Florence%29) in Florence.[^o333]

A small portion of Viviani's collection, including the manuscripts of Galileo and those of his peers [Evangelista Torricelli](wikipedia://en/210413) and [Benedetto Castelli](wikipedia://en/855653), was left to his nephew, Abbot Jacopo Panzanini. This minor collection was preserved until Panzanini's death when it passed to his great-nephews, Carlo and Angelo Panzanini. The books from both Galileo and Viviani's collections began to disperse as the heirs failed to protect their inheritance. Their servants sold several of the volumes for waste paper. Around 1750 the Florentine senator Giovanni Battista Clemente de'Nelli heard of this and purchased the books and manuscripts from the shopkeepers, and the remainder of Viviani's collection from the Panzanini brothers. As recounted in Nelli's memoirs: "My great fortune in obtaining such a wonderful treasure so cheaply came about through the ignorance of the people selling it, who were not aware of the value of those manuscripts."

The library remained in Nelli's care until his death in 1793. Knowing the value of their father's collected manuscripts, Nelli's sons attempted to sell what was left to them to the French government. [Ferdinand III, Grand Duke of Tuscany](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferdinand_III,_Grand_Duke_of_Tuscany) intervened in the sale and purchased the entire collection. The archive of manuscripts, printed books and personal papers was deposited with the [Biblioteca Palatina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Central_Library_%28Florence%29) in Florence, merging the collection with the Biblioteca Magliabechiana in 1861.[^o333]
