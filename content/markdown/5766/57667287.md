# Dirichlet hyperbola method

*Image: Dirichlet hyperbola example\_4.svg.* An example of the Dirichlet hyperbola method with $n = 10,$ $a \approx 2.7,$ and $b \approx 3.7.$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Dirichlet hyperbola method"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **Dirichlet hyperbola method** is a technique to evaluate the sum

:::

$$F(n) = \sum_{k=1}^n f(k).$$

The first step is to find a pair of functions $g$ and $h$ such that, using [Dirichlet convolution](wikipedia://en/179017), we have $f = g \ast h$; the sum then becomes

$$F(n) = \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{xy=k}^{} g(x) h(y),$$

where the inner sum runs over all ordered pairs $(x,y)$ of positive [integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers) such that $xy = k$. In the [Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane), these pairs lie on a [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052), and when the double sum is fully expanded, there is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between the terms of the sum and the [lattice points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lattice_points) in the first quadrant on the hyperbolas of the form $xy = k$, where $k$ runs over the integers $1 \le k \le n$: for each such point $(x,y)$, the sum contains a term $g(x)h(y)$, and vice versa.

Let $a$ be a real number, not necessarily an integer, such that $1 < a < n$, and let $b = n/a$. Then the lattice points can be split into three overlapping regions: one region is bounded by $1 \le x \le a$ and $1 \le y \le n/x$, another region is bounded by $1 \le y \le b$ and $1 \le x \le n/y$, and the third is bounded by $1 \le x \le a$ and $1 \le y \le b$. In the diagram, the first region is the [union](wikipedia://en/47949) of the blue and red regions, the second region is the union of the red and green, and the third region is the red. Note that this third region is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of the first two regions. By the [principle of inclusion and exclusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principle_of_inclusion_and_exclusion), the full sum is therefore the sum over the first region, plus the sum over the second region, minus the sum over the third region. This yields the formula[^o2]

::: {#o3 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\begin{aligned} F(n) &= \sum_{k=1}^n f(k) \\ &= \sum_{k=1}^n \sum_{xy=k}^{} g(x)h(y) \\ &= \sum_{x=1}^a \sum_{y=1}^{n/x} g(x)h(y) + \sum_{y=1}^b \sum_{x=1}^{n/y} g(x)h(y) - \sum_{x=1}^a \sum_{y=1}^b g(x)h(y) \text{.}\end{aligned} \tag{1}$$

:::

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Let $\sigma_{0}(n)$ be the [divisor-counting function](wikipedia://en/407249), and let $D(n)$ be [its summatory function](wikipedia://en/5943744):

$$D(n) = \sum_{k=1}^n \sigma_0(k).$$

Computing $D(n)$ naïvely requires [factoring](wikipedia://en/15491) every integer in the interval $[1, n]$; an improvement can be made by using a modified [Sieve of Eratosthenes](wikipedia://en/73415), but this still requires $\text{Õ}(n)$ [time](wikipedia://en/44578#Extensions_to_the_Bachmann–Landau_notations). Since $\sigma_{0}$ admits the Dirichlet convolution $\sigma_{0} = 1 \ast 1$, taking $a = b = \sqrt{n}$ in ((1)) yields the formula

$$D(n) = \sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{y=1}^{n/x} 1 \cdot 1 + \sum_{y=1}^{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{x=1}^{n/y} 1 \cdot 1 - \sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{n}} \sum_{y=1}^{\sqrt{n}} 1 \cdot 1,$$

which simplifies to

$$D(n) = 2 \cdot \sum_{x=1}^{\sqrt{n}} \left\lfloor \frac{n}{x} \right\rfloor - \left\lfloor \sqrt{n} \right\rfloor^2,$$

which can be evaluated in $O(\sqrt{n})$ operations.

The method also has theoretical applications: for example, [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002) introduced the technique in 1849 to obtain the estimate[^o6][^o7]

$$D(n) = n \log n + (2\gamma - 1)n + O(\sqrt{n}),$$

where $\gamma$ is the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant).
