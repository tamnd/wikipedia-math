# Mathematical diagram

*Image: Euclid Lueneburg ms page 8.jpg.* [Euclid's Elements](wikipedia://en/244107), ms. from Lüneburg, A.D. 1200

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematical diagram"}

**Mathematical diagrams**, such as [charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chart) and [graphs](wikipedia://en/87352), are mainly designed to convey [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) relationships—for example, comparisons over time.[^o2]

:::

## Types {#Types}

### Argand diagram {#Argand_diagram}

*Image: Complex number illustration.svg.* Argand diagram.

A [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) can be visually represented as a pair of numbers forming a vector on a diagram called an [Argand diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argand_diagram). The [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) is sometimes called the *Argand plane* because it is used in *Argand diagrams*. These are named after [Jean-Robert Argand](wikipedia://en/543727) (1768–1822), although they were first described by Norwegian-Danish land surveyor and mathematician [Caspar Wessel](wikipedia://en/21418268) (1745–1818).[^o5] Argand diagrams are frequently used to plot the positions of the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) and [zeroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_function) of a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_function) in the complex plane.

The concept of the complex plane allows a [geometric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric) interpretation of complex numbers. Under [addition](wikipedia://en/61338), they add like [vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_%28spatial%29). The [multiplication](wikipedia://en/20845) of two complex numbers can be expressed most easily in [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) — the magnitude or *modulus* of the product is the product of the two [absolute values](wikipedia://en/991), or moduli, and the angle or *argument* of the product is the sum of the two angles, or arguments. In particular, multiplication by a complex number of modulus 1 acts as a rotation.

### Butterfly diagram {#Butterfly_diagram}

*Image: Butterfly-FFT.png.* Butterfly diagram

In the context of [fast Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/11512) algorithms, a [butterfly](wikipedia://en/2707212) is a portion of the computation that combines the results of smaller [discrete Fourier transforms](wikipedia://en/8811) (DFTs) into a larger DFT, or vice versa (breaking a larger DFT up into subtransforms). The name "butterfly" comes from the shape of the data-flow diagram in the radix-2 case, as described below. The same structure can also be found in the [Viterbi algorithm](wikipedia://en/228015), used for finding the most likely sequence of hidden states.

The [butterfly diagram](wikipedia://en/2707212) show a data-flow diagram connecting the inputs *x* (left) to the outputs *y* that depend on them (right) for a "butterfly" step of a radix-2 [Cooley–Tukey FFT algorithm](wikipedia://en/352702). This diagram resembles a [butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Butterfly) as in the [Morpho butterfly](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morpho_%28genus%29) shown for comparison, hence the name.

### Commutative diagram {#Commutative_diagram}

*Image: 5 lemma.svg.* A commutative diagram depicting the [five lemma](wikipedia://en/138214)

In mathematics, and especially in [category theory](wikipedia://en/5869), a commutative diagram is a diagram of [objects](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Object_%28category_theory%29), also known as vertices, and [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891), also known as arrows or edges, such that when selecting two objects any directed path through the diagram leads to the same result by composition.

Commutative diagrams play the role in category theory that equations play in algebra.

### Hasse diagrams {#Hasse_diagrams}

*Image: Hasse diagram of powerset of 3.svg.* Hasse diagram.

A [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) is a simple picture of a finite [partially ordered set](wikipedia://en/23572), forming a [drawing](wikipedia://en/325813) of the partial order's [transitive reduction](wikipedia://en/3757117). Concretely, one represents each element of the set as a vertex on the page and draws a line segment or curve that goes upward from *x* to *y* precisely when *x* \< *y* and there is no *z* such that *x* \< *z* \< *y*. In this case, we say y [covers](wikipedia://en/14835049) x, or y is an immediate successor of x. In a Hasse diagram, it is required that the curves be drawn so that each meets exactly two vertices: its two endpoints. Any such diagram (given that the vertices are labeled) uniquely determines a partial order, and any partial order has a unique transitive reduction, but there are many possible placements of elements in the plane, resulting in different Hasse diagrams for a given order that may have widely varying appearances.

### Knot diagrams {#Knot_diagrams}

*Image: TrefoilKnot 01.svg.* Knot diagram.

In [Knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008) a useful way to visualise and manipulate knots is to project the knot onto a plane — to imagine the knot casting a shadow on the wall. A small perturbation in the choice of projection will ensure that it is [one-to-one](wikipedia://en/45196) except at the double points, called *crossings*, where the "shadow" of the knot crosses itself once transversely.[^o10]

At each crossing we must indicate which section is "over" and which is "under", so as to be able to recreate the original knot. This is often done by creating a break in the strand going underneath. If by following the diagram the knot alternately crosses itself "over" and "under", then the diagram represents a particularly well-studied class of knot, [alternating knots](wikipedia://en/1236143).

### Venn diagram {#Venn_diagram}

*Image: Venn diagram cmyk.svg.* Venn diagram.

A [Venn diagram](wikipedia://en/61701) is a representation of mathematical sets: a mathematical diagram representing sets as circles, with their relationships to each other expressed through their overlapping positions, so that all possible relationships between the sets are shown.[^o12]

The Venn diagram is constructed with a collection of simple closed curves drawn in the plane. The principle of these diagrams is that classes be represented by regions in such relation to one another that all the possible logical relations of these classes can be indicated in the same diagram. That is, the diagram initially leaves room for any possible relation of the classes, and the actual or given relation, can then be specified by indicating that some particular region is null or is not null.[^o13]

### Voronoi diagram {#Voronoi_diagram}

*Image: Voronoi centerlines skeleton.gif.* Voronoi centerlines.

A [Voronoi diagram](wikipedia://en/177668) is a special kind of decomposition of a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) determined by distances to a specified discrete set of objects in the space, e.g., by a [discrete set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_set) of points. This diagram is named after [Georgy Voronoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgy_Voronoi), also called a Voronoi [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671), a Voronoi decomposition, or a Dirichlet tessellation after [Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002).

In the simplest case, we are given a set of points S in the plane, which are the Voronoi sites. Each site s has a Voronoi cell V(s) consisting of all points closer to s than to any other site. The segments of the Voronoi diagram are all the points in the plane that are equidistant to two sites. The Voronoi nodes are the points equidistant to three (or more) sites.

### Wallpaper group diagrams {#Wallpaper_group_diagrams}

*Image: Wallpaper group diagram p4m square.svg.* Wallpaper group diagram.

A [wallpaper group](wikipedia://en/386214) or *plane symmetry group* or *plane crystallographic group* is a mathematical classification of a two-dimensional repetitive pattern, based on the symmetries in the pattern. Such patterns occur frequently in architecture and decorative art. There are 17 possible distinct [groups](wikipedia://en/19447).

Wallpaper groups are two-dimensional [symmetry groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symmetry_groups), intermediate in complexity between the simpler [frieze groups](wikipedia://en/375503) and the three-dimensional [crystallographic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_group), also called [space groups](wikipedia://en/463721). Wallpaper groups categorize patterns by their symmetries. Subtle differences may place similar patterns in different groups, while patterns which are very different in style, color, scale or orientation may belong to the same group.

### Young diagram {#Young_diagram}

A *Young diagram* or [Young tableau](wikipedia://en/683368), also called [Ferrers diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferrers_diagram), is a finite collection of boxes, or cells, arranged in left-justified rows, with the row sizes weakly decreasing (each row has the same or shorter length than its predecessor).

*Image: Young diagram for 541 partition.svg.* Young diagram.

Listing the number of boxes in each row gives a [partition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_%28number_theory%29) $\lambda$ (lambda) of a positive integer *n*, the total number of boxes of the diagram. The Young diagram is said to be of shape $\lambda$, and it carries the same information as that partition. Listing the number of boxes in each column gives another partition, the **conjugate** or *transpose* partition of $\lambda$; one obtains a Young diagram of that shape by reflecting the original diagram along its main diagonal.

Young tableaux were introduced by [Alfred Young](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alfred_Young_%28mathematician%29), a [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) at [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Cambridge), in 1900. They were then applied to the study of symmetric group by [Georg Frobenius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georg_Frobenius) in 1903. Their theory was further developed by many mathematicians.
