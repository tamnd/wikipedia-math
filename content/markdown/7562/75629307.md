# Supersolvable lattice

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Supersolvable lattice"}

In mathematics, a **supersolvable lattice** is a [graded](wikipedia://en/2514970) [lattice](wikipedia://en/469304) that has a maximal [chain](wikipedia://en/30330#Chains) of elements, each of which obeys a certain modularity relationship. The definition encapsulates many of the nice properties of [lattices of subgroups](wikipedia://en/3983172) of [supersolvable groups](wikipedia://en/4966866).

:::

## Motivation {#Motivation}

A finite [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $G$ is said to be *supersolvable* if it admits a maximal [chain (or *series*) of subgroups](wikipedia://en/1545790) so that each subgroup in the chain is normal in $G$. A normal subgroup has been known since the 1940s to be left and (dual) right [modular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_pair) as an element of the lattice of subgroups.[^o3] [Richard Stanley](wikipedia://en/1633397) noticed in the 1970s that certain [geometric lattices](wikipedia://en/17243191), such as the [partition lattice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partition_lattice), obeyed similar properties, and gave a lattice-theoretic abstraction.[^o4][^o16]

::: {#o6 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A finite graded lattice $L$ is **supersolvable** if it admits a maximal chain $\mathbf{m}$ of elements (called an **M-chain** or **chief chain**) obeying any of the following equivalent properties.

1. For any chain $\mathbf{c}$ of elements, the smallest sublattice of $L$ containing all the elements of $\mathbf{m}$ and $\mathbf{c}$ is distributive.[^o7] This is the original condition of Stanley.[^o4]
1. Every element of $\mathbf{m}$ is [left modular](wikipedia://en/1089311#Modular_pairs_and_related_notions). That is, for each $m$ in $\mathbf{m}$ and each $x \leq y$ in $L$, we have $(x\vee m)\wedge y=x\vee(m\wedge y).$[^o8][^o9]
1. Every element of $\mathbf{m}$ is rank modular, in the following sense: if $\rho$ is the rank function of $L$, then for each $m$ in $\mathbf{m}$ and each $x$ in $L$, we have $\rho(m\wedge x)+\rho(m\vee x)=\rho(m)+\rho(x).$[^o10][^o11]

For comparison, a finite lattice is geometric if and only if it is [atomistic](wikipedia://en/998824) and the elements of the [antichain](wikipedia://en/348973) of atoms are all left modular.[^o12]

An extension of the definition is that of a **left modular lattice**: a not-necessarily graded lattice with a maximal chain consisting of left modular elements. Thus, a left modular lattice requires the condition of (2), but relaxes the requirement of gradedness.[^o13]

:::

::: {#o15 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Noncrossing partitions 4; Hasse.svg.* [Hasse diagram](wikipedia://en/338199) of the [noncrossing partition](wikipedia://en/695026) lattice on a 4 element set. The leftmost maximal chain is a chief chain.

A group is supersolvable if and only if its lattice of subgroups is supersolvable. A [chief series](wikipedia://en/12505519) of subgroups forms a chief chain in the lattice of subgroups.[^o16]

The partition lattice of a finite set is supersolvable. A partition is left modular in this lattice if and only if it has at most one non-[singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) part;[^o16] thus, the partition lattice is also geometric.[^o17] The [noncrossing partition](wikipedia://en/695026) lattice is similarly supersolvable,[^o18] although it is not geometric.[^o19]

The lattice of flats of the [graphic matroid](wikipedia://en/745714) for a [graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) is supersolvable if and only if the graph is [chordal](wikipedia://en/744165). Working from the top, the chief chain is obtained by removing vertices in a [perfect elimination ordering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_elimination_ordering) one by one.[^o20]

Every [modular lattice](wikipedia://en/1089311) is supersolvable, as every element in such a lattice is left modular and rank modular.[^o16]

:::

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

A finite [matroid](wikipedia://en/244321) with a supersolvable lattice of flats (equivalently, a lattice that is both geometric and supersolvable) has a real-rooted [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/244321#Polynomial_invariants).[^o23][^o24] This is a consequence of a more general factorization theorem for characteristic polynomials over modular elements.[^o25]

The [Orlik-Solomon algebra](wikipedia://en/864438#The_Orlik–Solomon_algebra) of an [arrangement of hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/864438) with a supersolvable intersection lattice is a [Koszul algebra](wikipedia://en/7987653).[^o26] For more information, see [Supersolvable arrangement](wikipedia://en/21620407).

Any finite supersolvable lattice has an [edge lexicographic labeling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_lexicographic_labeling) (or EL-labeling), hence its [order complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order_complex) is [shellable](wikipedia://en/32457161) and [Cohen-Macaulay](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cohen-Macaulay). Indeed, supersolvable lattices can be characterized in terms of edge lexicographic labelings: a finite lattice of height $n$ is supersolvable if and only if it has an edge lexicographic labeling that assigns to each maximal chain a permutation of $\{ 1, \dots, n\}.$[^o27]

:::
