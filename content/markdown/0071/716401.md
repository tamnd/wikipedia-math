::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Cross-polytope"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **cross-polytope**,[^n1] **hyperoctahedron**, **orthoplex**,[^o6] **staurotope**,[^o7] or **cocube** is a [regular](wikipedia://en/518693), [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885) that exists in *n*-[dimensional Euclidean space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensions). A 2-dimensional cross-polytope is a square, a 3-dimensional cross-polytope is a regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635), and a 4-dimensional cross-polytope is a [16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422). Its facets are [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) of the previous dimension, while the cross-polytope's [vertex figure](wikipedia://en/1005719) is another cross-polytope from the previous dimension.

:::

The vertices of a cross-polytope can be chosen as the unit vectors pointing along each co-ordinate axis – i.e. all the permutations of (±1, 0, 0, ..., 0). The cross-polytope is the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of its vertices. The *n*-dimensional cross-polytope can also be defined as the closed [unit ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_ball) (or, according to some authors, its boundary) in the [ℓ<sub>1</sub>-norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/L1-norm) on **R**<sup>*n*</sup>, those points *x* = (*x*<sub>1</sub>, *x*<sub>2</sub>..., *x*<sub>*n*</sub>) satisfying

$$|x_1| + |x_2| + \cdots + |x_n| \le 1.$$

An *n*-orthoplex can be constructed as a [bipyramid](wikipedia://en/4153#Other_dimensions) with an (*n*−1)-orthoplex base.

The cross-polytope is the [dual polytope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dual_polytope) of the [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783). The [vertex-edge graph](wikipedia://en/74718534) of an *n*-dimensional cross-polytope is the [Turán graph](wikipedia://en/360581) *T*(2*n*, *n*) (also known as a *cocktail party graph* ).

## Low-dimensional examples {#Low-dimensional_examples}

In 1 dimension the cross-polytope is a [line segment](wikipedia://en/22634860), which can be chosen as the [interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_interval) \[−1, +1\].

In 2 dimensions the cross-polytope is a [square](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_%28geometry%29). If the vertices are chosen as {(±1, 0), (0, ±1)}, the square's sides are at right angles to the axes; in this orientation a square is often called a *diamond*.

In 3 dimensions the cross-polytope is a [regular octahedron](wikipedia://en/22458)—one of the five convex regular [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) known as the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905).

The 4-dimensional cross-polytope also goes by the name **hexadecachoron** or **[16-cell](wikipedia://en/716422)**. It is one of the six [convex regular 4-polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_regular_4-polytope). These [4-polytopes](wikipedia://en/24979) were first described by the Swiss mathematician [Ludwig Schläfli](wikipedia://en/1452408) in the mid-19th century. The vertices of the 4-dimensional hypercube, or [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112), can be divided into two sets of eight, the convex hull of each set forming a cross-polytope. Moreover, the polytope known as the [24-cell](wikipedia://en/293485) can be constructed by symmetrically arranging three cross-polytopes.[^o10]

## *n* dimensions {#n_dimensions}

The cross-polytope family is one of three [regular polytope](wikipedia://en/518693) families, labeled by [Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter) as *β<sub>n</sub>*, the other two being the [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) family, labeled as *γ<sub>n</sub>*, and the [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781) family, labeled as *α<sub>n</sub>*. A fourth family, the [infinite tessellations of hypercubes](wikipedia://en/12237982), he labeled as *δ<sub>n</sub>*.[^n2]

The *n*-dimensional cross-polytope has 2*n* vertices, and 2<sup>*n*</sup> facets ((*n* − 1)-dimensional components) all of which are (*n* − 1)-[simplices](wikipedia://en/39781). The [vertex figures](wikipedia://en/1005719) are all (*n* − 1)-cross-polytopes. The [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) of the cross-polytope is {3,3,...,3,4}.

The [dihedral angle](wikipedia://en/338046#Geometry) of the *n*-dimensional cross-polytope is $\delta_n = \arccos\left(\frac{2-n}{n}\right)$. This gives: δ<sub>2</sub> = arccos(0/2) = 90°, δ<sub>3</sub> = arccos(−1/3) = 109.47°, δ<sub>4</sub> = arccos(−2/4) = 120°, δ<sub>5</sub> = arccos(−3/5) = 126.87°, ... δ<sub>∞</sub> = arccos(−1) = 180°.

The hypervolume of the *n*-dimensional cross-polytope is

$$\frac{2^n}{n!}.$$

For each pair of non-opposite vertices, there is an edge joining them. More generally, each set of *k* + 1 orthogonal vertices corresponds to a distinct *k*-dimensional component which contains them. The number of *k*-dimensional components (vertices, edges, faces, ..., facets) in an *n*-dimensional cross-polytope is thus given by (see [binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/4668)):

$2^{k+1}{n \choose {k+1}}$[^n3]

The extended [f-vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/F-vector) for an *n*-orthoplex can be computed by (**1**,2)<sup>*n*</sup>, like the coefficients of [polynomial products](wikipedia://en/23000#Multiplication). For example a 16-cell is (**1**,2)<sup>4</sup> = (**1**,4,4)<sup>2</sup> = (**1**,8,24,32,16).

There are many possible [orthographic projections](wikipedia://en/203052) that can show the cross-polytopes as 2-dimensional graphs. [Petrie polygon](wikipedia://en/18571869) projections map the points into a regular 2*n*-gon or lower order regular polygons. A second projection takes the 2(*n*−1)-gon petrie polygon of the lower dimension, seen as a [bipyramid](wikipedia://en/4153), projected down the axis, with 2 vertices mapped into the center.

The vertices of an axis-aligned cross polytope are all at equal distance from each other in the [Manhattan distance](wikipedia://en/408354) ([L<sup>1</sup> norm](wikipedia://en/45194)). [Kusner's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kusner's_conjecture) states that this set of 2*d* points is the largest possible [equidistant set](wikipedia://en/48517304) for this distance.[^o22]

## Generalized orthoplex {#Generalized_orthoplex}

Regular [complex polytopes](wikipedia://en/8581934#Regular_complex_polytopes) can be defined in [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) called *generalized orthoplexes* (or cross polytopes), β*p* = <sub>2</sub>{3}<sub>2</sub>{3}...<sub>2</sub>{4}<sub>*p*</sub>, or ... Real solutions exist with *p* = 2, i.e. β2 = β<sub>*n*</sub> = <sub>2</sub>{3}<sub>2</sub>{3}...<sub>2</sub>{4}<sub>2</sub> = {3,3,..,4}. For *p* > 2, they exist in $\mathbb{\mathbb{C}}^n$. A *p*-generalized *n*-orthoplex has *pn* vertices. *Generalized orthoplexes* have regular [simplexes](wikipedia://en/39781) (real) as [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647).[^o25] Generalized orthoplexes make [complete multipartite graphs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_multipartite_graph), β*p* make K<sub>*p*,*p*</sub> for [complete bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/525320), β*p* make K<sub>*p*,*p*,*p*</sub> for complete tripartite graphs β*p* creates K<sub>*p*<sup>*n*</sup></sub> or [Turán graphs](wikipedia://en/360581) $T(np,n)$. An [orthogonal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection) can be defined that maps all the vertices equally-spaced on a circle, with all pairs of vertices connected, except multiples of *n*. The [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) perimeter in these orthogonal projections is called a [petrie polygon](wikipedia://en/18571869).

## Related polytope families {#Related_polytope_families}

Cross-polytopes can be combined with their dual cubes to form compound polytopes:

- In two dimensions, we obtain the [octagrammic](wikipedia://en/2667613) star figure $\{\tfrac{8}{2}\}$,
- In three dimensions we obtain the [compound of cube and octahedron](wikipedia://en/8010874),
- In four dimensions we obtain the compound of tesseract and 16-cell.
