# Operator theory

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Operator theory"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **operator theory** is the study of [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) on [function spaces](wikipedia://en/340630), beginning with [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886) and [integral operators](wikipedia://en/2649421). The operators may be presented abstractly by their characteristics, such as [bounded linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) or [closed operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_operator), and consideration may be given to [nonlinear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonlinear_operator). The study, which depends heavily on the [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) of function spaces, is a branch of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180).

:::

If a collection of operators forms an [algebra over a field](wikipedia://en/191788), then it is an [operator algebra](wikipedia://en/455987). The description of operator algebras is part of operator theory.

## Single operator theory {#Single_operator_theory}

Single operator theory deals with the properties and classification of operators, considered one at a time. For example, the classification of [normal operators](wikipedia://en/190167) in terms of their [spectra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_of_an_operator) falls into this category.

### Spectrum of operators {#Spectrum_of_operators}

The **spectral theorem** is any of a number of results about [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) or about [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859).[^o4] In broad terms the spectral [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977) provides conditions under which an [operator](wikipedia://en/22213) or a matrix can be [diagonalized](wikipedia://en/246325) (that is, represented as a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) in some basis). This concept of diagonalization is relatively straightforward for operators on [finite-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite-dimensional) spaces, but requires some modification for operators on infinite-dimensional spaces. In general, the spectral theorem identifies a class of [linear operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) that can be modelled by [multiplication operators](wikipedia://en/976642), which are as simple as one can hope to find. In more abstract language, the spectral theorem is a statement about [commutative](wikipedia://en/61346) [C\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/7184). See also [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713) for a historical perspective.

Examples of operators to which the spectral theorem applies are [self-adjoint operators](wikipedia://en/187408) or more generally [normal operators](wikipedia://en/190167) on [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932).

The spectral theorem also provides a [canonical](wikipedia://en/515096) decomposition, called the **spectral decomposition**, **eigenvalue decomposition**, or **[eigendecomposition](wikipedia://en/13576645)**, of the underlying vector space on which the operator acts.

#### Normal operators {#Normal_operators}

A **normal operator** on a [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [Hilbert space](wikipedia://en/20598932) $H$ is a [continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) $N\colon H \rightarrow H$ that [commutes](wikipedia://en/7193) with its [hermitian adjoint](wikipedia://en/667175) $N^{\ast}$, that is: $NN^{\ast} = N^{\ast}N$.[^o6]

Normal operators are important because the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) holds for them. Today, the class of normal operators is well understood. Examples of normal operators are

- [unitary operators](wikipedia://en/225999): $U^{\ast} = U^{-1}$
- [Hermitian operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_operator) (i.e., selfadjoint operators): $N^{\ast} = N$; (also, anti-selfadjoint operators: $N^{\ast} = -N$)
- [positive operators](wikipedia://en/454936): $N = M^{\ast}M$, where $M$ is any operator
- [normal matrices](wikipedia://en/170366) can be seen as normal operators if one takes the Hilbert space to be $\mathbb{C}^{n}$.

The spectral theorem extends to a more general class of matrices. Let $A$ be an operator on a finite-dimensional [inner product space](wikipedia://en/14856). $A$ is said to be [normal](wikipedia://en/170366) if $A^{\ast}A = AA^{\ast}$. One can show that $A$ is normal if and only if it is unitarily diagonalizable: By the [Schur decomposition](wikipedia://en/372514), we have $A = UTU^{\ast}$, where $U$ is unitary and $T$ [upper triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular). Since $A$ is normal, $T^{\ast}T = TT^{\ast}$. Therefore, $T$ must be diagonal since normal upper triangular matrices are diagonal. The converse is obvious.

In other words, $A$ is normal if and only if there exists a [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222) $U$ such that

$$A = U D U^*$$

where $D$ is a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080). Then, the entries of the diagonal of $D$ are the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of $A$. The column vectors of $U$ are the [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of $A$ and they are [orthonormal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthonormal). Unlike the Hermitian case, the entries of $D$ need not be real.

### Polar decomposition {#Polar_decomposition}

The **polar decomposition** of any [bounded linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_operator) *A* between complex [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) is a canonical factorization as the product of a [partial isometry](wikipedia://en/1348079) and a non-negative operator.[^o8]

The polar decomposition for matrices generalizes as follows: if *A* is a bounded linear operator then there is a unique factorization of *A* as a product *A* = *UP* where *U* is a partial isometry, *P* is a non-negative self-adjoint operator and the initial space of *U* is the closure of the range of *P*.

The operator *U* must be weakened to a partial isometry, rather than unitary, because of the following issues. If *A* is the [one-sided shift](wikipedia://en/467384) on *l*${}^{2}$(**N**), then |*A*| = (*A\*A*)<sup>1/2</sup> = *I*. So if *A* = *U* |*A*|, *U* must be *A*, which is not unitary.

The existence of a polar decomposition is a consequence of [Douglas' lemma](wikipedia://en/21607408):

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=math-theorem name="Lemma"}

**Lemma.**

If *A*, *B* are bounded operators on a Hilbert space *H*, and *A\*A* ≤ *B\*B*, then there exists a contraction *C* such that *A* = *CB*. Furthermore, *C* is unique if *Ker*(*B\**) ⊂ *Ker*(*C*).

:::

The operator *C* can be defined by $C(Bh) = Ah$, extended by continuity to the closure of *Ran*(*B*), and by zero on the orthogonal complement of $\operatorname{Ran}(B)$. The operator *C* is well-defined since $A*A \le B*B$ implies $\operatorname{Ker}(B) \subset \operatorname{Ker}(A)$. The lemma then follows.

In particular, if $A*A = B*B$, then *C* is a partial isometry, which is unique if $\operatorname{Ker}(B*) \subset \operatorname{Ker}(C).$ In general, for any bounded operator *A*,

$$A^*A = (A^*A)^{\frac{1}{2}} (A^*A)^{\frac{1}{2}},$$

where (*A\*A*)<sup>1/2</sup> is the unique positive square root of *A\*A* given by the usual [functional calculus](wikipedia://en/965348). So by the lemma, we have

$$A = U (A^*A)^{\frac{1}{2}}$$

for some partial isometry *U*, which is unique if Ker(*A*) ⊂ Ker(*U*). (Note $\operatorname{Ker}(A) = \operatorname{Ker}(A*A) = \operatorname{Ker}(B) = \operatorname{Ker}(B*)$, where $B = B* = (A*A)^{1/2}$.) Take *P* to be (*A\*A*)<sup>1/2</sup> and one obtains the polar decomposition *A* = *UP*. Notice that an analogous argument can be used to show *A = P'U'*, where *P'* is positive and *U'* a partial isometry.

When *H* is finite dimensional, *U* can be extended to a unitary operator; this is not true in general (see example above). Alternatively, the polar decomposition can be shown using the operator version of [singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207#Bounded_operators_on_Hilbert_spaces).

By property of the [continuous functional calculus](wikipedia://en/784808), |*A*| is in the [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184) generated by *A*. A similar but weaker statement holds for the partial isometry: the polar part *U* is in the [von Neumann algebra](wikipedia://en/294185) generated by *A*. If *A* is invertible, *U* will be in the [C\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/7184) generated by *A* as well.

### Connection with complex analysis {#Connection_with_complex_analysis}

Many operators that are studied are operators on Hilbert spaces of [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110), and the study of the operator is intimately linked to questions in function theory. For example, [Beurling's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beurling's_theorem) describes the [invariant subspaces](wikipedia://en/585826) of the unilateral shift in terms of inner functions, which are bounded holomorphic functions on the unit disk with unimodular boundary values almost everywhere on the circle. Beurling interpreted the unilateral shift as multiplication by the independent variable on the [Hardy space](wikipedia://en/314780).[^o11] The success in studying multiplication operators, and more generally [Toeplitz operators](wikipedia://en/14598730) (which are multiplication, followed by projection onto the Hardy space) has inspired the study of similar questions on other spaces, such as the [Bergman space](wikipedia://en/3205927).

## Operator algebras {#Operator_algebras}

The theory of [operator algebras](wikipedia://en/455987) brings [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788) of operators such as [C\*-algebras](wikipedia://en/7184) to the fore.

### C\*-algebras {#C*-algebras}

A C\*-algebra, *A*, is a [Banach algebra](wikipedia://en/4665) over the field of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), together with a [map](wikipedia://en/516931) $* : A \to A$. One writes *x\** for the image of an element *x* of *A*. The map \* has the following properties:[^o14]

- It is an [involution](wikipedia://en/22679231), for every *x* in *A* $$x^{**} = (x^*)^* = x$$
- For all *x*, *y* in *A*: $$(x + y)^* = x^* + y^*$$ $$(x y)^* = y^* x^*$$
- For every λ in **C** and every *x* in *A*: $$(\lambda x)^* = \overline{\lambda} x^* .$$
- For all *x* in *A*: $$\|x^* x \| = \left\|x\right\| \left\|x^*\right\|.$$

::: {#o15 .remark cue=inline-head name="Remark"}

**Remark.** The first three identities say that *A* is a [\*-algebra](wikipedia://en/293426). The last identity is called the **C\* identity** and is equivalent to:

$$\|xx^*\| = \|x\|^2,$$

The C\*-identity is a very strong requirement. For instance, together with the [spectral radius formula](wikipedia://en/386169), it implies that the C\*-norm is uniquely determined by the algebraic structure:

$$\|x\|^2 = \|x^* x\| = \sup\{|\lambda| : x^* x - \lambda \,1 \text{ is not invertible} \}.$$

:::
