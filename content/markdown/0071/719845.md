# Trapezohedron

::: {#o4 .subject cue=lead name="Trapezohedron"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), an **$n$-gonal** **trapezohedron**, **$n$-trapezohedron**, **$n$-antidipyramid**, **$n$-antibipyramid**, **$n$-deltohedron, or $n$**-**antitegum,**[^o5][^o6] is the [dual polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8815) of an $n$-gonal [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851). The $\mathbf{2}n$ faces of an $n$-trapezohedron are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) and symmetrically staggered; they are called *twisted kites*. With a higher symmetry, its $2n$ faces are [*kites*](wikipedia://en/84840) (sometimes also called *deltoids*).[^n1] They are topologically related to the [scalenohedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalenohedron), which are half-symmetry variants with [irregular trigons.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Scalene_triangle)

:::

As there is no consistent analog in higher dimensions to the 3D [antiprisms](wikipedia://en/1851), there is similarly no general generalization of 3D trapezohedra either. The "$n$-gonal" part of the name does not refer to faces here, but to two arrangements of each $n$ [vertices](wikipedia://en/11308417) around an axis of $n$-fold symmetry. The dual $n$-gonal [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851) has two actual $n$-gon faces. An $n$-gonal trapezohedron can be [dissected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissection_%28geometry%29) into two equal $n$-gonal [pyramids](wikipedia://en/3307045) and an $n$-gonal [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851).

::: {#o9 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Terminology"}

## Terminology {#Terminology}

These figures, sometimes called delt**o**hedra,[^o5] are not to be confused with [deltahedra](wikipedia://en/493995),[^o6] whose faces are equilateral triangles.

*Twisted* *trigonal*, *tetragonal*, and *hexagonal trapezohedra* (with six, eight, and twelve *twisted* [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) kite faces) exist as crystals; in [crystallography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallography) (describing the [crystal habits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystal_habit) of [minerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mineral)), they are just called *trigonal*, *tetragonal*, and *hexagonal trapezohedra*. They have no plane of symmetry, and no center of inversion symmetry;[^n2][^n3] but they have a [center of symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Center_of_symmetry): the intersection point of their symmetry axes. The trigonal trapezohedron has one 3-fold symmetry axis, perpendicular to three 2-fold symmetry axes.[^n2] The tetragonal trapezohedron has one 4-fold symmetry axis, perpendicular to four 2-fold symmetry axes of two kinds. The hexagonal trapezohedron has one 6-fold symmetry axis, perpendicular to six 2-fold symmetry axes of two kinds.[^n4]

[Crystal arrangements](wikipedia://en/456410) of atoms can repeat in space with trigonal and hexagonal trapezohedron cells.[^o14]

Also in crystallography, the word *trapezohedron* is often used for the polyhedron with 24 [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) non-twisted kite faces properly known as a *[deltoidal icositetrahedron](wikipedia://en/723339)*,[^n5] which has eighteen order-4 vertices and eight order-3 vertices. This is not to be confused with the *dodecagonal trapezohedron*, which also has 24 congruent kite faces, but two order-12 apices (i.e. poles) and two rings of twelve order-3 vertices each.

Still in crystallography, the *deltoid dodecahedron*[^n6] has 12 congruent non-twisted kite faces, six order-4 vertices and eight order-3 vertices (the *[rhombic dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/607631)* is a special case). This is not to be confused with the *[hexagonal trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/3504442)*, which also has 12 congruent kite faces,[^n4] but two order-6 apices (i.e. poles) and two rings of six order-3 vertices each.

:::

## Forms {#Forms}

An **$n$-trapezohedron** is defined by a [regular zig-zag skew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon) $\mathbf{2}n$-gon base, two symmetric [apices](wikipedia://en/6022079) with no [degree of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622) right above and right below the base, and [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) faces connecting each pair of [adjacent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacent_side_%28polygon%29) basal edges to one apex.

An $n$-trapezohedron has two apical vertices on its polar axis, and $2n$ basal vertices in two regular $n$-gonal rings. It has $\mathbf{2}n$ [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [kite](wikipedia://en/84840) faces, and it is [isohedral](wikipedia://en/6286120).

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Special cases"}

### Special cases {#Special_cases}

- $n = 2$. A degenerate trapezohedron: a geometric figure with 6 vertices, 8 edges, and 4 degenerate [kite](wikipedia://en/84840) faces that are visually identical to triangles. As such, the trapezohedron itself is visually identical to the regular [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606). Its dual is a degenerate form of [antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851) that also resembles the regular tetrahedron.
- $n = 3$. The dual of a *triangular antiprism*: the kites are rhombi (or squares); hence these trapezohedra are also [zonohedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zonohedra). They are called **[rhombohedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rhombohedra)**. They are [cubes](wikipedia://en/6285) scaled in the direction of a body diagonal. They are also the [parallelepipeds](wikipedia://en/23975) with congruent rhombic faces. *Image: Gyroelongated triangular bipyramid.png.* A $60^{\circ}$ rhombohedron, [dissected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dissection_%28geometry%29) into a central regular octahedron and two regular tetrahedra
  - A special case of a rhombohedron is one in which the rhombi forming the faces have angles of $60^{\circ}$ and $120^{\circ}$. It can be decomposed into two equal regular tetrahedra and a regular [octahedron](wikipedia://en/80177635). Since parallelepipeds can [fill space](wikipedia://en/321671), so can a [combination of regular tetrahedra and regular octahedra](wikipedia://en/3735260).
- $n = 5$. The [pentagonal trapezohedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pentagonal_trapezohedron) is the only polyhedron other than the [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) commonly used as a [die](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice) in [roleplaying games](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roleplaying_games) such as *[Dungeons & Dragons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dungeons_&_Dragons)*. Being [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) and [face-transitive](wikipedia://en/6286120), it makes [fair dice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fair_dice). Having 10 sides, it can be used in repetition to generate any decimal-based [uniform probability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28discrete%29) desired. Typically, two dice of different colors are used for the two [digits](wikipedia://en/280582) to represent numbers from $00$ to $99$.

:::

## Symmetry {#Symmetry}

The [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of an $n$-gonal trapezohedron is $D_{nd} = D_{nv}$, of order $4n$, except in the case of $n = 3$: a cube has the larger symmetry group $O_{d}$ of order $48 = 4\times(4\times3)$, which has four versions of $D_{3d}$ as subgroups.

The [rotation group](wikipedia://en/2694525#Rotation_groups) of an $n$-trapezohedron is $D_{n}$, of order $2n$, except in the case of $n = 3$: a cube has the larger rotation group $O$ of order $24 = 4\times(2\times3)$, which has four versions of $D_{3}$ as subgroups.

Note: Every $n$-trapezohedron with a [regular zig-zag skew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon) $\mathbf{2}n$-gon base and $2n$ congruent non-twisted kite faces has the same (dihedral) symmetry group as the [dual-uniform](wikipedia://en/51069784) $n$-trapezohedron, for $n \ge 4$.

One degree of freedom within symmetry from $D_{nd}$ (order $4n$) to $D_{n}$ (order $2n$) changes the congruent kites into congruent quadrilaterals with three edge lengths, called *twisted kites*, and the $n$-trapezohedron is called a *twisted trapezohedron*. (In the limit, one edge of each quadrilateral goes to zero length, and the $n$-trapezohedron becomes an $n$-[bipyramid](wikipedia://en/4153).)

If the kites surrounding the two peaks are not twisted but are of two different shapes, the $n$-trapezohedron can only have $C_{nv}$ (cyclic with vertical mirrors) symmetry, order $2n$, and is called an *unequal* or *asymmetric trapezohedron*. Its dual is an *unequal $n$-[antiprism](wikipedia://en/1851)*, with the top and bottom $n$-gons of different radii.

If the kites are twisted and are of two different shapes, the $n$-trapezohedron can only have $C_{n}$ (cyclic) symmetry, order $n$, and is called an *unequal twisted trapezohedron*.

## Star trapezohedron {#Star_trapezohedron}

A **star $p/q$-trapezohedron** (where $2 \le q < p$) is defined by a [regular zig-zag skew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_skew_polygon) [star $2p/q$-gon](wikipedia://en/313303) base, two symmetric [apices](wikipedia://en/6022079) with no [degree of freedom](wikipedia://en/176622) right above and right below the base, and [quadrilateral](wikipedia://en/25278) faces connecting each pair of [adjacent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Adjacent_side_%28polygon%29) basal edges to one apex.

A star $p/q$-trapezohedron has two apical vertices on its polar axis, and $2p$ basal vertices in two regular $p$-gonal rings. It has $2p$ [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) [kite](wikipedia://en/84840) faces, and it is [isohedral](wikipedia://en/6286120).

Such a star $p/q$-trapezohedron is a *self-intersecting*, *crossed*, or *non-convex* form. It exists for any regular zig-zag skew star $2p/q$-gon base (where $2 \le q < p$).

But if $\tfrac{p}{q} < \tfrac{3}{2}$, then $(p - q)\tfrac{360^{\circ}}{p} < \tfrac{q}{2}\tfrac{360^{\circ}}{p}$, so the dual star antiprism (of the star trapezohedron) cannot be uniform (i.e. cannot have equal edge lengths); and if $\tfrac{p}{q} = \tfrac{3}{2}$, then $(p - q)\tfrac{360^{\circ}}{p} = \tfrac{q}{2}\tfrac{360^{\circ}}{p}$, so the dual star antiprism must be flat, thus degenerate, to be uniform.

A [dual-uniform](wikipedia://en/51069784) star $p/q$-trapezohedron has [Coxeter-Dynkin diagram](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter-Dynkin_diagram) .
