# Wagstaff prime

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wagstaff prime"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **Wagstaff prime** is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) of the form

:::

$${{2^p+1}\over 3}$$

where *p* is an [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) prime. Wagstaff primes are named after the mathematician [Samuel S. Wagstaff Jr.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Samuel_S._Wagstaff_Jr.); the [prime pages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_pages) credit François Morain for naming them in a lecture at the Eurocrypt 1990 conference. Wagstaff primes appear in the [New Mersenne conjecture](wikipedia://en/8478563#New_Mersenne_conjecture) and have applications in [cryptography](wikipedia://en/18934432).

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

The first three Wagstaff primes are 3, 11, and 43 because

$$\begin{aligned} 3 & = {2^3+1 \over 3}, \\[5pt] 11 & = {2^5+1 \over 3}, \\[5pt] 43 & = {2^7+1 \over 3}. \end{aligned}$$

:::

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

It is natural to consider[^o9] more generally numbers of the form

$$Q(b,n)=\frac{b^n+1}{b+1}$$

where the base $b \ge 2$. Since for $n$ odd we have

$$\frac{b^n+1}{b+1}=\frac{(-b)^n-1}{(-b)-1}=R_n(-b)$$

these numbers are called "Wagstaff numbers base $b$", and sometimes considered[^o10] a case of the [repunit](wikipedia://en/215056) numbers with negative base $-b$.

For some specific values of $b$, all $Q(b,n)$ (with a possible exception for very small $n$) are [composite](wikipedia://en/82289) because of an "algebraic" factorization. Specifically, if $b$ has the form of a [perfect power](wikipedia://en/4023591) with odd exponent (like 8, 27, 32, 64, 125, 128, 216, 243, 343, 512, 729, 1000, etc. ), then the fact that $x^m+1$, with $m$ odd, is divisible by $x+1$ shows that $Q(a^m, n)$ is divisible by $a^n+1$ in these special cases. Another case is $b=4k^4$, with *k* a positive integer (like 4, 64, 324, 1024, 2500, 5184, etc. ), where we have the [aurifeuillean factorization](wikipedia://en/23683488).

However, when $b$ does not admit an algebraic factorization, it is [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) that an infinite number of $n$ values make $Q(b,n)$ prime, notice all $n$ are odd primes.

For $b=10$, the primes themselves have the following appearance: 9091, 909091, 909090909090909091, 909090909090909090909090909091, … , and these *n*s are: 5, 7, 19, 31, 53, 67, 293, 641, 2137, 3011, 268207, ... .

See Repunit#Repunit primes for the list of the generalized Wagstaff primes base $b$. (Generalized Wagstaff primes base $b$ are generalized repunit primes base $-b$ with odd $n$)

The least primes *p* such that $Q(n, p)$ is prime are (starts with *n* = 2, 0 if no such *p* exists)
