# Wind wave

*Image: Porto Covo Outubro 2014-3.jpg.* A man standing next to large ocean waves at [Porto Covo, Portugal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porto_Covo)

*Image: Large waves from Hurricane Marie at the Wedge, Newport Beach, California Video Don Ramey Logan.ogv.* Video of large waves from [Hurricane Marie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hurricane_Marie_%282014%29) along the coast of [Newport Beach](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Newport_Beach,_California), [California](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/California)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wind wave"}

In [fluid dynamics](wikipedia://en/11034), a **wind wave**, or **wind-generated water wave**, is a [surface wave](wikipedia://en/41763) that occurs on the [free surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) of [bodies of water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_of_water) as a result of the [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind) blowing over the water's surface. The contact distance in the [direction of the wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_direction) is known as the *[fetch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_fetch)*. Waves in the oceans can travel thousands of kilometers before reaching land. Wind waves on Earth range in size from small [ripples](wikipedia://en/669713) to waves over 100 ft high, being limited by wind speed, duration, fetch, and water depth.[^o2]

:::

When directly generated and affected by local wind, a wind wave system is called a **wind sea**. Wind waves will travel in a [great circle route](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_circle_route) after being generated – curving slightly left in the southern hemisphere and slightly right in the northern hemisphere. After moving out of the area of fetch and no longer being affected by the local wind, wind waves are called *[swells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swell_%28ocean%29)* and can travel thousands of kilometers. A noteworthy example of this is waves generated south of Tasmania during heavy winds that will travel across the Pacific to southern California, producing desirable surfing conditions.[^o3] Wind waves in the ocean are also called **ocean surface waves** and are mainly *[gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_waves)*, where [gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity) is the main equilibrium force.

Wind waves have a certain amount of [randomness](wikipedia://en/19196523): subsequent waves differ in height, duration, and shape with limited predictability. They can be described as a [stochastic process](wikipedia://en/47895), in combination with the physics governing their generation, growth, propagation, and decay – as well as governing the interdependence between flow quantities such as the [water surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_surface) movements, [flow velocities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flow_velocity), and water [pressure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pressure). The key [statistics](wikipedia://en/26703) of wind waves (both seas and swells) in evolving [sea states](wikipedia://en/1212631) can be predicted with [wind wave models](wikipedia://en/20260077).

Although waves are usually considered in the water seas of Earth, the hydrocarbon seas of [Titan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Titan_%28moon%29) may also have wind-driven waves.[^o4][^o5][^o6] Waves in bodies of water may also be generated by other causes, both at the surface and underwater (such as [watercraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Watercraft), [animals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Animal), [waterfalls](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waterfall), [landslides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landslide), [earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake), [bubbles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bubble_%28physics%29), and [impact events](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Impact_event)).

## Formation {#Formation}

*Image: Wea00810.jpg.* [NOAA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NOAA) ship *Delaware II* in bad weather on [Georges Bank](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georges_Bank)

The great majority of large breakers seen at a beach result from distant winds. Five factors influence the formation of the flow structures in wind waves:[^o9]

1. [Wind speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_speed) or strength relative to wave speed – the wind must be moving faster than the wave crest for energy transfer to the wave.
1. The uninterrupted distance of open water over which the wind blows without significant change in direction (called the *[fetch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetch_%28geography%29)*)
1. Width of the area affected by fetch (at a right angle to the distance)
1. Wind duration – the time for which the wind has blown over the water.
1. Water depth

All of these factors work together to determine the size of the water waves and the structure of the flow within them.

The main dimensions associated with [wave propagation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_propagation) are:

- [Wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_height) (vertical distance from trough to [crest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crest_%28physics%29))
- [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) (distance from crest to crest in the direction of propagation)
- [Wave period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_period) (time interval between arrival of consecutive crests at a stationary point)
- Wave direction or [azimuth](wikipedia://en/47487) (predominantly driven by [wind direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind_direction))

A fully developed sea has the maximum wave size theoretically possible for a wind of specific strength, duration, and fetch. Further exposure to that specific wind could only cause a dissipation of energy due to the breaking of wave tops and formation of "whitecaps". Waves in a given area typically have a range of heights. For weather reporting and for scientific analysis of wind wave statistics, their characteristic height over a period of time is usually expressed as *[significant wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_wave_height)*. This figure represents an [average](wikipedia://en/60167) height of the highest one-third of the waves in a given time period (usually chosen somewhere in the range from 20 minutes to twelve hours), or in a specific wave or storm system. The significant wave height is also the value a "trained observer" (e.g. from a ship's crew) would estimate from visual observation of a sea state. Given the variability of wave height, the largest individual waves are likely to be somewhat less than twice the reported significant wave height for a particular day or storm.[^o10]

Wave formation on an initially flat water surface by wind is started by a random distribution of normal pressure of turbulent wind flow over the water. This pressure fluctuation produces normal and tangential stresses in the surface water, which generates waves. It is usually assumed for the purpose of theoretical analysis that:[^o11]

1. The water is originally at rest.
1. The water is not viscous.
1. The water is [irrotational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irrotational).
1. There is a random distribution of normal pressure to the water surface from the turbulent wind.
1. Correlations between air and water motions are neglected.

The second mechanism involves wind shear forces on the water surface. [John W. Miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Miles) suggested a surface wave generation mechanism that is initiated by turbulent wind shear flows based on the inviscid [Orr–Sommerfeld equation](wikipedia://en/10106425) in 1957. He found the energy transfer from the wind to the water surface is proportional to the curvature of the velocity profile of the wind at the point where the mean wind speed is equal to the wave speed. Since the wind speed profile is logarithmic to the water surface, the curvature has a negative sign at this point. This relation shows the wind flow transferring its kinetic energy to the water surface at their interface.

Assumptions:

1. two-dimensional parallel shear flow
1. incompressible, inviscid water and wind
1. irrotational water
1. slope of the displacement of the water surface is small[^o12]

Generally, these wave formation mechanisms occur together on the water surface and eventually produce fully developed waves.

For example,[^o13] if we assume a flat sea surface (Beaufort state 0), and a sudden wind flow blows steadily across the sea surface, the physical wave generation process follows the sequence:

1. Turbulent wind forms random pressure fluctuations at the sea surface. Ripples with wavelengths in the order of a few centimeters are generated by the pressure fluctuations. (The [Phillips](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Owen_Martin_Phillips) mechanism[^o11])
1. The winds keep acting on the initially rippled sea surface causing the waves to become larger. As the waves grow, the pressure differences get larger causing the growth rate to increase. Finally, the shear instability expedites the wave growth exponentially. (The Miles mechanism[^o11])
1. The interactions between the waves on the surface generate longer waves[^o14] and the interaction will transfer wave energy from the shorter waves generated by the Miles mechanism to the waves which have slightly lower frequencies than the frequency at the peak wave magnitudes, then finally the waves will be faster than the crosswind speed (Pierson & Moskowitz[^o15]).

## Types {#Types}

*Image: Porto Covo pano April 2009-4.jpg.* Surf on a rocky irregular bottom. [Porto Covo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Porto_Covo), west coast of Portugal

Three different types of wind waves develop over time:

- [Capillary waves](wikipedia://en/669713), or ripples, dominated by surface tension effects.
- [Gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_waves), dominated by gravitational and inertial forces.
  - Seas, raised locally by the wind.
- [Swells](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swell_%28ocean%29), which have traveled away from where they were raised by the wind, and have to a greater or lesser extent dispersed.

Ripples appear on smooth water (i.e. glassy[^o18]) when the wind blows, but will die quickly if the wind stops. The restoring force that allows them to propagate is [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension). Sea waves are larger-scale, often irregular motions that form under sustained winds. These waves tend to last much longer, even after the wind has died, and the restoring force that allows them to propagate is gravity. As waves propagate away from their area of origin, they naturally separate into groups of common direction and wavelength. The sets of waves formed in this manner are known as swells. The [Pacific Ocean](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pacific_Ocean) is 19,800 km from [Indonesia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indonesia) to the coast of [Colombia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Colombia) and, based on an average wavelength of \76.5 m, would have ~258,824 swells over that width.

It is sometimes alleged that out of a set of waves, the seventh wave in a set is always the largest; while this isn't the case, the waves in the middle of a given set tend to be larger than those before and after them.[^o19]

Individual "[rogue waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rogue_wave)" (also called "freak waves", "monster waves", "killer waves", or "king waves") much higher than the other waves in the [sea state](wikipedia://en/1212631) can occur. In the case of the [Draupner wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Draupner_wave), its 25 m height was 2.2 times the [significant wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Significant_wave_height). Such waves are distinct from [tides](wikipedia://en/30718), caused by the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) and [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun)'s [gravitational pull](wikipedia://en/30719), [tsunamis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tsunami) that are caused by underwater [earthquakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earthquake) or [landslides](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landslide), and waves generated by [underwater explosions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underwater_explosion) or the fall of [meteorites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteorite)—all having far longer [wavelengths](wikipedia://en/33125) than wind waves.

The largest ever recorded wind waves are not rogue waves, but standard waves in extreme sea states. For example, \29.1 m high waves were recorded aboard the [RRS *Discovery*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RRS_Discovery_%281962%29), in a sea with \18.5 m significant wave height, so the highest wave was only 1.6 times the significant wave height.[^o20] The biggest recorded by a buoy (as of 2011) was \32.3 m high during the [2007 typhoon Krosa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2007_Pacific_typhoon_season#Typhoon_Krosa_%28Ineng%29) near Taiwan.[^o21]

## Spectrum {#Spectrum}

*Image: Munk ICCE 1950 Fig1.svg.* Classification of the [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum) of ocean waves according to wave [period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_%28physics%29)[^o23]

Ocean waves can be classified based on: the disturbing force that creates them; the extent to which the disturbing force continues to influence them after formation; the extent to which the restoring force weakens or flattens them; and their wavelength or period. Seismic sea waves have a period of about 20 minutes, and speeds of 760 km/h. Wind waves (deep-water waves) have a period up to about 20 seconds.

The speed of all ocean waves is controlled by gravity, wavelength, and water depth. Most characteristics of ocean waves depend on the relationship between their wavelength and water depth. Wavelength determines the size of the orbits of water molecules within a wave, but water depth determines the shape of the orbits. The paths of water molecules in a wind wave are circular only when the wave is traveling in deep water. A wave cannot "feel" the bottom when it moves through water deeper than half its wavelength because too little wave energy is contained in the water movement below that depth. Waves moving through water deeper than half their wavelength are known as deep-water waves. On the other hand, the orbits of water molecules in waves moving through shallow water are flattened by the proximity of the sea bottom surface. Waves in water shallower than 1/20 their original wavelength are known as shallow-water waves. Transitional waves travel through water deeper than 1/20 their original wavelength but shallower than half their original wavelength.

In general, the longer the wavelength, the faster the wave energy will move through the water. The relationship between the wavelength, period and velocity of any wave is:

$$C = {L}/{T}$$

where C is speed (celerity), L is the wavelength, and T is the period (in seconds). Thus the speed of the wave derives from the functional dependence $L(T)$ of the wavelength on the period (the [dispersion relation](wikipedia://en/853778)).

The speed of a deep-water wave may also be approximated by:

$$C = \sqrt{{gL}/{2\pi}}$$

where g is the acceleration due to gravity, \9.8 m per second squared. Because g and π (3.14) are constants, the equation can be reduced to:

$$C = 1.251\sqrt{L}$$

when C is measured in meters per second and L in meters. In both formulas the wave speed is proportional to the square root of the wavelength.

The speed of shallow-water waves is described by a different equation that may be written as:

$$C = \sqrt{gd} = 3.1\sqrt{d}$$

where C is speed (in meters per second), g is the acceleration due to gravity, and d is the depth of the water (in meters). The period of a wave remains unchanged regardless of the depth of water through which it is moving. As deep-water waves enter the shallows and feel the bottom, however, their speed is reduced, and their crests "bunch up", so their wavelength shortens.

### Spectral models {#Spectral_models}

[Sea state](wikipedia://en/1212631) can be described by the **sea wave spectrum** or just **wave spectrum** $S(\omega, \Theta)$. It is composed of a **wave height spectrum** (WHS) $S(\omega)$ and a **wave direction spectrum** (WDS) $f(\Theta)$. Many interesting properties about the sea state can be found from the wave spectra.

WHS describes the [spectral density](wikipedia://en/202672) of [wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_height) [variance](wikipedia://en/32344) ("power") versus [wave frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_frequency), with [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28physics%29) $\{S(\omega)\} = \{{\text{length}}^2\cdot\text{time}\}$. The relationship between the spectrum $S(\omega_j)$ and the wave amplitude $A_j$ for a wave component $j$ is:

$$\frac{1}{2} A_j^2 = S(\omega_j)\, \Delta \omega$$

Some WHS models are listed below.

- The International Towing Tank Conference (ITTC) [^o28] recommended spectrum model for fully developed sea (ISSC[^o29] spectrum/modified [Pierson-Moskowitz spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierson-Moskowitz_spectrum)):[^o30]

$$\frac{S(\omega)}{H_{1/3}^2 T_1} = \frac{0.11}{2\pi} \left(\frac{\omega T_1}{2\pi}\right)^{-5} \mathrm{exp} \left[-0.44 \left(\frac{\omega T_1}{2\pi}\right)^{-4} \right]$$

- ITTC recommended spectrum model for limited [fetch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fetch_%28geography%29) ([JONSWAP spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JONSWAP_spectrum))

$$S(\omega) = 155 \frac{H_{1/3}^2}{T_1^4 \omega^5} \mathrm{exp} \left(\frac{-944}{T_1^4 \omega^4}\right)(3.3)^Y,$$

where

$$Y = \exp \left[-\left(\frac{0.191 \omega T_1 -1}{2^{1/2}\sigma}\right)^2\right]$$

$$\sigma = \begin{cases} 0.07 & \text{if }\omega \le 5.24 / T_1, \\ 0.09 & \text{if }\omega > 5.24 / T_1. \end{cases}$$

(The latter model has since its creation improved based on the work of Phillips and Kitaigorodskii to better model the wave height spectrum for high [wavenumbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavenumber).[^o31])

As for WDS, an example model of $f(\Theta)$ might be:

$$f(\Theta) = \frac{2}{\pi}\cos^2\Theta, \qquad -\pi/2 \le \Theta \le \pi/2$$

Thus the sea state is fully determined and can be recreated by the following function where $\zeta$ is the wave elevation, $\epsilon_{j}$ is uniformly distributed between 0 and $2\pi$, and $\Theta_j$ is randomly drawn from the directional distribution function $\sqrt{f(\Theta)}:$[^o32]

$$\zeta = \sum_{j=1}^N \sqrt{2 S(\omega_j) \Delta \omega_j}\; \sin(\omega_j t - k_j x \cos \Theta_j - k_j y \sin \Theta_j + \epsilon_{j}).$$

## Shoaling and refraction {#Shoaling_and_refraction}

*Image: Ripples Hierkonpolku Reila.jpg.* Waves create [ripple marks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ripple_marks) in beaches.

As waves travel from deep to shallow water, their shape changes (wave height increases, speed decreases, and length decreases as wave orbits become asymmetrical). This process is called [shoaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_shoaling).

Wave [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction) is the process that occurs when waves interact with the sea bed to slow the velocity of propagation as a function of wavelength and period. As the waves slow down in shoaling water, the [crests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crest_%28physics%29) tend to realign at a decreasing angle to the depth contours. Varying depths along a wave crest cause the crest to travel at different [phase speeds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_speed), with those parts of the wave in deeper water moving faster than those in [shallow water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waves_in_shallow_water). This process continues while the depth decreases, and reverses if it increases again, but the wave leaving the shoal area may have changed direction considerably. [Rays](wikipedia://en/17003295)—lines [normal](wikipedia://en/173224) to wave crests between which a fixed amount of energy [flux](wikipedia://en/43590) is contained—converge on local shallows and shoals. Therefore, the [wave energy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_energy) between rays is concentrated as they converge, with a resulting increase in wave height.

Because these effects are related to a spatial variation in the phase speed, and because the phase speed also changes with the ambient current—due to the [Doppler shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_shift)—the same effects of refraction and altering wave height also occur due to current variations. In the case of meeting an adverse current the wave *steepens*, i.e. its wave height increases while the wavelength decreases, similar to the shoaling when the water depth decreases.[^o34]

## Breaking {#Breaking}

*Image: Big wave breaking in Santa Cruz.jpg.* Large wave breaking

*Image: Giant ocean wave.jpg.* Giant ocean wave

Some waves undergo a [phenomenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phenomenon) called "breaking".[^o36] A [breaking wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Breaking_wave) is one whose base can no longer support its top, causing it to collapse. A wave breaks when it runs into [shallow water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waves_and_shallow_water), or when two wave systems oppose and combine forces. When the slope, or steepness ratio, of a wave, is too great, breaking is inevitable.

Individual waves in deep water break when the wave steepness—the [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) of the [wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_height) *H* to the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) *λ*—exceeds about 0.17, so for *H* > 0.17 *λ*. In shallow water, with the water depth small compared to the wavelength, the individual waves break when their wave height *H* is larger than 0.8 times the water depth *h*, that is *H* > 0.8 *h*.[^o37] Waves can also break if the wind grows strong enough to blow the crest off the base of the wave.

In shallow water, the base of the wave is decelerated by drag on the seabed. As a result, the upper parts will propagate at a higher velocity than the base and the leading face of the crest will become steeper and the trailing face flatter. This may be exaggerated to the extent that the leading face forms a barrel profile, with the crest falling forward and down as it extends over the air ahead of the wave.

Three main types of breaking waves are identified by [surfers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surfer) or [surf lifesavers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surf_lifesaving). Their varying characteristics make them more or less suitable for surfing and present different dangers.

1. Spilling, or rolling: these are the safest waves on which to surf. They can be found in most areas with relatively flat shorelines. They are the most common type of shorebreak. The deceleration of the wave base is gradual, and the velocity of the upper parts does not differ much with height. Breaking occurs mainly when the steepness ratio exceeds the stability limit.
1. Plunging, or dumping: these break suddenly and can "dump" swimmers—pushing them to the bottom with great force. These are the preferred waves for experienced surfers. Strong offshore winds and long wave periods can cause dumpers. They are often found where there is a sudden rise in the seafloor, such as a reef or sandbar. Deceleration of the wave base is sufficient to cause upward acceleration and a significant forward velocity excess of the upper part of the crest. The peak rises and overtakes the forward face, forming a "barrel" or "tube" as it collapses.
1. Surging: these may never actually break as they approach the water's edge, as the water below them is very deep. They tend to form on steep shorelines. These waves can knock swimmers over and drag them back into deeper water.

When the shoreline is near vertical, waves do not break but are reflected. Most of the energy is retained in the wave as it returns to seaward. Interference patterns are caused by superposition of the incident and reflected waves, and the superposition may cause localized instability when peaks cross, and these peaks may break due to instability. (see also [clapotic waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clapotis))

## Physics of waves {#Physics_of_waves}

*Image: Shallow water wave.png.* Stokes drift in shallow water waves (Animation)

Wind waves are mechanical [waves](wikipedia://en/33516) that propagate along the interface between [water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Water) and [air](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Air); the restoring force is provided by gravity, and so they are often referred to as [surface gravity waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_wave). As the [wind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wind) blows, pressure and friction perturb the equilibrium of the water surface and transfer energy from the air to the water, forming waves. The initial formation of waves by the wind is described in the theory of Phillips from 1957, and the subsequent growth of the small waves has been modeled by [Miles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_W._Miles), also in 1957.[^o39][^o40]

*Image: Deep water wave.png.* Stokes drift in a deeper water wave (Animation)

*Image: Orbital wave motion-Wiegel Johnson ICCE 1950 Fig 6.png.* Photograph of the water particle orbits under a—progressive and periodic—[surface gravity wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_gravity_wave) in a [wave flume](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_flume). The wave conditions are: mean water depth *d* = \2.50 ft, [wave height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_height) *H* = \0.339 ft, wavelength λ = \6.42 ft, [period](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Period_%28physics%29) *T* = 1.12 s.[^o41]

In linear plane waves of one wavelength in deep water, [parcels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fluid_parcel) near the surface move not plainly up and down but in circular orbits: forward above and backward below (compared to the wave propagation direction). As a result, the surface of the water forms not an exact [sine wave](wikipedia://en/324749), but more a [trochoid](wikipedia://en/2738094) with the sharper curves upwards—as modeled in [trochoidal wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trochoidal_wave) theory. Wind waves are thus a combination of [transversal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transversal_wave) and [longitudinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitudinal_wave) waves.

When waves propagate in [shallow water](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Waves_and_shallow_water), (where the depth is less than half the wavelength) the particle trajectories are compressed into [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277).[^o42][^o43]

In reality, for finite values of the wave amplitude (height), the particle paths do not form closed orbits; rather, after the passage of each crest, particles are displaced slightly from their previous positions, a phenomenon known as [Stokes drift](wikipedia://en/15344641).[^o44][^o45]

As the depth below the free surface increases, the radius of the circular motion decreases. At a depth equal to half the [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125) λ, the orbital movement has decayed to less than 5% of its value at the surface. The [phase speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_speed) (also called the celerity) of a surface gravity wave is—for pure [periodic](wikipedia://en/76848) wave motion of small-[amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) waves—well approximated by

$$c=\sqrt{\frac{g \lambda}{2\pi} \tanh \left(\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda}\right)}$$

where

*c* = [phase speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_speed);

*λ* = [wavelength](wikipedia://en/33125);

*d* = water depth;

*g* = [acceleration due to gravity at the Earth's surface](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_gravity).

In deep water, where $d \ge \frac{1}{2}\lambda$, so $\frac{2\pi d}{\lambda} \ge \pi$ and the hyperbolic tangent approaches $1$, the speed $c$ approximates

$$c_\text{deep}=\sqrt{\frac{g\lambda}{2\pi}}.$$

In SI units, with $c_\text{deep}$ in m/s, $c_\text{deep} \approx 1.25\sqrt\lambda$, when $\lambda$ is measured in metres. This expression tells us that waves of different wavelengths travel at different speeds. The fastest waves in a storm are the ones with the longest wavelength. As a result, after a storm, the first waves to arrive on the coast are the long-wavelength swells.

For intermediate and shallow water, the [Boussinesq equations](wikipedia://en/14993993) are applicable, combining [frequency dispersion](wikipedia://en/1744650) and nonlinear effects. And in very shallow water, the [shallow water equations](wikipedia://en/8631522) can be used.

If the wavelength is very long compared to the water depth, the phase speed (by taking the [limit](wikipedia://en/285759) of $c$ when the wavelength approaches infinity) can be approximated by

$$c_\text{shallow} = \lim_{\lambda\rightarrow\infty} c = \sqrt{gd}.$$

On the other hand, for very short wavelengths, [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) plays an important role and the phase speed of these [gravity-capillary waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity-capillary_wave) can (in deep water) be approximated by

$$c_\text{gravity-capillary}=\sqrt{\frac{g \lambda}{2\pi} + \frac{2\pi S}{\rho\lambda}}$$

where

*S* = [surface tension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surface_tension) of the air-water interface;

$\rho$ = [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) of the water.[^o46]

When several wave trains are present, as is always the case in nature, the waves form groups. In deep water, the groups travel at a [group velocity](wikipedia://en/12778) which is half of the [phase speed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_speed).[^o47] Following a single wave in a group one can see the wave appearing at the back of the group, growing, and finally disappearing at the front of the group.

As the water depth $d$ decreases towards the [coast](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coast), this will have an effect: wave height changes due to [wave shoaling](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_shoaling) and [refraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refraction). As the wave height increases, the wave may become unstable when the [crest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crest_%28physics%29) of the wave moves faster than the [trough](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trough_%28physics%29). This causes *surf*, a breaking of the waves.

The movement of wind waves can be captured by [wave energy devices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wave_power). The energy density (per unit area) of regular sinusoidal waves depends on the water [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) $\rho$, gravity acceleration $g$ and the wave height $H$ (which, for regular waves, is equal to twice the [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude), $a$):

$$E=\frac{1}{8}\rho g H^2=\frac{1}{2}\rho g a^2.$$

The velocity of propagation of this energy is the [group velocity](wikipedia://en/12778).

## Models {#Models}

*Image: Global Wave Height Speed.jpg.* The image shows the global distribution of wind speed and wave height as observed by NASA's TOPEX/Poseidon's dual-frequency radar altimeter from October 3 to October 12, 1992. Simultaneous observations of wind speed and wave height are helping scientists to predict ocean waves. Wind speed is determined by the strength of the radar signal after it has bounced off the ocean surface and returned to the satellite. A calm sea serves as a good reflector and returns a strong signal; a rough sea tends to scatter the signals and returns a weak pulse. Wave height is determined by the shape of the return radar pulse. A calm sea with low waves returns a condensed pulse whereas a rough sea with high waves returns a stretched pulse. Comparing the two images above shows a high degree of correlation between wind speed and wave height. The strongest winds (\33.6 mph) and highest waves are found in the Southern Ocean. The weakest winds — shown as areas of magenta and dark blue — are generally found in the tropical oceans.

Surfers are very interested in the [wave forecasts](wikipedia://en/20260077). There are many websites that provide predictions of the surf quality for the upcoming days and weeks. Wind wave models are driven by more general [weather models](wikipedia://en/1505381) that predict the winds and pressures over the oceans, seas, and lakes.

Wind wave models are also an important part of examining the impact of [shore protection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coastal_protection) and [beach nourishment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Beach_nourishment) proposals. For many beach areas there is only patchy information about the wave climate, therefore estimating the effect of wind waves is important for managing [littoral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Littoral) environments.

A wind-generated wave can be predicted based on two parameters: wind speed at 10 m above sea level and wind duration, which must blow over long periods of time to be considered fully developed. The significant wave height and peak frequency can then be predicted for a certain fetch length.[^o49]

## Seismic signals {#Seismic_signals}

Ocean water waves generate seismic waves that are globally visible on [seismographs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seismographs).[^o51] There are two principal constituents of the ocean wave-generated seismic microseism.[^o52] The strongest of these is the secondary microseism which is created by ocean floor pressures generated by interfering ocean waves and has a spectrum that is generally between approximately 6–12 s periods, or at approximately half of the period of the responsible interfering waves. The theory for microseism generation by standing waves was provided by [Michael Longuet-Higgins](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_S._Longuet-Higgins) in 1950 after in 1941 Pierre Bernard suggested this relation with standing waves on the basis of observations.[^o53][^o54] The weaker primary microseism, also globally visible, is generated by dynamic seafloor pressures of propagating waves above shallower (less than several hundred meters depth) regions of the global ocean. Microseisms were first reported in about 1900, and seismic records provide long-term proxy measurements of seasonal and climate-related large-scale wave intensity in Earth's oceans [^o55] including those associated with [anthropogenic global warming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anthropogenic_global_warming).[^o56][^o57][^o58]
