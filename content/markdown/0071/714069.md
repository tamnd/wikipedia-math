# Church–Turing–Deutsch principle

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Church–Turing–Deutsch principle"}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) and [quantum physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_physics), the **Church–Turing–Deutsch-Wolfram principle** (**CTDW principle**) is a stronger, physical form of the [Church&ndash;Turing thesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Church&ndash;Turing_thesis) articulated in separate works by [Stephen Wolfram](wikipedia://en/52469)[^o2] and [David Deutsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Deutsch)[^o3] in 1985.

:::

The principle states that a [universal](wikipedia://en/30621) [computing device](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer) can [simulate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simulation) every [physical process](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_process).

## History {#History}

A formulation of the principle was given by [Stephen Wolfram](wikipedia://en/52469) in February 1985. In *Undecidability and Intractability in Theoretical Physics*[^o5], Wolfram wrote that

Wolfram states that such a principle would hold provided that physical systems have a finite density of information and that information can be transmitted only at a finite rate in a finite-dimensional space.

Later in 1985, [David Deutsch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Deutsch) stated the principle with respect to [finitary](wikipedia://en/404456) machines and processes.[^o6] He observed that [classical physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_physics), which makes use of the concept of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), cannot be simulated by a [Turing machine](wikipedia://en/30403), which can only represent [computable reals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computable_real). Deutsch proposed that [quantum computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quantum_computer) may obey the principle, assuming that the laws of [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202) can completely describe every physical process.

An earlier version of this thesis for classical computers was stated by Alan Turing's friend and student [Robin Gandy](wikipedia://en/3155895) in 1980.[^o7][^o8]

A similar thesis was later stated by [Michael Freedman](wikipedia://en/658970) in an early review of [topological quantum computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Topological_quantum_computing) with [Alexei Kitaev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexei_Kitaev), [Michael J. Larsen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_J._Larsen), and [Zhenghan Wang](wikipedia://en/76425950), known as the Freedman–Church–Turing thesis:[^o9]

This thesis differs from the Church–Turing–Deutsch-Wolfram thesis insofar as it is a statement about computational complexity, and not computability.
