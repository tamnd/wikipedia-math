# Alphabetic numeral system

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alphabetic numeral system"}

An **alphabetic numeral system** is a type of [numeral system](wikipedia://en/21170). Developed in [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity), it flourished during the [early Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Early_Middle_Ages).[^n1] In alphabetic numeral systems, [numbers](wikipedia://en/21690) are written using the [characters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grapheme) of an [alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabet), [syllabary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Syllabary), or another [writing system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Writing_system). Unlike [acrophonic numeral systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrophony), where a numeral is represented by the first letter of the lexical name of the numeral, alphabetic numeral systems can arbitrarily assign letters to numerical values. Some systems, including the [Arabic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic), [Georgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgian_numerals) and [Hebrew](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_numerals) systems, use an already established [alphabetical order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alphabetical_order).[^n1] Alphabetic numeral systems originated with [Greek numerals](wikipedia://en/166697) around 600 BC and became largely extinct by the 16th century.[^n1] After the development of [positional numeral](wikipedia://en/437052) systems like [Hindu–Arabic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hindu–Arabic_numerals), the use of alphabetic numeral systems dwindled to predominantly ordered lists, [pagination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pagination), religious functions, and divinatory magic.[^n1]

:::

## History {#History}

The first attested alphabetic numeral system is the [Greek alphabetic](wikipedia://en/166697) system (named the [Ionic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionic_Greek) or [Milesian](wikipedia://en/166697) system due to its origin in west [Asia Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia)). The system's structure follows the structure of the [Egyptian demotic numerals](wikipedia://en/1149201); Greek letters replaced Egyptian signs. The first examples of the Greek system date back to the 6th century BC, written with the letters of the [archaic Greek script](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_the_Greek_alphabet#Standardization_–_the_Ionic_alphabet) used in [Ionia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ionia).[^n2]

Other cultures in contact with Greece adopted this numerical notation, replacing the Greek letters with their own script; these included the Hebrews in the late 2nd century BC. The [Gothic alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_alphabet) adopted their own [alphabetic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gothic_alphabet#Letters) along with the Greek-influenced script.[^n3] In [North Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/North_Africa), the [Coptic system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_alphabet#Alphabet_table) was developed in the 4th century AD,[^n4] and the [Ge'ez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geʽez_script#Numerals) system in Ethiopia was developed around 350 AD.[^n5] Both were developed from the Greek model.

The Arabs developed their own alphabetic numeral system, the [abjad numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abjad_numerals), in the 7th century AD, and used it for mathematical and astrological purposes even as late as the 13th century far after the introduction of the [Hindu–Arabic numeral system](wikipedia://en/3393371).[^n6] After the adoption of Christianity, [Armenians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Armenians) and [Georgians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Georgians) developed their alphabetical numeral system in the 4th or early 5th century, while in the [Byzantine Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) [Cyrillic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyrillic_numerals) and [Glagolitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Glagolitic_script) were introduced in the 9th century. Alphabetic numeral systems were known and used as far north as England, Germany, and Russia, as far south as Ethiopia, as far east as Persia, and in North Africa from Morocco to Central Asia.

By the 16th century AD, most alphabetic numeral systems had died out or were in little use, displaced by [Arabic positional](wikipedia://en/3655571) and [Western numerals](wikipedia://en/1786) as the ordinary numerals of commerce and administration throughout Europe and the Middle East.[^n1]

The newest alphabetic numeral systems in use, all of them positional, are part of tactile writing systems for [visually impaired](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visually_impaired). Even though [1829 braille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1829_braille) had a simple ciphered-positional system copied from Western numerals with a separate symbol for each digit, early experience with students forced its designer [Louis Braille](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Louis_Braille) to simplify the system, bringing the number of available patterns (symbols) from 125 down to 63, so he had to repurpose a supplementary symbol to mark letters a–j as numerals. Besides this traditional system, [another one](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_Braille#Numbers) was developed in France in the 20th century, and [yet another one](wikipedia://en/696794#Number_Signs) in the US.

## Systems {#Systems}

An alphabetic numeral system employs the [letters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Letter_%28alphabet%29) of a script in the specific order of the alphabet in order to express numerals.

In Greek, letters are assigned to respective numbers in the following sets: 1 through 9, 10 through 90, 100 through 900, and so on. Decimal places are represented by a single symbol. As the alphabet ends, higher numbers are represented with various multiplicative methods. However, since writing systems have a differing number of letters, other systems of writing do not necessarily group numbers in this way. The [Greek alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_alphabet) has 24 letters; three additional letters had to be incorporated in order to reach 900. Unlike the Greek, the [Hebrew alphabet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hebrew_alphabet)'s 22 letters allowed for numerical expression up to 400. The [Arabic abjad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arabic_alphabet)'s 28 consonant signs could represent numbers up to 1000. Ancient Aramaic alphabets had enough letters to reach up to 9000. In mathematical and astronomical manuscripts, other methods were used to represent larger numbers. [Roman numerals](wikipedia://en/25657) and [Attic numerals](wikipedia://en/1455703), both of which were also alphabetic numeral systems, became more concise over time, but required their users to be familiar with many more signs. [Acrophonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acrophonic) numerals do not belong to this group of systems because their letter-numerals do not follow the order of an alphabet.

These various systems do not have a single unifying trait or feature. The most common structure is ciphered-additive with a decimal base, with or without the use of multiplicative-additive structuring for the higher numbers. Exceptions include the Armenian notation of [Shirakatsi](wikipedia://en/2442701), which is multiplicative-additive and sometimes uses a base 1,000, and the Greek and Arabic astronomical notation systems.

### Higher numbers {#Higher_numbers}

As the alphabet ended, various multiplicative methods were used for the expression of higher numbers in the different systems. In the Greek alphabetic system, for multiples of 1,000, the *hasta* sign was placed to the left below a numeral-sign to indicate that it should be multiplied by 1,000.[^n9]

**β** = **2**

**͵β** = **2,000**

**͵κ** = **20,000**

With a second level of multiplicative method – multiplication by 10,000 – the numeral set could be expanded. The most common method, used by [Aristarchus](wikipedia://en/74857), involved placing a numeral-phrase above a large M character (M = myriads = 10,000) to indicate multiplication by 10,000.[^n10] This method could express numbers up to 100,000,000 (10<sup>8</sup>).

### Distinguishing numeral-phrases from text {#Distinguishing_numeral-phrases_from_text}

Alphabetic numerals were distinguished from the words with special signs, most commonly a horizontal stroke above the numeral-phrase, but occasionally with dots placed to either side of it. The latter was manifested in the Greek alphabet with the *hasta* sign.

In [Ethiopic numerals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geʽez_script#Numerals), known as [Geʽez](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geʽez_script), the signs have marks both above and below them to indicate that their value is numerical. The Ethiopic numerals are the exception, where numeral signs are not letters of their script. This practice became universal from the 15th century onwards.[^n11]

The direction of numerals follows the writing system's direction. Writing is from left to right in Greek, Coptic, Ethiopic, Gothic, Armenian, Georgian, Glagolitic, and Cyrillic alphabetic numerals along with Shirakatsi's notation. Right-to-left writing is found in Hebrew and Syriac alphabetic numerals, Arabic abjad numerals, and Fez numerals.

## Fractions {#Fractions}

### Unit fractions {#Unit_fractions}

*Image: Greek minuscule numerals Cod.Const.Pal.Vet.f96r.svg.* Greek numerals in a c. 1100 [Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_Empire) manuscript of [Hero of Alexandria](wikipedia://en/68274)'s *Metrika*. The first line contains the number "$\overline{\text{͵}\theta\text{ϡ}\text{ϟ}\text{ϛ}}$ δ ϛ", i.e. "9996 + $\tfrac{1}{4}$ + $\tfrac{1}{6}$". It features unit fractions and each of the special numeral symbols [sampi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sampi) (ϡ), [koppa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Koppa_%28letter%29) (ϟ), and [stigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stigma_%28letter%29) (ϛ) in their [minuscule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_minuscule) forms.

[Unit fractions](wikipedia://en/491316) were a method to express fractions. In Greek alphabetic notation, unit fractions were indicated with the denominator – alphabetic numeral sign – followed by small accents or strokes placed to the right of a numeral, known as a *keraia* (ʹ). Therefore, **γʹ** indicated one third, **δʹ** one fourth, and so on. These fractions were additive and were also known as [Egyptian fractions](wikipedia://en/336349).

For example: $\delta \text{ϛ} = \tfrac{1}{4} + \tfrac{1}{6} = \tfrac{5}{12}$.

A mixed number could be written as such: $\overline{\text{͵}\theta\text{ϡ}\text{ϟ}\text{ϛ}} \delta \text{ϛ}$ = 9996 + $\tfrac{1}{4}$ + $\tfrac{1}{6}$

### Astronomical fractions {#Astronomical_fractions}

*Image: P. Lund, Inv. 35a.jpg.* Example of the early Greek symbol for zero (lower right corner) from a 2nd-century papyrus

In many astronomical texts, a distinct set of alphabetic numeral systems blend their ordinary alphabetical numerals with a base of 60, such as [Babylonian sexagesimal systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_numerals). In the 2nd century BC, a hybrid of Babylonian notation and Greek alphabetic numerals emerged and was used to express fractions.[^n12] Unlike the Babylonian system, the Greek base of 60 was not used for expressing integers.

With this [sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) [positional](wikipedia://en/437052) system – with a subbase of 10 – for expressing [fractions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29), fourteen of the alphabetic numerals were used (the units from 1 to 9 and the decades from 10 to 50) in order to write any number from 1 through 59. These could be a numerator of a fraction. The positional principle was used for the denominator of a fraction, which was written with an exponent of 60 (60, 3,600, 216,000, etc.). Sexagesimal fractions could be used to express any fractional value, with the successive positions representing 1/60, 1/60<sup>2</sup>, 1/60<sup>3</sup>, and so on.[^n13] The first major text in which this blended system appeared was [Ptolemy](wikipedia://en/23979)'s [Almagest](wikipedia://en/148060), written in the 2nd century AD.[^n14]

This blended system did not use a [radix point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radix_point), but the astronomical fractions had a special sign to indicate zero as a placeholder. Some late Babylonian texts used a similar placeholder. The Greeks adopted this technique using their own sign, whose form and character changed over time from early manuscripts (1st century AD) to an alphabetic notation.[^n15]

This sexagesimal notation was especially useful in astronomy and mathematics because of the division of the circle into 360 degrees (with subdivisions of 60 minutes per degree and 60 seconds per minute). In [Theon of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theon_of_Alexandria)'s (4th century AD) commentary on the Almagest, the numeral-phrase $\overline{\text{͵}\alpha\varphi\iota\varepsilon} \overline{\kappa} \overline{\iota\varepsilon}$ expresses 1515 ($\overline{\text{͵}\alpha\varphi\iota\varepsilon}$) degrees, 20 ($\overline{\kappa}$) minutes, and 15 ($\overline{\iota\varepsilon}$) seconds.[^n16] The degree's value is in the ordinary decimal alphabetic numerals, including the use of the multiplicative *hasta* for 1000, while the latter two positions are written in sexagesimal fractions.

Arabs adopted astronomical fractions directly from the Greeks, and similarly Hebrew astronomers used sexagesimal fractions, but Greek numeral signs were replaced by their own alphabetic numeral signs to express both integers and fractions.
