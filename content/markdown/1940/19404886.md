# James W. Cannon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="James W. Cannon"}

**James W. Cannon** (born January 30, 1943) is an American mathematician working in the areas of [low-dimensional topology](wikipedia://en/621774) and [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164). He was an Orson Pratt Professor of Mathematics at [Brigham Young University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young_University).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

James W. Cannon was born on January 30, 1943, in [Bellefonte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellefonte,_Pennsylvania), [Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pennsylvania).[^o4] Cannon received a [Ph.D.](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ph.D.) in mathematics from the [University of Utah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Utah) in 1969, under the direction of C. Edmund Burgess.

He was a professor at the [University of Wisconsin, Madison](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Wisconsin,_Madison) from 1977 to 1985.[^o4] In 1986 Cannon was appointed an Orson Pratt Professor of Mathematics at [Brigham Young University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young_University).[^o5] He held this position until his retirement in September 2012.[^o6]

Cannon gave an [American Mathematical Society](wikipedia://en/198822) (AMS) Invited address at the meeting of the AMS in [Seattle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seattle) in August 1977, an [invited address](wikipedia://en/47738065) at the [International Congress of Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/627842) in Helsinki 1978, and delivered the 1982 [Mathematical Association of America](wikipedia://en/198772) Hedrick Lectures in [Toronto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Toronto), Canada.[^o4][^o7]

Cannon was elected to the American Mathematical Society Council in 2003 with the term of service February 1, 2004, to January 31, 2007.[^o5][^o8] In 2012 he became a fellow of the American Mathematical Society.[^o9]

In 1993 Cannon delivered the 30th annual Karl G. Maeser Distinguished Faculty Lecture at [Brigham Young University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brigham_Young_University).[^o10]

Cannon is a member of [the Church of Jesus Christ of Latter-day Saints](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Church_of_Jesus_Christ_of_Latter-day_Saints).[^o11]

## Mathematical contributions {#Mathematical_contributions}

### Early work {#Early_work}

Cannon's early work concerned topological aspects of embedded surfaces in **R**<sup>3</sup> and understanding the difference between "tame" and "wild" surfaces.

His first famous result came in late 1970s when Cannon gave a complete solution to a long-standing "double suspension" problem posed by [John Milnor](wikipedia://en/61632). Cannon proved that the double [suspension](wikipedia://en/1251702) of a [homology sphere](wikipedia://en/585388) is a topological sphere.[^o14][^o15] R. D. Edwards had previously proven this in many cases.

The results of Cannon's paper[^o15] were used by Cannon, Bryant and Lacher to prove (1979)[^o16] an important case of the so-called *characterization conjecture* for topological manifolds. The conjecture says that a [generalized *n*-manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_manifold) $M$, where $n \ge 5$, which satisfies the "disjoint disk property" is a topological manifold. Cannon, Bryant and Lacher established[^o16] that the conjecture holds under the assumption that $M$ be a manifold except possibly at a set of dimension $(n-2)/2$. Later [Frank Quinn](wikipedia://en/8488854)[^o17] completed the proof that the characterization conjecture holds if there is even a single manifold point. In general, the conjecture is false as was proved by John Bryant, Steven Ferry, [Washington Mio](wikipedia://en/80484755) and [Shmuel Weinberger](wikipedia://en/19159322).[^o18]

### 1980s: Hyperbolic geometry, 3-manifolds and geometric group theory {#1980s:_Hyperbolic_geometry,_3-manifolds_and_geometric_group_theory}

In 1980s the focus of Cannon's work shifted to the study of [3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1018257), [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291) and [Kleinian groups](wikipedia://en/1362652) and he is considered one of the key figures in the birth of [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) as a distinct subject in late 1980s and early 1990s. Cannon's 1984 paper "The combinatorial structure of cocompact discrete hyperbolic groups"[^o20] was one of the forerunners in the development of the theory of [word-hyperbolic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group), a notion that was introduced and developed three years later in a seminal 1987 monograph of [Mikhail Gromov](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Gromov_%28mathematician%29).[^o21] Cannon's paper explored combinatorial and algorithmic aspects of the [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) of Kleinian groups and related them to the geometric features of the actions of these groups on the [hyperbolic space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space). In particular, Cannon proved that convex-cocompact Kleinian groups admit [finite presentations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_presented_group) where the [Dehn algorithm](wikipedia://en/19882883#Dehn.27s_algorithm) solves the [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563). The latter condition later turned out to give one of equivalent characterization of being [word-hyperbolic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) and, moreover, Cannon's original proof essentially went through without change to show that the word problem in word-hyperbolic groups is solvable by Dehn's algorithm.[^o22] Cannon's 1984 paper[^o20] also introduced an important notion a *cone type* of an element of a [finitely generated group](wikipedia://en/963042) (roughly, the set of all geodesic extensions of an element). Cannon proved that a convex-cocompact Kleinian group has only finitely many cone types (with respect to a fixed finite generating set of that group) and showed how to use this fact to conclude that the growth series of the group is a [rational function](wikipedia://en/361210). These arguments also turned out to generalize to the [word-hyperbolic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) context.[^o21] Now standard proofs[^o35] of the fact that the set of geodesic words in a [word-hyperbolic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) is a [regular language](wikipedia://en/25723) also use finiteness of the number of cone types.

Cannon's work also introduced an important notion of *almost convexity* for Cayley graphs of [finitely generated groups](wikipedia://en/963042),[^o23] a notion that led to substantial further study and generalizations.[^o24][^o25][^o26]

An influential paper of Cannon and [William Thurston](wikipedia://en/39190) "Group invariant Peano curves",[^o27] that first circulated in a preprint form in the mid-1980s,[^o28] introduced the notion of what is now called the [Cannon–Thurston map](wikipedia://en/63553560). They considered the case of a closed hyperbolic 3-manifold *M* that [fibers](wikipedia://en/276526) over the circle with the fiber being a closed hyperbolic surface *S*. In this case the universal cover of *S*, which is identified with the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/241291), admits an embedding into the universal cover of *M*, which is the [hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space). Cannon and Thurston proved that this embedding extends to a continuous π<sub>1</sub>(*S*)-equivariant [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) map (now called the *Cannon–Thurston map*) from the ideal boundary of the hyperbolic plane (the circle) to the ideal boundary of the hyperbolic 3-space (the [2-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-sphere)). Although the paper of Cannon and Thurston was finally published only in 2007, in the meantime it has generated considerable further research and a number of significant generalizations (both in the contexts of Kleinian groups and of word-hyperbolic groups), including the work of [Mahan Mitra](wikipedia://en/33274079),[^o29][^o30] Erica Klarreich,[^o31] [Brian Bowditch](wikipedia://en/13677348)[^o32] and others.

### 1990s and 2000s: Automatic groups, discrete conformal geometry and Cannon's conjecture {#1990s_and_2000s:_Automatic_groups,_discrete_conformal_geometry_and_Cannon's_conjecture}

Cannon was one of the co-authors of the 1992 book *[Word Processing in Groups](wikipedia://en/63150483)*[^o35] which introduced, formalized and developed the theory of [automatic groups](wikipedia://en/4224977). The theory of automatic groups brought new computational ideas from [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science) to [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) and played an important role in the development of the subject in 1990s.

A 1994 paper of Cannon gave a proof of the "[combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Combinatorial_Riemann_mapping_theorem)"[^o36] that was motivated by the classic [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215) in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759). The goal was to understand when an [action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) of a group by [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660) on a [2-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-sphere) is (up to a topological conjugation) an action on the standard [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799) by [Möbius transformations](wikipedia://en/314493). The "combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem" of Cannon gave a set of sufficient conditions when a sequence of finer and finer combinatorial subdivisions of a topological surface determine, in the appropriate sense and after passing to the limit, an actual [conformal structure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_structure) on that surface. This paper of Cannon led to an important conjecture, first explicitly formulated by Cannon and Swenson in 1998[^o46] (but also suggested in implicit form in Section 8 of Cannon's 1994 paper) and now known as [Cannon's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cannon's_conjecture), regarding characterizing [word-hyperbolic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) with the 2-sphere as the boundary. The conjecture (Conjecture 5.1 in [^o46]) states that if the ideal boundary of a [word-hyperbolic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) *G* is [homeomorphic](wikipedia://en/13660) to the 2-sphere, then *G* admits a properly discontinuous cocompact isometric action on the [hyperbolic 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_3-space) (so that *G* is essentially a 3-dimensional [Kleinian group](wikipedia://en/1362652)). In analytic terms Cannon's conjecture is equivalent to saying that if the ideal boundary of a [word-hyperbolic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Word-hyperbolic_group) *G* is homeomorphic to the [2-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-sphere) then this boundary, with the visual metric coming from the [Cayley graph](wikipedia://en/358277) of *G*, is [quasisymmetric](wikipedia://en/30220726) to the standard 2-sphere.

The 1998 paper of Cannon and Swenson[^o46] gave an initial approach to this conjecture by proving that the conjecture holds under an extra assumption that the family of standard "disks" in the boundary of the group satisfies a combinatorial "conformal" property. The main result of Cannon's 1994 paper[^o36] played a key role in the proof. This approach to Cannon's conjecture and related problems was pushed further later in the joint work of Cannon, Floyd and Parry.[^o37][^o38][^o39]

Cannon's conjecture motivated much of subsequent work by other mathematicians and to a substantial degree informed subsequent interaction between [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) and the theory of analysis on metric spaces.[^o40][^o41][^o42][^o43][^o44][^o45] Cannon's conjecture was motivated (see [^o46]) by [Thurston's Geometrization Conjecture](wikipedia://en/220642) and by trying to understand why in dimension three variable negative curvature can be promoted to constant negative curvature. Although the [Geometrization conjecture](wikipedia://en/220642) was recently settled by [Perelman](wikipedia://en/225266), Cannon's conjecture remains wide open and is considered one of the key outstanding open problems in geometric group theory and [geometric topology](wikipedia://en/621732).

:::

### Applications to biology {#Applications_to_biology}

The ideas of combinatorial conformal geometry that underlie Cannon's proof of the "combinatorial Riemann mapping theorem",[^o36] were applied by Cannon, Floyd and Parry (2000) to the study of large-scale growth patterns of biological organisms.[^o48] Cannon, Floyd and Parry produced a mathematical growth model which demonstrated that some systems determined by simple [finite subdivision rules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finite_subdivision_rules) can results in objects (in their example, a tree trunk) whose large-scale form oscillates wildly over time even though the local subdivision laws remain the same.[^o48] Cannon, Floyd and Parry also applied their model to the analysis of the growth patterns of rat tissue.[^o48] They suggested that the "negatively curved" (or non-euclidean) nature of microscopic growth patterns of biological organisms is one of the key reasons why large-scale organisms do not look like crystals or polyhedral shapes but in fact in many cases resemble self-similar [fractals](wikipedia://en/10913).[^o48] In particular they suggested (see section 3.4 of [^o48]) that such "negatively curved" local structure is manifested in highly folded and highly connected nature of the brain and the lung tissue.
