# Bella Subbotovskaya

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Bella Subbotovskaya"}

**Bella Abramovna Subbotovskaya** (17 December 1937 – 23 September 1982)[^o3] was a Soviet mathematician who founded the short-lived [Jewish People's University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jewish_People's_University) (1978–1983) in [Moscow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moscow).[^o4][^o5] The school's purpose was to offer free education to those affected by structured anti-Semitism within the Soviet educational system. Its existence was outside Soviet authority and it was investigated by the [KGB](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/KGB). Subbotovskaya herself was interrogated a number of times by the KGB and shortly thereafter was hit by a truck and died, in what has been speculated was an [assassination](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assassination).[^o6]

:::

## Academic work {#Academic_work}

Prior to founding the Jewish People's University, Subbotovskaya published papers in [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636). Her results on Boolean formulas written in terms of $\land$, $\lor$, and $\lnot$ were influential in the then nascent field of [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543).

### Random restrictions {#Random_restrictions}

Subbotovskaya invented the method of random restrictions to [Boolean functions](wikipedia://en/753349).[^o9] Starting with a function $f$, a restriction $\rho$ of $f$ is a partial assignment to $n-k$ of the $n$ variables, giving a function $f_\rho$ of fewer variables. Take the following function:

$$f(x_1, x_2, x_3) = (x_1 \lor x_2 \lor x_3) \land (\lnot x_1 \lor x_2) \land (x_1 \lor \lnot x_3).$$

The following is a restriction of one variable

$$f_\rho(y_1, y_2) = f(1, y_1, y_2) = (1 \lor y_1 \lor y_2) \land (\lnot 1 \lor y_1) \land (1 \lor \lnot y_2).$$

Under the usual identities of [Boolean algebra](wikipedia://en/54476844) this simplifies to $f_\rho(y_1, y_2) = y_1$.

To sample a random restriction, retain $k$ variables uniformly at random. For each remaining variable, assign it 0 or 1 with equal probability.

::: {#o11 .statement cue=named-heading name="Formula Size and Restrictions"}

### Formula Size and Restrictions {#Formula_Size_and_Restrictions}

As demonstrated in the above example, applying a restriction to a function can massively reduce the size of its formula. Though $f$ is written with 7 variables, by only restricting one variable, we found that $f_\rho$ uses only 1.

Subbotovskaya proved something much stronger: if $\rho$ is a random restriction of $n-k$ variables, then the expected shrinkage between $f$ and $f_\rho$ is large, specifically

$$\mathbb{E} \left [ L(f_\rho) \right ] \le \left ( \frac k n \right )^{3/2} L(f),$$

where $L$ is the minimum number of variables in the formula.[^o9] Applying [Markov's inequality](wikipedia://en/238232) we see

$$\Pr \left [ L(f_\rho) \le 4 \left ( \frac k n \right )^{3/2} L(f) \right ] \ge \frac 3 4.$$

:::

### Example application {#Example_application}

Take $f$ to be the [parity function](wikipedia://en/22399369) over $n$ variables. After applying a random restriction of $n-1$ variables, we know that $f_\rho$ is either $x_i$ or $\lnot x_i$ depending the parity of the assignments to the remaining variables. Thus clearly the size of the circuit that computes $f_\rho$ is exactly 1. Then applying the [probabilistic method](wikipedia://en/173525), for sufficiently large $n$, we know there is some $\rho$ for which

$$L(f_\rho) \le 4 \left ( \frac 1 {n} \right )^{3/2} L(f).$$

Plugging in $L(f_\rho) = 1$, we see that $L(f) \ge n^{3/2}/4$. Thus we have proven that the smallest circuit to compute the parity of $n$ variables using only $\land, \lor, \lnot$ must use at least this many variables.[^o13]

### Influence {#Influence}

Although this is not an exceptionally strong lower bound, random restrictions have become an essential tool in complexity. In a similar vein to this proof, the exponent $3/2$ in the main lemma has been increased through careful analysis to $1.63$ by [Paterson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mike_Paterson) and [Zwick](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uri_Zwick) (1993) and then to $2$ by [Håstad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johan_Håstad) (1998).[^o9] Additionally, Håstad's [Switching lemma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switching_lemma) (1987) applied the same technique to the much richer model of constant depth [Boolean circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_circuits).
