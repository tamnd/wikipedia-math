# Paul Bernays

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Paul Bernays"}

**Paul Isaac Bernays** ( ; ; 17 October 1888 – 18 September 1977) was a [Swiss](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland) mathematician who made significant contributions to [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636), [axiomatic](wikipedia://en/928) [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), and the [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439). He was an assistant and close collaborator of [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302).

:::

## Biography {#Biography}

Bernays was born into a distinguished [German-Jewish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German-Jewish) family of scholars and businessmen. His great-grandfather, [Isaac ben Jacob Bernays](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isaac_ben_Jacob_Bernays), served as chief rabbi of Hamburg from 1821 to 1849.

Bernays spent his childhood in Berlin, and attended the [Köllnische Gymnasium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Köllnisches_Gymnasium), 1895–1907. At the [University of Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Berlin), he studied mathematics under [Issai Schur](wikipedia://en/617522), [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230), [Ferdinand Georg Frobenius](wikipedia://en/341048), and [Friedrich Schottky](wikipedia://en/5872505); philosophy under [Alois Riehl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alois_Riehl), [Carl Stumpf](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carl_Stumpf) and [Ernst Cassirer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Cassirer); and physics under [Max Planck](wikipedia://en/19848). At the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen), he studied mathematics under [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302), [Edmund Landau](wikipedia://en/222230), [Hermann Weyl](wikipedia://en/187544), and [Felix Klein](wikipedia://en/41887); physics under [Woldemar Voigt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Woldemar_Voigt) and [Max Born](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Born); and philosophy under [Leonard Nelson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonard_Nelson).

In 1912, the [University of Göttingen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Göttingen) awarded him a Ph.D. in mathematics for a thesis, supervised by Landau, on the [analytic number theory](wikipedia://en/251513) of [binary quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/1867129). That same year, the [University of Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Zurich) awarded him [habilitation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Habilitation) for a thesis on [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) and [Picard's theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picard's_theorem). The examiner was [Ernst Zermelo](wikipedia://en/178655). Bernays was Privatdozent at the University of Zurich, 1912–1917, where he came to know [George Pólya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Pólya). His collected communications with [Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736) span many decades.

Starting in 1917, [David Hilbert](wikipedia://en/8302) employed Bernays to assist him with his investigations of the foundation of arithmetic. Bernays also lectured on other areas of mathematics at the University of Göttingen. In 1918, that university awarded him a second habilitation for a thesis on the axiomatics of the [propositional calculus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) of *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)*.[^o5]

In 1922, Göttingen appointed Bernays extraordinary professor without tenure. His most successful student there was [Gerhard Gentzen](wikipedia://en/46027). After Nazi Germany enacted the [Law for the Restoration of the Professional Civil Service](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_for_the_Restoration_of_the_Professional_Civil_Service) in 1933, the university fired Bernays because of his Jewish ancestry.

After working privately for Hilbert for six months, Bernays and his family moved to [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland), whose nationality he had inherited from his father, and where the [ETH Zurich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETH_Zurich) employed him on occasion. He also visited the [University of Pennsylvania](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Pennsylvania) and was a visiting scholar at the [Institute for Advanced Study](wikipedia://en/184179) in 1935–36 and again in 1959–60.[^o6]

## Mathematical work {#Mathematical_work}

His habilitation thesis was written under the supervision of Hilbert himself, on the topic of the axiomatisation of propositional logic in [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) and [Russell](wikipedia://en/4163)'s [Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133). It contains the first known proof of [semantic completeness](wikipedia://en/31271451) of propositional logic, which was proved again independently also by [Emil Post](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Emil_Post) later on.

Bernays's collaboration with Hilbert culminated in the two volume work, *[Grundlagen der Mathematik](wikipedia://en/30709197)* (English: *Foundations of Mathematics*) published in 1934 and 1939, which is discussed in Sieg and Ravaglia (2005). A proof in this work that a sufficiently strong consistent theory cannot contain its own reference [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) is known as the [Hilbert–Bernays paradox](wikipedia://en/44168089).

In seven papers, published between 1937 and 1954 in the *[Journal of Symbolic Logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Journal_of_Symbolic_Logic)* (republished in Müller 1976), Bernays set out an axiomatic set theory whose starting point was a related theory [John von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942) had set out in the 1920s. Von Neumann's theory took the notions of [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Function_%28set_theory%29) and [argument](wikipedia://en/32842052) as primitive. Bernays recast von Neumann's theory so that [classes](wikipedia://en/20696) and [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) were primitive. Bernays's theory, with modifications by [Kurt Gödel](wikipedia://en/16736), is known as [von Neumann–Bernays–Gödel set theory](wikipedia://en/528491).
