# Statistical randomness

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Statistical randomness"}

*Image: Josef\_Wenzel\_Süss\_Jungen\_beim\_Würfelspiel.jpg.* Dice rolls are a standard example of statistical randomness.

A numeric [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) is said to be **statistically random** when it contains no recognizable [patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pattern) or regularities; sequences such as the results of an ideal [dice roll](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dice) or the digits of [π](wikipedia://en/23601) exhibit statistical randomness.[^o2]

:::

Statistical randomness does not necessarily imply "true" [randomness](wikipedia://en/19196523), i.e., objective [unpredictability](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Predictability). [Pseudorandomness](wikipedia://en/23210) is sufficient for many uses, such as statistics, hence the name *statistical* randomness.

*Global randomness* and *local randomness* are different. Most philosophical conceptions of randomness are global—because they are based on the idea that "in the long run" a sequence looks truly random, even if certain sub-sequences would *not* look random. In a "truly" random sequence of numbers of sufficient length, for example, it is probable there would be long sequences of nothing but repeating numbers, though on the whole the sequence might be random. *Local* randomness refers to the idea that there can be minimum sequence lengths in which random distributions are approximated. Long stretches of the same numbers, even those generated by "truly" random processes, would diminish the "local randomness" of a sample (it might only be locally random for sequences of 10,000 numbers; taking sequences of less than 1,000 might not appear random at all, for example).

A sequence exhibiting a pattern is not thereby proved not statistically random. According to principles of [Ramsey theory](wikipedia://en/157175), sufficiently large objects must necessarily contain a given substructure ("[complete disorder is impossible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_disorder_is_impossible)").

Legislation concerning [gambling](wikipedia://en/11921) imposes certain standards of statistical randomness to [slot machines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Slot_machine).

## Tests {#Tests}

The first tests for random numbers were published by [M.G. Kendall](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/M.G._Kendall) and [Bernard Babington Smith](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernard_Babington_Smith) in the *[Journal of the Royal Statistical Society](wikipedia://en/13327143)* in 1938.[^o4] They were built on statistical tools such as [Pearson's chi-squared test](wikipedia://en/226676) that were developed to distinguish whether experimental phenomena matched their theoretical probabilities. Pearson developed his test originally by showing that a number of dice experiments by [W.F.R. Weldon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/W.F.R._Weldon) did not display "random" behavior.

Kendall and Smith's original four tests were [hypothesis tests](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_hypothesis_testing), which took as their [null hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Null_hypothesis) the idea that each number in a given random sequence had an equal chance of occurring, and that various other patterns in the data should be also distributed equiprobably.

- The *frequency test*, was very basic: checking to make sure that there were roughly the same number of 0s, 1s, 2s, 3s, etc.
- The *serial test*, did the same thing but for sequences of two digits at a time (00, 01, 02, etc.), comparing their observed frequencies with their hypothetical predictions were they equally distributed.
- The *poker test*, tested for certain sequences of five numbers at a time (AAAAA, AAAAB, AAABB, etc.) based on hands in the game [poker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Poker).
- The *gap test*, looked at the distances between zeroes (00 would be a distance of 0, 030 would be a distance of 1, 02250 would be a distance of 3, etc.).

If a given sequence was able to pass all of these tests within a given degree of significance (generally 5%), then it was judged to be, in their words "locally random". Kendall and Smith differentiated "local randomness" from "true randomness" in that many sequences generated with truly random *methods* might not display "local randomness" to a given degree — *very* large sequences might contain many rows of a single digit. This might be "random" on the scale of the entire sequence, but in a smaller block it would not be "random" (it would not pass their tests), and would be useless for a number of statistical applications.

As random number sets became more and more common, more tests, of increasing sophistication were used. Some modern tests plot random digits as points on a three-dimensional plane, which can then be rotated to look for hidden patterns. In 1995, the statistician [George Marsaglia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Marsaglia) created a set of tests known as the [diehard tests](wikipedia://en/2167005), which he distributes with a [CD-ROM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CD-ROM) of 5 billion [pseudorandom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudorandom) numbers. In 2015, [Yongge Wang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongge_Wang) distributed a Java software package [^o5] for statistically distance based randomness testing.

[Pseudorandom number generators](wikipedia://en/45524) require tests as exclusive verifications for their "randomness," as they are decidedly *not* produced by "truly random" processes, but rather by deterministic algorithms. Over the history of random number generation, many sources of numbers thought to appear "random" under testing have later been discovered to be very non-random when subjected to certain types of tests. The notion of [quasi-random](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasi-random) numbers was developed to circumvent some of these problems, though pseudorandom number generators are still extensively used in many applications (even ones known to be extremely "non-random"), as they are "good enough" for most applications.

Other tests:

- The [Monobit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monobit) test treats each output bit of the random number generator as a coin flip test, and determine if the observed number of heads and tails are close to the expected 50% frequency. The number of heads in a coin flip trail forms a [binomial distribution](wikipedia://en/3876).
- The [Wald–Wolfowitz runs test](wikipedia://en/3714048) tests for the number of bit transitions between 0 bits, and 1 bits, comparing the observed frequencies with expected frequency of a random bit sequence.
- [Information entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Information_entropy)
- [Autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) test
- [Kolmogorov–Smirnov test](wikipedia://en/16714)
- Statistically distance based randomness test. [Yongge Wang](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yongge_Wang) showed [^o6][^o7] that NIST SP800-22 testing standards are not sufficient to detect some weakness in randomness generators and proposed statistically distance based randomness test.
- [Spectral Density Estimation](wikipedia://en/13070117)[^o8] - performing a Fourier transform on a "random" signal transforms it into a sum of periodic functions in order to detect non random repetitive trends
- Maurer's Universal Statistical Test
- The [Diehard tests](wikipedia://en/2167005)
