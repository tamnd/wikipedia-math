# Cunningham number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Cunningham number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), specifically in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), a **Cunningham number** is a certain kind of [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) named after English mathematician [A. J. C. Cunningham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Allan_Joseph_Champneys_Cunningham).

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Cunningham numbers are a simple type of [binomial number](wikipedia://en/4861681) – they are of the form

$$b^n\pm1$$

where *b* and *n* are integers and *b* is not a [perfect power](wikipedia://en/4023591). They are denoted *C*<sup>±</sup>(*b*, *n*).

:::

## Terms {#Terms}

The Cunningham numbers are:

3, 5, 7, 8, 9, 10, 15, 17, 24, 26, 28, 31, 33, 35, 37, 48, 50, 63, 65, 80, 82, 99, 101, 120, 122, 124, 126, 127, 129, 143, 145, 168, 170, 195, 197, ...

The Cunningham numbers *C*<sup>−</sup>(*b*, *n*) are:

3, 7, 8, 15, 24, 26, 31, 35, 48, 63, 80, 99, 120, 124, 127, 143, 168, 195, 215, 224, 242, 255, 288, 323, 342, 360, 399, 440, 483, ...

The Cunningham numbers *C*<sup>\+</sup>(*b*, *n*) are:

5, 9, 10, 17, 26, 28, 33, 37, 50, 65, 82, 101, 122, 126, 129, 145, 170, 197, 217, 226, 244, 257, 290, 325, 344, 362, 401, 442, 485, ...

It is conjectured that 26 is the only number that is both a Cunningham number *C*<sup>−</sup>(*b*, *n*) and a Cunningham number *C*<sup>\+</sup>(*b*, *n*), this conjecture is equivalent to that 25 and 27 are the only two [perfect powers](wikipedia://en/4023591) differ by 2 (see [Pillai's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pillai's_conjecture)). Note that the proven [Catalan's conjecture](wikipedia://en/62382) is that 8 and 9 are the only two [perfect powers](wikipedia://en/4023591) differ by 1.

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- There are infinitely many [even and odd](wikipedia://en/143135) Cunningham numbers. Provable through observation that the infinite series $6^n\pm1$ and $5^n\pm1$ are both contained within the Cunningham numbers, and contain only odd and even numbers respectively.
  - By the same logic, there are infinitely many Cunningham numbers which are 5 [modulo](wikipedia://en/1352566) 10, 7 modulo 10, 4 modulo 10, or 6 modulo 10. In fact, for every [modulo number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integers_mod_n) $n<36$, there are infinitely many Cunningham numbers congruent to every residue mod $n$, however, for $n=36$, there is no Cunningham number congruent to 11, 13, 23, or 25 mod 36, this is because for the numbers *m* = 11, 13, 23, or 25 mod 36, neither *m*+1 nor *m*−1 can be [powerful](wikipedia://en/462499).
- Although by the proven [Catalan's conjecture](wikipedia://en/62382), 8 and 9 are the only two Cunningham numbers that are [perfect powers](wikipedia://en/4023591), there are infinitely many Cunningham numbers that are [powerful numbers](wikipedia://en/462499), since if *m* is a Cunningham number *C*<sup>−</sup>(*b*, *n*) that is a powerful number (such as 8), then so is $4m(m+1)$. The first ten such Cunningham numbers are:
  - 8 = *C*<sup>−</sup>(3,2)
  - 9 = *C*<sup>\+</sup>(2,3)
  - 288 = *C*<sup>−</sup>(17,2)
  - 675 = *C*<sup>−</sup>(26,2)
  - 9800 = *C*<sup>−</sup>(99,2)
  - 12168 = *C*<sup>\+</sup>(23,3)
  - 235224 = *C*<sup>−</sup>(485,2)
  - 332928 = *C*<sup>−</sup>(577,2)
  - 465125 = *C*<sup>\+</sup>(682,2)
  - 1825200 = *C*<sup>−</sup>(1351,2)

:::

## Primality {#Primality}

Establishing whether or not a given Cunningham number is [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) has been the main focus of research around this type of number.[^o8] Two particularly famous families of Cunningham numbers in this respect are the [Fermat numbers](wikipedia://en/91127), which are those of the form *C*<sup>\+</sup>(2, 2<sup>*m*</sup>), and the [Mersenne numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mersenne_numbers), which are those of the form *C*<sup>−</sup>(2, *p*) with prime *p*.

The prime Cunningham numbers are:

3, 5, 7, 17, 31, 37, 101, 127, 197, 257, 401, 577, 677, 1297, 1601, 2917, 3137, 4357, 5477, 7057, 8101, 8191, 8837, ...

In fact, these numbers are exactly the [generalized Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_Fermat_prime) (which are those of the form *C*<sup>\+</sup>(*b*, 2<sup>*m*</sup>) with even *b*) and the [Mersenne primes](wikipedia://en/18908) (which are those of the form *C*<sup>−</sup>(2, *p*) with prime *p*), since if the Cunningham number *C*<sup>−</sup>(*b*, *n*) is prime, then *b* = 2 and *n* is prime, and if the Cunningham number *C*<sup>\+</sup>(*b*, *n*) is prime, then *b* is even and *n* is [power of 2](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_of_2), this is because the [algebraic factorization](wikipedia://en/82341) of the [binomial numbers](wikipedia://en/4861681), such as [difference of two squares](wikipedia://en/1102297) and [sum of two cubes](wikipedia://en/72077175).

Cunningham worked on gathering together all known data on which of these numbers were prime. In 1925 he published tables which summarised his findings with [H. J. Woodall](wikipedia://en/1561620), and much computation has been done in the intervening time to fill these tables.[^o9]
