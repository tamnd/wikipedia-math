# Fictitious play

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Fictitious play"}

In [game theory](wikipedia://en/11924), **fictitious play** is a [learning rule](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Learning_rule) that describes how players might learn over time in repeated [strategic interactions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strategic_interaction). In fictitious play, each player assumes that opponents are using stationary (possibly [mixed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mixed_strategy)) strategies, and responds optimally to the historical [empirical distribution](wikipedia://en/2690455) of their opponents' past actions. Specifically, at each round, a player calculates the empirical frequency of each strategy their opponents have played in previous rounds and selects their own [best response](wikipedia://en/759831) to these frequencies.

:::

This approach provides a simple model of [bounded rationality](wikipedia://en/70400) in which players gradually learn about their strategic environment through repeated observation. Fictitious play converges to [Nash equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45337) in several important classes of games, including [zero-sum games](wikipedia://en/34417), [potential games](wikipedia://en/9394324), and games with [dominant strategies](wikipedia://en/2352847). However, the method has notable limitations when opponents employ non-stationary or adaptive strategies. For example, if an opponent conditions their play on the fictitious player's recent moves or deliberately exploits the predictable nature of the best-response pattern, the fictitious play approach may fail to converge or may be systematically exploited.

It was first introduced by mathematician [George W. Brown](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Brown_%28academic%29) in 1951.[^o2]

## History {#History}

Brown first introduced fictitious play as an explanation for [Nash equilibrium](wikipedia://en/45337) play. He imagined that a player would "simulate" play of the game in their mind and update their future play based on this simulation; hence the name *fictitious* play. In terms of current use, the name is a bit of a misnomer, since each play of the game actually occurs. The play is not exactly fictitious.

## Convergence properties {#Convergence_properties}

In fictitious play, strict [Nash equilibria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nash_equilibria) are [absorbing states](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absorbing_state). That is, if at any time period all the players play a Nash equilibrium, then they will do so for all subsequent rounds. (Fudenberg and Levine 1998, Proposition 2.1) In addition, if fictitious play converges to any distribution, those probabilities correspond to a Nash equilibrium of the underlying game. (Proposition 2.2)

Therefore, the interesting question is, under what circumstances does fictitious play converge? The process will converge for a 2-person game if:

1. Both players have only a finite number of strategies and the game is [zero sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_sum) (Robinson 1951)
1. The game is solvable by iterated elimination of [strictly dominated strategies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dominance_%28game_theory%29) (Nachbar 1990)
1. The game is a [potential game](wikipedia://en/9394324) (Monderer and Shapley 1996-a,1996-b)
1. The game has generic payoffs and is 2 × *N* (Berger 2005)

Fictitious play does not always converge, however. Shapley (1964) proved that in the game pictured here (a nonzero-sum version of [Rock, Paper, Scissors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_paper_scissors)), if the players start by choosing *(a, B)*, the play will cycle indefinitely.

## Terminology {#Terminology}

Berger (2007) states that "what modern game theorists describe as 'fictitious play' is not the learning process that George W. Brown defined in his 1951 paper": Brown's "original version differs in a subtle detail..." in that modern usage involves the players updating their beliefs *simultaneously*, whereas Brown described the players updating *alternatingly*. Berger then uses Brown's original form to present a simple and intuitive proof of convergence in the case of two-player nondegenerate ordinal [potential games](wikipedia://en/9394324).

The term "fictitious" had earlier been given another meaning in game theory. Von Neumann and Morgenstern \[1944\] defined a "fictitious player" as a player with only one strategy, added to an *n*-player game to turn it into a (*n* + 1)-player zero-sum game.
