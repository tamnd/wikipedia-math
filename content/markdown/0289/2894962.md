# Miura fold

*Image: Miura-Ori CP.svg.* [Crease pattern](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crease_pattern) for a Miura fold. The parallelograms of this example have 84° and 96° angles.

*Image: Miura-ori.gif.* Animation of the folding and unfolding of a Miura-creased material

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Miura fold"}

The **Miura fold** is a method of folding a flat surface such as a sheet of paper into a smaller area. The fold is named for its inventor, Japanese astrophysicist [Kōryō Miura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kōryō_Miura).[^o2]

:::

The [crease patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crease_pattern) of the Miura fold form a [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) of the surface by [parallelograms](wikipedia://en/133496). In one direction, the creases lie along straight lines, with each parallelogram forming the [mirror reflection](wikipedia://en/1390873) of its neighbor across each crease. In the other direction, the creases zigzag, and each parallelogram is the [translation](wikipedia://en/204682) of its neighbor across the crease. Each of the zigzag paths of creases consists solely of [mountain folds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mountain_fold) or of [valley folds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Valley_fold), with mountains alternating with valleys from one zigzag path to the next. Each of the straight paths of creases alternates between mountain and valley folds.[^o3] The Miura fold is related to the [Kresling fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kresling_fold), the [Yoshimura fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshimura_fold) and the Hexagonal fold, and can be framed as a generalization of these folds.[^o4]

The Miura fold is a form of [rigid origami](wikipedia://en/20332029), meaning that the fold can be carried out by a continuous motion in which, at each step, each parallelogram is completely flat. This property allows it to be used to fold surfaces made of rigid materials, making it distinct from the [Kresling fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kresling_fold) and [Yoshimura fold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yoshimura_fold) which cannot be rigidly folded and require panel [deformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_%28science%29) to compress to a compact state.[^o5] For instance, large [solar panel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_panel) arrays for space satellites in the [Japanese space program](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japanese_space_program) have been Miura folded before launch and then spread out in space.[^o6] A folded Miura fold can be packed into a compact shape, its thickness reflecting only the thickness of the folded material. Folded material can be unpacked in one motion by pulling on its opposite ends, and likewise folded by pushing the two ends together. In the solar array application, this property reduces the number of [motors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_motor) required to unfold this shape, reducing weight and complexity.

::: {#o8 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

The 1996 [Space Flyer Unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space_Flyer_Unit) deployed the 2D Array from a Miura folded configuration.[^o9]

The inflatable membrane structure of the SPROUT satellite is carried into space in the Miura-folded state, and then deployed using inflatable tubes themselves carried into space in the Octagon-folded state.[^o10][^o11]

Other potential applications of this fold include surgical devices such as [stents](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stent) and flat-foldable furniture.[^o12]

Researchers at the [University of Fribourg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Fribourg) used the Miura fold to stack [hydrogel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogel) films, generating electricity similarly to [electric eels](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electric_eel). The Miura fold is used to cause many parts of the stack to contact each other simultaneously.[^o13]
