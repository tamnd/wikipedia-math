# Sociable number

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Sociable number"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **sociable numbers** are numbers whose [aliquot sums](wikipedia://en/12374274#Definition) form a [periodic sequence](wikipedia://en/810250). They are generalizations of the concepts of [perfect numbers](wikipedia://en/23670) and [amicable numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amicable_number). The first two sociable sequences, or sociable chains, were discovered and named by the [Belgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Belgium) [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) [Paul Poulet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Poulet_%28mathematician%29) in 1918.[^o2] In a sociable sequence, each number is the sum of the [proper divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_divisors) of the preceding number, i.e., the sum excludes the preceding number itself. For the sequence to be sociable, the sequence must be cyclic and return to its starting point.

:::

The [period](wikipedia://en/76848) of the sequence, or order of the set of sociable numbers, is the number of numbers in this cycle.

If the period of the sequence is 1, the number is a sociable number of order 1, or a [perfect number](wikipedia://en/23670)—for example, the [proper divisors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_divisor) of 6 are 1, 2, and 3, whose sum is again 6. A pair of [amicable numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amicable_number) is a set of sociable numbers of order 2. There are no known sociable numbers of order 3, and searches for them have been made up to $5 \times 10^7$ as of 1970.[^o3]

It is an open question whether all numbers end up at either a sociable number or at a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) (and hence 1), or, equivalently, whether there exist numbers whose [aliquot sequence](wikipedia://en/486266) never terminates, and hence grows without bound.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

As an example, the number 1,264,460 is a sociable number whose cyclic aliquot sequence has a period of 4:

The sum of the proper divisors of $1264460$ ($=2^2\cdot5\cdot17\cdot3719$) is

1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 10 + 17 + 20 + 34 + 68 + 85 + 170 + 340 + 3719 + 7438 + 14876 + 18595 + 37190 + 63223 + 74380 + 126446 + 252892 + 316115 + 632230 = 1547860,

the sum of the proper divisors of $1547860$ ($=2^2\cdot5\cdot193\cdot401$) is

1 + 2 + 4 + 5 + 10 + 20 + 193 + 386 + 401 + 772 + 802 + 965 + 1604 + 1930 + 2005 + 3860 + 4010 + 8020 + 77393 + 154786 + 309572 + 386965 + 773930 = 1727636,

the sum of the proper divisors of $1727636$ ($=2^2\cdot521\cdot829$) is

1 + 2 + 4 + 521 + 829 + 1042 + 1658 + 2084 + 3316 + 431909 + 863818 = 1305184, and

the sum of the proper divisors of $1305184$ ($=2^5\cdot40787$) is

1 + 2 + 4 + 8 + 16 + 32 + 40787 + 81574 + 163148 + 326296 + 652592 = 1264460.

:::

## Searching for sociable numbers {#Searching_for_sociable_numbers}

The [aliquot sequence](wikipedia://en/486266) can be represented as a [directed graph](wikipedia://en/19721986), $G_{n,s}$, for a given integer $n$, where $s(k)$ denotes the sum of the proper divisors of $k$.[^o10] [Cycles](wikipedia://en/168609) in $G_{n,s}$ represent sociable numbers within the interval $[1,n]$. Two special cases are loops that represent [perfect numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perfect_numbers) and cycles of length two that represent [amicable pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amicable_pairs).

::: {#o12 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Conjecture of the sum of sociable number cycles"}

## Conjecture of the sum of sociable number cycles {#Conjecture_of_the_sum_of_sociable_number_cycles}

It is conjectured that as the number of sociable number cycles with length greater than 2 approaches infinity, the proportion of the sums of the sociable number cycles divisible by 10 approaches 1 .

:::
