# Kernel (set theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Kernel"}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), the **kernel** of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f$ (or **equivalence kernel**[^o2]) may be taken to be either

:::

- the [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) on the function's [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) that roughly expresses the idea of "equivalent as far as the function $f$ can tell",[^o3] or
- the corresponding [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of the domain.

An unrelated notion is that of the **kernel** of a non-empty [family of sets](wikipedia://en/723043) $\mathcal{B},$ which by definition is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/41780044) of all its elements:

$$\ker \mathcal{B} ~=~ \bigcap_{B \in \mathcal{B}} \, B.$$

This definition is used in the theory of [filters](wikipedia://en/12646549) to classify them as being [free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_filter) or [principal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Principal_filter).

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

**Kernel of a function**

For the formal definition, let $f : X \to Y$ be a function between two [sets](wikipedia://en/26691). Elements $x_1, x_2 \in X$ are *equivalent* if and only if $f\left(x_1\right)$ and $f\left(x_2\right)$ are [equal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equal_%28math%29), that is, are the same element of $Y.$ The kernel of $f$ is the equivalence relation thus defined.[^o3]

**Kernel of a family of sets**

The kernel of a family of sets is[^n1]

$$\ker \mathcal{B} ~:=~ \bigcap_{B \in \mathcal{B}} B.$$

The kernel of $\mathcal{B}$ is also sometimes denoted by $\cap \mathcal{B}.$ The kernel of the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566), $\ker \varnothing,$ is typically left undefined. A family is called fixed and is said to have non-empty intersection if its kernel is not empty.[^n1] A family is said to be free if it is not fixed; that is, if its kernel is the empty set.[^n1]

:::

## Quotients {#Quotients}

Like any equivalence relation, the kernel can be [modded out](wikipedia://en/25977) to form a [quotient set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set), and the quotient set is the partition:

$$\left\{\, \{w \in X : f(x) = f(w)\} ~:~ x \in X \,\right\} ~=~ \left\{f^{-1}(y) ~:~ y \in f(X)\right\}.$$

This quotient set $X /=_f$ is called the *[coimage](wikipedia://en/632685)* of the function $f,$ and denoted $\operatorname{coim} f$ (or a variation). The coimage is [naturally isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_isomorphism) (in the set-theoretic sense of a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942)) to the [image](wikipedia://en/579311), $\operatorname{im} f;$ specifically, the [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of $x$ in $X$ (which is an element of $\operatorname{coim} f$) corresponds to $f(x)$ in $Y$ (which is an element of $\operatorname{im} f$).

## As a subset of the Cartesian product {#As_a_subset_of_the_Cartesian_product}

Like any [binary relation](wikipedia://en/3931), the kernel of a function may be thought of as a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) $X \times X.$ In this guise, the kernel may be denoted $\ker f$ (or a variation) and may be defined symbolically as[^o3]

$$\ker f := \{(x,x') : f(x) = f(x')\}.$$

The study of the properties of this subset can shed light on $f.$

## Algebraic structures {#Algebraic_structures}

If $X$ and $Y$ are [algebraic structures](wikipedia://en/106364) of some fixed type (such as [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), [rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_%28algebra%29), or [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370)), and if the function $f : X \to Y$ is a [homomorphism](wikipedia://en/13564), then $\ker f$ is a [congruence relation](wikipedia://en/48167) (that is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) that is compatible with the algebraic structure), and the coimage of $f$ is a [quotient](wikipedia://en/310953) of $X.$[^o3] The bijection between the coimage and the image of $f$ is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828) in the algebraic sense; this is the most general form of the [first isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_isomorphism_theorem).

## In topology {#In_topology}

If $f : X \to Y$ is a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) between two [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) then the topological properties of $\ker f$ can shed light on the spaces $X$ and $Y.$ For example, if $Y$ is a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) then $\ker f$ must be a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279). Conversely, if $X$ is a Hausdorff space and $\ker f$ is a closed set, then the coimage of $f,$ if given the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) topology, must also be a Hausdorff space.

A [space](wikipedia://en/30450) is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) if and only if the kernel of every family of [closed subsets](wikipedia://en/47279) having the [finite intersection property](wikipedia://en/365026) (FIP) is non-empty;[^o13] said differently, a space is compact if and only if every family of closed subsets with F.I.P. is fixed.
