# Riemann–Stieltjes integral

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Riemann–Stieltjes integral"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Riemann–Stieltjes integral** is a generalization of the [Riemann integral](wikipedia://en/26390), named after [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) and [Thomas Joannes Stieltjes](wikipedia://en/40589). The definition of this integral was first published in 1894 by Stieltjes.[^n1] It serves as an instructive and useful precursor of the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288), and an invaluable tool in unifying equivalent forms of statistical theorems that apply to discrete and continuous probability.

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

The Riemann–Stieltjes [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) of a [real-valued function](wikipedia://en/22284647) $f$ of a real variable on the interval $[a,b]$ with respect to another real-to-real function $g$ is denoted by

$$\int_{x=a}^b f(x) \, \mathrm{d}g(x).$$

Its definition uses a sequence of [partitions](wikipedia://en/471332) $P$ of the interval $[a,b]$

$$P=\{ a = x_0 < x_1 < \cdots < x_n = b\}.$$

The integral, then, is defined to be the limit, as the [mesh](wikipedia://en/471332#Norm_of_a_partition) (the length of the longest subinterval) of the partitions approaches $0$, of the approximating sum

$$S(P,f,g) = \sum_{i=1}^{n} f(c_i)\left[ g(x_{i}) - g(x_{i-1}) \right]$$

where $c_i$ is in the $i$-th subinterval $[x_{i-1},x_{i}]$. The two functions $f$ and $g$ are respectively called the *integrand* and the *integrator*. Typically $g$ is taken to be [monotone](wikipedia://en/48260) (or at least of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673)) and [right-semicontinuous](wikipedia://en/57824) (however this last is essentially convention). We specifically do not require $g$ to be continuous, which allows for integrals that have point mass terms.

The "limit" is here understood to be a number *A* (the value of the Riemann–Stieltjes integral) such that for every *ε* > 0, there exists *δ* > 0 such that for every partition *P* with mesh(*P*) \< *δ*, and for every choice of points *c*<sub>*i*</sub> in \[*x*<sub>*i*−1</sub>, *x*<sub>*i*</sub>\],

$$|S(P,f,g)-A| < \varepsilon. \,$$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The Riemann–Stieltjes integral admits [integration by parts](wikipedia://en/147252) in the form

$$\int_a^b f(x) \, \mathrm{d}g(x)=f(b)g(b)-f(a)g(a)-\int_a^b g(x) \, \mathrm{d}f(x)$$

and the existence of either integral implies the existence of the other.[^n2]

On the other hand, a classical result[^n3] shows that the integral is well-defined if *f* is *α*-[Hölder continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hölder_continuous) and *g* is *β*-Hölder continuous with *α* + *β* > 1 .

If $f$ is bounded on $[a,b]$, $g$ differentiable, monotonically increasing, and $g'$ is Riemann integrable, then the Riemann–Stieltjes integral is related to the Riemann integral by

$$\int_a^b f(x) \, \mathrm{d}g(x) = \int_a^b f(x) g'(x) \, \mathrm{d}x$$

For a step function

$$g(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } x \leq s \\ 1 & \text{if } x > s \\ \end{cases}$$

where $a < s < b$, if $f$ is continuous at $s$, then

$$\int_a^b f(x) \, \mathrm{d}g(x) = f(s)$$

:::

## Application to probability theory {#Application_to_probability_theory}

If *g* is the [cumulative probability distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) *X* that has a [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) with respect to [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), and *f* is any function for which the [expected value](wikipedia://en/9653) $\operatorname{E}\left[\,\left|f(X)\right|\,\right]$ is finite, then the probability density function of *X* is the derivative of *g* and we have

$$\operatorname{E}[f(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)g'(x)\,\mathrm{d}x.$$

But this formula does not work if *X* does not have a probability density function with respect to Lebesgue measure. In particular, it does not work if the distribution of *X* is discrete (i.e., all of the probability is accounted for by point-masses), and even if the cumulative distribution function *g* is continuous, it does not work if *g* fails to be [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670) (again, the [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819) may serve as an example of this failure). But the identity

$$\operatorname{E}[f(X)]=\int_{-\infty}^\infty f(x)\, \mathrm{d}g(x)$$

holds if *g* is *any* cumulative probability distribution function on the real line, no matter how ill-behaved. In particular, no matter how ill-behaved the cumulative distribution function *g* of a random variable *X*, if the [moment](wikipedia://en/368684) E(*X*<sup>*n*</sup>) exists, then it is equal to

$$\operatorname{E}\left[X^n\right] = \int_{-\infty}^\infty x^n\,\mathrm{d}g(x).$$

## Application to functional analysis {#Application_to_functional_analysis}

The Riemann–Stieltjes integral appears in the original formulation of [F. Riesz's theorem](wikipedia://en/38948504) which represents the [dual space](wikipedia://en/7988) of the [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) *C*\[*a*,*b*\] of continuous functions in an interval \[*a*,*b*\] as Riemann–Stieltjes integrals against functions of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673). Later, that theorem was reformulated in terms of measures.

The Riemann–Stieltjes integral also appears in the formulation of the [spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/123495) for (non-compact) self-adjoint (or more generally, normal) operators in a Hilbert space. In this theorem, the integral is considered with respect to a spectral family of projections.[^o11]

## Existence of the integral {#Existence_of_the_integral}

The best simple existence theorem states that if *f* is continuous and *g* is of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673) on \[*a*, *b*\], then the integral exists.[^n4][^n5][^n6] Because of the integration by part formula, the integral exists also if the condition on *f* and *g* are inversed, that is, if *f* is of bounded variation and *g* is continuous.

A function *g* is of bounded variation if and only if it is the difference between two (bounded) monotone functions. If *g* is not of bounded variation, then there will be continuous functions which cannot be integrated with respect to *g*. In general, the integral is not well-defined if *f* and *g* share any points of [discontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discontinuity_%28mathematics%29), but there are other cases as well.

## Geometric interpretation {#Geometric_interpretation}

A 3D plot, with *x*, *f(x)*, and *g(x)* all along orthogonal axes, leads to a geometric interpretation of the Riemann–Stieltjes integral.[^o17]

*Image: Basic geometry of riemann-stieljes integral f g x.png.* The basic geometry of the Riemann-Stieltjes integral.

If the *g(x)-x* plane is horizontal and the *f(x)*-direction is pointing upward, then the surface to be considered is like a curved fence. The fence follows the curve traced by *g(x)*, and the height of the fence is given by *f(x)*. The fence is the section of the *g(x)*-sheet (i.e., the *g(x)* curve extended along the *f(x)* axis) that is bounded between the *g(x)-x* plane and the *f(x)*-sheet. The Riemann-Stieltjes integral is the area of the projection of this fence onto the *f(x)-g(x)* plane — in effect, its "shadow".

The slope of *g(x)* weights the area of the projection. The values of *x* for which *g(x)* has the steepest slope *g'(x)* correspond to regions of the fence with the greater projection and thereby carry the most weight in the integral.

*Image: Curvature effects on geometry of riemann-stieljes integral f g x.png.* The effects of curvature in *g(x)* on the geometry of the Riemann-Stieltjes integral.

When *g* is a step function

$$g(x) = \begin{cases} 0 & \text{if } x \leq s \\ 1 & \text{if } x > s \\ \end{cases}$$

the fence has a rectangular "gate" of width 1 and height equal to *f(s)*. Thus the gate, and its projection, have area equal to *f(s)*, the value of the Riemann-Stieltjes integral.

*Image: Step function effect on geometry of riemann-stieljes integral f g x.png.* The effect of a step function $g(x)$ on the geometry of the Riemann-Stieltjes integral.

## Generalization {#Generalization}

An important generalization is the [Lebesgue&ndash;Stieltjes integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue&ndash;Stieltjes_integral), which generalizes the Riemann–Stieltjes integral in a way analogous to how the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288) generalizes the Riemann integral. If [improper](wikipedia://en/346030) Riemann–Stieltjes integrals are allowed, then the Lebesgue integral is not strictly more general than the Riemann–Stieltjes integral.

The Riemann–Stieltjes integral also generalizes to the case when either the integrand *ƒ* or the integrator *g* take values in a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989). If *g* : \[*a*,*b*\] → *X* takes values in the Banach space *X*, then it is natural to assume that it is of **strongly bounded variation**, meaning that

$$\sup \sum_i {\| g(t_{i-1})-g(t_i) \|}_X < \infty$$

the supremum being taken over all finite partitions

$$a=t_0\le t_1\le\cdots\le t_n=b$$

of the interval \[*a*,*b*\]. This generalization plays a role in the study of [semigroups](wikipedia://en/1644938), via the [Laplace–Stieltjes transform](wikipedia://en/365929).

The [Itô integral](wikipedia://en/1498680) extends the Riemann–Stietjes integral to encompass integrands and integrators which are [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895) rather than simple functions; see also [stochastic calculus](wikipedia://en/228001).

::: {#o21 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Generalized Riemann-Stieltjes integral"}

### Generalized Riemann–Stieltjes integral {#Generalized_Riemann–Stieltjes_integral}

A slight generalization[^o22] is to consider in the above definition partitions *P* that *refine* another partition *P*<sub>*ε*</sub>, meaning that *P* arises from *P*<sub>*ε*</sub> by the addition of points, rather than from partitions with a finer mesh. Specifically, the **generalized Riemann–Stieltjes integral** of *f* with respect to *g* is a number *A* such that for every *ε* > 0 there exists a partition *P*<sub>*ε*</sub> such that for every partition *P* that refines *P*<sub>*ε*</sub>,

$$|S(P,f,g) - A| < \varepsilon \,$$

for every choice of points *c*<sub>*i*</sub> in \[*x*<sub>*i*</sub>, *x*<sub>*i*+1</sub>\].

This generalization exhibits the Riemann–Stieltjes integral as the [Moore–Smith limit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moore–Smith_limit) on the [directed set](wikipedia://en/9165) of partitions of \[*a*, *b*\] .[^n7][^o24]

A consequence is that with this definition, the integral $\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}g(x)$ can still be defined in cases where *f* and *g* have a point of discontinuity in common.

:::

### Darboux sums {#Darboux_sums}

The Riemann–Stieltjes integral can be efficiently handled using an appropriate generalization of [Darboux sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Darboux_sum). For a partition *P* and a nondecreasing function *g* on \[*a*, *b*\] define the upper Darboux sum of *f* with respect to *g* by

$$U(P,f,g) = \sum_{i=1}^n \,\, [\,g(x_i)-g(x_{i-1})\,] \,\sup_{x\in [x_{i-1},x_i]} f(x)$$

and the lower sum by

$$L(P,f,g) = \sum_{i=1}^n \,\, [\,g(x_i)-g(x_{i-1})\,] \,\inf_{x\in [x_{i-1},x_i]} f(x).$$

Then the generalized Riemann–Stieltjes of *f* with respect to *g* exists if and only if, for every ε > 0, there exists a partition *P* such that

$$U(P,f,g)-L(P,f,g) < \varepsilon.$$

Furthermore, *f* is Riemann–Stieltjes integrable with respect to *g* (in the classical sense) if

$\lim_{\operatorname{mesh}(P)\to 0} [\,U(P,f,g)-L(P,f,g)\,] = 0.\quad$[^n8]

:::

## Examples and special cases {#Examples_and_special_cases}

### Differentiable *g* {#Differentiable_g}

Given a $g(x)$ which is continuously [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) over $\mathbb{R}$ it can be shown that there is the equality Let $g$ be continuously differentiable on $\mathbb{R}$. Then,

$$\int_a^b f(x) \, \mathrm{d}g(x) = \int_a^b f(x)g'(x) \, \mathrm{d}x$$

where the integral on the right-hand side is the standard Riemann integral, assuming that $f$ can be integrated by the Riemann–Stieltjes integral.

More generally, the Riemann integral equals the Riemann–Stieltjes integral if $g$ is the [Lebesgue integral](wikipedia://en/26064288) of its derivative; in this case $g$ is said to be [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous).

It may be the case that $g$ has jump discontinuities, or may have derivative zero *almost* everywhere while still being continuous and increasing (for example, $g$ could be the [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819) or “Devil's staircase”), in either of which cases the Riemann–Stieltjes integral is not captured by any expression involving derivatives of *g*.

### Riemann integral {#Riemann_integral}

The standard Riemann integral is a special case of the Riemann–Stieltjes integral where $g(x) = x$.

### Rectifier {#Rectifier}

Consider the function $g(x) = \max\{ 0, x \}$ used in the study of [neural networks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neural_network), called a [*rectified linear unit* (ReLU)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rectifier_%28neural_networks%29). Then the Riemann–Stieltjes can be evaluated as

$$\int_a^b f(x)\,\mathrm{d}g(x) = \int_{g(a)}^{g(b)}f(x)\,\mathrm{d}x$$

where the integral on the right-hand side is the standard Riemann integral.

### Cavalieri integration {#Cavalieri_integration}

*Image: Riemann–Stieltjes integral.png.* Visualisation of the Cavalieri integral for the function $f(x)=(2x+8)^3$

[Cavalieri's principle](wikipedia://en/21697672) can be used to calculate areas bounded by curves using Riemann–Stieltjes integrals.[^o32] The integration strips of Riemann integration are replaced with strips that are non-rectangular in shape. The method is to transform a "Cavalieri region" with a transformation $h$, or to use $g = h^{-1}$ as integrand.

For a given function $f(x)$ on an interval $[a,b]$, a "translational function" $a(y)$ must intersect $(x,f(x ))$ exactly once for any shift in the interval. A "Cavalieri region" is then bounded by $f(x),a(y)$, the $x$-axis, and $b(y) = a(y) + (b-a)$. The area of the region is then

$$\int_{a(y)}^{b(y)} f(x) \, dx \ = \ \int_{a'}^{b'} f(x) \, dg(x) ,$$

where $a'$ and $b'$ are the $x$-values where $a(y)$ and $b(y)$ intersect $f(x)$.
