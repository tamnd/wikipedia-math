# Prime-counting function

*Image: PrimePi.svg.* The values of $\pi(n)$ for the first 60 positive integers

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Prime-counting function"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **prime-counting function** is the [function](wikipedia://en/185427) counting the number of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) less than or equal to some [real number](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x$.[^o2] It is denoted by $\pi(x)$ (unrelated to the [number $\pi$](wikipedia://en/23601)).

:::

A symmetric variant seen sometimes is $\pi_{0}(x)$, which is equal to $\pi(x) - \tfrac{1}{2}$ if $x$ is exactly a prime number, and equal to $\pi(x)$ otherwise. That is, the number of prime numbers less than $x$, plus half if $x$ equals a prime.

## Growth rate {#Growth_rate}

Of great interest in [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527) is the [growth rate](wikipedia://en/641995) of the prime-counting function.[^o5][^o6] It was [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) in the end of the 18th century by [Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) and by [Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) to be approximately

$$\frac{x}{\log x}$$

where $\log$ is the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), in the sense that

$$\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty} \frac{\pi(x)}{x/\log x}=1.$$

This statement is the [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692). An equivalent statement is

$$\lim_{x\rightarrow\infty}\frac{\pi(x)}{\operatorname{li}(x)}=1$$

where $\operatorname{li}$ is the [logarithmic integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logarithmic_integral) function. The prime number theorem was first proved in 1896 by [Jacques Hadamard](wikipedia://en/312675) and by [Charles de la Vallée Poussin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jean_de_la_Vallée-Poussin) independently, using properties of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) introduced by [Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) in 1859. Proofs of the prime number theorem not using the zeta function or [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) were found around 1948 by [Atle Selberg](wikipedia://en/2021) and by [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) (for the most part independently).[^o7]

### More precise estimates {#More_precise_estimates}

In 1899, [de la Vallée Poussin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Charles_Jean_de_la_Vallée_Poussin) proved that [^o9]

$$\pi(x) = \operatorname{li} (x) + O \left(x e^{-a\sqrt{\log x}}\right) \quad\text{as } x \to \infty$$

for some positive constant $a$. Here, $O(...)$ is the [big $O$ notation](wikipedia://en/44578).

More precise estimates of $\pi(x)$ are now known. For example, in 2002, [Kevin Ford](wikipedia://en/55700462) proved that[^o10]

$$\pi(x) = \operatorname{li} (x) + O \left(x \exp \left( -0.2098(\log x)^{3/5} (\log \log x)^{-1/5} \right) \right).$$

Mossinghoff and [Trudgian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Timothy_Trudgian) proved[^o11] an explicit upper bound for the difference between $\pi(x)$ and $\operatorname{li}(x)$:

$$\bigl| \pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x) \bigr| \le 0.2593 \frac{x}{(\log x)^{3/4}} \exp \left( -\sqrt{ \frac{\log x}{6.315} } \right) \quad \text{for } x \ge 229.$$

For values of $x$ that are not unreasonably large, $\operatorname{li}(x)$ is greater than $\pi(x)$. However, $\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)$ is known to change sign infinitely many times. For a discussion of this, see [Skewes' number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skewes'_number).

### Exact form {#Exact_form}

For $x>1$, let $\pi_0(x)=\pi(x)-1/2$ when $x$ is prime, and let $\pi_0(x)=\pi(x)$ otherwise. Riemann's explicit formula is most naturally stated in terms of the prime-power counting function $\Pi_0(x)$. In modern notation,

$$\Pi_0(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) -\sum_\rho \operatorname{li}(x^\rho) -\log 2 +\int_x^\infty \frac{dt}{t(t^2-1)\log t},$$

where the sum is over the non-trivial zeros $\rho$ of the Riemann zeta function, taken in the usual symmetric order. Möbius inversion then yields

$$\pi_0(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{n}\, \Pi_0\!\left(x^{1/n}\right).$$

This is the form used, for example, in treatments of Riemann's prime-number formula such as Riesel and Göhl.

Writing the result instead in the frequently quoted form involving $\operatorname{R}(x^\rho)$ requires an additional interchange between the Möbius summation and the sum over the zeros of the Riemann zeta function. Since the relevant series are not absolutely convergent, this interchange does not follow from Möbius inversion alone and requires a separate justification.[^o13][^o14] Its validity in this form has consequently been questioned in the literature.[^o13][^o14] More recently, a preprint has claimed that, under the standard symmetric ordering of the non-trivial zeros, the corresponding partial sums are in fact unbounded.[^o15]

## Algorithms for evaluating $\pi(x)$ {#Algorithms_for_evaluating}

A simple way to find $\pi(x)$, if $x$ is not too large, is to use the [sieve of Eratosthenes](wikipedia://en/73415) to produce the primes less than or equal to $x$ and then to count them.

A more elaborate way of finding $\pi(x)$ is due to [Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) (using the [inclusion–exclusion principle](wikipedia://en/342684)): given $x$, if $p_{1}, p_{2},\ldots, p_{n}$ are distinct prime numbers, then the number of integers less than or equal to $x$ which are divisible by no $p_{i}$ is

$$\lfloor x\rfloor - \sum_{i}\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p_i}\right\rfloor + \sum_{i<j} \left\lfloor\frac{x}{p_ip_j}\right\rfloor - \sum_{i<j<k}\left\lfloor\frac{x}{p_ip_jp_k}\right\rfloor + \cdots$$

(where $\lfloor x\rfloor$ denotes the [floor function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Floor_function)). This number is therefore equal to

$$\pi(x)-\pi\left(\sqrt{x}\right)+1$$

when the numbers $p_{1}, p_{2},\ldots, p_{n}$ are the prime numbers less than or equal to the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of $x$.

### The Meissel–Lehmer algorithm {#The_Meissel–Lehmer_algorithm}

In a series of articles published between 1870 and 1885, [Ernst Meissel](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Meissel) described (and used) a practical combinatorial way of evaluating $\pi(x)$: Let $p_{1}, p_{2},\ldots, p_{n}$ be the first $n$ primes and denote by $\Phi(m,n)$ the number of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) not greater than $m$ which are divisible by none of the $p_{i}$ for any $i \le n$. Then

$$\Phi(m,n)=\Phi(m,n-1)-\Phi\left(\frac m {p_n},n-1\right).$$

Given a natural number $m$, if $n = \pi(\sqrt[3]{m})$ and if $\mu = \pi(\sqrt{m}) - n$, then

$$\pi(m) = \Phi(m,n)+n(\mu+1)+\frac{\mu^2-\mu} 2 - 1 - \sum_{k=1}^\mu\pi\left(\frac m {p_{n+k}}\right) .$$

Using this approach, Meissel computed $\pi(x)$, for $x$ equal to 5e5, 10<sup>6</sup>, 10<sup>7</sup>, and 10<sup>8</sup>.

In 1959, [Derrick Henry Lehmer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derrick_Henry_Lehmer) extended and simplified Meissel's method. Define, for real $m$ and for natural numbers $n$ and $k$, $P_{k}(m,n)$ as the number of numbers not greater than $m$ with exactly $k$ prime factors, all greater than $p_{n}$. Furthermore, set $P_{0}(m,n) = 1$. Then

$$\Phi(m,n) = \sum_{k=0}^{+\infty} P_k(m,n)$$

where the sum actually has only finitely many nonzero terms. Let $y$ denote an integer such that $\sqrt[3]{m} \le y \le \sqrt{m}$, and set $n = \pi(y)$. Then $P_{1}(m,n) = \pi(m) - n$ and $P_{k}(m,n) = 0$ when $k \ge 3$. Therefore,

$$\pi(m) = \Phi(m,n) + n - 1 - P_2(m,n)$$

The computation of $P_{2}(m,n)$ can be obtained this way:

$$P_2(m,n) = \sum_{y < p \le \sqrt{m} } \left( \pi \left( \frac m p \right) - \pi(p) + 1\right)$$

where the sum is over prime numbers.

On the other hand, the computation of $\Phi(m,n)$ can be done using the following rules:

1. $$\Phi(m,0) = \lfloor m\rfloor$$
1. $$\Phi(m,b) = \Phi(m,b-1) - \Phi\left(\frac m{p_b},b-1\right)$$

Using his method and an [IBM 701](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IBM_701), Lehmer was able to compute the correct value of $\pi(10^{9})$ and missed the correct value of $\pi(10^{10})$ by 1.[^o28]

Further improvements to this method were made by Lagarias, Miller, Odlyzko, Deléglise, and Rivat.[^o29]

## Other prime-counting functions {#Other_prime-counting_functions}

Other prime-counting functions are also used because they are more convenient to work with.

### Riemann's prime-power counting function {#Riemann's_prime-power_counting_function}

Riemann's prime-power counting function is usually denoted as $\Pi_{0}(x)$ or $J_{0}(x)$. It has jumps of $\tfrac{1}{n}$ at prime powers $p^{n}$ and it takes a value halfway between the two sides at the discontinuities of $\pi(x)$. That added detail is used because the function may then be defined by an inverse [Mellin transform](wikipedia://en/387297).

Formally, we may define $\Pi_{0}(x)$ by

$$\Pi_0(x) = \frac{1}{2} \left( \sum_{p^n < x} \frac{1}{n} + \sum_{p^n \le x} \frac{1}{n} \right)\$$

where the variable $p$ in each sum ranges over all primes within the specified limits.

We may also write

$$\ \Pi_0(x) = \sum_{n=2}^x \frac{\Lambda(n)}{\log n} - \frac{\Lambda(x)}{2\log x} = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac 1 n \pi_0\left(x^{1/n}\right)$$

where $\Lambda$ is the [von Mangoldt function](wikipedia://en/1928862) and

$$\pi_0(x) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{\pi(x-\varepsilon) + \pi(x+\varepsilon)}{2}.$$

The [Möbius inversion formula](wikipedia://en/20963) then gives

$$\pi_0(x) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \frac{\mu(n)}{n}\ \Pi_0\left(x^{1/n}\right),$$

where $\mu(n)$ is the [Möbius function](wikipedia://en/20961).

Knowing the relationship between the logarithm of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809) and the [von Mangoldt function](wikipedia://en/1928862) $\Lambda$, and using the [Perron formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perron_formula) we have

$$\log \zeta(s) = s \int_0^\infty \Pi_0(x) x^{-s-1}\, \mathrm{d}x$$

### Chebyshev's function {#Chebyshev's_function}

The [Chebyshev function](wikipedia://en/5793598) weights primes or prime powers $p^{n}$ by $\log p$:

$$\begin{aligned} \vartheta(x) &= \sum_{p\le x} \log p \\ \psi(x)&=\sum_{p^n \le x} \log p = \sum_{n=1}^\infty \vartheta \left( x^{1/n} \right) = \sum_{n \le x}\Lambda(n) . \end{aligned}$$

For $x \ge 2$,[^o33]

$$\vartheta(x) = \pi(x)\log x - \int_2^x \frac{\pi(t)}{t}\, \mathrm{d}t$$

and

$$\pi(x)=\frac{\vartheta(x)}{\log x} + \int_2^x \frac{\vartheta(t)}{t\log^{2}(t)} \mathrm{d} t .$$

## Formulas for prime-counting functions {#Formulas_for_prime-counting_functions}

Formulas for prime-counting functions come in two kinds: arithmetic formulas and analytic formulas. Analytic formulas for prime-counting were the first used to prove the [prime number theorem](wikipedia://en/23692). They stem from the work of Riemann and [von Mangoldt](wikipedia://en/1928734), and are generally known as [explicit formulae](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Explicit_formulae_%28L-function%29).[^o36]

We have the following expression for the second [Chebyshev function](wikipedia://en/5793598) $\psi$:

$$\psi_0(x) = x - \sum_\rho \frac{x^\rho}{\rho} - \log 2\pi - \frac{1}{2} \log\left(1-x^{-2}\right),$$

where

$$\psi_0(x) = \lim_{\varepsilon \to 0} \frac{\psi(x - \varepsilon) + \psi(x + \varepsilon)}{2}.$$

Here $\rho$ are the zeros of the Riemann zeta function in the critical strip, where the real part of $\rho$ is between zero and one. The formula is valid for values of $x$ greater than one, which is the region of interest. The sum over the roots is [conditionally convergent](wikipedia://en/1198956), and should be taken in order of increasing [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the imaginary part. Note that the same sum over the trivial roots gives the last [subtrahend](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subtrahend) in the formula.

For $\Pi_{0}(x)$ we have a more complicated formula

$$\Pi_0(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) - \sum_\rho \operatorname{li}\left(x^\rho\right) - \log 2 + \int_x^\infty \frac{\mathrm{d}t}{t \left(t^2 - 1\right) \log t}.$$

Again, the formula is valid for $x > 1$, while $\rho$ are the nontrivial zeros of the zeta function ordered according to their absolute value. The first term $\operatorname{li}(x)$ is the usual [logarithmic integral function](wikipedia://en/47137); the expression $\operatorname{li}(x^{\rho})$ in the second term should be considered as $\operatorname{Ei}(\rho \log x)$, where $\operatorname{Ei}$ is the [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842) of the [exponential integral](wikipedia://en/648614) function from negative reals to the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) with branch cut along the positive reals. The final integral is equal to the series over the trivial zeros:

$$\int_x^\infty \frac{\mathrm dt}{t \left(t^2 - 1\right) \log t}=\int_x^\infty \frac{1}{t\log t} \left(\sum_{m}t^{-2m}\right)\,\mathrm dt=\sum_{m}\int_x^\infty \frac{t^{-2m}}{t\log t} \,\mathrm dt \,\,\overset{\left(u=t^{-2m}\right)}{=}-\sum_{m} \operatorname{li}\left(x^{-2m}\right)$$

Applying Möbius inversion to the explicit formula for $\Pi_0$ gives

$$\pi_0(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{n}\, \Pi_0\!\left(x^{1/n}\right).$$

Introducing Riemann's $R$-function,

$$\operatorname{R}(x) = \sum_{n=1}^{\infty} \frac{\mu(n)}{n} \operatorname{li}\!\left(x^{1/n}\right),$$

and formally interchanging the Möbius summation with the sums over the non-trivial and trivial zeros leads to the commonly quoted expression

$$\operatorname{R}(x) -\sum_\rho \operatorname{R}(x^\rho) -\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{R}(x^{-2m}).$$

As noted above, this rearrangement would require an additional justification and cannot be a consequence of Möbius inversion alone.[^o13][^o14] In fact, more recently it has shown that, with the standard symmetric ordering of the non-trivial zeros, the partial sums $\sum_{|\operatorname{Im}\rho|\leq T} \operatorname{R}(x^\rho)$ are unbounded for every fixed $x>1$, and that the corresponding series over the trivial zeros is likewise divergent.[^o15]

Folkmar Bornemann proved,[^o37] when assuming the [conjecture](wikipedia://en/6138) that all zeros of the Riemann zeta function are simple,[^o38] that

$$\operatorname{R}\left(e^{-2\pi t}\right)=\frac{1}{\pi}\sum_{k=1}^\infty\frac{(-1)^{k-1}t^{-2k-1}}{(2k+1)\zeta(2k+1)}+\frac12\sum_{\rho}\frac{t^{-\rho}}{\rho\cos\frac{\pi\rho}{2}\zeta'(\rho)}$$

where $\rho$ runs over the non-trivial zeros of the Riemann zeta function and $t > 0$.

The Riemann $R$-function itself provides a very good smooth approximation to the prime-counting function. Riesel and Göhl considered the refined approximation[^o39]

$$g(x) = \operatorname{R}(x) -\frac{1}{\log x} +\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\frac{\pi}{\log x},$$

which differs from $\operatorname{R}(x)$ by a term tending to zero as $x\to\infty$. The difference between $\pi_0(x)$ and this smooth approximation is heuristically expected to have size about $\sqrt{x}/\log x$. This motivates representing the fluctuations in the distribution of primes by

$$\Delta(x) = \left( \pi_0(x) -\operatorname{R}(x) +\frac{1}{\log x} -\frac{1}{\pi}\arctan\frac{\pi}{\log x} \right) \frac{\log x}{\sqrt{x}}.$$

As explained above, the formally rearranged expression $\sum_{m=1}^{\infty}\operatorname{R}(x^{-2m})$ should not be identified with the smooth correction.[^o15]

## Inequalities {#Inequalities}

[Ramanujan](wikipedia://en/47717)[^o41] proved that the inequality

$$\pi(x)^2 < \frac{ex}{\log x} \pi\left( \frac{x}{e} \right)$$

holds for all sufficiently large values of $x$.

Here are some useful inequalities for $\pi(x)$.

$$\frac x {\log x} < \pi(x) < 1.25506 \frac x {\log x} \quad \text{for }x \ge 17.$$

The left inequality holds for $x \ge 17$ and the right inequality holds for $x > 1$. The constant is $30\tfrac{\log 113}{113}$ to 5 decimal places, as $\pi(x) \tfrac{\log x}{x}$ has its maximum value at $x = p_{30} = 113$.[^o42]

[Pierre Dusart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pierre_Dusart) proved in 2010:[^o43]

$$\frac {x} {\log x - 1} < \pi(x) < \frac {x} {\log x - 1.1}\quad \text{for }x \ge 5393 \text{ and }x \ge 60184,\text{ respectively.}$$

More recently, Dusart has proved[^o44] (Theorem 5.1) that

$$\frac{x}{\log x} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{\log x} + \frac{2}{\log^2 x} \right) \le \pi(x) \le \frac{x}{\log x} \left( 1 + \frac{1}{\log x} + \frac{2}{\log^2 x} + \frac{7.59}{\log^3 x} \right),$$

for $x \ge 88789$ and $x > 1$, respectively.

Going in the other direction, an approximation for the $n$th prime, $p_{n}$, is

$$p_n = n \left(\log n + \log\log n - 1 + \frac {\log\log n - 2}{\log n} + O\left( \frac {(\log\log n)^2} {(\log n)^2}\right)\right).$$

Here are some inequalities for the $n$th prime. The lower bound is due to Dusart (1999)[^o45] and the upper bound to Rosser (1941).[^o46]

$$n (\log n + \log\log n - 1) < p_n < n (\log n + \log\log n)\quad \text{for } n \ge 6.$$

The left inequality holds for $n \ge 2$ and the right inequality holds for $n \ge 6$. A variant form sometimes seen substitutes $\log n +\log\log n = \log(n \log n).$ An even simpler lower bound is[^o47]

$$n \log n < p_n,$$

which holds for all $n \ge 1$, but the lower bound above is tighter for $n > e^{e} \approx$.

In 2010 Dusart proved[^o43] (Propositions 6.7 and 6.6) that

$$n \left( \log n + \log \log n - 1 + \frac{\log \log n - 2.1}{\log n} \right) \le p_n \le n \left( \log n + \log \log n - 1 + \frac{\log \log n - 2}{\log n} \right),$$

for $n \ge 3$ and $n \ge 688383$, respectively.

In 2024, Axler[^o48] further tightened this (equations 1.12 and 1.13) using bounds of the form

$$f(n,g(w)) = n \left( \log n + \log\log n - 1 + \frac{\log\log n - 2}{\log n} - \frac{g(\log\log n)}{2\log^2 n} \right)$$

proving that

$$f(n, w^2 - 6w + 11.321) \le p_n \le f(n, w^2 - 6w)$$

for $n \ge 2$ and $n \ge 3468$, respectively. The lower bound may also be simplified to $f(n, w^{2})$ without altering its validity. The upper bound may be tightened to $f(n, w^{2} - 6w + 10.667)$ if $n \ge 46254381$.

There are additional bounds of varying complexity.[^o49][^o50][^o51]

::: {#o53 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="The Riemann hypothesis"}

## The Riemann hypothesis {#The_Riemann_hypothesis}

The [Riemann hypothesis](wikipedia://en/19344125) implies a much tighter bound on the error in the estimate for $\pi(x)$, and hence to a more regular distribution of prime numbers,

$$\pi(x) = \operatorname{li}(x) + O(\sqrt{x} \log{x}).$$

Specifically,[^o54]

$$|\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)| < \frac{\sqrt{x}}{8\pi} \, \log{x}, \quad \text{for all } x \ge 2657.$$

(Dudek 2015) proved that the Riemann hypothesis implies that for all $x \ge 2$ there is a prime $p$ satisfying

$$x - \frac{4}{\pi} \sqrt{x} \log x < p \leq x.$$

:::
