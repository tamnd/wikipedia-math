# Bounded variation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bounded variation"}

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), a function of **bounded variation**, also known as ***$\operatorname{BV}$* function**, is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued [function](wikipedia://en/185427) whose [total variation](wikipedia://en/683561) is bounded (finite): the [graph of a function](wikipedia://en/87352) having this property is well behaved in a precise sense. For a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) of a single [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109), being of bounded variation means that the [distance](wikipedia://en/39378) along the [direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direction_%28geometry,_geography%29) of the [$y$-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Y-axis), neglecting the contribution of motion along [$x$-axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-axis), traveled by a [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28mathematics%29) moving along the graph has a finite value. For a continuous function of several variables, the meaning of the definition is the same, except for the fact that the continuous path to be considered cannot be the whole graph of the given function (which is a [hypersurface](wikipedia://en/507354#H) in this case), but can be every [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of the graph itself with a [hyperplane](wikipedia://en/99862) (in the case of functions of two variables, a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852)) parallel to a fixed $x$-axis and to the $y$-axis.

:::

A function $g$ has bounded variation if and only if the [Riemann–Stieltjes integral](wikipedia://en/330491) $\int_a^b f\,dg$ exists for every continuous function $f$.

Another characterization states that the functions of bounded variation on a compact interval are exactly those $f$ which can be written as a difference $g - h$, where both $g$ and $h$ are bounded [monotone](wikipedia://en/48260). In particular, a BV function may have discontinuities, but at most countably many.

In the case of several variables, a function $f$ defined on an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) $\Omega$ of $\mathbb{R}^n$ is said to have bounded variation if its [distributional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) is a [vector-valued](wikipedia://en/3675281) finite [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933).

One of the most important aspects of functions of bounded variation is that they form an [algebra](wikipedia://en/2112) of [discontinuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) whose first derivative exists [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688): due to this fact, they can and frequently are used to define [generalized solutions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_solution) of nonlinear problems involving [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948) and [ordinary](wikipedia://en/32742753) and [partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/52564) in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering).

We have the following chains of inclusions for continuous functions over a closed, bounded interval of the real line:

**[Continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable)** ⊆ **[Lipschitz continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous)** ⊆ **[absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous)** ⊆ **continuous and bounded variation** ⊆ **[differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688)**

## History {#History}

According to Boris Golubov, BV functions of a single variable were first introduced by [Camille Jordan](wikipedia://en/355931), in the paper (Jordan 1881) dealing with the convergence of [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038). "The properties of functions of bounded variation became widely known because they were discussed by Jordan in a note appended to the third volume of his *[Course d’analyse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cours_d%27analyse_de_l%27%C3%89cole_polytechnique)* (1887).[^o4]

The first successful step in the generalization of this concept to functions of several variables was due to [Leonida Tonelli](wikipedia://en/10018991),[^o5] who introduced a class of *continuous* BV functions in 1926 (Cesari 1986, 47–48), to extend his [direct method](wikipedia://en/24499402) for finding solutions to problems in the [calculus of variations](wikipedia://en/171882) in more than one variable. Ten years after, in (Cesari 1936), [Lamberto Cesari](wikipedia://en/17581349) *changed the continuity requirement* in Tonelli's definition *to a less restrictive [integrability](wikipedia://en/15532) requirement*, obtaining for the first time the class of functions of bounded variation of several variables in its full generality: as Jordan did before him, he applied the concept to resolve of a problem concerning the convergence of Fourier series, but for functions of *two variables*. After him, several authors applied BV functions to study [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) in several variables, [geometric measure theory](wikipedia://en/12169694), calculus of variations, and [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416). [Renato Caccioppoli](wikipedia://en/4262313) and [Ennio De Giorgi](wikipedia://en/1752401) used them to define [measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) of [nonsmooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) [boundaries](wikipedia://en/72824) of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) (see the entry "*[Caccioppoli set](wikipedia://en/8907443)*" for further information). [Olga Arsenievna Oleinik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olga_Arsenievna_Oleinik) introduced her view of generalized solutions for [nonlinear partial differential equations](wikipedia://en/16678376) as functions from the space BV in the paper (Oleinik 1957), and was able to construct a generalized solution of bounded variation of a [first order](wikipedia://en/4722013) partial differential equation in the paper (Oleinik 1959): few years later, [Edward D. Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edward_D._Conway) and [Joel A. Smoller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joel_A._Smoller) applied BV-functions to the study of a single [nonlinear hyperbolic partial differential equation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hyperbolic_equation) of first order in the paper (Conway Smoller 1966), proving that the solution of the [Cauchy problem](wikipedia://en/1725027) for such equations is a function of bounded variation, provided the [initial value](wikipedia://en/1725025) belongs to the same class. [Aizik Isaakovich Vol'pert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aizik_Isaakovich_Vol'pert) developed extensively a calculus for BV functions: in the paper (Vol'pert 1967) he proved the [chain rule for BV functions](wikipedia://en/330673#Chain_rule_for_BV_functions) and in the book (Hudjaev Vol'pert 1985) he, jointly with his pupil [Sergei Ivanovich Hudjaev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sergei_Ivanovich_Hudjaev), explored extensively the properties of BV functions and their application. His chain rule formula was later extended by [Luigi Ambrosio](wikipedia://en/25337146) and [Gianni Dal Maso](wikipedia://en/39451526) in the paper (Ambrosio Dal Maso 1990).

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

### BV functions of one variable {#BV_functions_of_one_variable}

(Definition 1.1.) The **[total variation](wikipedia://en/683561)** of a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued (or more generally [complex](wikipedia://en/5826)-valued) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) *f*, defined on an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a,b] \subset \mathbb{R}$ is the quantity

$$V_a^b(f)=\sup_{P \in \mathcal{P}} \sum_{i=0}^{n_{P}-1} | f(x_{i+1})-f(x_i) |. \,$$

where the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) is taken over the set $\mathcal{P} =\left\{P=\{ x_0, \dots , x_{n_P}\} \mid P\text{ is a partition of } [a, b]\text{ satisfying } x_i\leq x_{i+1}\text{ for } 0\leq i\leq n_P-1 \right\}$ of all [partitions](wikipedia://en/471332) of the interval considered.

If *f* is [differentiable](wikipedia://en/7921) and its derivative is Riemann-integrable, its total variation is the vertical component of the [arc-length](wikipedia://en/1898401) of its graph, that is to say,

$$V_a^b(f) = \int _a^b |f'(x)|\,\mathrm{d}x.$$

(Definition 1.2.) A real-valued function $f$ on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) is said to be of **bounded variation** (**BV function**) on a chosen [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a,b] \subset \mathbb{R}$ if its total variation is finite, *i.e.*

$$f \in \operatorname{BV}([a,b]) \iff V_a^b(f) < +\infty$$

It can be proved that a real function $f$ is of bounded variation in $[a,b]$ if and only if it can be written as the difference $f=f_1-f_2$ of two non-decreasing functions $f_1$ and $f_2$ on $[a,b]$: this result is known as the [Jordan decomposition of a function](https://www.encyclopediaofmath.org/index.php/Jordan_decomposition_%28of_a_function%29) and it is related to the [Jordan decomposition of a measure](wikipedia://en/2985858#Jordan_measure_decomposition).

Through the [Stieltjes integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stieltjes_integral), any function of bounded variation on a closed interval $[a, b]$ defines a [bounded linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bounded_linear_functional) on $C([a, b])$. In this special case,[^o17] the [Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem](wikipedia://en/38948504) states that every bounded linear functional arises uniquely in this way. The normalized positive functionals or [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437) correspond to positive non-decreasing lower [semicontinuous functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicontinuous_function). This point of view has been important in [spectral theory](wikipedia://en/506713),[^o18] in particular in its application to [ordinary differential equations](wikipedia://en/16990545).

### BV functions of several variables {#BV_functions_of_several_variables}

Functions of bounded variation, BV [functions](wikipedia://en/185427), are functions whose distributional [derivative](wikipedia://en/487859) is a finite[^o20] [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933). More precisely:

(Definition 2.1.) Let **$\Omega$** be an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of $\mathbb{R}^n$. A function **$u$** belonging to **[$L^1(\Omega)$](wikipedia://en/45194)** is said to be of **bounded variation** (**BV function**), and written

$$u\in \operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$$

if there exists a [finite](wikipedia://en/4316185) [vector](wikipedia://en/3675281) [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933) $Du\in\mathcal M(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$ such that the following equality holds

$$\int_\Omega u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = - \int_\Omega \langle\boldsymbol{\phi}, Du(x)\rangle \qquad \forall\boldsymbol{\phi}\in C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$$

that is, **$u$** defines a [linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_functional) on the space $C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$ of [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) [vector functions](wikipedia://en/3675281) $\boldsymbol{\phi}$ of [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) contained in **$\Omega$**: the vector [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) **$Du$** represents therefore the [distributional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Definitions_of_test_functions_and_distributions) or [weak](wikipedia://en/1411087) [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of **$u$**.

BV can be defined equivalently in the following way.

(Definition 2.2.) Given a function **$u$** belonging to **$L^1(\Omega)$**, the **total variation of $u$**[^o21] in $\Omega$ is defined as

$$V(u,\Omega):=\sup\left\{\int_\Omega u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x) \, \mathrm{d}x : \boldsymbol{\phi} \in C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n),\ \Vert\boldsymbol{\phi}\Vert_{L^\infty(\Omega)}\le 1\right\}$$

where $\Vert\;\Vert_{L^\infty(\Omega)}$ is the [essential supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_supremum) [norm](wikipedia://en/990534). Sometimes, especially in the theory of [Caccioppoli sets](wikipedia://en/8907443), the following notation is used

$$\int_\Omega\vert D u\vert = V(u,\Omega)$$

in order to emphasize that $V(u,\Omega)$ is the total variation of the [distributional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29#Definitions_of_test_functions_and_distributions) / [weak](wikipedia://en/1411087) [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of **$u$**. This notation reminds also that if **$u$** is of class **$C^1$** (i.e. a [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) and [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) having [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921)) then its [variation](wikipedia://en/683561) is exactly the [integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_%28measure_theory%29) of the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of its [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461).

The space of **functions of bounded variation** (**BV functions**) can then be defined as

$$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)=\{ u\in L^1(\Omega)\colon V(u,\Omega)<+\infty\}$$

The two definitions are equivalent since if $V(u,\Omega)<+\infty$ then

$$\left|\int_\Omega u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x) \, \mathrm{d}x \right |\leq V(u,\Omega)\Vert\boldsymbol{\phi}\Vert_{L^\infty(\Omega)} \qquad \forall \boldsymbol{\phi}\in C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$$

therefore $\displaystyle \boldsymbol{\phi}\mapsto\,\int_\Omega u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x) \, dx$ defines a [continuous linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_linear_functional) on the space $C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$. Since $C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n) \subset C^0(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$ as a [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357), this [continuous linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_linear_functional) can be extended [continuously](wikipedia://en/6122) and [linearly](wikipedia://en/91591) to the whole $C^0(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$ by the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860). Hence the continuous linear functional defines a [Radon measure](wikipedia://en/521933#Duality) by the [Riesz–Markov–Kakutani representation theorem](wikipedia://en/38948504).

### Locally BV functions {#Locally_BV_functions}

If the [function space](wikipedia://en/340630) of [locally integrable functions](wikipedia://en/4258398), i.e. [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) belonging to $L^1_\text{loc}(\Omega)$, is considered in the preceding definitions (1.2), (2.1) and (2.2) instead of the one of [globally integrable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function), then the function space defined is that of **functions of locally bounded variation**. Precisely, developing this idea for (definition 2.2), a **[local](wikipedia://en/1462457) variation** is defined as follows,

$$V(u,U):=\sup\left\{\int_\Omega u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x) \, \mathrm{d}x : \boldsymbol{\phi} \in C_c^1(U,\mathbb{R}^n),\ \Vert\boldsymbol{\phi}\Vert_{L^\infty(\Omega)}\le 1\right\}$$

for every [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $U\in\mathcal{O}_c(\Omega)$, having defined $\mathcal{O}_c(\Omega)$ as the set of all [precompact](wikipedia://en/596600) [open subsets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of **$\Omega$** with respect to the standard [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) of [finite-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28mathematics%29) [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370), and correspondingly the class of functions of locally bounded variation is defined as

$$\operatorname{BV}_\text{loc}(\Omega)=\{ u\in L^1_\text{loc}(\Omega)\colon \, (\forall U\in\mathcal{O}_c(\Omega)) \, V(u,U)<+\infty\}$$

::: {#o24 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

### Notation {#Notation}

There are basically two distinct conventions for the notation of spaces of functions of locally or globally bounded variation, and unfortunately they are quite similar: the first one, which is the one adopted in this entry, is used for example in references (Giusti 1984) (partially), (Hudjaev Vol'pert 1985) (partially), (Giaquinta Modica Souček 1998) and is the following one

- $\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$ identifies the [space](wikipedia://en/5308894) of functions of globally bounded variation
- $\operatorname\operatorname{BV}_{\text{loc}}(\Omega)$ identifies the [space](wikipedia://en/5308894) of functions of locally bounded variation

The second one, which is adopted in references (Vol'pert 1967) and (Maz'ya 1985) (partially), is the following:

- $\overline{\operatorname\operatorname{BV}}(\Omega)$ identifies the [space](wikipedia://en/5308894) of functions of globally bounded variation
- $\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$ identifies the [space](wikipedia://en/5308894) of functions of locally bounded variation

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

Only the properties common to [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) of one variable and to [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) of several variables will be considered in the following, and [proofs](wikipedia://en/82285) will be carried on only for functions of several variables since the [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) for the case of one variable is a straightforward adaptation of the several variables case: also, in each section it will be stated if the property is shared also by functions of locally bounded variation or not. References (Giusti 1984, 7–9), (Hudjaev Vol'pert 1985) and (Màlek Nečas Rokyta Růžička 1996) are extensively used.

### BV functions have only jump-type or removable discontinuities {#BV_functions_have_only_jump-type_or_removable_discontinuities}

In the case of one variable, the assertion is clear: for each point $x_0$ in the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[a , b]\subset\mathbb{R}$ of definition of the function **$u$**, either one of the following two assertions is true

$$\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0^-}}\!\!\!u(x) = \!\!\!\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0^+}}\!\!\!u(x)$$

$$\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0^-}}\!\!\!u(x) \neq \!\!\!\lim_{x\rightarrow x_{0^+}}\!\!\!u(x)$$

while both [limits](wikipedia://en/285759) exist and are finite. In the case of functions of several variables, there are some premises to understand: first of all, there is a [continuum](wikipedia://en/17368856) of [directions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direction_%28geometry,_geography%29) along which it is possible to approach a given point **$x_0$** belonging to the domain **$\Omega$**⊂$\mathbb{R}^n$. It is necessary to make precise a suitable concept of [limit](wikipedia://en/285759): choosing a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) ${\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}}\in\mathbb{R}^n$ it is possible to divide **$\Omega$** in two sets

$$\Omega_{({\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}},\boldsymbol{x}_0)} = \Omega \cap \{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n|\langle\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_0,{\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}}\rangle>0\} \qquad \Omega_{(-{\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}},\boldsymbol{x}_0)} = \Omega \cap \{\boldsymbol{x}\in\mathbb{R}^n|\langle\boldsymbol{x}-\boldsymbol{x}_0,-{\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}}\rangle>0\}$$

Then for each point **$x_0$** belonging to the domain $\Omega\in\mathbb{R}^n$ of the BV function **$u$**, only one of the following two assertions is true

$$\lim_{\overset{\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{x}_0}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{({\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}},\boldsymbol{x}_0)}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!u(\boldsymbol{x}) = \!\!\!\!\!\!\!\lim_{\overset{\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{x}_0}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{(-{\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}},\boldsymbol{x}_0)}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!u(\boldsymbol{x})$$

$$\lim_{\overset{\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{x}_0}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{({\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}},\boldsymbol{x}_0)}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!u(\boldsymbol{x}) \neq \!\!\!\!\!\!\!\lim_{\overset{\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{x}_0}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{(-{\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}},\boldsymbol{x}_0)}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!u(\boldsymbol{x})$$

or **$x_0$** belongs to a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of **$\Omega$** having zero $n-1$-dimensional [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109). The quantities

$$\lim_{\overset{\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{x}_0}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{({\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}},\boldsymbol{x}_0)}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!u(\boldsymbol{x})=u_{\boldsymbol{\hat a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_0) \qquad \lim_{\overset{\boldsymbol{x}\rightarrow \boldsymbol{x}_0}{\boldsymbol{x}\in\Omega_{(-{\boldsymbol{\hat{a}}},\boldsymbol{x}_0)}}}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!u(\boldsymbol{x})=u_{-\boldsymbol{\hat a}}(\boldsymbol{x}_0)$$

are called **approximate limits** of the BV function **$u$** at the point **$x_0$**.

### *V*(⋅, Ω) is lower semi-continuous on *L*<sup>1</sup>(Ω) {#V(&sdot;,_&Omega;)_is_lower_semi-continuous_on_L1(&Omega;)}

The [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948) $V(\cdot,\Omega):\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)\rightarrow \mathbb{R}^+$ is [lower semi-continuous](wikipedia://en/57824): to see this, choose a [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) of BV-functions **$\{u_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$** converging to **[$u\in L^1_\text{loc}(\Omega)$](wikipedia://en/4258398)**. Then, since all the functions of the sequence and their limit function are [integrable](wikipedia://en/15532) and by the definition of [lower limit](wikipedia://en/44837)

$$\begin{aligned} \liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}V(u_n,\Omega) &\geq \liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty} \int_\Omega u_n(x)\operatorname{div}\, \boldsymbol{\phi}\, \mathrm{d}x \\ &\geq \int_\Omega \lim_{n\rightarrow\infty} u_n(x)\operatorname{div}\, \boldsymbol{\phi}\, \mathrm{d}x \\ &= \int_\Omega u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}\, \mathrm{d}x \qquad\forall\boldsymbol{\phi}\in C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n),\quad\Vert\boldsymbol{\phi}\Vert_{L^\infty(\Omega)}\leq 1 \end{aligned}$$

Now considering the [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) on the set of functions $\boldsymbol{\phi}\in C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$ such that $\Vert\boldsymbol{\phi}\Vert_{L^\infty(\Omega)}\leq 1$ then the following inequality holds true

$$\liminf_{n\rightarrow\infty}V(u_n,\Omega)\geq V(u,\Omega)$$

which is exactly the definition of [lower semicontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicontinuity).

### BV(Ω) is a Banach space {#BV(&Omega;)_is_a_Banach_space}

By definition **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$** is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of **[$L^1(\Omega)$](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function)**, while [linearity](wikipedia://en/91591) follows from the linearity properties of the defining [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) i.e.

$$\begin{aligned} \int_\Omega [u(x)+v(x)]\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x & = \int_\Omega u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x +\int_\Omega v(x) \operatorname{div} \boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = \\ & =- \int_\Omega \langle\boldsymbol{\phi}(x), Du(x)\rangle- \int_\Omega \langle \boldsymbol{\phi}(x), Dv(x)\rangle =- \int_\Omega \langle \boldsymbol{\phi}(x), [Du(x)+Dv(x)]\rangle \end{aligned}$$

for all $\phi\in C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$ therefore $u+v\in \operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$for all $u,v\in \operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$, and

$$\int_\Omega c\cdot u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = c \int_\Omega u(x)\operatorname{div}\boldsymbol{\phi}(x)\,\mathrm{d}x = -c \int_\Omega \langle \boldsymbol{\phi}(x), Du(x)\rangle$$

for all $c\in\mathbb{R}$, therefore $cu\in \operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$ for all $u\in \operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$, and all $c\in\mathbb{R}$. The proved [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) properties imply that **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$** is a [vector subspace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vector_subspace) of **[$L^1(\Omega)$](wikipedia://en/45194)**. Consider now the function $\|\;\|_{\operatorname{BV}}:\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^+$ defined as

$$\| u \|_{\operatorname{BV}} := \| u \|_{L^1} + V(u,\Omega)$$

where $\| \; \|_{L^1}$ is the usual **[$L^1(\Omega)$ norm](wikipedia://en/45194#Lp_spaces_and_Lebesgue_integrals)**: it is easy to prove that this is a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) on **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$**. To see that **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$** is [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) respect to it, i.e. it is a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989), consider a [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) $\{u_n\}_{n\in\mathbb{N}}$ in **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$**. By definition it is also a [Cauchy sequence](wikipedia://en/6085) in **$L^1(\Omega)$** and therefore has a [limit](wikipedia://en/285773) **$u$** in **$L^1(\Omega)$**: since **$u_n$** is bounded in **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$** for each **$n$**, then $\Vert u \Vert_{\operatorname{BV}} < +\infty$ by [lower semicontinuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semicontinuity) of the variation $V(\cdot,\Omega)$, therefore **$u$** is a BV function. Finally, again by lower semicontinuity, choosing an arbitrary small positive number **$\varepsilon$**

$$\Vert u_j - u_k \Vert_{\operatorname{BV}}<\varepsilon\quad\forall j,k\geq N\in\mathbb{N} \quad\Rightarrow\quad V(u_k-u,\Omega)\leq \liminf_{j\rightarrow +\infty} V(u_k-u_j,\Omega)\leq\varepsilon$$

From this we deduce that $V(\cdot,\Omega)$ is continuous because it's a norm.

### BV(Ω) is not separable {#BV(&Omega;)_is_not_separable}

To see this, it is sufficient to consider the following example belonging to the space **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}([0,1])$**:[^o39] for each 0 \< *α* \< 1 define

$$\chi_\alpha=\chi_{[\alpha,1]}= \begin{cases} 0 & \mbox{if } x \notin\; [\alpha,1] \\ 1 & \mbox{if } x \in [\alpha,1] \end{cases}$$

as the [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/240790) of the [left-closed interval](wikipedia://en/49172#Definitions) $[\alpha,1]$. Then, choosing $\alpha,\beta \in [0,1]$ such that $\alpha \ne \beta$ the following relation holds true:

$$\Vert \chi_\alpha - \chi_\beta \Vert_{\operatorname{BV}}=2$$

Now, in order to prove that every [dense subset](wikipedia://en/23523594) of **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(]0,1[)$** cannot be [countable](wikipedia://en/6026), it is sufficient to see that for every $\alpha\in[0,1]$ it is possible to construct the [balls](wikipedia://en/160556)

$$B_\alpha=\left\{\psi\in \operatorname\operatorname{BV}([0,1]);\Vert \chi_\alpha - \psi \Vert_{\operatorname{BV}}\leq 1\right\}$$

Obviously those balls are [pairwise disjoint](wikipedia://en/39359), and also are an [indexed family](wikipedia://en/23624339) of [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) whose [index set](wikipedia://en/393055) is $[0,1]$. This implies that this family has the [cardinality of the continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901): now, since every dense subset of $\operatorname\operatorname{BV}([0,1])$ must have at least a point inside each member of this family, its cardinality is at least that of the continuum and therefore cannot a be countable subset.[^o40] This example can be obviously extended to higher dimensions, and since it involves only [local properties](wikipedia://en/1462457), it implies that the same property is true also for **$\operatorname{BV}_{loc}$**.

### Chain rule for locally BV(Ω) functions {#Chain_rule_for_locally_BV(&Omega;)_functions}

[Chain rules](wikipedia://en/6113) for [nonsmooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) are very important in [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) and [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416) since there are several important [physical models](wikipedia://en/20590) whose behaviors are described by [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) or [functionals](wikipedia://en/1374948) with a very limited degree of [smoothness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function). The following chain rule is proved in the paper (Vol'pert 1967, 248). Note all [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) must be interpreted in a generalized sense, i.e., as [generalized derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_derivative#Basic_idea).

::: {#o43 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**. Let $f:\mathbb{R}^p\rightarrow\mathbb{R}$ be a function of class **$C^1$** (i.e. a [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) and [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) having [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921)) and let $\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=(u_1(\boldsymbol{x}),\ldots,u_p(\boldsymbol{x}))$ be a function in **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}_{loc} (\Omega)$** with **$\Omega$** being an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) of $\mathbb{R}^n$. Then $f\circ\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})=f(\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}))\in \operatorname\operatorname{BV}_{loc} (\Omega)$ and

$$\frac{\partial f(\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}))}{\partial x_i}=\sum_{k=1}^p\frac{\partial\bar{f}(\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}))}{\partial u_k}\frac{\partial{u_k(\boldsymbol{x})}}{\partial x_i} \qquad\forall i=1,\ldots,n$$

where $\bar f(\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x}))$ is the mean value of the function at the point **$x \in\Omega$**, defined as

$$\bar f(\boldsymbol{u}(\boldsymbol{x})) = \int_0^1 f\left(\boldsymbol{u}_{\boldsymbol{\hat a}}(\boldsymbol{x})t + \boldsymbol{u}_{-\boldsymbol{\hat a}}(\boldsymbol{x})(1-t)\right) \, dt$$

A more general [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) [formula](wikipedia://en/164040) for [Lipschitz continuous functions](wikipedia://en/51731) $f:\mathbb{R}^p\rightarrow\mathbb{R}^s$ has been found by [Luigi Ambrosio](wikipedia://en/25337146) and [Gianni Dal Maso](wikipedia://en/39451526) and is published in the paper (Ambrosio Dal Maso 1990). However, even this formula has very important direct consequences: we use $( u(\boldsymbol{x}), v(\boldsymbol{x}))$ in place of $\boldsymbol u(\boldsymbol{x})$, where $v(\boldsymbol{x})$ is also a $BV_{loc}$ function. We have to assume also that $\bar u(\boldsymbol{x})$ is locally integrable with respect to the measure $\frac{\partial v(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_i}$ for each $i$, and that $\bar v(\boldsymbol{x})$ is locally integrable with respect to the measure $\frac{\partial u(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_i}$ for each $i$. Then choosing $f((u,v))=uv$, the preceding formula gives the ***[Leibniz rule](wikipedia://en/248988)*** for 'BV' functions

$$\frac{\partial v(\boldsymbol{x})u(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_i} = {\bar u(\boldsymbol{x})}\frac{\partial v(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_i} + {\bar v(\boldsymbol{x})}\frac{\partial u(\boldsymbol{x})}{\partial x_i}$$

:::

## Generalizations and extensions {#Generalizations_and_extensions}

### Weighted BV functions {#Weighted_BV_functions}

It is possible to generalize the above notion of [total variation](wikipedia://en/683561) so that different variations are weighted differently. More precisely, let $\varphi : [0, +\infty)\longrightarrow [0, +\infty)$ be any increasing function such that $\varphi(0) = \varphi(0+) =\lim_{x\rightarrow 0_+}\varphi(x) = 0$ (the **[weight function](wikipedia://en/245523)**) and let $f: [0, T]\longrightarrow X$ be a function from the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $[0 , T]$$\subset \mathbb{R}$ taking values in a [normed vector space](wikipedia://en/21538) $X$. Then the $\boldsymbol\varphi$**\-variation** of $f$ over $[0, T]$ is defined as

$$\mathop{\varphi\text{-}\operatorname{Var}}_{[0, T]} (f) := \sup \sum_{j = 0}^k \varphi \left( | f(t_{j + 1}) - f(t_j) |_X \right),$$

where, as usual, the supremum is taken over all finite [partitions](wikipedia://en/471332) of the interval $[0, T]$, i.e. all the [finite sets](wikipedia://en/11742) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $t_i$ such that

$$0 = t_0 < t_1 < \cdots < t_k = T.$$

The original notion of [variation](wikipedia://en/683561) considered above is the special case of $\varphi$-variation for which the weight function is the [identity function](wikipedia://en/15069): therefore an [integrable function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_function) $f$ is said to be a **weighted BV function** (of weight $\varphi$) if and only if its $\varphi$-variation is finite.

$$f\in \operatorname{BV}_\varphi([0, T];X)\iff \mathop{\varphi\text{-}\operatorname{Var}}_{[0, T]} (f) <+\infty$$

The space $\operatorname{BV}_\varphi([0, T];X)$ is a [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) with respect to the [norm](wikipedia://en/990534)

$$\| f \|_{\operatorname{BV}_\varphi} := \| f \|_\infty + \mathop{\varphi\text{-}\operatorname{Var}}_{[0, T]} (f),$$

where $\| f \|_{\infty}$ denotes the usual [supremum norm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum_norm) of *$f$*. Weighted BV functions were introduced and studied in full generality by [Władysław Orlicz](wikipedia://en/362718) and [Julian Musielak](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Julian_Musielak) in the paper (Musielak Orlicz 1959): [Laurence Chisholm Young](wikipedia://en/9620408) studied earlier the case $\varphi(x)=x^p$ where *$p$* is a positive integer.

### SBV functions {#SBV_functions}

**SBV functions** *i.e.* *Special functions of Bounded Variation* were introduced by [Luigi Ambrosio](wikipedia://en/25337146) and [Ennio De Giorgi](wikipedia://en/1752401) in the paper (Ambrosio De Giorgi 1988), dealing with free discontinuity [variational problems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Variational_problem): given an [open subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_subset) **$\Omega$** of $\mathbb{R}^n$, the space **$\operatorname{SBV}(\Omega)$** is a proper [linear subspace](wikipedia://en/56357) of **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$**, since the [weak](wikipedia://en/1411087) [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of each function belonging to it consists precisely of the [sum](wikipedia://en/246160) of an $n$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [support](wikipedia://en/381013) and an $n-1$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [support](wikipedia://en/381013) [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) and *no intermediate-dimensional terms*, as seen in the following definition.

::: {#o50 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition**. Given a [locally integrable function](wikipedia://en/4258398) **$u$**, then $u\in \operatorname{SBV}(\Omega)$ if and only if

**\1.** There exist two [Borel functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_function) $f$ and $g$ of [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) **$\Omega$** and [codomain](wikipedia://en/50264) $\mathbb{R}^n$ such that

$$\int_\Omega\vert f\vert \, dH^n+ \int_\Omega\vert g\vert \, dH^{n-1}<+\infty.$$

**\2.** For all of [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) [vector functions](wikipedia://en/3675281) $\phi$ of [compact support](wikipedia://en/381013#Compact_support) contained in **$\Omega$**, *i.e.* for all $\phi \in C_c^1(\Omega,\mathbb{R}^n)$ the following formula is true:

$$\int_\Omega u\operatorname{div} \phi \, dH^n = \int_\Omega \langle \phi, f\rangle \, dH^n +\int_\Omega \langle \phi, g\rangle \, dH^{n-1}.$$

where $H^\alpha$ is the $\alpha$-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/8398) [Hausdorff measure](wikipedia://en/504109).

Details on the properties of *SBV* functions can be found in works cited in the bibliography section: particularly the paper (De Giorgi 1992) contains a useful [bibliography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bibliography).

:::

::: {#o53 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Sequence of bounded variation"}

### BV sequences {#BV_sequences}

As particular examples of [Banach spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Banach_spaces), (Dunford Schwartz 1958, Chapter IV) consider spaces of **sequences of bounded variation**, in addition to the spaces of functions of bounded variation. The total variation of a [sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sequence_%28mathematics%29) *x* = (*x*<sub>*i*</sub>) of real or complex numbers is defined by

$$\operatorname{TV}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty |x_{i+1}-x_i|.$$

The space of all sequences of finite total variation is denoted by BV. The norm on BV is given by

$$\|x\|_{\operatorname{BV}} = |x_1| + \operatorname{TV}(x) = |x_1| + \sum_{i=1}^\infty |x_{i+1}-x_i|.$$

With this norm, the space BV is a Banach space which is isomorphic to $\ell_1$.

The total variation itself defines a norm on a certain subspace of BV, denoted by BV<sub>0</sub>, consisting of sequences *x* = (*x*<sub>i</sub>) for which

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} x_n =0.$$

The norm on BV<sub>0</sub> is denoted

$$\|x\|_{\operatorname{BV}_0} = \operatorname{TV}(x) = \sum_{i=1}^\infty |x_{i+1}-x_i|.$$

With respect to this norm BV<sub>0</sub> becomes a Banach space as well, which is isomorphic *and* isometric to $\ell_1$ (although not in the natural way).

:::

### Measures of bounded variation {#Measures_of_bounded_variation}

A [signed](wikipedia://en/2985811) (or [complex](wikipedia://en/3057532)) [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) *$\mu$* on a [measurable space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) $(X,\Sigma)$ is said to be of bounded variation if its [total variation](wikipedia://en/683561#Total_variation_in_measure_theory)*$\Vert \mu\Vert=|\mu|(X)$* is bounded: see (Halmos 1950, 123), (Kolmogorov Fomin 1969, 346) or the entry "[Total variation](wikipedia://en/683561)" for further details.

## Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Sin x^-1.svg.* The function *f*(*x*) = sin(1/*x*) is *not* of bounded variation on the interval $[0,2 / \pi]$.

As mentioned in the introduction, two large class of examples of BV functions are monotone functions, and absolutely continuous functions. For a negative example: the function

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & \mbox{if }x =0 \\ \sin(1/x), & \mbox{if } x \neq 0 \end{cases}$$

is *not* of bounded variation on the interval $[0, 2/\pi]$

*Image: Xsin(x^-1).svg.* The function *f*(*x*) = *x* sin(1/*x*) is *not* of bounded variation on the interval $[0,2 / \pi]$.

While it is harder to see, the continuous function

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & \mbox{if }x =0 \\ x \sin(1/x), & \mbox{if } x \neq 0 \end{cases}$$

is *not* of bounded variation on the interval $[0, 2/\pi]$ either.

*Image: X^2sin(x^-1).svg.* The function *f*(*x*) = *x*<sup>2</sup> sin(1/*x*) *is* of bounded variation on the interval $[0,2 / \pi]$.

At the same time, the function

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & \mbox{if }x =0 \\ x^2 \sin(1/x), & \mbox{if } x \neq 0 \end{cases}$$

is of bounded variation on the interval $[0,2/\pi]$. However, *all three functions are of bounded variation on each interval* $[a,b]$ *with* $a>0$.

Every monotone, bounded function is of bounded variation. For such a function $f$ on the interval $[a,b]$ and any partition $P=\{x_0,\ldots,x_{n_P}\}$ of this interval, it can be seen that

$$\sum_{i=0}^{n_P-1}|f(x_{i+1})-f(x_i)|=|f(b)-f(a)|$$

from the fact that the sum on the left is [telescoping](wikipedia://en/508070). From this, it follows that for such $f$,

$$V_a^b(f)=|f(b)-f(a)|.$$

In particular, the monotone [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819) is a well-known example of a function of bounded variation that is not [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670).[^o60]

The [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) **$W^{1,1}(\Omega)$** is a [proper subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_subset) of **$\operatorname\operatorname{BV}(\Omega)$**. In fact, for each **$u$** in **$W^{1,1}(\Omega)$** it is possible to choose a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) $\mu:=\nabla u \mathcal L$ (where $\mathcal L$ is the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on $\Omega$) such that the equality

$$\int u\operatorname{div}\phi = -\int \phi\, d\mu = -\int \phi \,\nabla u \qquad \forall \phi\in C_c^1$$

holds, since it is nothing more than the definition of [weak derivative](wikipedia://en/1411087), and hence holds true. One can easily find an example of a BV function which is not **$W^{1,1}$**: in dimension one, any step function with a non-trivial jump will do.

## Applications {#Applications}

### Mathematics {#Mathematics}

Functions of bounded variation have been studied in connection with the set of [discontinuities](wikipedia://en/2661487) of functions and differentiability of real functions, and the following results are well-known. If $f$ is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) of bounded variation on an interval $[a,b]$ then

- $f$ is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) except at most on a [countable set](wikipedia://en/6026);
- $f$ has [one-sided limits](wikipedia://en/379840) everywhere (limits from the left everywhere in $(a,b]$, and from the right everywhere in $[a,b)$ ;
- the [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) $f'(x)$ exists [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688) (i.e. except for a set of [measure zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_zero)).

For [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) of several real variables

- the [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/240790) of a [Caccioppoli set](wikipedia://en/8907443) is a BV function: BV functions lie at the basis of the modern theory of perimeters.
- [Minimal surfaces](wikipedia://en/276734) are [graphs](wikipedia://en/87352) of BV functions: in this context, see reference (Giusti 1984).

### Physics and engineering {#Physics_and_engineering}

The ability of BV functions to deal with discontinuities has made their use widespread in the applied sciences: solutions of problems in mechanics, physics, chemical kinetics are very often representable by functions of bounded variation. The book (Hudjaev Vol'pert 1985) details a very ample set of mathematical physics applications of BV functions. Also there is some modern application which deserves a brief description.

- The [Mumford–Shah functional](wikipedia://en/35244294): the segmentation problem for a two-dimensional image, i.e. the problem of faithful reproduction of contours and grey scales is equivalent to the [minimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimum) of such [functional](wikipedia://en/1374948).
- [Total variation denoising](wikipedia://en/26792343)
