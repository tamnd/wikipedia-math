# Radon–Nikodym theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Radon–Nikodym theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Radon–Nikodym theorem**, named after [Johann Radon](wikipedia://en/339331) and [Otto M. Nikodym](wikipedia://en/5471556), is a result in [measure theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_theory) that expresses the relationship between two measures defined on the same [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333). A *measure* is a [set function](wikipedia://en/25911217) that assigns a consistent magnitude to the measurable subsets of a measurable space. Examples of a measure include area and volume, where the subsets are sets of points; or the probability of an event, which is a subset of possible outcomes within a wider [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325).

:::

One way to derive a new measure from one already given is to assign a density to each point of the space, then [integrate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration) over the measurable subset of interest. This can be expressed as

$$\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu,$$

where $\nu$ is the new measure being defined for any measurable subset $A$ and the function $f$ is the density at a given point. The integral is with respect to an existing measure $\mu$, which may often be the canonical [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$ or the $n$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^n$ (corresponding to our standard notions of length, area and volume). For example, if $f$ represented mass density and $\mu$ was the Lebesgue measure in three-dimensional space $\mathbb{R}^3$, then $\nu(A)$ would equal the total mass in a spatial region $A$.

The Radon–Nikodym theorem essentially states that, under certain conditions, any measure $\nu$ can be expressed in this way with respect to another measure $\mu$ on the same space. The function $f$ is then called the **Radon–Nikodym derivative** and is denoted by $d\nu/d\mu$.[^o2] An important application is in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), leading to the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685).

The theorem is named after [Johann Radon](wikipedia://en/339331), who proved the theorem for the special case where the underlying space is $\mathbb{R}^n$ in 1913, and for [Otto Nikodym](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Otto_Nikodym) who proved the general case in 1930.[^o3] In 1936, [Hans Freudenthal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Freudenthal) generalized the Radon–Nikodym theorem by proving the [Freudenthal spectral theorem](wikipedia://en/34121965), a result in [Riesz space](wikipedia://en/8099018) theory; this contains the Radon–Nikodym theorem as a special case.[^o4]

A [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $Y$ is said to have the [Radon–Nikodym property](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_property) if the generalization of the Radon–Nikodym theorem also holds (with the necessary adjustments made) for functions with values in $Y$. All [Hilbert spaces](wikipedia://en/20598932) have the Radon–Nikodym property.

## Formal description {#Formal_description}

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Radon–Nikodym theorem"}

### Radon–Nikodym theorem {#Radon–Nikodym_theorem}

The **Radon–Nikodym theorem** involves a [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(X, \Sigma)$ on which two [σ-finite measures](wikipedia://en/4316174) are defined, $\mu$ and $\nu.$ It states that, if $\nu \ll \mu$ (that is, if $\nu$ is [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670#Absolute_continuity_of_measures) with respect to $\mu$), then there exists a $\Sigma$-[measurable function](wikipedia://en/44055) $f : X \to [0, \infty),$ such that for any measurable set $A \in \Sigma,$

$$\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu.$$

:::

### Radon–Nikodym derivative {#Radon–Nikodym_derivative}

The function $f$ satisfying the above equality is uniquely defined [up to](wikipedia://en/44787) a $\mu$-[null set](wikipedia://en/21520), that is, if $g$ is another function which satisfies the same property, then $f = g$ $\mu$-[almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688). The function $f$ is commonly written $d\nu/d\mu$ and is called the **Radon–Nikodym derivative**. The choice of notation and the name of the function reflects the fact that the function is analogous to a [derivative](wikipedia://en/7921) in [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) in the sense that it describes the rate of change of density of one measure with respect to another (the way the [Jacobian determinant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jacobian_determinant) is used in multivariable integration).

### Extension to signed or complex measures {#Extension_to_signed_or_complex_measures}

A similar theorem can be proven for [signed](wikipedia://en/2985811) and [complex measures](wikipedia://en/3057532): namely, that if $\mu$ is a nonnegative σ-finite measure, and $\nu$ is a finite-valued signed or complex measure such that $\nu \ll \mu,$ that is, $\nu$ is [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous) with respect to $\mu,$ then there is a $\mu$-integrable real- or complex-valued function $g$ on $X$ such that for every measurable set $A,$

$$\nu(A) = \int_A g \, d\mu.$$

::: {#o11 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

In the following examples, the set $X$ is the real interval $[0,1]$, and $\Sigma$ is the [Borel sigma-algebra](wikipedia://en/43327) on $X$.

1. Let $\mu$ be the length measure on $X$, and let $\nu$ assign to each subset $Y$ of $X$ twice the length of $Y$. Then $\frac{d\nu}{d\mu} = 2$.
1. Let $\mu$ be the length measure on $X$, and let $\nu$ assign to each subset $Y$ of $X$ the number of points from the set $\{0.1,\dots,0.9\}$ that are contained in $Y$. Then $\nu$ is not absolutely continuous with respect to $\mu$ since it assigns non-zero measure to zero-length points. Indeed, there is no derivative $\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}$: there is no finite function that, when integrated e.g. from $(0.1 - \varepsilon)$ to $(0.1 + \varepsilon)$, gives $1$ for all $\varepsilon > 0$.
1. $\mu = \nu + \delta_0$, where $\nu$ is the length measure on $X$ and $\delta_0$ is the [Dirac measure](wikipedia://en/1019002) on 0 (it assigns a measure of 1 to any set containing 0 and a measure of 0 to any other set). Then, $\nu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mu$, and $\frac{d\nu}{d\mu} = 1_{X\setminus \{0\}}$ – the derivative is 0 at $x = 0$ and 1 at $x > 0$.[^o12]

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

- Let *ν*, *μ*, and *λ* be σ-finite measures on the same measurable space. If *ν* ≪ *λ* and *μ* ≪ *λ* (*ν* and *μ* are both [absolutely continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Absolutely_continuous#Absolute_continuity_of_measures) with respect to *λ*), then $$\frac{d(\nu+\mu)}{d\lambda} = \frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}+\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda} \quad \lambda\text{-almost everywhere}.$$
- If *ν* ≪ *μ* ≪ *λ*, then $$\frac{d\nu}{d\lambda}=\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}\quad\lambda\text{-almost everywhere}.$$
- In particular, if *μ* ≪ *ν* and *ν* ≪ *μ*, then $$\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}=\left(\frac{d\nu}{d\mu}\right)^{-1}\quad\nu\text{-almost everywhere}.$$
- If *μ* ≪ *λ* and $g$ is a *μ*-integrable function, then $$\int_X g\,d\mu = \int_X g\frac{d\mu}{d\lambda}\,d\lambda.$$
- If *ν* is a finite signed or complex measure, then $${d|\nu|\over d\mu} = \left|{d\nu\over d\mu}\right|.$$

:::

::: {#o16 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

### Probability theory {#Probability_theory}

The theorem is very important in extending the ideas of [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) from probability masses and probability densities defined over real numbers to [probability measures](wikipedia://en/45437) defined over arbitrary sets. It tells if and how it is possible to change from one probability measure to another. Specifically, the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) of a [random variable](wikipedia://en/25685) is the Radon–Nikodym derivative of the induced measure with respect to some base measure (usually the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) for [continuous random variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_random_variable)).

For example, it can be used to prove the existence of [conditional expectation](wikipedia://en/435099) for probability measures. The latter itself is a key concept in [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), as [conditional probability](wikipedia://en/24104134) is just a special case of it.

### Financial mathematics {#Financial_mathematics}

Amongst other fields, [financial mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_mathematics) uses the theorem extensively, in particular via the [Girsanov theorem](wikipedia://en/336940). Such changes of probability measure are the cornerstone of the [rational pricing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_pricing) of [derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28finance%29) and are used for converting actual probabilities into those of the [risk neutral probabilities](wikipedia://en/441911).

### Information divergences {#Information_divergences}

If *μ* and *ν* are measures over $X$, and *μ* ≪ *ν*

- The [Kullback–Leibler divergence](wikipedia://en/1115052) from *ν* to *μ* is defined to be $$D_\text{KL}(\mu \parallel \nu) = \int_X \log \left( \frac{d \mu}{d \nu} \right) \; d\mu.$$
- For *α* > 0, *α* ≠ 1 the [Rényi divergence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renyi_divergence) of order *α* from *ν* to *μ* is defined to be $$D_\alpha(\mu \parallel \nu) = \frac{1}{\alpha - 1} \log\left(\int_X\left(\frac{d\mu}{d\nu}\right)^{\alpha-1}\; d\mu\right).$$

:::

## The assumption of σ-finiteness {#The_assumption_of_σ-finiteness}

The Radon–Nikodym theorem above makes the assumption that the measure *μ* with respect to which one computes the rate of change of *ν* is [σ-finite](wikipedia://en/4316174).

### Negative example {#Negative_example}

Here is an example when *μ* is not σ-finite and the Radon–Nikodym theorem fails to hold.

Consider the [Borel σ-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_algebra) on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line). Let the [counting measure](wikipedia://en/191786), $\mu$, of a Borel set $A$ be defined as the number of elements of $A$ if $A$ is finite, and $\infty$ otherwise. One can check that $\mu$ is indeed a measure. It is not $\sigma$-finite, as not every Borel set is at most the union of countably many finite sets. Let $\nu$ be the usual [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on this Borel algebra. Then, $\nu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\mu$, since for a set $A$ one has $\mu(A) = 0$ only if $A$ is the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566), and then $\nu(A)$ is also zero.

Assume that the Radon–Nikodym theorem holds, that is, for some measurable function $f$ one has

$$\nu(A) = \int_A f \,d\mu$$

for all Borel sets. Taking $A$ to be a [singleton set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singleton_set), $A = \{a\}$, and using the above equality, one finds

$$0 = f(a)$$

for all real numbers $a$. This implies that the function $\,f\,$, and therefore the Lebesgue measure $\nu$, is zero, which is a contradiction.

### Positive result {#Positive_result}

Assuming $\nu\ll\mu,$ the Radon–Nikodym theorem also holds if $\mu$ is [localizable](wikipedia://en/19873#Localizable_measures) and $\nu$ is *accessible with respect to* $\mu$,[^o23] i.e., $\nu(A)=\sup\{\nu(B):B\in{\cal P}(A)\cap\mu^\operatorname{pre}(\mathbb{R}_{\ge0})\}$ for all $A\in\Sigma.$[^o24][^o23]

::: {#o26 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

This section gives a measure-theoretic proof of the theorem. There is also a functional-analytic proof, using Hilbert space methods, that was first given by [von Neumann](wikipedia://en/15942).

For finite measures $\mu$ and $\nu$, the idea is to consider functions $\,f\,$ with $f\,d\mu \le d\nu$. The supremum of all such functions, along with the [monotone convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/146263), then furnishes the Radon–Nikodym derivative. The fact that the remaining part of $\mu$ is singular with respect to $\nu$ follows from a technical fact about finite measures. Once the result is established for finite measures, extending to $\sigma$-finite, signed, and complex measures can be done naturally. The details are given below.

### For finite measures {#For_finite_measures}

**Constructing an extended-valued candidate** First, suppose $\mu$ and $\nu$ are both finite-valued nonnegative measures. Let $F$ be the set of those extended-value measurable functions $f\, : X \to [0, \infty]$ such that:

$$\forall A \in \Sigma:\qquad \int_A f\,d\mu \leq \nu(A)$$

$F \ne \emptyset$, since it contains at least the zero function. Now let $f_{1}, \,f_{2} \in F$, and suppose $A$ is an arbitrary measurable set, and define:

$$\begin{aligned} A_1 &= \left\{ x \in A : f_1(x) > f_2(x) \right\}, \\ A_2 &= \left\{ x \in A : f_2(x) \geq f_1(x) \right\}. \end{aligned}$$

Then one has

$$\int_A\max\left\{f_1, f_2\right\}\,d\mu = \int_{A_1} f_1\,d\mu + \int_{A_2} f_2\,d\mu \leq \nu\left(A_1\right) + \nu\left(A_2\right) = \nu(A),$$

and therefore, $\max\{\,f\,_{1}, \,f\,_{2}\} \in F$.

Now, let $\{\,f_{n}\,\}$ be a sequence of functions in $F$ such that

$$\lim_{n\to\infty}\int_X f_n\,d\mu = \sup_{f\in F} \int_X f\,d\mu.$$

By replacing $\,f_{n}\,$ with the maximum of the first $n$ functions, one can assume that the sequence $\{\,f_{n}\,\}$ is increasing. Let $g$ be an extended-valued function defined as

$$g(x) := \lim_{n\to\infty}f_n(x).$$

By Lebesgue's [monotone convergence theorem](wikipedia://en/146263), one has

$$\lim_{n\to\infty} \int_A f_n\,d\mu = \int_A \lim_{n\to\infty} f_n(x)\,d\mu(x) = \int_A g\,d\mu \leq \nu(A)$$

for each $A \in \Sigma$, and hence, $g \in F$. Also, by the construction of $g$,

$$\int_X g\,d\mu = \sup_{f\in F}\int_X f\,d\mu.$$

**Proving equality** Now, since $g \in F$,

$$\nu_0(A) := \nu(A) - \int_A g\,d\mu$$

defines a nonnegative measure on $\Sigma$. To prove equality, we show that $\nu_{0} = 0$.

Suppose $\nu_{0} \ne 0$; then, since $\mu$ is finite, there is an $\varepsilon > 0$ such that $\nu_{0}(X) > \varepsilon\,\mu(X)$. To derive a contradiction from $\nu_{0} \ne 0$, we look for a [positive set](wikipedia://en/3375352) $P \in \Sigma$ for the signed measure $\nu_{0} - \varepsilon\,\mu$ (i.e. a measurable set $P$, all of whose measurable subsets have non-negative $\nu_{0} - \varepsilon\mu$ measure), where also $P$ has positive $\mu$-measure. Conceptually, we're looking for a set $P$, where $\nu_{0} \ge \varepsilon\,\mu$ in every part of $P$. A convenient approach is to use the [Hahn decomposition](wikipedia://en/2985858) $(P, N)$ for the signed measure $\nu_{0} - \varepsilon\,\mu$.

Note then that for every $A \in \Sigma$ one has $\nu_{0}(A \cap P) \ge \varepsilon\,\mu(A \cap P)$, and hence,

$$\begin{aligned} \nu(A) &= \int_A g\,d\mu + \nu_0(A) \\ &\geq \int_A g\,d\mu + \nu_0(A\cap P)\\ &\geq \int_A g\,d\mu + \varepsilon\mu(A\cap P) = \int_A\left(g + \varepsilon 1_P\right)\,d\mu, \end{aligned}$$

where $1_{P}$ is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) of $P$. Also, note that $\mu(P) > 0$ as desired; for if $\mu(P) = 0$, then (since $\nu$ is absolutely continuous in relation to $\mu$) $\nu_{0}(P) \le \nu(P) = 0$, so $\nu_{0}(P) = 0$ and

$$\nu_0(X) - \varepsilon\mu(X) = \left(\nu_0 - \varepsilon\mu\right)(N) \leq 0,$$

contradicting the fact that $\nu_{0}(X) > \varepsilon\mu(X)$.

Then, since also

$$\int_X\left(g + \varepsilon1_P\right)\,d\mu \leq \nu(X) < +\infty,$$

$g + \varepsilon\,1_{P} \in F$ and satisfies

$$\int_X\left(g + \varepsilon 1_P\right)\,d\mu > \int_X g\,d\mu = \sup_{f\in F}\int_X f\,d\mu.$$

This is [impossible](wikipedia://en/22456146) because it violates the definition of a [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum); therefore, the initial assumption that $\nu_{0} \ne 0$ must be false. Hence, $\nu_{0} = 0$, as desired.

**Restricting to finite values** Now, since $g$ is $\mu$-integrable, the set $\{x \in X : g(x) = \infty\}$ is $\mu$-[null](wikipedia://en/21520). Therefore, if a $\,f\,$ is defined as

$$f(x) = \begin{cases} g(x) & \text{if }g(x) < \infty \\ 0 & \text{otherwise,} \end{cases}$$

then $f$ has the desired properties.

**Uniqueness** As for the uniqueness, let $\,f, g : X \to [0, \infty)$ be measurable functions satisfying

$$\nu(A) = \int_A f\,d\mu = \int_A g\,d\mu$$

for every measurable set $A$. Then, $g - f\,$ is $\mu$-integrable, and

$\int_A(g - f)\,d\mu = 0.$ (Recall that we can split the integral into two as long as they are measurable and non-negative)

In particular, for $A = \{x \in X : f(x) > g(x)\},$ or $\{x \in X : f(x) < g(x)\}$. It follows that

$$\int_X(g - f)^+\,d\mu = 0 = \int_X(g - f)^-\,d\mu,$$

and so, that $(g - f\,)^{+} = 0$ $\mu$-almost everywhere; the same is true for $(g - f\,)^{-}$, and thus, $f\,= g$ $\mu$-almost everywhere, as desired.

### For $\sigma$-finite positive measures {#For_-finite_positive_measures}

If $\mu$ and $\nu$ are $\sigma$-finite, then $X$ can be written as the union of a sequence $\{B_{n}\}_{n}$ of [disjoint sets](wikipedia://en/39359) in $\Sigma$, each of which has finite measure under both $\mu$ and $\nu$. For each $n$, by the finite case, there is a $\Sigma$-measurable function $\,f_{n}\, : B_{n} \to [0, \infty)$ such that

$$\nu_n(A) = \int_A f_n\,d\mu$$

for each $\Sigma$-measurable subset $A$ of $B_{n}$. The sum $\left(\sum_n f_n 1_{B_n}\right) := f$ of those functions is then the required function such that $\nu(A) = \int_A f \, d\mu$.

As for the uniqueness, since each of the $f_{n}$ is $\mu$-almost everywhere unique, so is $f$.

### For signed and complex measures {#For_signed_and_complex_measures}

If $\nu$ is a $\sigma$-finite signed measure, then it can be Hahn–Jordan decomposed as $\nu = \nu^{+} - \nu^{-}$ where one of the measures is finite. Applying the previous result to those two measures, one obtains two functions, $g, h : X \to [0, \infty)$, satisfying the Radon–Nikodym theorem for $\nu^{+}$ and $\nu^{-}$ respectively, at least one of which is $\mu$-integrable (i.e., its integral with respect to $\mu$ is finite). It is clear then that $f = g - h$ satisfies the required properties, including uniqueness, since both $g$ and $h$ are unique up to $\mu$-almost everywhere equality.

If $\nu$ is a [complex measure](wikipedia://en/3057532), it can be decomposed as $\nu = \nu_{1} + i\nu_{2}$, where both $\nu_{1}$ and $\nu_{2}$ are finite-valued signed measures. Applying the above argument, one obtains two functions, $g, h : X \to [0, \infty)$, satisfying the required properties for $\nu_{1}$ and $\nu_{2}$, respectively. Clearly, $f\,= g + ih$ is the required function.

:::

::: {#o31 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="The Lebesgue decomposition theorem"}

## The Lebesgue decomposition theorem {#The_Lebesgue_decomposition_theorem}

[Lebesgue's decomposition theorem](wikipedia://en/4316238) shows that the assumptions of the Radon–Nikodym theorem can be found even in a situation which is seemingly more general. Consider a σ-finite positive measure $\mu$ on the measure space $(X,\Sigma)$ and a σ-finite signed measure $\nu$ on $\Sigma$, without assuming any absolute continuity. Then there exist unique signed measures $\nu_a$ and $\nu_s$ on $\Sigma$ such that $\nu=\nu_a+\nu_s$, $\nu_a\ll\mu$, and $\nu_s\perp\mu$. The Radon–Nikodym theorem can then be applied to the pair $\nu_a,\mu$.

:::
