# F-distribution

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="F-distribution"}

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542) and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the ***F*-distribution** or ***F*-ratio**, also known as **Snedecor's *F* distribution** or the **Fisher–Snedecor distribution** (after [Ronald Fisher](wikipedia://en/140824) and [George W. Snedecor](wikipedia://en/1134409)), is a [continuous probability distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_probability_distribution) that arises frequently as the [null distribution](wikipedia://en/8919561) of a [test statistic](wikipedia://en/1416975), most notably in the [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634) (ANOVA) and other [*F*-tests](wikipedia://en/318976).[^o3][^o4][^o5][^o6]

:::

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

The *F*-distribution with $d_1$ and $d_2$ degrees of freedom is the distribution of

$$X = \frac{U_1/d_1}{U_2/d_2}$$

where $U_1$ and $U_2$ are [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) [random variables](wikipedia://en/25685) with [chi-square distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi-square_distribution) with respective degrees of freedom $d_1$ and $d_2$.

It can be shown to follow that the [probability density function](wikipedia://en/43487) (PDF) for $X$ is given by

$$\begin{aligned} f(x; d_1,d_2) &= \frac{\sqrt{\frac{(d_1x)^{d_1}\,\,d_2^{d_2}} {(d_1x+d_2)^{d_1+d_2}}}} {x\operatorname{B}\left(\frac{d_1}{2},\frac{d_2}{2}\right)} \\[5pt] &=\frac{\left(\frac{d_1}{d_2}\right)^{\frac{d_1}{2}} {x\vphantom{\left({d_1\over d_2}\right)}}^{\frac{d_1}{2} - 1} \left(1+\frac{d_1}{d_2} \, x \right)^{-\frac{d_1+d_2}{2}}}{\operatorname{B}\left(\frac{d_1}{2},\frac{d_2}{2}\right)} \end{aligned}$$

for [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) $x > 0$. Here $\mathrm{B}$ is the [beta function](wikipedia://en/245660). In many applications, the parameters $d_1$ and $d_2$ are [positive integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer), but the distribution is well-defined for positive real values of these parameters.

The [cumulative distribution function](wikipedia://en/5793) is

$$F(x; d_1,d_2)=I_\frac{d_1 x}{(d_1 x + d_2)}\left (\tfrac{d_1}{2}, \tfrac{d_2}{2} \right) ,$$

where $I_x(a,b)$ is the [regularized incomplete beta function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regularized_incomplete_beta_function).

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

The expectation, variance, and other details about the *F*-distribution $F(d_1, d_2)$ are given in the sidebox; for $d_2 > 8$, the [excess kurtosis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Excess_kurtosis) is

$$\gamma_2 = 12\frac{d_1(5d_2-22)(d_1+d_2-2)+(d_2-4)(d_2-2)^2}{d_1(d_2-6)(d_2-8)(d_1+d_2-2)}.$$

The $k$-th moment of an $F(d_1, d_2)$ distribution exists and is finite only when $2k < d_2$ and it is equal to[^o11]

$$\mu _X(k) =\left( \frac{d_2}{d_1}\right)^k \frac{\Gamma \left(\tfrac{d_1}{2}+k\right) }{\Gamma \left(\tfrac{d_1}{2}\right)} \frac{\Gamma \left(\tfrac{d_2}{2}-k\right) }{\Gamma \left( \tfrac{d_2}{2}\right) }.$$

The *F*-distribution is a particular [parametrization](wikipedia://en/453198) of the [beta prime distribution](wikipedia://en/3126406), which is also called the beta distribution of the second kind.

The [characteristic function](wikipedia://en/3229401) is listed incorrectly in many standard references (e.g.,[^o4]). The correct expression [^o12] is

$$\varphi^F_{d_1, d_2}(s) = \frac{\Gamma{\left(\frac{d_1+d_2}{2}\right)}}{\Gamma{\left(\tfrac{d_2}{2}\right)}} U \! \left(\frac{d_1}{2},1-\frac{d_2}{2},-\frac{d_2}{d_1} \imath s \right)$$

where $U(a, b, z)$ is the [confluent hypergeometric function](wikipedia://en/1516095) of the second kind.

:::

## Related distributions {#Related_distributions}

### Relation to the chi-squared distribution {#Relation_to_the_chi-squared_distribution}

In instances where the *F*-distribution is used, for example in the [analysis of variance](wikipedia://en/634), independence of $U_1$ and $U_2$ (defined above) might be demonstrated by applying [Cochran's theorem](wikipedia://en/574337).

Equivalently, since the [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) is the sum of squares of [independent](wikipedia://en/27593) [standard normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Standard_normal) random variables, the random variable of the *F*-distribution may also be written

$$X = \frac{s_1^2}{\sigma_1^2} \div \frac{s_2^2}{\sigma_2^2},$$

where $s_1^2 = \frac{S_1^2}{d_1}$ and $\textstyle s_2^2 = \frac{S_2^2}{d_2}$, $S_1^2$ is the sum of squares of $d_1$ random variables from normal distribution $N(0,\sigma_1^2)$ and $S_2^2$ is the sum of squares of $d_2$ random variables from normal distribution $N(0,\sigma_2^2)$.

In a [frequentist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequentist) context, a scaled *F*-distribution therefore gives the probability $\textstyle p(s_1^2/s_2^2 \mid \sigma_1^2, \sigma_2^2)$, with the *F*-distribution itself, without any scaling, applying where $\sigma_1^2$ is being taken equal to $\sigma_2^2$. This is the context in which the *F*-distribution most generally appears in [*F*-tests](wikipedia://en/318976): where the null hypothesis is that two independent normal variances are equal, and the observed sums of some appropriately selected squares are then examined to see whether their ratio is significantly incompatible with this null hypothesis.

The quantity $X$ has the same distribution in Bayesian statistics, if an uninformative rescaling-invariant [Jeffreys prior](wikipedia://en/1901158) is taken for the [prior probabilities](wikipedia://en/472877) of $\sigma_1^2$ and $\sigma_2^2$.[^o15] In this context, a scaled *F*-distribution thus gives the posterior probability $\textstyle p(\sigma^2_2 /\sigma_1^2 \mid s^2_1, s^2_2)$, where the observed sums $s^2_1$ and $s^2_2$ are now taken as known.

### In general {#In_general}

- If $X \sim \chi^2_{d_1}$ and $Y \sim \chi^2_{d_2}$ ([Chi squared distribution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chi_squared_distribution)) are [independent](wikipedia://en/27593), then $\textstyle \frac{X / d_1}{Y / d_2} \sim \mathrm{F}(d_1, d_2)$.
- If $X_k \sim \Gamma(\alpha_k,\beta_k)\,$ ([Gamma distribution](wikipedia://en/207079)) are independent, then $\textstyle \frac{\alpha_2\beta_1 X_1}{\alpha_1\beta_2 X_2} \sim \mathrm{F}(2\alpha_1, 2\alpha_2)$.
- If $X \sim \operatorname{Beta}\left({d_1\over2},{d_2\over2}\right)$ ([Beta distribution](wikipedia://en/207074)) then $\textstyle \frac{d_2 X}{d_1(1-X)} \sim \operatorname{F}(d_1,d_2)$.
  - Equivalently, if $X \sim F(d_1, d_2)$, then $\textstyle \frac{d_1 X/d_2}{1+d_1 X/d_2} \sim \operatorname{Beta}\left({d_1\over2},{d_2\over2}\right)$.
- If $X \sim F(d_1, d_2)$, then $\frac{d_1}{d_2}X$ has a [beta prime distribution](wikipedia://en/3126406): $\textstyle \frac{d_1}{d_2}X \sim \operatorname{\beta^\prime}\left(\tfrac{d_1}{2},\tfrac{d_2}{2}\right)$.
- If $X \sim F(d_1, d_2)$ then $Y = \lim_{d_2 \to \infty} d_1 X$ has the [chi-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/113424) $\chi^2_{d_1}$.
- $F(d_1, d_2)$ is equivalent to the scaled [Hotelling's T-squared distribution](wikipedia://en/934711) $\textstyle \frac{d_2}{d_1(d_1+d_2-1)} \operatorname{T}^2 (d_1, d_1 +d_2-1)$.
- If $X \sim F(d_1, d_2)$ then $X^{-1} \sim F(d_2, d_1)$.
- If $X\sim t_{(n)}$ – [Student's t-distribution](wikipedia://en/105375) – then: $$\begin{aligned} X^{2} &\sim \operatorname{F}(1, n), \\ X^{-2} &\sim \operatorname{F}(n, 1). \end{aligned}$$
- *F*-distribution is a special case of type 6 [Pearson distribution](wikipedia://en/1804023).
- If $X$ and $Y$ are independent, with $X,Y\sim \operatorname{Laplace}(\mu,b)$ ([Laplace distribution](wikipedia://en/1106213)), then $$\frac{|X-\mu|}{|Y-\mu|} \sim \operatorname{F}(2,2).$$
- If $X\sim F(n,m)$ then $\tfrac{\log{X}}{2} \sim \operatorname{FisherZ}(n,m)$ ([Fisher's z-distribution](wikipedia://en/4967719)).
- The [noncentral *F*-distribution](wikipedia://en/1368010) simplifies to the *F*-distribution if $\lambda=0$.
- The doubly [noncentral *F*-distribution](wikipedia://en/1368010) simplifies to the *F*-distribution if $\lambda_1 = \lambda_2 = 0$
- If $\operatorname{Q}_X(p)$ is the quantile $p$ for $X\sim F(d_1,d_2)$ and $\operatorname{Q}_Y(1-p)$ is the quantile $1-p$ for $Y\sim F(d_2,d_1)$, then $$\operatorname{Q}_X(p)=\frac{1}{\operatorname{Q}_Y(1-p)}.$$
- *F*-distribution is an instance of [ratio distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratio_distributions).
- [W](wikipedia://en/6028339)-distribution is a unique parametrization of F-distribution.
