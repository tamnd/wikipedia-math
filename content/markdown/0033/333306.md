# Regular polygon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Regular polygon"}

In [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417), a **regular polygon** is a [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) that is [direct equiangular](wikipedia://en/2476494) (all angles are equal in measure) and [equilateral](wikipedia://en/214547) (all sides have the same length). Regular polygons may be either *[convex](wikipedia://en/660019)* or *[star](wikipedia://en/313303)*. In the [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602), a sequence of regular polygons with an increasing number of sides approximates a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), if the [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) or [area](wikipedia://en/1209) is fixed, or a regular [apeirogon](wikipedia://en/5658261) (effectively a [straight line](wikipedia://en/946975)), if the edge length is fixed.

:::

## General properties {#General_properties}

*Image: regular star polygons.svg.* Regular convex and star polygons with 3 to 12 vertices labelled with their Schläfli symbols

These properties apply to all regular polygons, whether convex or [star](wikipedia://en/313303):

- A regular *n*-sided polygon has [rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) of order *n*.
- All vertices of a regular polygon lie on a common circle (the [circumscribed circle](wikipedia://en/161262)); i.e., they are concyclic points. That is, a regular polygon is a [cyclic polygon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cyclic_polygon).
- Together with the property of equal-length sides, this implies that every regular polygon also has an inscribed circle or [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) that is tangent to every side at the midpoint. Thus a regular polygon is a [tangential polygon](wikipedia://en/35520654).
- A regular *n*-sided polygon can be constructed with [compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass_and_straightedge) if and only if the [odd](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) factors of *n* are distinct [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime). (See [constructible polygon](wikipedia://en/523430).)
- A regular *n*-sided polygon can be constructed with [origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origami) if and only if $n = 2^{a} 3^{b} p_1 \cdots p_r$ for some $r \in \mathbb{N}$, where each distinct $p_i$ is a [Pierpont prime](wikipedia://en/2595060).[^o3]

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Symmetry"}

### Symmetry {#Symmetry}

The [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of an *n*-sided regular polygon is the [dihedral group](wikipedia://en/100267) D<sub>*n*</sub> (of order 2*n*): D<sub>2</sub>, [D<sub>3</sub>](wikipedia://en/2375824), D<sub>4</sub>, ... It consists of the rotations in C<sub>*n*</sub>, together with [reflection symmetry](wikipedia://en/1390873) in *n* axes that pass through the center. If *n* is even then half of these axes pass through two opposite vertices, and the other half through the midpoint of opposite sides. If *n* is odd then all axes pass through a vertex and the midpoint of the opposite side.

:::

## Regular convex polygons {#Regular_convex_polygons}

All regular [simple polygons](wikipedia://en/1059530) (a simple polygon is one that does not intersect itself anywhere) are convex. Those having the same number of sides are also [similar](wikipedia://en/39377).

An *n*-sided convex regular polygon is denoted by its [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) $\{n\}$. For $n<3$, we have two [degenerate](wikipedia://en/232952) cases:

**[Monogon](wikipedia://en/5483767) {1}; point**

Degenerate in [ordinary space](wikipedia://en/9417). (Most authorities do not regard the monogon as a true polygon, partly because of this, and also because the formulae below do not work, and its structure is not that of any [abstract polygon](wikipedia://en/1211056).)

**[Digon](wikipedia://en/5442728) {2}; line segment**

Degenerate in [ordinary space](wikipedia://en/9417). (Some authorities do not regard the digon as a true polygon because of this.)

In certain contexts all the polygons considered will be regular. In such circumstances it is customary to drop the prefix regular. For instance, all the faces of [uniform polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_polyhedra) must be regular and the faces will be described simply as triangle, square, pentagon, etc.

*Image: annuli\_with\_same\_area\_around\_unit\_regular\_polygons.svg.* As a corollary of the [annulus](wikipedia://en/356158) chord formula, the area bounded by the [circumcircle](wikipedia://en/1926432) and [incircle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Incircle) of every unit convex regular polygon is $\pi$/4

### Angles {#Angles}

For a regular convex *n*-gon, each [interior angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interior_angle) has a measure of:

$\frac{(n - 2)180}{n}$ degrees;

$\frac{(n - 2)\pi}{n}$ radians; or

$\frac{(n - 2)}{2n}=\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{n}$ full [turns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turn_%28geometry%29),

and each [exterior angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exterior_angle) (i.e., [supplementary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supplementary_angle) to the interior angle) has a measure of $\tfrac{360}{n}$ degrees, with the sum of the exterior angles equal to 360 degrees or 2π radians or one full turn.

As *n* approaches infinity, the internal angle approaches 180 degrees. For a regular polygon with 10,000 sides (a [myriagon](wikipedia://en/523703)) the internal angle is 179.964°. As the number of sides increases, the internal angle can come very close to 180°, and the shape of the polygon approaches that of a circle. However the polygon can never become a circle. The value of the internal angle can never become exactly equal to 180°, as the circumference would effectively become a straight line (see [apeirogon](wikipedia://en/5658261)). For this reason, a circle is not a polygon with an infinite number of sides.

### Diagonals {#Diagonals}

For $n>2$, the number of [diagonals](wikipedia://en/349251) is $\tfrac{1}{2}n(n - 3)$; i.e., 0, 2, 5, 9, ..., for a triangle, square, pentagon, hexagon, ... . The diagonals divide the polygon into 1, 4, 11, 24, ... pieces.

For a regular *n*-gon inscribed in a circle of radius $1$, the product of the distances from a given vertex to all other vertices (including adjacent vertices and vertices connected by a diagonal) equals *n*.

### Points in the plane {#Points_in_the_plane}

For a regular simple $n$-gon with [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) $R$ and distances $d_{i}$ from an arbitrary point in the plane to the vertices, we have[^o10]

$$\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n d_i^4 + 3R^4 = \biggl(\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2 + R^2\biggr)^2.$$

For higher powers of distances $d_i$ from an arbitrary point in the plane to the vertices of a regular $n$-gon, if

$$S^{(2m)}_{n}=\frac 1n\sum_{i=1}^n d_i^{2m},$$

then[^o11]

$$S^{(2m)}_{n} = \left(S^{(2)}_{n}\right)^m + \sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}\binom{m}{2k}\binom{2k}{k}R^{2k}\left(S^{(2)}_{n} - R^2\right)^k\left(S^{(2)}_{n}\right)^{m-2k},$$

and

$$S^{(2m)}_{n} = \left(S^{(2)}_{n}\right)^m + \sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}\frac{1}{2^k}\binom{m}{2k}\binom{2k}{k} \left(S^{(4)}_{n} -\left(S^{(2)}_{n}\right)^2\right)^k\left(S^{(2)}_{n}\right)^{m-2k},$$

where $m$ is a positive integer less than $n$.

If $L$ is the distance from an arbitrary point in the plane to the centroid of a regular $n$-gon with circumradius $R$, then[^o11]

$$\sum_{i=1}^n d_i^{2m}=n\left(\left(R^2+L^2\right)^m+ \sum_{k=1}^{\left\lfloor m/2\right\rfloor}\binom{m}{2k}\binom{2k}{k}R^{2k}L^{2k}\left(R^2+L^2\right)^{m-2k}\right),$$

where $m = 1, 2, \dots, n - 1$.

#### Interior points {#Interior_points}

For a regular $n$-gon, the sum of the perpendicular distances from any interior point to the $n$ sides is $n$ times the [apothem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothem)[^o13] (the apothem being the distance from the center to any side). This is a generalization of [Viviani's theorem](wikipedia://en/10460511) for the *n* = 3 case.[^o14][^o15]

### Circumradius {#Circumradius}

*Image: PolygonParameters.png.* Regular [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014) (*n* = 5) with [side](wikipedia://en/12158034) *s*, [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) *R* and [apothem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothem) *a*

The [circumradius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circumradius) *R* from the center of a regular polygon to one of the vertices is related to the side length *s* or to the [apothem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothem) *a* by

$$R = \frac{s}{2 \sin\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)} = \frac{a}{\cos\left(\frac{\pi}{n} \right)} \quad_,\quad a = \frac{s}{2 \tan\left(\frac{\pi}{n}\right)}$$

For [constructible polygons](wikipedia://en/523430), [algebraic expressions](wikipedia://en/4283745) for these relationships exist (see Bicentric polygon).

The sum of the perpendiculars from a regular *n*-gon's vertices to any line tangent to the circumcircle equals *n* times the circumradius.[^o13]

The sum of the squared distances from the vertices of a regular *n*-gon to any point on its circumcircle equals 2*nR*<sup>2</sup> where *R* is the circumradius.[^o13]

The sum of the squared distances from the midpoints of the sides of a regular *n*-gon to any point on the circumcircle is 2*nR*<sup>2</sup> − $\tfrac{1}{4}$*ns*<sup>2</sup>, where *s* is the side length and *R* is the circumradius.[^o13]

If $d_i$ are the distances from the vertices of a regular $n$-gon to any point on its circumcircle, then[^o11]

$$3\biggl(\sum_{i=1}^n d_i^2\biggr)^2 = 2n \sum_{i=1}^n d_i^4.$$

### Dissections {#Dissections}

[Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter) states that every [zonogon](wikipedia://en/55312103) (a 2*m*-gon whose opposite sides are parallel and of equal length) can be dissected into $\tbinom{m}{2}$ or $\tfrac{1}{2}$*m*(*m* − 1) parallelograms. These tilings are contained as subsets of vertices, edges and faces in orthogonal projections [*m*-cubes](wikipedia://en/39783).[^o18]

In particular, this is true for any regular polygon with an even number of sides, in which case the parallelograms are all rhombi. Regular polygons with 4m+2 sides can be dissected in a way with (2m+1)-fold radial symmetry. The list gives the number of solutions for smaller polygons.

### Area {#Area}

The area *A* of a convex regular *n*-sided polygon having [side](wikipedia://en/12158034) *s*, [circumradius](wikipedia://en/161262) *R*, [apothem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apothem) *a*, and [perimeter](wikipedia://en/23636) *p* is given by[^o22][^o23]

$$\begin{aligned} A &= \tfrac{1}{2}nsa \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}pa \\ &= \tfrac{1}{4}ns^2\cot\left(\tfrac{\pi}{n}\right) \\ &= na^2\tan\left(\tfrac{\pi}{n}\right) \\ &= \tfrac{1}{2}nR^2\sin\left(\tfrac{2\pi}{n}\right) \end{aligned}$$

## Constructible polygon {#Constructible_polygon}

Some regular polygons are easy to [construct with compass and straightedge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compass-and-straightedge_construction); other regular polygons are not constructible at all. The [ancient Greek mathematicians](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greek_mathematics) knew how to construct a regular polygon with 3, 4, or 5 sides,[^o26] and they knew how to construct a regular polygon with double the number of sides of a given regular polygon.[^o26] This led to the question being posed: is it possible to construct *all* regular *n*-gons with compass and straightedge? If not, which *n*-gons are constructible and which are not?

[Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) proved the constructibility of the regular [17-gon](wikipedia://en/196890) in 1796. Five years later, he developed the theory of [Gaussian periods](wikipedia://en/456085) in his *[Disquisitiones Arithmeticae](wikipedia://en/736713)*. This theory allowed him to formulate a [sufficient condition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sufficient_condition) for the constructibility of regular polygons:

A regular *n*-gon can be constructed with compass and straightedge if *n* is the product of a power of 2 and any number of distinct [Fermat primes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat_prime) (including none).

(A Fermat prime is a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666) of the form $2^{\left(2^n\right)} + 1.$) Gauss stated without proof that this condition was also [necessary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Necessary_condition), but never published his proof. A full proof of necessity was given by [Pierre Wantzel](wikipedia://en/2610953) in 1837. The result is known as the **Gauss–Wantzel theorem**.

Equivalently, a regular *n*-gon is constructible if and only if the [cosine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosine) of its common angle is a [constructible number](wikipedia://en/7439)—that is, can be written in terms of the four basic arithmetic operations and the extraction of square roots.

## Regular star polygons {#Regular_star_polygons}

A non-convex regular polygon is a regular [star polygon](wikipedia://en/313303). The most common example is the [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544), which has the same vertices as a [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014), but connects alternating vertices.

For an *n*-sided star polygon, the [Schläfli symbol](wikipedia://en/489394) is modified to indicate the *density* or "starriness" *m* of the polygon, as {*n*/*m*}. If *m* is 2, for example, then every second point is joined. If *m* is 3, then every third point is joined. The boundary of the polygon winds around the center *m* times.

The (non-degenerate) regular stars of up to 12 sides are:

- [Pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544) – {5/2}
- [Heptagram](wikipedia://en/180673) – {7/2} and {7/3}
- [Octagram](wikipedia://en/2667613) – {8/3}
- [Enneagram](wikipedia://en/34963576) – {9/2} and {9/4}
- [Decagram](wikipedia://en/19546742) – {10/3}
- [Hendecagram](wikipedia://en/1173083) – {11/2}, {11/3}, {11/4} and {11/5}
- [Dodecagram](wikipedia://en/26173953) – {12/5}

*m* and *n* must be [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime), or the figure will degenerate.

The degenerate regular stars of up to 12 sides are:

- Tetragon – {4/2}
- Hexagons – {6/2}, {6/3}
- Octagons – {8/2}, {8/4}
- Enneagon – {9/3}
- Decagons – {10/2}, {10/4}, and {10/5}
- Dodecagons – {12/2}, {12/3}, {12/4}, and {12/6}

Depending on the precise derivation of the Schläfli symbol, opinions differ as to the nature of the degenerate figure. For example, {6/2} may be treated in either of two ways:

- For much of the 20th century (see for example (Coxeter 1948)), we have commonly taken the /2 to indicate joining each vertex of a convex {6} to its near neighbors two steps away, to obtain the regular [compound](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_polygon) of two triangles, or [hexagram](wikipedia://en/302441). Coxeter clarifies this regular compound with a notation {*kp*}\[*k*{*p*}\]{*kp*} for the compound {*p*/*k*}, so the [hexagram](wikipedia://en/302441) is represented as {6}\[2{3}\]{6}.[^o38] More compactly Coxeter also writes 2{*n*/2}, like 2{3} for a hexagram as compound as [alternations](wikipedia://en/4349117) of regular even-sided polygons, with italics on the leading factor to differentiate it from the coinciding interpretation.[^o39]
- Many modern geometers, such as Grünbaum (2003),[^o36] regard this as incorrect. They take the /2 to indicate moving two places around the {6} at each step, obtaining a "double-wound" triangle that has two vertices superimposed at each corner point and two edges along each line segment. Not only does this fit in better with modern theories of [abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056), but it also more closely copies the way in which Poinsot (1809) created his star polygons – by taking a single length of wire and bending it at successive points through the same angle until the figure closed.

:::

::: {#o41 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Self-dual polygon"}

## Duality of regular polygons {#Duality_of_regular_polygons}

All regular polygons are self-dual to [congruency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Congruency_%28geometry%29), and for odd *n* they are self-dual to identity.

In addition, the regular star figures (compounds), being composed of regular polygons, are also self-dual.

:::

## Regular polygons as faces of polyhedra {#Regular_polygons_as_faces_of_polyhedra}

A [uniform polyhedron](wikipedia://en/2537223) has regular polygons as faces, such that for every two vertices there is an [isometry](wikipedia://en/254777) mapping one into the other (just as there is for a regular polygon).

A [quasiregular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/8810651) is a uniform polyhedron which has just two kinds of face alternating around each vertex.

A [regular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/333299) is a uniform polyhedron which has just one kind of face.

The remaining (non-uniform) [convex polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Convex_polyhedra) with regular faces are known as the [Johnson solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johnson_solids).

A polyhedron having regular triangles as faces is called a [deltahedron](wikipedia://en/493995).
