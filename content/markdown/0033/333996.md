# Ultrametric space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ultrametric space"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **ultrametric space** is a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) in which the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) is strengthened to $d(x,z)\leq\max\left\{d(x,y),d(y,z)\right\}$ for all $x$, $y$, and $z$. Sometimes the associated metric is also called a **non-Archimedean metric** or **super-metric**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Formal definition"}

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

An **ultrametric** on a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) $M$ is a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued function

$$d\colon M \times M \rightarrow \mathbb{R}$$

(where $\mathbb{R}$ denotes the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438)), such that for all $x,y,z\in M$:

1. $d(x,y)\geq0$ with equality if and only if $x=y$ (*non-degeneracy*)
1. $d(x,y)=d(y,x)$ (*symmetry*)
1. $d(x,z)\leq\max\{d(x,y),d(y,z)\}$ (*strong triangle inequality* or *ultrametric inequality*).

An **ultrametric space** is a pair $(M,d)$ consisting of a set $M$ together with an ultrametric $d$ on $M$, which is called the space's associated distance function (also called a [metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metric_%28mathematics%29)).

If $d$ satisfies all of the conditions but the "if and only if" in condition 1 is weakened to "if", then $d$ is called an **ultrapseudometric** on $M$. An **ultrapseudometric space** is a pair $(M,d)$ consisting of a set $M$ and an ultrapseudometric $d$ on $M$.[^n1]

In the case when $M$ is an [Abelian group](wikipedia://en/2974) (written additively) and $d$ is generated by a [length function](wikipedia://en/3021393) $\|\cdot\|$ (so that $d(x,y) = \|x - y\|$), the last property can be made stronger using the [Krull](wikipedia://en/964328) sharpening to:

$\|x+y\|\le \max \left\{ \|x\|, \|y\| \right\}$ with equality if $\|x\| \ne \|y\|$.

We want to prove that if $\|x+y\| \le \max \left\{ \|x\|, \|y\|\right\}$, then the equality occurs if $\|x\| \ne \|y\|$. [Without loss of generality](wikipedia://en/296000), let us assume that $\|x\| > \|y\|.$ This implies that $\|x + y\| \le \|x\|$. But we can also compute $\|x\|=\|(x+y)-y\| \le \max \left\{ \|x+y\|, \|y\|\right\}$. Now, the value of $\max \left\{ \|x+y\|, \|y\|\right\}$ cannot be $\|y\|$, for if that is the case, we have $\|x\| \le \|y\|$ contrary to the initial assumption. Thus, $\max \left\{ \|x+y\|, \|y\|\right\}=\|x+y\|$, and $\|x\| \le \|x+y\|$. Using the initial inequality, we have $\|x\| \le \|x + y\| \le \|x\|$ and therefore $\|x+y\| = \|x\|$.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

*Image: Strong\_triangle\_ineq.svg.* In the triangle on the right, the two bottom points *x* and *y* violate the condition *d*(*x*, *y*) ≤ max{*d*(*x*, *z*), *d*(*y*, *z*)}.

From the above definition, one can conclude several typical properties of ultrametrics. For example, for all $x,y,z \in M$, at least one of the three equalities $d(x,y) = d(y,z)$ or $d(x,z) = d(y,z)$ or $d(x,y) = d(z,x)$ holds. That is, every triple of points in the space forms an [isosceles triangle](wikipedia://en/315428), so the whole space is an [isosceles set](wikipedia://en/57831158).

Defining the [(open) ball](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_ball) of radius $r > 0$ centred at $x \in M$ as $B(x;r) := \{y \in M \mid d(x,y) < r\}$, we have the following properties:

- Every point inside a ball is one of its centers, i.e. if $d(x,y)<r$ then $B(x;r)=B(y;r)$.
- Intersecting balls are contained in each other, i.e. if $B(x;r)\cap B(y;s)$ is [non-empty](wikipedia://en/9566) then either $B(x;r) \subseteq B(y;s)$ or $B(y;s) \subseteq B(x;r)$.
- All balls of strictly positive radius are both [open](wikipedia://en/39358) and [closed sets](wikipedia://en/47279) in the induced [topology](wikipedia://en/29954). That is, open balls are also closed, and closed balls (replace $<$ with $\leq$) are also open.
- The set of all open balls with radius $r$ and center in a closed ball of radius $r>0$ forms a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of the latter, and the mutual distance of two distinct open balls is (greater or) equal to $r$.

Proving these statements is an instructive exercise.[^o7] All directly derive from the ultrametric triangle inequality. Note that, by the second statement, a ball may have several center points that have non-zero distance. The intuition behind such seemingly strange effects is that, due to the strong triangle inequality, distances in ultrametrics do not add up.

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- The [discrete metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_metric) is an ultrametric.
- The [*p*-adic numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P-adic_numbers) form a complete ultrametric space.
- Consider the [set of words](wikipedia://en/10939) of arbitrary length (finite or infinite),$\Sigma^*$, over some alphabet $\Sigma$. Define the distance between two different words to be $2^{-n}$, where $n$ is the first place at which the words differ. The resulting metric is an ultrametric.
- The [set of words](wikipedia://en/10939) with glued ends of the length $n$ over some alphabet $\Sigma$ is an ultrametric space with respect to the *p*-close distance. Two words $x$ and $y$ are *p*-close if any substring of *p* consecutive letters ( $p < n$) appears the same number of times (which could also be zero) both in $x$ and $y$.[^o10]
- If $r = (r_n)$ is a sequence of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) decreasing to zero, then $|x|_r := \limsup_{n\rightarrow\infty} |x_n|^{r_n}$ induces an ultrametric on the space of all complex sequences for which it is finite. (Note that this is not a [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067) since it lacks [homogeneity](wikipedia://en/622844) — If the $r_n$ are allowed to be zero, one should use here the rather unusual convention that $0^0=0$ ([Zero to the power of zero](wikipedia://en/32130416)).
- If $G$ is an edge-weighted [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph), all edge weights are positive, and $d(u,v)$ is the weight of the [minimax path](wikipedia://en/31567349) between $u$ and $v$ (that is, the largest weight of an edge, on a path chosen to minimize this largest weight), then the vertices of the graph, with distance measured by $d$, form an ultrametric space, and all finite ultrametric spaces may be represented in this way.[^o11]

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

- A [contraction mapping](wikipedia://en/6239) may then be thought of as a way of approximating the final result of a computation (which can be guaranteed to exist by the [Banach fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/46676)). Similar ideas can be found in [domain theory](wikipedia://en/325077). [*p*-adic analysis](wikipedia://en/291327) makes heavy use of the ultrametric nature of the [*p*-adic metric](wikipedia://en/51423).
- In [condensed matter physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Condensed_matter_physics), the [self-averaging](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Self-averaging) overlap between spins in the [SK Model](wikipedia://en/148555#Sherrington–Kirkpatrick_model) of [spin glasses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spin_glasses) exhibits an ultrametric structure, with the solution given by the full replica symmetry breaking procedure first outlined by [Giorgio Parisi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_Parisi) and coworkers.[^o14] Ultrametricity also appears in the theory of aperiodic solids.[^o15]
- In [taxonomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Taxonomy_%28biology%29) and [phylogenetic tree](wikipedia://en/149326) construction, ultrametric distances are also utilized by the [UPGMA](wikipedia://en/355968) and [WPGMA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/WPGMA) methods.[^o16] These algorithms require a constant-rate assumption and produce trees in which the distances from the root to every branch tip are equal. When [DNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/DNA), [RNA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/RNA) and [protein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Protein) data are analyzed, the ultrametricity assumption is called the [molecular clock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Molecular_clock).
- Models of [intermittency](wikipedia://en/4662960) in three dimensional [turbulence](wikipedia://en/154664) of fluids make use of so-called cascades, and in discrete models of dyadic cascades, which have an ultrametric structure.[^o17]
- In [geography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geography) and [landscape ecology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape_ecology), ultrametric distances have been applied to measure landscape complexity and to assess the extent to which one landscape function is more important than another.[^o18]
