# Isolated singularity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Isolated singularity"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **isolated singularity** is one that has no other [singularities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_singularity) close to it. In other words, a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826) $z_0$ is an isolated singularity of a function $f$ if there exists an [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [disk](wikipedia://en/329542) $D$ centered at $z_0$ such that *f* is [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110) on $D \smallsetminus \{z_0\}$, that is, on the [set](wikipedia://en/26691) obtained from $D$ by removing $z_0$ .

:::

Formally, and within the general scope of [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649), an isolated singularity of a [holomorphic function](wikipedia://en/14110) $f: \Omega\to \mathbb {C}$ is any [isolated point](wikipedia://en/390557) of the boundary $\partial \Omega$ of the domain $\Omega$. In other words, if $U$ is an open subset of $\mathbb {C}$, $a\in U$ and $f: U\smallsetminus \{a\}\to \mathbb {C}$ is a holomorphic function, then $a$ is an isolated singularity of $f$.

Every singularity of a [meromorphic function](wikipedia://en/80857) on an open subset $U\subset \mathbb{C}$ is isolated, but isolation of singularities alone is not sufficient to guarantee a function is meromorphic. Many important tools of complex analysis such as [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213) and the [residue theorem](wikipedia://en/73390) require that all relevant singularities of the function be isolated. Isolated singularities may be classified into three distinct types: [removable singularities](wikipedia://en/81644), [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) and [essential singularities](wikipedia://en/80862).

::: {#o3 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- The function $\textstyle \frac{1}{z}$ has $0$ as an isolated singularity.
- The [cosecant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosecant) function $\csc \left(\pi z\right)$ has every [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) as an isolated singularity.

:::

## Nonisolated singularities {#Nonisolated_singularities}

Other than isolated singularities, complex functions of one variable may exhibit other singular behavior. Namely, two kinds of nonisolated singularities exist:

- **Cluster points**, i.e. [limit points](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Limit_points) of isolated singularities: if they are all poles, despite admitting [Laurent series](wikipedia://en/61213) expansions on each of them, no such expansion is possible at its limit.
- **Natural boundaries**, i.e. any non-isolated set (e.g. a curve) around which functions cannot be [analytically continued](wikipedia://en/80842) (or outside them if they are closed curves in the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799)).

::: {#o6 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Natural\_boundary\_example.gif.* The natural boundary of this power series is the unit circle (read examples).

- The function $\tan\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)$ is [meromorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meromorphic) on $\mathbb{C}\smallsetminus\{0\}$, with simple poles at $\textstyle z_n = \left(\frac{\pi}{2}+n\pi\right)^{-1}$, for every $n\in\mathbb{N}_0$. Since $z_n\rightarrow 0$, every punctured disk centered at $0$ has an infinite number of singularities within it, so no Laurent expansion is available for $\textstyle \tan\left(\frac{1}{z}\right)$ around $0$, which is in fact a cluster point of its poles.
- The function $\textstyle \csc \left(\frac {\pi} {z}\right)$ has a singularity at $0$ that is *not* isolated, since there are additional singularities at the [reciprocal](wikipedia://en/229940) of every [integer](wikipedia://en/14563), which are located arbitrarily close to $0$ (though the singularities at these reciprocals are themselves isolated).
- The function defined via the [Maclaurin series](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maclaurin_series) $\textstyle \sum_{n=0}^{\infty}z^{2^n}$ converges inside the open unit disk centred at $0$ and has the unit circle as its natural boundary.

:::
