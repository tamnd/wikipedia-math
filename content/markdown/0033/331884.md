# Particle horizon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Particle horizon"}

The **particle horizon** (also called the **cosmological horizon**, the **comoving horizon**[^o5], or the **cosmic light horizon**) is the maximum distance from which light from [particles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elementary_particle) could have traveled to the [observer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observation) in the [age of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_universe). Much like the concept of a [terrestrial horizon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizon), it represents the boundary between the observable and the unobservable regions of the universe,[^o2] so its distance at the present epoch defines the size of the [observable universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Observable_universe).[^o3] Due to the expansion of the universe, it is not simply the [age of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_universe) times the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) (approximately 13.8 billion light-years), but rather the speed of light times the conformal time. The existence, properties, and significance of a cosmological horizon depend on the particular [cosmological model](wikipedia://en/5378).

:::

## Kinematic model {#Kinematic_model}

The particle horizon is a distance in a comoving coordinate system, a system that has the expansion of the universe built in. The expansion is defined by a (dimensionless) [scale factor](wikipedia://en/921168) $a(t)$ set to have a value of one today. The time that light takes to travel a distance $dx$ in the comoving coordinate system will be $dx=dt/a(t)$ in units of light years ($c=1$). The total distance light can travel in the time $t$ since the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116) at $t=0$ sums all the incremental distances:[^o5]

$$\eta = \int_{0}^{t} \frac{dt'}{a(t')}$$

The *comoving horizon* $\eta$ increases monotonically and thus can be used a time parameter: the particle horizon is equal to the *conformal time* $\eta$ that has passed since the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116), times the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) $c$.[^o5]

By convention, a subscript 0 indicates "today" so that the conformal time today $\eta(t_0) = \eta_0 = 1.48 \times 10^{18}\text{ s}$. Note that the conformal time is *not* the [age of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Age_of_the_universe) as generally understood. *That* age refers instead to a time as defined by the Robertson-Walker form of the cosmological metric, which time is presumed to be measured by a traditional clock and estimated to be around $4.35 \times 10^{17}\text{ s}$. By contrast $\eta_0$ is the age of the universe as measured by a Marzke-Wheeler "light clock".[^o6]

The particle horizon recedes constantly as time passes and the conformal time grows. As such, the observed size of the universe always increases.[^o2][^o7] Since proper distance at a given time is just comoving distance times the scale factor[^o8] (with [comoving distance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Comoving_distance) normally defined to be equal to proper distance at the present time, so $a(t_0) = 1$ at present), the proper distance, $d_p(t),$ to the particle horizon at time $t$ is given by[^o9]

$$d_p(t) = a(t) \int_{0}^{t} \frac{c\,dt'}{a(t')}$$

The value of the distance to the horizon depends on details in $a(t)$.

## Evolution of the particle horizon {#Evolution_of_the_particle_horizon}

In this section we consider the [FLRW](wikipedia://en/343031) cosmological model. In that context, the universe can be approximated as composed by non-interacting constituents, each one being a [perfect fluid](wikipedia://en/2137509) with density $\rho_i$, partial pressure $p_i$ and [state equation](wikipedia://en/1540704) $p_i=\omega_i \rho_i$, such that they add up to the total density $\rho$ and total pressure $p$.[^o11] Let us now define the following functions:

- Hubble function $H=\frac{\dot a}{a}$
- The critical density $\rho_c=\frac{3}{8\pi G}H^2$
- The *i*-th dimensionless energy density $\Omega_i=\frac{\rho_i}{\rho_c}$
- The dimensionless energy density $\Omega=\frac \rho {\rho_c}=\sum \Omega_i$
- The redshift $z$ given by the formula $1+z=\frac{a_0}{a(t)}$

Any function with a zero subscript denote the function evaluated at the present time $t_0$ (or equivalently $z=0$). The last term can be taken to be $1$ including the curvature state equation.[^o12] It can be proved that the Hubble function is given by

$$H(z)=H_0\sqrt{\sum \Omega_{i0}(1+z)^{n_i}}$$

where the dilution exponent $n_i=3(1+\omega_i)$. Notice that the addition ranges over all possible partial constituents and in particular there can be countably infinitely many. With this notation we have:[^o12]

$$\text{The particle horizon } d_p \text{ exists if and only if } N>2$$

where $N$ is the largest $n_i$ (possibly infinite). The evolution of the particle horizon for an expanding universe ($\dot{a}>0$) is:[^o12]

$$\frac{d}{dt}d_p=d_p(z)H(z)+c$$

where $c$ is the speed of light and can be taken to be $1$ ([natural units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_units)). Notice that the derivative is made with respect to the FLRW-time $t$, while the functions are evaluated at the redshift $z$ which are related as stated before. We have an analogous but slightly different result for [event horizon](wikipedia://en/29320146).

## Horizon problem {#Horizon_problem}

The concept of a particle horizon can be used to illustrate the [horizon problem](wikipedia://en/695215), which is an unresolved issue associated with the [Big Bang](wikipedia://en/4116) model. Extrapolating back to the time of [recombination](wikipedia://en/26244469) when the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376) (CMB) was emitted, we obtain a particle horizon of about

$$H_p(t_\text{CMB}) = c\eta_\text{CMB} = 284\text{ Mpc} = 8.9 \times 10^{-3} H_p(t_0)$$

which corresponds to a proper size at that time of:

$$a_\text{CMB}H_p(t_\text{CMB}) = 261\text{ kpc}$$

Since we observe the CMB to be emitted essentially from our particle horizon ($284\text{ Mpc} \ll 14.4\text{ Gpc}$), our expectation is that parts of the [cosmic microwave background](wikipedia://en/7376) (CMB) that are separated by about a fraction of a [great circle](wikipedia://en/48082) across the sky of

$$f = \frac{H_p(t_\text{CMB})}{H_p(t_0)}$$

(an [angular size](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angular_size) of $\theta \sim 1.7^\circ$)[^o14] should be out of [causal contact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Causal_contact) with each other. That the entire CMB is in [thermal equilibrium](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thermal_equilibrium) and approximates a [blackbody](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Blackbody) so well is therefore not explained by the standard explanations about the way the [expansion of the universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Expansion_of_the_universe) proceeds. The most popular resolution to this problem is [cosmic inflation](wikipedia://en/5382).
