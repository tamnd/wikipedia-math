# Girsanov theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Girsanov theorem"}

*Image: Girsanov.png.* Visualisation of the Girsanov theorem. The left side shows a [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984) with negative drift under a canonical measure *P*; on the right side each path of the process is colored according to its [likelihood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Likelihood) under the martingale measure *Q*. The density transformation from *P* to *Q* is given by the Girsanov theorem.

In [probability theory](wikipedia://en/23542), **Girsanov's theorem** or the **Cameron-Martin-Girsanov theorem** explains how [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895) change under changes in [measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measure_%28probability%29). The theorem is especially important in the theory of [financial mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_mathematics) as it explains how to convert from the [physical measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_measure), which describes the probability that an [underlying instrument](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Underlying_instrument) (such as a [share](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stock) price or [interest rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Interest_rate)) will take a particular value or values, to the [risk-neutral measure](wikipedia://en/441911) which is a very useful tool for evaluating the value of [derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28finance%29) on the underlying instrument.

:::

## History {#History}

Results of this type were first proved by [Robert Horton Cameron](wikipedia://en/37645495) and [W. T. Martin](wikipedia://en/37661503) in the 1940s and by [Igor Girsanov](wikipedia://en/26554897) in 1960. They have been subsequently extended to more general classes of process culminating in the general form of Érik Lenglart (1977).

## Significance {#Significance}

Girsanov's theorem is important in the general theory of stochastic processes since it enables the key result that if *Q* is a [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) that is [absolutely continuous](wikipedia://en/252670) with respect to *P* then every *P*-semimartingale is a *Q*-semimartingale.

::: {#o5 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Statement of theorem"}

## Statement of theorem {#Statement_of_theorem}

We state the theorem first for the special case when the underlying stochastic process is a [Wiener process](wikipedia://en/149984). This special case is sufficient for risk-neutral pricing in the [Black–Scholes model](wikipedia://en/113515).

Let $\{W_t\}$ be a Wiener process on the Wiener probability space $\{\Omega,\mathcal{F},P\}$. Let $X_t$ be a measurable process adapted to the [natural filtration](wikipedia://en/5864648) of the Wiener process $\{\mathcal{F}^W_t\}$; we assume that the usual conditions have been satisfied.

Given an adapted process $X_t$ define

$$Z_t=\mathcal{E} (X)_t,\,$$

where $\mathcal{E}(X)$ is the [stochastic exponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_exponential) of *X* with respect to *W*, i.e.

$$\mathcal{E}(X)_t=\exp \left ( X_t - \frac{1}{2} [X]_t \right ),$$

and $[X]_t$ denotes the [quadratic variation](wikipedia://en/2143889) of the process *X*.

If $Z_t$ is a [martingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martingale_%28probability%29) then a probability measure *Q* can be defined on $\{\Omega,\mathcal{F}\}$ such that the [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative) of *Q* with respect to *P* satisfies

$$\left .\frac{d Q}{d P} \right|_{\mathcal{F}_t} = Z_t = \mathcal{E} (X )_t.$$

Then for each *t* the measure *Q* restricted to the unaugmented sigma fields $\mathcal{F}^o_t$ is equivalent to *P* restricted to

$$\mathcal{F}^o_t.\,$$

Furthermore, if $Y_t$ is a [local martingale](wikipedia://en/7302795) under *P* then the process

$$\tilde Y_t = Y_t - \left[ Y,X \right]_t$$

is a *Q* local martingale on the filtered probability space $\{\Omega,F,Q,\{\mathcal{F}^W_t\}\}$.

:::

::: {#o7 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=named-heading name="Corollary"}

## Corollary {#Corollary}

If *X* is a continuous process and *W* is a Brownian motion under measure *P* then

$$\tilde W_t =W_t - \left [ W, X \right]_t$$

is a Brownian motion under *Q*.

The fact that $\tilde W_t$ is continuous is trivial; by Girsanov's theorem it is a *Q* local martingale, and by computing

$$\left[\tilde W \right]_t= \left [ W \right]_t = t$$

it follows by Levy's characterization of Brownian motion that this is a *Q* Brownian motion.

### Comments {#Comments}

In many common applications, the process $X$ is defined by

$$X_t = \int_0^t Y_s\, d W_s.$$

For $X$ of this form then a necessary and sufficient condition for $\mathcal{E}(X)$ to be a martingale is [Novikov's condition](wikipedia://en/9268061) which requires that

$$E_P\left [\exp\left (\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T Y_s^2\, ds\right )\right ] < \infty.$$

The stochastic exponential $\mathcal{E}(X)$ is the process $Z$ which solves the stochastic differential equation

$$Z_t = 1 + \int_0^t Z_s\, d X_s.\,$$

The measure $Q$ constructed above is not equivalent to $P$ on $\mathcal{F}_\infty$ as this would only be the case if the Radon–Nikodym derivative were a uniformly integrable martingale, which the exponential martingale described above is not. On the other hand, as long as Novikov's condition is satisfied the measures are equivalent on $\mathcal{F}_T$.

Additionally, then combining this above observation in this case, we see that the process

$$\tilde{W}_t=W_t-\int_0^tY_sds$$

for $t\in [0,T]$ is a $Q-$ Brownian motion. This was Igor Girsanov's original formulation of the above theorem.

:::

## Application to finance {#Application_to_finance}

Consider a non-dividend-paying risky asset whose price under the [physical measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_measure) $P$ follows the [stochastic differential equation](wikipedia://en/1361454)

$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=\mu_t\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t^P, \qquad \sigma_t>0,$$

where $\mu_t$ is the asset's drift, $\sigma_t$ is its instantaneous [volatility](wikipedia://en/11930108), and $W^P$ is a [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/149984) under $P$. Let

$$\lambda_t=\frac{\mu_t-r_t}{\sigma_t},$$

where $r_t$ is the [risk-free rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Risk-free_rate), and suppose that the associated [stochastic exponential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stochastic_exponential) is a martingale. Define a new probability measure $Q$ by

$$\left.\frac{dQ}{dP}\right|_{\mathcal F_t} = \mathcal E\left(-\int_0^t\lambda_s\,dW_s^P\right).$$

Girsanov's theorem then implies that

$$W_t^Q=W_t^P+\int_0^t\lambda_s\,ds$$

is a Brownian motion under $Q$. Substitution into the asset-price equation gives

$$\frac{dS_t}{S_t}=r_t\,dt+\sigma_t\,dW_t^Q.$$

Thus, the change from the physical measure to the [risk-neutral measure](wikipedia://en/441911) changes the asset's drift from $\mu_t$ to the risk-free rate $r_t$, while leaving the diffusion coefficient $\sigma_t$, or instantaneous volatility, unchanged.[^o10][^o11]

This invariance concerns the diffusion coefficient within a specified model. It does not imply that estimates of [historical volatility](wikipedia://en/11930108) and [implied volatility](wikipedia://en/253568) must be equal; those estimates may differ in practice.[^o11]

Under $Q$, the discounted asset-price process is a martingale. In an arbitrage-free and [complete market](wikipedia://en/2189987), such as the [Black–Scholes-Merton model](wikipedia://en/113515), the risk-neutral measure is unique, and the value at time $t$ of a [contingent claim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contingent_claim) paying $H_T$ at time $T$ is

$$V_t = E_Q\left[ \left. \exp\left(-\int_t^T r_s\,ds\right)H_T \right|\mathcal F_t \right].$$

Girsanov's theorem provides the change of measure, while the uniqueness of the measure and the valuation formula depend on the model's no-arbitrage and completeness assumptions.[^o12][^o13]

## Application to Langevin equations {#Application_to_Langevin_equations}

Another application of this theorem, also given in the original paper of Igor Girsanov, is for [stochastic differential equations](wikipedia://en/1361454). Specifically, let us consider the equation:

$$dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t.$$

where $W_t$ denotes a Brownian motion. Here $\mu$ and $\sigma$ are fixed deterministic functions. We assume that this equation has a unique strong solution on $[0,T]$. In this case Girsanov's theorem may be used to compute functionals of $X_t$ directly in terms of a related functional for Brownian motion. More specifically, we have for any bounded functional $\Phi$ on continuous functions $C([0,T])$ that

$$\mathbb{E} \Phi(X)=\mathbb{E} \left[ \Phi(W)\exp\left(\int_0^T \mu(W_s,s)dW_s-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T\mu(W_s,s)^2ds\right)\right].$$

This follows by applying Girsanov's theorem, and the above observation, to the martingale process

$$Y_t=\int_0^t\mu(W_s,s)dW_s.$$

In particular, with the notation above, the process

$$\tilde{W}_t=W_t-\int_0^t\mu(W_s,s)ds$$

is a $Q$ Brownian motion. Rewriting this in differential notation as

$$dW_t=d\tilde{W}_t+\mu(W_t,t)dt,$$

we see that the law of $W_t$ under $Q$ solves the equation defining $X_t$, as $\tilde{W}_t$ is a $Q$ Brownian motion. In particular, we see that the right-hand side may be written as $\mathbb{E}_Q[\Phi(W)]$, where $Q$ is the measure taken with respect to the process $Y$, so the result now is just the statement of Girsanov's theorem.

A more general form of this application is that if both

$dX_t=\mu(X_t,t)dt+\sigma(X_t,t)dW_t,$ $dY_t=(\mu(Y_t,t)+\nu(Y_t,t))dt+\sigma(Y_t,t)dW_t,$

admit unique strong solutions on $[0,T]$, then for any bounded functional on $C([0,T])$, we have that

$$\mathbb{E} \Phi(X)= \mathbb{E} \left[ \Phi(Y)\exp\left(-\int_0^T \frac{\nu(Y_s,s)}{\sigma(Y_s,s)}dW_s-\frac{1}{2}\int_0^T \frac{\nu(Y_s,s)^2}{\sigma(Y_s,s)^2}ds\right)\right].$$
