# Horn clause

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Horn clause"}

*Image: HornClauseExampleInImplicationForm.svg.* A definite Horn clause example in implication form

In [mathematical logic](wikipedia://en/19636) and [logic programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_programming), a **Horn clause** is a [logical formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logical_formula) of a particular rule-like form that gives it useful properties for use in logic programming, [formal specification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Formal_specification), [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200) and [model theory](wikipedia://en/19858). Horn clauses are named for the logician [Alfred Horn](wikipedia://en/3115802), who first pointed out their significance in 1951.[^n1]

:::

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A Horn clause is a disjunctive [clause](wikipedia://en/4638484) (a [disjunction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Disjunction) of [literals](wikipedia://en/7792761)) with at most one positive, i.e. [unnegated](wikipedia://en/161019), literal.

Conversely, a disjunction of literals with at most one negated literal is called a **dual-Horn clause**.

A Horn clause with exactly one positive literal is a **definite clause** or a **strict Horn clause**;[^n2] a definite clause with no negative literals is a **unit clause**,[^n3] and a unit clause without variables is a **fact**;[^n4] a Horn clause without a positive literal is a **goal clause**. The empty clause, consisting of no literals (which is equivalent to *false*), is a goal clause. These three kinds of Horn clauses are illustrated in the following [propositional](wikipedia://en/1557634) example:

::: {#o8 .table cue=table}

| Type of Horn clause | Disjunction form | [Implication](wikipedia://en/658808) form | Read intuitively as |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Definite clause** | ¬*p* ∨ ¬*q* ∨ ... ∨ ¬*t* ∨ *u* | *u* ← (*p* ∧ *q* ∧ ... ∧ *t*) | assume that, if *p* and *q* and ... and *t* all hold, then also *u* holds |
| **Fact** | *u* | *u* ← *true* | assume that *u* holds |
| **Goal clause** | ¬*p* ∨ ¬*q* ∨ ... ∨ ¬*t* | *false* ← (*p* ∧ *q* ∧ ... ∧ *t*) | show that *p* and *q* and ... and *t* all hold[^o9] |

:::

All variables in a clause are implicitly [universally quantified](wikipedia://en/76174) with the scope being the entire clause. Thus, for example:

¬ *human*(*X*) ∨ *mortal*(*X*)

stands for:

∀X( ¬ *human*(*X*) ∨ *mortal*(*X*) ),

which is logically equivalent to:

∀X ( *human*(*X*) → *mortal*(*X*) ).

### Significance {#Significance}

Horn clauses play a basic role in [constructive logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constructive_logic) and [computational logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_logic). They are important in [automated theorem proving](wikipedia://en/2546) by [first-order resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First-order_resolution), because the [resolvent](wikipedia://en/2724082) of two Horn clauses is itself a Horn clause, and the resolvent of a goal clause and a definite clause is a goal clause. These properties of Horn clauses can lead to greater efficiency of proving a theorem: the goal clause is the negation of this theorem; see *Goal clause* in the above table. Intuitively, if we wish to prove φ, we assume ¬φ (the goal) and check whether such assumption leads to a contradiction. If so, then φ must hold. This way, a mechanical proving tool needs to maintain only one set of formulas (assumptions), rather than two sets (assumptions and (sub)goals).

Propositional Horn clauses are also of interest in [computational complexity](wikipedia://en/7543). The problem of finding truth-value assignments to make a conjunction of propositional Horn clauses true is known as [HORNSAT](wikipedia://en/1227519). This problem is [P-complete](wikipedia://en/54683) and solvable in [linear time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_time).[^n5] In contrast, the unrestricted [Boolean satisfiability problem](wikipedia://en/4715) is an [NP-complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-complete) problem.

In [universal algebra](wikipedia://en/45200), definite Horn clauses are generally called [quasi-identities](wikipedia://en/12748025); classes of algebras definable by a set of quasi-identities are called [quasivarieties](wikipedia://en/12747933) and enjoy some of the good properties of the more restrictive notion of a [variety](wikipedia://en/1021753), i.e., an equational class.[^n6] From the model-theoretical point of view, Horn sentences are important since they are exactly (up to logical equivalence) those sentences preserved under [reduced products](wikipedia://en/2174269); in particular, they are preserved under [direct products](wikipedia://en/8887). On the other hand, there are sentences that are not Horn but are nevertheless preserved under arbitrary direct products.[^n7]

:::

## Logic programming {#Logic_programming}

Horn clauses are also the basis of [logic programming](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_programming), where it is common to write definite clauses in the form of an [implication](wikipedia://en/658808):

(*p* ∧ *q* ∧ ... ∧ *t*) → *u*

In fact, the resolution of a goal clause with a definite clause to produce a new goal clause is the basis of the [SLD resolution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SLD_resolution) inference rule, used in implementation of the logic programming language [Prolog](wikipedia://en/23485).

In logic programming, a definite clause behaves as a goal-reduction procedure. For example, the Horn clause written above behaves as the procedure:

to show *u*, show *p* and show *q* and ... and show *t*.

To emphasize this reverse use of the clause, it is often written in the reverse form:

*u* ← (*p* ∧ *q* ∧ ... ∧ *t*)

In [Prolog](wikipedia://en/23485) this is written as:

```prolog
u :- p, q, ..., t.
```

In logic programming, a goal clause, which has the logical form

∀*X* (*false* ← *p* ∧ *q* ∧ ... ∧ *t*)

represents the negation of a problem to be solved. The problem itself is an existentially quantified conjunction of positive literals:

∃*X* (*p* ∧ *q* ∧ ... ∧ *t*)

The Prolog notation does not have explicit quantifiers and is written in the form:

```prolog
:- p, q, ..., t.
```

This notation is ambiguous in the sense that it can be read either as a statement of the problem or as a statement of the denial of the problem. However, both readings are correct. In both cases, solving the problem amounts to deriving the empty clause. In Prolog notation this is equivalent to deriving:

```prolog
:- true.
```

If the top-level goal clause is read as the denial of the problem, then the empty clause represents *false* and the proof of the empty clause is a refutation of the denial of the problem. If the top-level goal clause is read as the problem itself, then the empty clause represents *true*, and the proof of the empty clause is a proof that the problem has a solution.

The solution of the problem is a substitution of terms for the variables *X* in the top-level goal clause, which can be extracted from the resolution proof. Used in this way, goal clauses are similar to [conjunctive queries](wikipedia://en/13477276) in [relational databases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relational_database), and Horn clause logic is equivalent in computational power to a [universal Turing machine](wikipedia://en/71435).

Van Emden and Kowalski (1976) investigated the model-theoretic properties of Horn clauses in the context of logic programming, showing that every set of definite clauses **D** has a unique minimal model **M**. An [atomic formula](wikipedia://en/4472066) **A** is logically implied by **D** if and only if **A** is true in **M**. It follows that a problem **P** represented by an existentially quantified conjunction of positive literals is logically implied by **D** if and only if **P** is true in **M**. The minimal model semantics of Horn clauses is the basis for the [stable model semantics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stable_model_semantics) of logic programs.[^n8]
