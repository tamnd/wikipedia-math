# Quotient

*Image: Divide12by3.svg.* The quotient of 12 apples by 3 apples is 4.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quotient"}

In [arithmetic](wikipedia://en/3118), a **quotient** (from quotiens 'how many times', pronounced ) is a quantity produced by the [division](wikipedia://en/53696) of two numbers.[^o2] The quotient has widespread use throughout mathematics. It has two definitions: either the integer part of a division (in the case of [Euclidean division](wikipedia://en/679987))[^o3] or a [fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraction_%28mathematics%29) or [ratio](wikipedia://en/87837) (in the case of a general division). For example, when dividing 20 (the *dividend*) by 3 (the *divisor*), the *quotient* is 6 (with a remainder of 2) in the first sense and $6+\tfrac{2}{3}=6.66...$ (a [repeating decimal](wikipedia://en/13612447)) in the second sense.

:::

## Unit of measurement {#Unit_of_measurement}

In [metrology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metrology) ([International System of Quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Quantities) and the [International System of Units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_System_of_Units)), "quotient" refers to the general case with respect to the [units of measurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Units_of_measurement) of [physical quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_quantity).[^o7][^o5] [^o6] *Ratios* is the special case for [dimensionless](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless) quotients of two quantities of the same [kind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kind_of_quantity).[^o7][^o8] Quotients with a non-trivial [dimension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimension_%28physics%29) and [compound units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compound_unit), especially when the divisor is a duration (e.g., "[per second](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Per_second)"), are known as [*rates*](wikipedia://en/332372).[^o9] For example, [density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Density) (mass divided by volume, in units of [kg/m<sup>3</sup>](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilogram_per_cubic_metre)) is said to be a "quotient", whereas [mass fraction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mass_fraction_%28chemistry%29) (mass divided by mass, in kg/kg or in percent) is a "ratio".[^o10] *[Specific quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Specific_quantity)* are [intensive quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Intensive_quantity) resulting from the quotient of a physical quantity by mass, volume, or other measures of the system "size".[^o7]

::: {#o12 .definition kind=definition/notation cue=heading name="Notation"}

## Notation {#Notation}

The quotient is most frequently encountered as two numbers, or two variables, divided by a horizontal line. The words "dividend" and "divisor" refer to each individual part, while the word "quotient" refers to the whole.

$$\dfrac{1}{2} \quad \begin{aligned} & \leftarrow \text{dividend or numerator} \\ & \leftarrow \text{divisor or denominator} \end{aligned} \Biggr \} \leftarrow \text{quotient}$$

:::

## Integer part definition {#Integer_part_definition}

The quotient is also less commonly defined as the greatest [whole number](wikipedia://en/21474) of times a divisor may be subtracted from a dividend—before making the [remainder](wikipedia://en/502897) negative. For example, the divisor 3 may be subtracted up to 6 times from the dividend 20, before the remainder becomes negative:

20 − 3 − 3 − 3 − 3 − 3 − 3 ≥ 0,

while

20 − 3 − 3 − 3 − 3 − 3 − 3 − 3 \< 0.

In this sense, a quotient is the [integer part](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integer_part) of the ratio of two numbers.

## Quotient of two integers {#Quotient_of_two_integers}

A [rational number](wikipedia://en/19727024) can be defined as the quotient of two [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) (as long as the denominator is non-zero).

A more detailed definition goes as follows:[^o16]

A real number *r* is rational, if and only if it can be expressed as a quotient of two integers with a nonzero denominator. A real number that is not rational is irrational.

Or more formally:

Given a real number *r*, *r* is rational if and only if there exists integers *a* and *b* such that $r = \tfrac a b$ and $b \neq 0$.

The existence of [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689)—numbers that are not a quotient of two integers—was first discovered in geometry, in such things as the ratio of the diagonal to the side in a square.[^o17]

## More general quotients {#More_general_quotients}

Outside of arithmetic, many branches of mathematics have borrowed the word "quotient" to describe structures built by breaking larger structures into pieces. Given a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) with an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259) defined on it, a "[quotient set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_set)" may be created which contains those equivalence classes as elements. A [quotient group](wikipedia://en/11526) may be formed by breaking a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) into a number of similar [cosets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosets), while a [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) may be formed in a similar process by breaking a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) into a number of similar [linear subspaces](wikipedia://en/56357).
