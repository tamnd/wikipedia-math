# Alternating factorial

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Alternating factorial"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), an **alternating factorial** is the [absolute value](wikipedia://en/991) of the [alternating sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alternating_sum) of the first *n* [factorials](wikipedia://en/10606) of [positive integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_integer).

:::

This is the same as their sum, with the [odd](wikipedia://en/143135)-indexed factorials multiplied by [−1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/-1_%28number%29) if *n* is [even](wikipedia://en/143135), and the even-indexed factorials multiplied by −1 if *n* is odd, resulting in an alternation of signs of the summands (or alternation of addition and subtraction operators, if preferred). To put it algebraically,

$$\operatorname{af}(n) = \sum_{i = 1}^n (-1)^{n - i}i!$$

or with the [recurrence relation](wikipedia://en/146806)

$$\operatorname{af}(n) = n! - \operatorname{af}(n - 1)$$

in which af(1) = 1.

The first few alternating factorials are

[1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1_%28number%29), 1, [5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/5_%28number%29), [19](wikipedia://en/208292), [101](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/101_%28number%29), 619, 4421, 35899, 326981, 3301819, 36614981, 442386619, 5784634181, 81393657019

For example, the third alternating factorial is 1! – 2! + 3!. The fourth alternating factorial is −1! + 2! − 3! + 4! = 19. Regardless of the [parity](wikipedia://en/143135) of *n*, the last (*n*th) summand, *n*!, is given a positive sign, the (*n* – 1)th summand is given a negative sign, and the signs of the lower-indexed summands are alternated accordingly.

This pattern of alternation ensures the resulting sums are all positive integers. Changing the rule so that either the odd- or even-indexed summands are given negative signs (regardless of the parity of *n*) changes the signs of the resulting sums but not their absolute values.

(Živković 1999) proved that there are only a finite number of alternating factorials that are also [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), since 3612703 [divides](wikipedia://en/49492) af(3612702) and therefore divides af(*n*) for all *n* ≥ 3612702.[^n1] The primes are af(*n*) for

*n* = 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 10, 15, 19, 41, 59, 61, 105, 160, 661, ...

with several higher [probable primes](wikipedia://en/160560) that have not been proven prime.
