# Krein–Milman theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Krein–Milman theorem"}

*Image: Extreme points.svg.* Given a convex shape $K$ (light blue) and its set of extreme points $B$ (red), the convex hull of $B$ is $K.$

In the [mathematical theory](wikipedia://en/18831) of [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180), the **Krein–Milman theorem** is a [proposition](wikipedia://en/30977) about [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) [convex sets](wikipedia://en/6292) in [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector spaces](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVSs).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Krein–Milman theorem"}

**Krein–Milman theorem[^n1].**

A [compact](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_set) [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) subset of a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) is equal to the closed [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of its [extreme points](wikipedia://en/454968).

:::

This theorem generalizes to infinite-dimensional spaces and to arbitrary compact convex sets the following basic observation: a convex (i.e. "filled") triangle, including its perimeter and the area "inside of it", is equal to the convex hull of its three vertices, where these vertices are exactly the extreme points of this shape. This observation also holds for any other convex [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621) in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2.$

## Statement and definitions {#Statement_and_definitions}

### Preliminaries and definitions {#Preliminaries_and_definitions}

*Image: Extreme points.svg.* A convex set in light blue, and its extreme points in red.

Throughout, $X$ will be a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) or [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370).

For any elements $x$ and $y$ in a vector space, the set $[x, y] := \{tx + (1-t)y : 0 \leq t \leq 1\}$ is called the **closed line segment** or **closed interval** between $x$ and $y.$ The **open line segment** or **open interval** between $x$ and $y$ is $(x, y) := \varnothing$ when $x = y$ while it is $(x, y) := \{tx + (1-t)y : 0 < t < 1\}$ when $x \neq y;$[^n2] it satisfies $(x, y) = [x, y] \setminus \{ x, y \}$ and $[x, y] = (x, y) \cup \{x, y\}.$ The points $x$ and $y$ are called the **endpoints** of these interval. An interval is said to be **non-degenerate** or **proper** if its endpoints are distinct.

The intervals $[x, x] = \{x\}$ and $[x, y]$ always contain their endpoints while $(x, x) = \varnothing$ and $(x, y)$ never contain either of their endpoints. If $x$ and $y$ are points in the real line $\mathbb{R}$ then the above definition of $[x, y]$ is the same as its usual definition as a [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval).

For any $p, x, y \in X,$ the point $p$ is said to (strictly) **lie between** $x$ and $y$ if $p$ belongs to the open line segment $(x, y).$[^n2]

If $K$ is a subset of $X$ and $p \in K,$ then $p$ is called an **[extreme point](wikipedia://en/454968)** of $K$ if it does not lie between any two distinct points of $K.$ That is, if there does not exist $x, y \in K$ and $0 < t < 1$ such that $x \neq y$ and $p = tx + (1-t) y.$ In this article, the set of all extreme points of $K$ will be denoted by $\operatorname{extreme}(K).$[^n2]

For example, the vertices of any convex polygon in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2$ are the extreme points of that polygon. The extreme points of the [closed unit disk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_unit_disk) in $\mathbb{R}^2$ is the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071). Every [open interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_interval) and degenerate closed interval in $\mathbb{R}$ has no extreme points while the extreme points of a non-degenerate [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval) $[x, y]$ are $x$ and $y.$

A set $S$ is called **convex** if for any two points $x, y \in S,$ $S$ contains the line segment $[x, y].$ The smallest convex set containing $S$ is called the **[convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634)** of $S$ and it is denoted by $\operatorname{co} S.$ The **closed convex hull** of a set $S,$ denoted by $\overline{\operatorname{co}}(S),$ is the smallest closed and convex set containing $S.$ It is also equal to the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all closed convex subsets that contain $S$ and to the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of the [convex hull](wikipedia://en/40634) of $S$; that is,

$$\overline{\operatorname{co}}(S) = \overline{\operatorname{co}(S)},$$

where the right hand side denotes the closure of $\operatorname{co}(S)$ while the left hand side is notation. For example, the convex hull of any set of three distinct points forms either a closed line segment (if they are [collinear](wikipedia://en/3189581)) or else a solid (that is, "filled") triangle, including its perimeter. And in the plane $\mathbb{R}^2,$ the unit circle is not convex but the closed unit disk is convex and furthermore, this disk is equal to the convex hull of the circle.

The separable Hilbert space [Lp space](wikipedia://en/45194) $\ell^2(\mathbb{N})$ of square-summable sequences with the usual norm $\|\cdot\|_2$ has a compact subset $S$ whose convex hull $\operatorname{co}(S)$ is not closed and thus also not compact.[^n3] However, like in all [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) Hausdorff locally convex spaces, the closed convex hull $\overline{\operatorname{co}} S$ of this compact subset is compact.[^n4] But, if a Hausdorff locally convex space is not complete, then it is in general not guaranteed that $\overline{\operatorname{co}} S$ is compact whenever $S$ is; an example can even be found in a (non-complete) [pre-Hilbert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Hilbert_space) vector subspace of $\ell^2(\mathbb{N}).$ Every compact subset is [totally bounded](wikipedia://en/1528346#Topological_vector_spaces) (also called "precompact") and the closed convex hull of a totally bounded subset of a Hausdorff locally convex space is guaranteed to be totally bounded.[^n5]

::: {#o13 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

### Statement {#Statement}

::: {#o15 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Krein–Milman theorem"}

**Krein–Milman theorem[^n6].**

If $K$ is a compact subset of a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) then the set of [extreme points](wikipedia://en/454968) of $K$ has the same closed convex hull as $K.$

:::

In the case where the compact set $K$ is also convex, the above theorem has as a corollary the first part of the next theorem,[^n6] which is also often called the Krein–Milman theorem.

::: {#o18 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="Krein–Milman theorem"}

**Krein–Milman theorem[^n2].**

Suppose $X$ is a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (for example, a [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space)) and $K$ is a compact and convex subset of $X.$ Then $K$ is equal to the closed convex hull of its [extreme points](wikipedia://en/454968):

$$K ~=~ \overline{\operatorname{co}} (\operatorname{extreme}(K)).$$

Moreover, if $B \subseteq K$ then $K$ is equal to the closed convex hull of $B$ [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) $\operatorname{extreme} K \subseteq \operatorname{cl} B,$ where $\operatorname{cl} B$ is closure of $B.$

:::

The convex hull of the extreme points of $K$ forms a convex subset of $K$ so the main burden of the proof is to show that there are enough extreme points so that their convex hull covers all of $K.$ For this reason, the following corollary to the above theorem is also often called the Krein–Milman theorem.

::: {#o20 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="() Krein–Milman theorem (Existence)"}

**(KM) Krein–Milman theorem (Existence)[^n2].**

Every non-empty compact convex subset of a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) has an [extreme point](wikipedia://en/454968); that is, the set of its extreme points is not empty.

:::

To visualized this theorem and its conclusion, consider the particular case where $K$ is a convex [polygon](wikipedia://en/23621). In this case, the corners of the polygon (which are its extreme points) are all that is needed to recover the polygon shape. The statement of the theorem is false if the polygon is not convex, as then there are many ways of drawing a polygon having given points as corners.

The requirement that the convex set $K$ be compact can be weakened to give the following strengthened generalization version of the theorem.[^n7]

::: {#o23 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=math-theorem name="()"}

**(SKM) Strong Krein–Milman theorem (Existence)[^o22].**

Suppose $X$ is a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) and $K$ is a non-empty convex subset of $X$ with the property that whenever $\mathcal{C}$ is a cover of $K$ by convex closed subsets of $X$ such that $\{K \cap C : C \in \mathcal{C}\}$ has the [finite intersection property](wikipedia://en/365026), then $K \cap \bigcap_{C \in \mathcal{C}} C$ is not empty. Then $\operatorname{extreme}(K)$ is not empty.

:::

The property above is sometimes called quasicompactness or convex compactness. [Compactness](wikipedia://en/6042) implies convex compactness because a [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) is compact if and only if every [family](wikipedia://en/723043) of closed subsets having the [finite intersection property](wikipedia://en/365026) (FIP) has non-empty intersection (that is, its [kernel](wikipedia://en/330095) is not empty). The definition of convex compactness is similar to this characterization of [compact spaces](wikipedia://en/6042) in terms of the FIP, except that it only involves those closed subsets that are also [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) (rather than all closed subsets).

:::

## More general settings {#More_general_settings}

The assumption of [local convexity](wikipedia://en/518844) for the ambient space is necessary, because (txt) constructed a counter-example for the non-locally convex space [$L^p[0, 1]$](wikipedia://en/45194) where $0 < p < 1.$[^o26]

Linearity is also needed, because the statement fails for weakly compact convex sets in [CAT(0) spaces](wikipedia://en/2850665), as proved by (txt).[^o28] However, (txt) proved that the Krein–Milman theorem does hold for metrically compact CAT(0) spaces.[^o30]

## Related results {#Related_results}

Under the previous assumptions on $K,$ if $T$ is a [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of $K$ and the closed convex hull of $T$ is all of $K,$ then every [extreme point](wikipedia://en/454968) of $K$ belongs to the [closure](wikipedia://en/42309) of $T.$ This result is known as Milman's (partial) converse to the Krein–Milman theorem.[^o33]

The [Choquet–Bishop–de Leeuw theorem](wikipedia://en/3655598) states that every point in $K$ is the [barycenter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barycenter) of a [probability measure](wikipedia://en/43325) supported on the set of [extreme points](wikipedia://en/454968) of $K.$

:::

## Relation to the axiom of choice {#Relation_to_the_axiom_of_choice}

Under the [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) (**ZF**) axiomatic framework, the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840) (**AC**) suffices to prove all versions of the Krein–Milman theorem given above, including statement **KM** and its generalization **SKM**. The axiom of choice also implies, but is not equivalent to, the [Boolean prime ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/314919) (**BPI**), which is equivalent to the [Banach–Alaoglu theorem](wikipedia://en/577366). Conversely, the Krein–Milman theorem **KM** together with the [Boolean prime ideal theorem](wikipedia://en/314919) (**BPI**) imply the axiom of choice.[^o36] In summary, **AC** holds if and only if both **KM** and **BPI** hold.[^o22] It follows that under **ZF**, the axiom of choice is equivalent to the following statement:

The closed unit ball of the continuous dual space of any real normed space has an extreme point.[^o22]

Furthermore, **SKM** together with the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860) for [real vector spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector_space) (**HB**) are also equivalent to the axiom of choice.[^o22] It is known that **BPI** implies **HB**, but that it is not equivalent to it (said differently, **BPI** is strictly stronger than **HB**).

:::

## History {#History}

The original statement proved by (txt) was somewhat less general than the form stated here.[^o39]

Earlier, (txt) proved that if $X$ is [3-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3-dimensional) then $K$ equals the convex hull of the set of its extreme points.[^o41] This assertion was expanded to the case of any finite dimension by (txt).[^o43] The Krein–Milman theorem generalizes this to arbitrary locally convex $X$; however, to generalize from finite to infinite dimensional spaces, it is necessary to use the closure.
