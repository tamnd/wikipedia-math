# Zappa–Szép product

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Zappa–Szép product"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), especially [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **Zappa–Szép product** (also known as the **Zappa–Rédei–Szép product**, **general product**, **knit product**, **exact factorization** or **bicrossed product**) describes a way in which a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) can be constructed from two [subgroups](wikipedia://en/28011). It is a generalization of the [direct](wikipedia://en/3706015) and [semidirect products](wikipedia://en/48246). It is named after [Guido Zappa](wikipedia://en/23748418) (1940) and [Jenő Szép](wikipedia://en/40387382) (1950) although it was independently studied by others including B.H. Neumann (1935), G.A. Miller (1935), and J.A. de Séguier (1904).[^o2]

:::

## Internal Zappa–Szép products {#Internal_Zappa–Szép_products}

Let *G* be a group with [identity element](wikipedia://en/14962) *e*, and let *H* and *K* be subgroups of *G*. The following statements are equivalent:

- *G* = *HK* and *H* ∩ *K* = {*e*}
- For each *g* in *G*, there exists a unique *h* in *H* and a unique *k* in *K* such that *g = hk*.

If either (and hence both) of these statements hold, then *G* is said to be an internal **Zappa–Szép product** of *H* and *K*.

::: {#o5 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Let *G* = GL(*n*,**C**), the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) of [invertible](wikipedia://en/217122) *n × n* [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) over the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826). For each matrix *A* in *G*, the [QR decomposition](wikipedia://en/305223) asserts that there exists a unique [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222) *Q* and a unique [upper triangular matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular_matrix) *R* with [positive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) entries on the main [diagonal](wikipedia://en/349251#Matrices) such that *A* = *QR*. Thus *G* is a Zappa–Szép product of the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) *U*(*n*) and the group (say) *K* of upper triangular matrices with positive diagonal entries.

One of the most important examples of this is [Philip Hall](wikipedia://en/1655033)'s 1937 theorem on the existence of [Sylow systems](wikipedia://en/1735335) for [soluble groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soluble_group). This shows that every soluble group is a Zappa–Szép product of a Hall *p'*-subgroup and a Sylow *p*-subgroup, and in fact that the group is a (multiple factor) Zappa–Szép product of a certain set of representatives of its Sylow subgroups.

In 1935, [George Miller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Abram_Miller) showed that any non-regular transitive permutation group with a regular subgroup is a Zappa–Szép product of the regular subgroup and a point stabilizer. He gives PSL(2,11) and the alternating group of degree 5 as examples, and every alternating group of prime degree is also an example. This same paper gives a number of examples of groups which cannot be realized as Zappa–Szép products of proper subgroups, such as the quaternion group and the alternating group of degree 6.

:::

## External Zappa–Szép products {#External_Zappa–Szép_products}

As with the direct and semidirect products, there is an external version of the Zappa–Szép product for groups which are not known *a priori* to be subgroups of a given group. To motivate this, let *G* = *HK* be an internal Zappa–Szép product of subgroups *H* and *K* of the group *G*. For each *k* in *K* and each *h* in *H*, there exist α(*k*, *h*) in *H* and β(*k*, *h*) in *K* such that *kh* = α(*k*, *h*) β(*k*, *h*). This defines [mappings](wikipedia://en/516931) α : *K* × *H* → *H* and β : *K* × *H* → *K* which turn out to have the following properties:

- α(*e*, *h*) = *h* and β(*k*, *e*) = *k* for all *h* in *H* and *k* in *K*.
- α(*k*<sub>1</sub>*k*<sub>2</sub>, *h*) = α(*k*<sub>1</sub>, α(*k*<sub>2</sub>, *h*))
- β(*k*, *h*<sub>1</sub>*h*<sub>2</sub>) = β(β(*k*, *h*<sub>1</sub>), *h*<sub>2</sub>)
- α(*k*, *h*<sub>1</sub>*h*<sub>2</sub>) = α(*k*, *h*<sub>1</sub>)α(β(*k*, *h*<sub>1</sub>), *h*<sub>2</sub>)
- β(*k*<sub>1</sub>*k*<sub>2</sub>, *h*) = β(*k*<sub>1</sub>, α(*k*<sub>2</sub>, *h*))β(*k*<sub>2</sub>, *h*)

for all *h*<sub>1</sub>, *h*<sub>2</sub> in *H*, *k*<sub>1</sub>, *k*<sub>2</sub> in *K*. From these, it follows that

- For each *k* in *K*, the mapping *h* α(*k*, *h*) is a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) of *H*.
- For each *h* in *H*, the mapping *k* β(*k*, *h*) is a bijection of *K*.

(Indeed, suppose α(*k*, *h*<sub>1</sub>) = α(*k*, *h*<sub>2</sub>). Then *h*<sub>1</sub> = α(*k*<sup>−1</sup>*k*, *h*<sub>1</sub>) = α(*k*<sup>−1</sup>, α(*k*, *h*<sub>1</sub>)) = α(*k*<sup>−1</sup>, α(*k*, *h*<sub>2</sub>)) = *h*<sub>2</sub>. This establishes injectivity, and for surjectivity, use *h* = α(*k*, α(*k*<sup>−1</sup>,*h*)).)

More concisely, the first three properties above assert the mapping α : *K* × *H* → *H* is a [left action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Definition) of *K* on (the underlying set of) *H* and that β : *K* × *H* → *K* is a [right action](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Definition) of *H* on (the underlying set of) *K*. If we denote the left action by *h* → <sup>*k*</sup>*h* and the right action by *k* → *k*<sup>*h*</sup>, then the last two properties amount to <sup>*k*</sup>(*h*<sub>1</sub>*h*<sub>2</sub>) = <sup>*k*</sup>*h*<sub>1</sub> <sup>*k*<sup>*h*<sub>1</sub></sup></sup>*h*<sub>2</sub> and (*k*<sub>1</sub>*k*<sub>2</sub>)<sup>*h*</sup> = *k*<sub>1</sub><sup><sup>*k*<sub>2</sub></sup>*h*</sup> *k*<sub>2</sub><sup>*h*</sup>.

Turning this around, suppose *H* and *K* are groups (and let *e* denote each group's identity element) and suppose there exist mappings α : *K* × *H* → *H* and β : *K* × *H* → *K* satisfying the properties above. On the [cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) *H* × *K*, define a multiplication and an inversion mapping by, respectively,

- (*h*<sub>1</sub>, *k*<sub>1</sub>)(*h*<sub>2</sub>, *k*<sub>2</sub>) = (*h*<sub>1</sub>α(*k*<sub>1</sub>, *h*<sub>2</sub>), β(*k*<sub>1</sub>, *h*<sub>2</sub>)*k*<sub>2</sub>)
- (*h*, *k*)<sup>−1</sup> = (α(*k*<sup>−1</sup>,*h*<sup>−1</sup>), β(*k*<sup>−1</sup>,*h*<sup>−1</sup>))

Then *H* × *K* is a group called the external **Zappa–Szép product** of the groups *H* and *K*. The [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) *H* × {*e*} and {*e*} × *K* are subgroups [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/12397) to *H* and *K*, respectively, and *H* × *K* is, in fact, an internal Zappa–Szép product of *H* × {*e*} and {*e*} × *K*.

## Relation to semidirect and direct products {#Relation_to_semidirect_and_direct_products}

Let *G* = *HK* be an internal Zappa–Szép product of subgroups *H* and *K*. If *H* is [normal](wikipedia://en/21918) in *G*, then the mappings α and β are given by, respectively, α(*k*,*h*) = *k h k*<sup>− 1</sup> and β(*k*, *h*) = *k*. This is easy to see because $(h_1k_1)(h_2k_2) = (h_1k_1h_2k_1^{-1})(k_1k_2)$ and $h_1k_1h_2k_1^{-1}\in H$ since by normality of $H$, $k_1h_2k_1^{-1}\in H$. In this case, *G* is an internal semidirect product of *H* and *K*.

If, in addition, *K* is normal in *G*, then α(*k*,*h*) = *h*. In this case, *G* is an internal direct product of *H* and *K*.
