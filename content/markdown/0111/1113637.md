# Hurwitz's automorphisms theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hurwitz's automorphisms theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Hurwitz's automorphisms theorem** bounds the order of the group of [automorphisms](wikipedia://en/1160), via [orientation-preserving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation-preserving) [conformal mappings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_mapping), of a compact [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) of [genus](wikipedia://en/61891) *g* > 1, stating that the number of such automorphisms cannot exceed 84(*g* − 1). A group for which the maximum is achieved is called a **Hurwitz group**, and the corresponding Riemann surface a **[Hurwitz surface](wikipedia://en/12807841)**. Because compact Riemann surfaces are synonymous with non-singular [complex projective algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260), a Hurwitz surface can also be called a **Hurwitz curve**.[^o2] The theorem is named after [Adolf Hurwitz](wikipedia://en/771690), who proved it in (Hurwitz 1893).

:::

Hurwitz's bound also holds for algebraic curves over a field of characteristic 0, and over fields of positive characteristic *p* > 0 for groups whose order is coprime to *p*, but can fail over fields of positive characteristic *p* > 0 when *p* divides the group order. For example, the double cover of the projective line *y*<sup>2</sup> = *x<sup>p</sup>* − *x* branched at all points defined over the prime field has genus *g* = (*p* − 1)/2 but is acted on by the group PGL<sub>2</sub>(*p*) of order *p*<sup>3</sup> − *p*.

## Interpretation in terms of hyperbolicity {#Interpretation_in_terms_of_hyperbolicity}

One of the fundamental themes in [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625) is a trichotomy between the [Riemannian manifolds](wikipedia://en/144652) of positive, zero, and negative [curvature](wikipedia://en/285622) *K*. It manifests itself in many diverse situations and on several levels. In the context of compact Riemann surfaces *X*, via the Riemann [uniformization theorem](wikipedia://en/288270), this can be seen as a distinction between the surfaces of different topologies:

- *X* a [sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799), a compact Riemann surface of [genus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Genus_%28topology%29) zero with *K* > 0;
- *X* a flat [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), or an [elliptic curve](wikipedia://en/10225), a Riemann surface of genus one with *K* = 0;
- and *X* a [hyperbolic surface](wikipedia://en/173181#Hyperbolic_Riemann_surfaces), which has genus greater than one and *K* \< 0.

While in the first two cases the surface *X* admits infinitely many conformal automorphisms (in fact, the conformal [automorphism group](wikipedia://en/56661772) is a complex [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945) of dimension three for a sphere and of dimension one for a torus), a hyperbolic Riemann surface only admits a discrete set of automorphisms. Hurwitz's theorem claims that in fact more is true: it provides a uniform bound on the order of the automorphism group as a function of the genus and characterizes those Riemann surfaces for which the bound is [sharp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_jargon#sharp).

## Statement and proof {#Statement_and_proof}

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem**: Let $X$ be a smooth compact Riemann surface of genus $g \ge 2$. Then its automorphism group $\operatorname{Aut}(X)$ has size at most $84(g-1)$.

*Proof:* Assume for now that $G = \operatorname{Aut}(X)$ is finite (this will be proved at the end).

- Consider the quotient map $X \to X/G$. Since $G$ acts by holomorphic functions, the quotient is locally of the form $z \to z^n$ and the quotient $X/G$ is a smooth Riemann surface. The quotient map $X \to X/G$ is a branched cover, and we will see below that the ramification points correspond to the orbits that have a non-trivial stabiliser. Let $g_0$ be the genus of $X/G$.
- By the [Riemann-Hurwitz formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann-Hurwitz_formula), $$2g-2 \ = \ |G| \cdot \left( 2g_0-2 + \sum_{i = 1}^k \left(1-\frac{1}{e_i}\right)\right)$$ where the sum is over the $k$ ramification points $p_i \in X/G$ for the quotient map $X \to X/G$. The ramification index $e_i$ at $p_i$ is just the order of the stabiliser group, since $e_i f_i = \deg(X/\, X/G)$ where $f_i$ the number of pre-images of $p_i$ (the number of points in the orbit), and $\deg(X/\, X/G) = |G|$. By definition of ramification points, $e_i \ge 2$ for all $k$ ramification indices.

Now call the righthand side $|G| R$ and since $g \ge 2$ we must have $R>0$. Rearranging the equation we find:

- If $g_0 \ge 2$ then $R \ge 2$, and $|G| \le (g-1)$
- If $g_0 = 1$, then $k \ge 1$ and $R\ge 0 + 1 - 1/2 = 1/2$ so that $|G| \le 4(g-1)$,
- If $g_0 = 0$, then $k \ge 3$ and
  - if $k \ge 5$ then $R \ge -2 + k(1 - 1/2) \ge 1/2$, so that $|G| \le 4(g-1)$
  - if $k=4$ then $R \ge -2 + 4 - 1/2 - 1/2 - 1/2 - 1/3 = 1/6$, so that $|G| \le 12(g-1)$,
  - if $k=3$ then write $e_1 = p,\, e_2 = q, \, e_3 = r$. We may assume $2 \le p\le q\ \le r$.
    - if $p \ge 3$ then $R \ge -2 + 3 - 1/3 - 1/3 - 1/4 = 1/12$ so that $|G| \le 24(g-1)$,
    - if $p = 2$ then
      - if $q \ge 4$ then $R \ge -2 + 3 - 1/2 - 1/4 - 1/5 = 1/20$ so that $|G| \le 40(g-1)$,
      - if $q = 3$ then $R \ge -2 + 3 - 1/2 - 1/3 - 1/7 = 1/42$ so that $|G| \le 84(g-1)$.

In conclusion, $|G| \le 84(g-1)$.

To show that $G$ is finite, note that $G$ acts on the [cohomology](wikipedia://en/194060) $H^*(X,\mathbf{C})$ preserving the [Hodge decomposition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_decomposition) and the [lattice](wikipedia://en/15782871) $H^1(X,\mathbf{Z})$.

- In particular, its action on $V=H^{0,1}(X,\mathbf{C})$ gives a homomorphism $h: G \to \operatorname{GL}(V)$ with [discrete](wikipedia://en/468536) image $h(G)$.
- In addition, the image $h(G)$ preserves the natural non-degenerate [Hermitian inner product](wikipedia://en/20598932) $(\omega,\eta)= i \int\bar{\omega}\wedge\eta$ on $V$. In particular the image $h(G)$ is contained in the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $\operatorname{U}(V) \subset \operatorname{GL}(V)$ which is [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). Thus the image $h(G)$ is not just discrete, but finite.
- It remains to prove that $h: G \to \operatorname{GL}(V)$ has finite kernel. In fact, we will prove $h$ is injective. Assume $\varphi \in G$ acts as the identity on $V$. If $\operatorname{fix}(\varphi)$ is finite, then by the [Lefschetz fixed-point theorem](wikipedia://en/582530), $$|\operatorname{fix}(\varphi)| = 1 - 2\operatorname{tr}(h(\varphi)) + 1 = 2 - 2\operatorname{tr}(\mathrm{id}_V) = 2 - 2g < 0.$$

This is a contradiction, and so $\operatorname{fix}(\varphi)$ is infinite. Since $\operatorname{fix}(\varphi)$ is a closed complex sub variety of positive dimension and $X$ is a smooth connected curve (i.e. $\dim_{\mathbf C}(X) = 1$), we must have $\operatorname{fix}(\varphi) = X$. Thus $\varphi$ is the identity, and we conclude that $h$ is injective and $G \cong h(G)$ is finite. Q.E.D.

:::

::: {#o7 .proof cue=inline-head name="Corollary of the proof"}

**Corollary of the proof**: A Riemann surface $X$ of genus $g \ge 2$ has $84(g-1)$ automorphisms if and only if $X$ is a branched cover $X \to \mathbf{P}^1$ with three ramification points, of indices *2*,*3* and *7*.

:::

## The idea of another proof and construction of the Hurwitz surfaces {#The_idea_of_another_proof_and_construction_of_the_Hurwitz_surfaces}

By the uniformization theorem, any hyperbolic surface *X* – i.e., the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) of *X* is equal to negative one at every point – is [covered](wikipedia://en/250323) by the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/318742). The conformal mappings of the surface correspond to orientation-preserving automorphisms of the hyperbolic plane. By the [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229), the area of the surface is

A(*X*) = − 2π χ(*X*) = 4π(*g* − 1).

In order to make the automorphism group *G* of *X* as large as possible, we want the area of its [fundamental domain](wikipedia://en/469365) *D* for this action to be as small as possible. If the fundamental domain is a triangle with the vertex angles π/p, π/q and π/r, defining a [tiling](wikipedia://en/321671) of the hyperbolic plane, then *p*, *q*, and *r* are integers greater than one, and the area is

A(*D*) = π(1 − 1/*p* − 1/*q* − 1/*r*).

Thus we are asking for integers which make the expression

1 − 1/*p* − 1/*q* − 1/*r*

strictly positive and as small as possible. This minimal value is 1/42, and

1 − 1/2 − 1/3 − 1/7 = 1/42

gives a unique triple of such integers. This would indicate that the order |*G*| of the automorphism group is bounded by

A(*X*)/A(*D*) ≤ 168(*g* − 1).

However, a more delicate reasoning shows that this is an overestimate by the factor of two, because the group *G* can contain orientation-reversing transformations. For the orientation-preserving conformal automorphisms the bound is 84(*g* − 1).

::: {#o10 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Construction"}

### Construction {#Construction}

*Image: 3-7 kisrhombille.svg.* Hurwitz groups and surfaces are constructed based on the tiling of the hyperbolic plane by the (2,3,7) [Schwarz triangle](wikipedia://en/2069325).

To obtain an example of a Hurwitz group, let us start with a (2,3,7)-tiling of the hyperbolic plane. Its full symmetry group is the full [(2,3,7) triangle group](wikipedia://en/12659803) generated by the reflections across the sides of a single fundamental triangle with the angles π/2, π/3 and π/7. Since a reflection flips the triangle and changes the orientation, we can join the triangles in pairs and obtain an orientation-preserving tiling polygon. A Hurwitz surface is obtained by 'closing up' a part of this infinite tiling of the hyperbolic plane to a compact Riemann surface of genus *g*. This will necessarily involve exactly 84(*g* − 1) double triangle tiles.

The following two [regular tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_tiling) have the desired symmetry group; the rotational group corresponds to rotation about an edge, a vertex, and a face, while the full symmetry group would also include a reflection. The polygons in the tiling are not fundamental domains – the tiling by (2,3,7) triangles refines both of these and is not regular.

[Wythoff constructions](wikipedia://en/2950777) yields further [uniform tilings](wikipedia://en/12126635), yielding [eight uniform tilings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Order-3_heptagonal_tiling#Wythoff_constructions_from_heptagonal_and_triangular_tilings), including the two regular ones given here. These all descend to Hurwitz surfaces, yielding tilings of the surfaces (triangulation, tiling by heptagons, etc.).

From the arguments above it can be inferred that a Hurwitz group *G* is characterized by the property that it is a finite quotient of the group with two generators *a* and *b* and three relations

$$a^2 = b^3 = (ab)^7 = 1,$$

thus *G* is a finite group generated by two elements of orders two and three, whose product is of order seven. More precisely, any Hurwitz surface, that is, a hyperbolic surface that realizes the maximum order of the automorphism group for the surfaces of a given genus, can be obtained by the construction given. This is the last part of the theorem of Hurwitz.

:::

## Examples of Hurwitz groups and surfaces {#Examples_of_Hurwitz_groups_and_surfaces}

*Image: Small cubicuboctahedron.png.* The [small cubicuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/4157031) is a polyhedral immersion of the tiling of the [Klein quartic](wikipedia://en/390976) by 56 triangles, meeting at 24 vertices.[^o14]

The smallest Hurwitz group is the projective special linear group [PSL(2,7)](wikipedia://en/390426), of order 168, and the corresponding curve is the [Klein quartic curve](wikipedia://en/390976). This group is also isomorphic to [PSL(3,2)](wikipedia://en/390426).

Next is the [Macbeath curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macbeath_surface), with automorphism group PSL(2,8) of order 504. Many more finite simple groups are Hurwitz groups; for instance all but 64 of the [alternating groups](wikipedia://en/49726) are Hurwitz groups, the largest non-Hurwitz example being of degree 167. The smallest alternating group that is a Hurwitz group is A<sub>15</sub>.

Most [projective special linear groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) of large rank are Hurwitz groups, (Lucchini Tamburini Wilson 2000). For lower ranks, fewer such groups are Hurwitz. For *n*<sub>*p*</sub> the order of *p* modulo 7, one has that PSL(2,*q*) is Hurwitz if and only if either *q*=7 or *q* = *p*<sup>*n*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>. Indeed, PSL(3,*q*) is Hurwitz if and only if *q* = 2, PSL(4,*q*) is never Hurwitz, and PSL(5,*q*) is Hurwitz if and only if *q* = 7<sup>4</sup> or *q* = *p*<sup>*n*<sub>*p*</sub></sup>, (Tamburini Vsemirnov 2006).

Similarly, many [groups of Lie type](wikipedia://en/1691490) are Hurwitz. The finite [classical groups](wikipedia://en/10172878) of large rank are Hurwitz, (Lucchini Tamburini 1999). The [exceptional Lie groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exceptional_Lie_group) of type G2 and the [Ree groups](wikipedia://en/1739637) of type 2G2 are nearly always Hurwitz, (Malle 1990). Other families of exceptional and twisted Lie groups of low rank are shown to be Hurwitz in (Malle 1995).

There are 12 [sporadic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_groups) that can be generated as Hurwitz groups: the [Janko groups](wikipedia://en/380191) J<sub>1</sub>, J<sub>2</sub> and J<sub>4</sub>, the [Fischer groups](wikipedia://en/327206) Fi<sub>22</sub> and Fi'<sub>24</sub>, the [Rudvalis group](wikipedia://en/1196760), the [Held group](wikipedia://en/1110818), the [Thompson group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thompson_group_%28finite%29), the [Harada–Norton group](wikipedia://en/1196772), the third [Conway group](wikipedia://en/345511) Co<sub>3</sub>, the [Lyons group](wikipedia://en/1110773), and the [Monster](wikipedia://en/47422), (Wilson 2001).

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The concept of a Hurwitz surface can be generalized in several ways to a definition that has examples in all but a few genera. Perhaps the most natural is a "maximally symmetric" surface: One that cannot be continuously modified through equally symmetric surfaces to a surface whose symmetry properly contains that of the original surface. This is possible for all orientable compact genera (see above section "Automorphism groups in low genus").

:::
