# Galois cohomology

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Galois cohomology"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **Galois cohomology** is the study of the [group cohomology](wikipedia://en/248635) of [Galois modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_module), that is, the application of [homological algebra](wikipedia://en/139410) to [modules](wikipedia://en/276410) for [Galois groups](wikipedia://en/12673). A Galois group *G* associated with a [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) *L*/*K* acts in a natural way on some [abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974), for example those constructed directly from *L*, but also through other [Galois representations](wikipedia://en/486929) that may be derived by more abstract means. Galois cohomology accounts for the way in which taking Galois-invariant elements fails to be an [exact functor](wikipedia://en/453945).

:::

## History {#History}

The current theory of Galois cohomology came together around 1950, when it was realised that the Galois cohomology of [ideal class groups](wikipedia://en/150907) in [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705) was one way to formulate [class field theory](wikipedia://en/242695). At the time it was in the process of ridding itself of connections to [L-functions](wikipedia://en/245283). Galois cohomology makes no assumption that Galois groups are abelian groups, so this was a [non-abelian theory](wikipedia://en/40814966). It was formulated abstractly as a theory of [class formations](wikipedia://en/6019404). Two developments of the 1960s turned the position around. Firstly, Galois cohomology appeared as the foundational layer of [étale cohomology](wikipedia://en/396231) theory (roughly speaking, the theory as it applies to zero-dimensional schemes). Secondly, [non-abelian class field theory](wikipedia://en/8354260) was launched as part of the [Langlands philosophy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Langlands_philosophy).

The earliest results identifiable as Galois cohomology had been known long before, in algebraic number theory and the [arithmetic of elliptic curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_of_elliptic_curves). The [normal basis theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_basis_theorem) implies that the first cohomology group of the [additive group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Additive_group_of_a_ring) of *L* will vanish; this is a result on general field extensions, but was known in some form to [Richard Dedekind](wikipedia://en/23475106). The corresponding result for the [multiplicative group](wikipedia://en/1110742) is known as [Hilbert's Theorem 90](wikipedia://en/1099709), and was known before 1900. [Kummer theory](wikipedia://en/581610) was another such early part of the theory, giving a description of the connecting homomorphism coming from the *m*-th [power map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_map).

In fact, for a while the multiplicative case of a 1-[cocycle](wikipedia://en/248635) for groups that are not necessarily cyclic was formulated as the solubility of **Noether's equations**, named for [Emmy Noether](wikipedia://en/149896); they appear under this name in [Emil Artin](wikipedia://en/245351)'s treatment of Galois theory, and may have been folklore in the 1920s. The case of 2-cocycles for the multiplicative group is that of the [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078), and the implications seem to have been well known to algebraists of the 1930s.

In another direction, that of [torsors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torsor), these were already implicit in the [infinite descent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinite_descent) arguments of [Fermat](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat) for [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225). Numerous direct calculations were done, and the proof of the [Mordell–Weil theorem](wikipedia://en/23087727) had to proceed by some surrogate of a finiteness proof for a particular *H*<sup>1</sup> group. The 'twisted' nature of objects over fields that are not [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed), which are not [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) but become so over the [algebraic closure](wikipedia://en/3129), was also known in many cases linked to other [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924) (such as [quadratic forms](wikipedia://en/251478), [simple algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_algebra), [Severi–Brauer varieties](wikipedia://en/1669368)), in the 1930s, before the general theory arrived.

The needs of number theory were in particular expressed by the requirement to have control of a [local-global principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local-global_principle) for Galois cohomology. This was formulated by means of results in class field theory, such as [Hasse's norm theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hasse's_norm_theorem). In the case of elliptic curves, it led to the key definition of the [Tate–Shafarevich group](wikipedia://en/25711736) in the [Selmer group](wikipedia://en/1970862), which is the obstruction to the success of a local-global principle. Despite its great importance, for example in the [Birch and Swinnerton-Dyer conjecture](wikipedia://en/435161), it proved very difficult to get any control of it, until results of [Karl Rubin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karl_Rubin) gave a way to show in some cases it was finite (a result generally believed, since its conjectural order was predicted by an L-function formula).

The other major development of the theory, also involving [John Tate](wikipedia://en/245933) was the [Tate–Poitou duality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tate–Poitou_duality) result.

Technically speaking, *G* may be a [profinite group](wikipedia://en/24266), in which case the definitions need to be adjusted to allow only continuous cochains.

## Formal details {#Formal_details}

Galois cohomology is the study of the group cohomology of Galois groups. Let $L|K$ be a field extension with Galois group $G(L|K)$ and $M$ an abelian group on which $G(L|K)$ acts. The cohomology group:

$$H^n(L|K, M) := H^n(G(L|K),M),\quad n\ge 0$$

is the Galois cohomology group associated to the representation of the Galois group on $M$. It is possible, moreover, to extend this definition to the case when $M$ is a non-abelian group and $n=0,1$, and this extension is required for some of the most important applications of the theory. In particular, $H^0(L|K,M)$ is the set of [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) of the Galois group in $M$, and $H^1(L|K,M)$ is related to the 1-cocycles (which parametrize [quaternion algebras](wikipedia://en/2776890) for instance).

When the extension field $L=K^s$ is the [separable closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_closure) of the field $K$, one often writes instead $G_K = G(K^s|K)$ (the [absolute Galois group](wikipedia://en/2996781)) and

$$H^n(K,M) := H^n(G_K,M).$$

[Hilbert's theorem 90](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilbert's_theorem_90) in cohomological language is the statement that the first cohomology group with values in the multiplicative group of $L$ is trivial for a Galois extension $L|K$:

$$H^1(L|K,L^*)=1.$$

This vanishing theorem can be generalized to a large class of [algebraic groups](wikipedia://en/247924), also formulated in the language of Galois cohomology. The most straightforward generalization is that for any [quasisplit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasisplit) $K$-torus $T$,

$$H^1(K,T) = 1.$$

Denote by $GL_n(L)$ the [general linear group](wikipedia://en/113564) in $n$ dimensions. Then Hilbert 90 is the $n=1$ special case of

$$H^1(L|K,GL_n(L))=1.$$

Likewise, the vanishing theorem holds for the [special linear group](wikipedia://en/144823) $SL_n(L)$ and for the [symplectic group](wikipedia://en/173977) $Sp(\omega)_L$ where $\omega$ is a non-degenerate alternating bilinear form defined over $K$.

The second cohomology group describes the [factor systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Factor_system) attached to the Galois group. Thus for any [normal extension](wikipedia://en/929925) $L|K$, the relative [Brauer group](wikipedia://en/404078) can be identified with the group

$$Br(L|K) = H^2(L|K, L^*).$$

As a special case, with the separable closure,

$$Br(K) = H^2(K,(K^s)^*).$$
