# Lyons group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Lyons group"}

In the area of modern algebra known as [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890), the **Lyons group** *Ly* or **Lyons-Sims group** *LyS* is a [sporadic simple group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_simple_group) of [order](wikipedia://en/493527)

:::

51,765,179,004,000,000

\= 2<sup>8</sup>3<sup>7</sup>5<sup>6</sup>711313767

≈ 5.

## History {#History}

*Ly* is one of the 26 sporadic groups and was discovered by [Richard Lyons](wikipedia://en/35256697) and [Charles Sims](wikipedia://en/20022166) in 1972-73. Lyons characterized 51765179004000000 as the unique possible order of any finite simple group where the [centralizer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centralizer) of some [involution](wikipedia://en/293450) is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to the nontrivial central extension of the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) A<sub>11</sub> of degree 11 by the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) C<sub>2</sub>. (Sims 1973) proved the existence of such a group and its uniqueness up to isomorphism with a combination of permutation group theory and machine calculations. In 2002, Ulrich Meierfrankenfeld and Chris Parker gave a computer-free construction of the Lyons group, as an amalgam of its maximal 3-local subgroups.

When the [McLaughlin sporadic group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/McLaughlin_group_%28mathematics%29) was discovered, it was noticed that a centralizer of one of its involutions was the perfect [double cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_covering_group) of the [alternating group](wikipedia://en/49726) *A*<sub>8</sub>. This suggested considering the double covers of the other alternating groups *A*<sub>*n*</sub> as possible centralizers of involutions in simple groups. The cases *n* ≤ 7 are ruled out by the [Brauer–Suzuki theorem](wikipedia://en/8239308), the case *n* = 8 leads to the McLaughlin group, the case *n* = 9 was ruled out by [Zvonimir Janko](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zvonimir_Janko), Lyons himself ruled out the case *n* = 10 and found the Lyons group for *n* = 11, while the cases *n* ≥ 12 were ruled out by [J.G. Thompson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Griggs_Thompson) and [Ronald Solomon](wikipedia://en/35255960).

The [Schur multiplier](wikipedia://en/1121587) and the [outer automorphism group](wikipedia://en/628466) are both [trivial](wikipedia://en/193059).

Since 37 and 67 are not [supersingular](wikipedia://en/22965333) primes, the Lyons group cannot be a [subquotient](wikipedia://en/1639294) of the [monster group](wikipedia://en/47422). Thus it is one of the 6 sporadic groups called the [pariahs](wikipedia://en/16569533).

## Representations {#Representations}

(Meyer Neutsch Parker 1985) showed that the Lyons group has a [modular representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_representation) of dimension 111 over the field of five elements, which is the smallest dimension of any faithful linear representation and is one of the easiest ways of calculating with it. It has also been given by several complicated presentations in terms of generators and relations, for instance those given by (Sims 1973) or (Gebhardt 2000).

The smallest faithful [permutation representation](wikipedia://en/59487764) is a rank 5 permutation representation on 8835156 points with stabilizer G<sub>2</sub>(5). There is also a slightly larger rank 5 permutation representation on 9606125 points with stabilizer 3.McL:2.

The degrees of [irreducible representations](wikipedia://en/385982) of the Lyons group are 1, 2480, 2480, 45694, 48174, ... .
