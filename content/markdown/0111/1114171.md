# Order (ring theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Order"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), certain subsets of some [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) are called **orders**. The set of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) is an order in the [rational numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_numbers) (the only one). In an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) $K$, an order is a [ring of algebraic integers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_algebraic_integers) whose [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271) is $K$, and the **maximal order**, often denoted $\mathcal O_K$, is the ring of all algebraic integers in $K$. In a [non-Archimedean local field](wikipedia://en/145375) $K$, an *order* is a subring which is generated by finitely many elements of non-negative valuation. In that case, the maximal order, denoted $\mathcal O_K$, is the [valuation ring](wikipedia://en/966856) formed by all elements of non-negative valuation.

:::

Giving the same name to such seemingly different notions is motivated by the [local–global principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Local–global_principle) that relates properties of a number field with properties of all its local fields.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

The definition of an order is somewhat context-dependent. The simplest definition is in an algebraic number field $F$, where an order $R$ is a [subring](wikipedia://en/24873145) of $F$ that is a finitely-generated $\mathbb Z$-[module](wikipedia://en/276410), which contains a rational [basis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_%28mathematics%29) of $F$, i.e., such that $\mathbb QR = F.$

On the other hand, if $F$ is a non-archimedean [local field](wikipedia://en/145375), an order is a compact-open subring $R$ of $F$. The maximal order in this case is the [valuation ring](wikipedia://en/966856) of the field.

More generally, which includes both of these special cases, if $R$ an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) with fraction field $K$, an $R$-order in a finite-dimensional $K$-algebra $A$ is a subring $\mathcal{O}$ of $A$ which is a full $R$-lattice; i.e. is a finite $R$-module with the property that $\mathcal{O}\otimes_RK=A$.[^o4]

When *$A$* is not a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346), the idea of order is still important, but the phenomena are different. For example, the [Hurwitz quaternions](wikipedia://en/772241) form a **maximal** order in the [quaternions](wikipedia://en/51440) with rational co-ordinates; they are not the quaternions with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) coordinates in the most obvious sense. Maximal orders exist in general, but need not be unique: there is in general no largest order, but a number of maximal orders. An important class of examples is that of integral [group rings](wikipedia://en/349223).

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

Some examples of orders are:[^o7]

- If $A$ is the [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) $M_n(K)$ over $K$, then the matrix ring $M_n(R)$ over $R$ is an $R$-order in $A$
- If $R$ is an integral domain and $L$ a finite [separable extension](wikipedia://en/363340) of $K$, then the [integral closure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure) $S$ of $R$ in $L$ is an $R$-order in $L$.
- If $a$ in $A$ is an [integral element](wikipedia://en/9478630) over $R$, then the [polynomial ring](wikipedia://en/373065) $R[a]$ is an $R$-order in the algebra $K[a]$
- If $A$ is the [group ring](wikipedia://en/349223) $K[G]$ of a [finite group](wikipedia://en/323707) $G$, then $R[G]$ is an $R$-order on $K[G]$

A fundamental property of $R$-orders is that every element of an $R$-order is [integral](wikipedia://en/9478630) over $R$.[^o8]

If the integral closure $S$ of $R$ in $A$ is an $R$-order then the integrality of every element of every $R$-order shows that $S$ must be the unique maximal $R$-order in $A$. However $S$ need not always be an $R$-order: indeed $S$ need not even be a ring, and even if $S$ is a ring (for example, when $A$ is commutative) then $S$ need not be an $R$-lattice.[^o8]

:::

## Algebraic number theory {#Algebraic_number_theory}

The leading example is the case where *$A$* is a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) *$K$* and $\mathcal{O}$ is its [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613). In [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705) there are examples for any *$K$* other than the rational field of proper subrings of the ring of integers that are also orders. For example, in the [field extension](wikipedia://en/11634) *$A=\mathbb{Q}(i)$* of [Gaussian rationals](wikipedia://en/318052) over $\mathbb{Q}$, the integral closure of *$\mathbb{Z}$* is the ring of [Gaussian integers](wikipedia://en/48628) *$\mathbb{Z}[i]$* and so this is the unique *maximal* *$\mathbb{Z}$*-order: all other orders in *$A$* are contained in it. For example, we can take the subring of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) of the form $a+2bi$, with $a$ and $b$ integers.[^o10]

The maximal order question can be examined at a [local field](wikipedia://en/145375) level. This technique is applied in algebraic number theory and [modular representation theory](wikipedia://en/1110685).
