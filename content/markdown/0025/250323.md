# Covering space

*Image: Covering space diagram.svg.* Intuitively, a covering locally projects a "stack of pancakes" above an [open neighborhood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_neighborhood) $U$ onto $U$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Covering space"}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), a **covering** or **covering projection** is a [map](wikipedia://en/6122) between [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) that, intuitively, [locally](wikipedia://en/1462457) acts like a [projection](wikipedia://en/3909097) of multiple copies of a space onto itself. In particular, coverings are special types of [local homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/248582). If $p : \tilde X \to X$ is a covering, $(\tilde X, p)$ is said to be a **covering space** or **cover** of $X$, and $X$ is said to be the **base of the covering**, or simply the **base**. By [abuse of terminology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abuse_of_terminology), $\tilde X$ and $p$ may sometimes be called **covering spaces** as well. Since coverings are local homeomorphisms, a covering space is a special kind of [étalé space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Étalé_space).

:::

Covering spaces first arose in the context of [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) (specifically, the technique of [analytic continuation](wikipedia://en/80842)), where they were introduced by [Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980) as domains on which naturally [multivalued](wikipedia://en/222367) complex functions become single-valued. These spaces are now called [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181).[^o2]p. 10

Covering spaces are an important tool in several areas of mathematics. In modern [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), covering spaces (or [branched coverings](wikipedia://en/3002253), which have slightly weaker conditions) are used in the construction of [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), [orbifolds](wikipedia://en/293504), and the [morphisms](wikipedia://en/27716891) between them. In [algebraic topology](wikipedia://en/38801), covering spaces are closely related to the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004): for one, since all coverings have the [homotopy lifting property](wikipedia://en/522786), covering spaces are an important tool in the calculation of [homotopy groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homotopy_groups). A standard example in this vein is the calculation of the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of the circle by means of the covering of [$S^1$](wikipedia://en/6220) by [$\mathbb{R}$](wikipedia://en/20646438) (see [below](wikipedia://en/250323#Lifting_property)).[^o3]p. 29 Under certain conditions, covering spaces also exhibit a [Galois correspondence](wikipedia://en/156411#Algebraic_topology:_covering_spaces) with the subgroups of the fundamental group.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let $X$ be a topological space. A **covering** of $X$ is a continuous map

$$\pi : \tilde X \rightarrow X$$

such that for every $x \in X$ there exists an [open neighborhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) $U_x$ of $x$ and a [discrete space](wikipedia://en/56061) $D_x$ such that $\pi^{-1}(U_x)$ is the [disjoint union](wikipedia://en/1564380) $\displaystyle \bigsqcup_{d \in D_x} V_d$ and $\pi|_{V_d}:V_d \rightarrow U_x$ is a [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) for every $d \in D_x$. The open sets $V_{d}$ are called **sheets**, which are uniquely determined up to homeomorphism if $U_x$ is [connected](wikipedia://en/6233).p. 56 For each $x \in X$ the discrete set $\pi^{-1}(x)$ is called the **fiber** of $x$. If $X$ is connected (and $\tilde X$ is non-empty), it can be shown that $\pi$ is [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective), and the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of $D_x$ is the same for all $x \in X$; this value is called the **degree** of the covering. If $\tilde X$ is [path-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_connected), then the covering $\pi : \tilde X \rightarrow X$ is called a **path-connected covering**. This definition is equivalent to the statement that $\pi$ is a locally trivial [fiber bundle](wikipedia://en/276526).

Some authors also require that $\pi$ be surjective in the case that $X$ is not connected.[^o6]

:::

## Examples {#Examples}

- For every topological space $X$, the [identity map](wikipedia://en/15069) $\operatorname{id}:X \rightarrow X$ is a covering. Likewise for any discrete space $D$ the projection $\pi:X \times D \rightarrow X$ taking $(x, i) \mapsto x$ is a covering. Coverings of this type are called **trivial coverings**; if $D$ has finitely many (say $k$) elements, the covering is called the **trivial *$k$-sheeted* covering** of $X$.

*Image: Covering\_map.svg.* The infinite strip $Y=[0,1] \times \mathbb{R}$ is a covering space of the annulus $X=[0,1] \times S^1$. The disjoint open sets $S_i$ are mapped homeomorphically onto $U$. The fiber of $x$ consists of the infinite point set $\{y_i\}_{i \in \mathbb{Z}}$.

- The map $r : \mathbb{R} \to S^1$ with $r(t)=(\cos(2 \pi t), \sin(2 \pi t))$ is a covering of the [unit circle](wikipedia://en/27072071) $S^1$. The base of the covering is $S^1$ and the covering space is $\mathbb{R}$. For any point $x = (x_1, x_2) \in S^1$ such that $x_1 > 0$, the set $U := \{(x_1, x_2) \in S^1 \mid x_1 > 0 \}$ is an open neighborhood of $x$. The preimage of $U$ under $r$ is

$$r^{-1}(U)=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{n \in \mathbb{Z}} \left( n - \frac 1 4, n + \frac 1 4\right)$$

and the sheets of the covering are $V_n = (n - 1/4, n+1/4)$ for $n \in \mathbb{Z}.$ The fiber of $x$ is

$$r^{-1}(x) = \{t \in \mathbb{R} \mid (\cos(2 \pi t), \sin(2 \pi t)) = x\}.$$

- Another covering of the unit circle is the map $q : S^1 \to S^1$ with $q(z)=z^{n}$ for some positive $n \in \mathbb{N}.$ For an open neighborhood $U$ of an $x \in S^1$, one has:

$$q^{-1}(U)=\displaystyle\bigsqcup_{i=1}^{n} U.$$

- A map which is a [local homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/248582) but not a covering of the unit circle is $p : \mathbb{R_{+}} \to S^1$ with $p(t)=(\cos(2 \pi t), \sin(2 \pi t))$. There is a sheet of an open neighborhood of $(1,0)$, which is not mapped homeomorphically onto $U$.
- Let $n \geq 1$ be odd. The map $p : \mathrm{O}(n) \to \mathrm{SO}(n)$ defined by $p(Q)=(\det Q) Q$ is a homomorphic double covering.

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

### Local homeomorphism {#Local_homeomorphism}

Since a covering $\pi:E \rightarrow X$ maps each of the disjoint open sets of $\pi^{-1}(U)$ homeomorphically onto $U$ it is a local homeomorphism, i.e. $\pi$ is a continuous map and for every $e \in E$ there exists an open neighborhood $V \subset E$ of $e$, such that $\pi|_V : V \rightarrow \pi(V)$ is a homeomorphism.

It follows that the covering space $E$ and the base space $X$ locally share the same properties.

- If $X$ is a connected and [non-orientable manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-orientable_manifold), then there is a covering $\pi:\tilde X \rightarrow X$ of degree $2$, whereby $\tilde X$ is a connected and orientable manifold.p. 234
- If $X$ is a connected [Lie group](wikipedia://en/17945), then there is a covering $\pi:\tilde X \rightarrow X$ which is also a [Lie group homomorphism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lie_group_homomorphism) and $\tilde X := \{\gamma:\gamma \text{ is a path in X with }\gamma(0)= \boldsymbol{1_X} \text{ modulo homotopy with fixed ends}\}$ is a Lie group.[^o12]p. 174
- If $X$ is a [graph](wikipedia://en/12401#Graph), then it follows for a covering $\pi:E \rightarrow X$ that $E$ is also a graph.p. 85
- If $X$ is a connected [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470), then there is a covering $\pi:\tilde X \rightarrow X$, whereby $\tilde X$ is a connected and [simply connected](wikipedia://en/523879) manifold.p. 32
- If $X$ is a connected [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181), then there is a covering $\pi:\tilde X \rightarrow X$ which is also a holomorphic mapp. 22 and $\tilde X$ is a connected and simply connected Riemann surface.p. 32

### Factorisation {#Factorisation}

Let $X, Y$ and $E$ be path-connected, locally path-connected spaces, and $p,q$ and $r$ be continuous maps, such that the diagram

*Image: Commutativ coverings.png.*

commutes.

- If $p$ and $q$ are coverings, so is $r$.
- If $p$ and $r$ are coverings, so is $q$.p. 485

### Product of coverings {#Product_of_coverings}

Let $X$ and $X'$ be topological spaces and $p:E \rightarrow X$ and $p':E' \rightarrow X'$ be coverings, then $p \times p':E \times E' \rightarrow X \times X'$ with $(p \times p')(e, e') = (p(e), p'(e'))$ is a covering.[^o15]p. 339 However, coverings of $X\times X'$ are not all of this form in general.

### Equivalence of coverings {#Equivalence_of_coverings}

Let $X$ be a topological space and $p:E \rightarrow X$ and $p':E' \rightarrow X$ be coverings. Both coverings are called **equivalent**, if there exists a homeomorphism $h:E \rightarrow E'$, such that the diagram

*Image: Kommutatives\_Diagramm\_Äquivalenz\_von\_Überlagerungen.png.*

commutes. If such a homeomorphism exists, then one calls the covering spaces $E$ and $E'$ [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828).

### Lifting property {#Lifting_property}

All coverings satisfy the [lifting property](wikipedia://en/53659730), i.e.:

Let $I$ be the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) and $p:E \rightarrow X$ be a covering. Let $F:Y \times I \rightarrow X$ be a continuous map and $\tilde F_0:Y \times \{0\} \rightarrow E$ be a lift of $F|_{Y \times \{0\}}$, i.e. a continuous map such that $p \circ \tilde F_0 = F|_{Y \times \{0\}}$. Then there is a uniquely determined, continuous map $\tilde F:Y \times I \rightarrow E$ for which $\tilde F(y,0) = \tilde F_0$ and which is a lift of $F$, i.e. $p \circ \tilde F = F$.p. 60

If $X$ is a path-connected space, then for $Y=\{0\}$ it follows that the map $\tilde F$ is a lift of a [path](wikipedia://en/1189553) in $X$ and for $Y=I$ it is a lift of a [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250) of paths in $X$.

As a consequence, one can show that the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) $\pi_{1}(S^1)$ of the unit circle is an [infinite cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327), which is generated by the homotopy classes of the loop $\gamma: I \rightarrow S^1$ with $\gamma (t) = (\cos(2 \pi t), \sin(2 \pi t))$.p. 29

Let $X$ be a path-connected space and $p:E \rightarrow X$ be a connected covering. Let $x,y \in X$ be any two points, which are connected by a path $\gamma$, i.e. $\gamma(0)= x$ and $\gamma(1)= y$. Let $\tilde \gamma$ be the unique lift of $\gamma$, then the map

$L_{\gamma}:p^{-1}(x) \rightarrow p^{-1}(y)$ with $L_{\gamma}(\tilde \gamma (0))=\tilde \gamma (1)$

is [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942).p. 69

If $X$ is a path-connected space and $p: E \rightarrow X$ a connected covering, then the induced [group homomorphism](wikipedia://en/12396)

$p_{\#}: \pi_{1}(E) \rightarrow \pi_{1}(X)$ with $p_{\#}([\gamma])=[p \circ \gamma]$,

is [injective](wikipedia://en/45196) and the [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $p_{\#}(\pi_1(E))$ of $\pi_1(X)$ consists of the homotopy classes of loops in $X$, whose lifts are loops in $E$.p. 61

:::

## Branched covering {#Branched_covering}

::: {#o20 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

#### Holomorphic maps between Riemann surfaces {#Holomorphic_maps_between_Riemann_surfaces}

Let $X$ and $Y$ be [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181), i.e. one dimensional [complex manifolds](wikipedia://en/509742), and let $f: X \rightarrow Y$ be a continuous map. $f$ is **holomorphic in a point** $x \in X$, if for any [charts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chart_%28mathematics%29) $\phi _x:U_1 \rightarrow V_1$ of $x$ and $\phi_{f(x)}:U_2 \rightarrow V_2$ of $f(x)$, with $\phi_x(U_1) \subset U_2$, the map $\phi _{f(x)} \circ f \circ \phi^{-1} _x: \mathbb{C} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ is [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110).

If $f$ is holomorphic at all $x \in X$, we say $f$ is **holomorphic.**

The map $F =\phi _{f(x)} \circ f \circ \phi^{-1} _x$ is called the **local expression** of $f$ in $x \in X$.

If $f: X \rightarrow Y$ is a non-constant, holomorphic map between [compact Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Compact_riemann_surface), then $f$ is [surjective](wikipedia://en/27873) and an [open map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Open_map),[^o22]p. 11 i.e. for every [open set](wikipedia://en/39358) $U \subset X$ the [image](wikipedia://en/579311) $f(U) \subset Y$ is also open.

#### Ramification point and branch point {#Ramification_point_and_branch_point}

Let $f: X \rightarrow Y$ be a non-constant, holomorphic map between compact Riemann surfaces. For every $x \in X$ there exist charts for $x$ and $f(x)$ and there exists a uniquely determined $k_x \in \mathbb{N_{>0}}$, such that the local expression $F$ of $f$ in $x$ is of the form $z \mapsto z^{k_{x}}$.p. 10 The number $k_x$ is called the **ramification index** of $f$ in $x$ and the point $x \in X$ is called a **ramification point** if $k_x \geq 2$. If $k_x =1$ for an $x \in X$, then $x$ is **unramified**. The image point $y=f(x) \in Y$ of a ramification point is called a **branch point.**

#### Degree of a holomorphic map {#Degree_of_a_holomorphic_map}

Let $f: X \rightarrow Y$ be a non-constant, holomorphic map between compact Riemann surfaces. The **degree $\operatorname{deg}(f)$** of $f$ is the cardinality of the fiber of an unramified point $y=f(x) \in Y$, i.e. $\operatorname{deg}(f):=|f^{-1}(y)|$.

This number is well-defined, since for every $y \in Y$ the fiber $f^{-1}(y)$ is discretep. 20 and for any two unramified points $y_1,y_2 \in Y$, it is: $|f^{-1}(y_1)|=|f^{-1}(y_2)|.$

It can be calculated by:

$\sum_{x \in f^{-1}(y)} k_x = \operatorname{deg}(f)$ p. 29

:::

### Branched covering {#Branched_covering}

::: {#o27 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

#### Definition {#Definition}

A continuous map $f: X \rightarrow Y$ is called a **branched covering**, if there exists a [closed set](wikipedia://en/47279) with [dense](wikipedia://en/23523594) complement $E \subset Y$, such that $f_{|X \smallsetminus f^{-1}(E)}:X \smallsetminus f^{-1}(E) \rightarrow Y \smallsetminus E$ is a covering.

:::

::: {#o29 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

- Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$ and $n \geq 2$, then $f:\mathbb{C} \rightarrow \mathbb{C}$ with $f(z)=z^n$ is a branched covering of degree $n$, where by $z=0$ is a [branch point](wikipedia://en/483173).
- Every non-constant, holomorphic map between compact Riemann surfaces $f: X \rightarrow Y$ of degree $d$ is a branched covering of degree $d$.

:::

## Universal covering {#Universal_covering}

::: {#o32 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

Let $p: \tilde X \rightarrow X$ be a [simply connected](wikipedia://en/523879) covering. If $\beta : E \rightarrow X$ is another simply connected covering, then there exists a uniquely determined homeomorphism $\alpha : \tilde X \rightarrow E$, such that the diagram

*Image: Universelle\_Überlagerung\_2.0.png.*

commutes.p. 482

This means that $p$ is, up to equivalence, uniquely determined and because of that [universal property](wikipedia://en/32245) denoted as the **universal covering** of the space $X$.

:::

### Existence {#Existence}

A universal covering does not always exist. The following theorem guarantees its existence for a certain class of base spaces.

Let $X$ be a connected, [locally simply connected](wikipedia://en/7541497) topological space. Then, there exists a universal covering $p:\tilde X \rightarrow X.$

The set $\tilde X$ is defined as $\tilde X = \{\gamma:\gamma \text{ is a path in }X \text{ with }\gamma(0) = x_0 \}/\text{homotopy with fixed ends},$ where $x_0 \in X$ is any chosen base point. The map $p:\tilde X \rightarrow X$ is defined by $p([\gamma])=\gamma(1).$p. 64

The [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) on $\tilde X$ is constructed as follows: Let $\gamma:I \rightarrow X$ be a path with $\gamma(0)=x_0.$ Let $U$ be a simply connected neighborhood of the endpoint $x=\gamma(1).$ Then, for every $y \in U,$ there is a [path](wikipedia://en/1189553) $\sigma_y$ inside $U$ from $x$ to $y$ that is unique up to [homotopy](wikipedia://en/212250). Now consider the set $\tilde U=\{\gamma\sigma_y:y \in U \}/\text{homotopy with fixed ends}.$ The restriction $p|_{\tilde U}: \tilde U \rightarrow U$ with $p([\gamma\sigma_y])=\gamma\sigma_y(1)=y$ is a bijection and $\tilde U$ can be equipped with the [final topology](wikipedia://en/1819736) of $p|_{\tilde U}.$

The fundamental group $\pi_{1}(X,x_0) = \Gamma$ acts [freely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_group_action) on $\tilde X$ by $([\gamma],[\tilde x]) \mapsto [\gamma\tilde x],$ and the orbit space $\Gamma \backslash \tilde X$ is homeomorphic to $X$ through the map $[\Gamma \tilde x]\mapsto\tilde x(1).$

::: {#o35 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

*Image: Hawaiian\_Earrings.svg.* The Hawaiian earring. Only the ten largest circles are shown.

- $p : \mathbb{R} \to S^1$ with $p(t)=(\cos(2 \pi t), \sin(2 \pi t))$ is the universal covering of the unit circle $S^1$.
- $p : S^n \to \mathbb{R}P^n \cong \{+1,-1\}\backslash S^n$ with $p(x)=[x]$ is the universal covering of the [projective space](wikipedia://en/242135) $\mathbb{R}P^n$ for $n>1$.
- $p : \mathrm{SU}(n) \times \mathbb{R} \to U(n)$ with $$p(A,t)= \exp(2 \pi i t) A$$ is the universal covering of the [unitary group](wikipedia://en/173993) $U(n)$.
- Since $\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3$, it follows that the [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map_%28topology%29) $$p : \mathrm{SU}(2) \rightarrow \mathrm{SU}(2) / \mathbb{Z_2} \cong \mathrm{SO}(3)$$ is the universal covering of $\mathrm{SO}(3)$.
- A topological space which has no universal covering is the [Hawaiian earring](wikipedia://en/910263): $$X = \bigcup_{n\in \mathbb{N}}\left\{(x_1,x_2)\in\mathbb{R}^{2} : \Bigl(x_1-\frac{1}{n}\Bigr)^2+x_2^2=\frac{1}{n^2}\right\}$$ One can show that no neighborhood of the origin $(0,0)$ is simply connected.p. 487

:::

## G-coverings {#G-coverings}

Let *G* be a [discrete group](wikipedia://en/468536) [acting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29) on the [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X*. This means that each element *g* of *G* is associated to a homeomorphism H<sub>*g*</sub> of *X* onto itself, in such a way that H<sub>*g* *h*</sub> is always equal to H<sub>*g*</sub> $\circ$ H<sub>*h*</sub> for any two elements *g* and *h* of *G*. (Or in other words, a group action of the group *G* on the space *X* is just a group homomorphism of the group *G* into the group Homeo(*X*) of self-homeomorphisms of *X*.) It is natural to ask under what conditions the projection from *X* to the [orbit space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_space) *X*/*G* is a covering map. This is not always true since the action may have fixed points. An example for this is the cyclic group of order 2 acting on a product *X* × *X* by the twist action where the non-identity element acts by (*x*, *y*) ↦ (*y*, *x*). Thus the study of the relation between the fundamental groups of *X* and *X*/*G* is not so straightforward.

However the group *G* does act on the fundamental [groupoid](wikipedia://en/12543) of *X*, and so the study is best handled by considering groups acting on groupoids, and the corresponding *orbit groupoids*. The theory for this is set down in Chapter 11 of the book *Topology and groupoids* referred to below. The main result is that for discontinuous actions of a group *G* on a [Hausdorff space](wikipedia://en/13637) *X* which admits a universal cover, then the fundamental groupoid of the orbit space *X*/*G* is isomorphic to the orbit groupoid of the fundamental groupoid of *X*, i.e. the quotient of that groupoid by the action of the group *G*. This leads to explicit computations, for example of the fundamental group of the symmetric square of a space.

## Smooth coverings {#Smooth_coverings}

Let $E$ and $M$ be [smooth manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_manifolds) with or without [boundary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boundary_of_a_manifold). A covering $\pi : E \to M$ is called a **smooth covering** if it is a [smooth map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_map) and the sheets are mapped *diffeomorphically* onto the corresponding open subset of $M$. (This is in contrast to the definition of a covering, which merely requires that the sheets are mapped *homeomorphically* onto the corresponding open subset.)

:::

## Deck transformation {#Deck_transformation}

::: {#o41 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

Let $p:E \rightarrow X$ be a covering. A **deck transformation** is a homeomorphism $d:E \rightarrow E$, such that the diagram of continuous maps

*Image: Diagramm\_Decktrafo.png.*

[commutes](wikipedia://en/138484). Together with the composition of maps, the set of deck transformation forms a [group](wikipedia://en/19447) $\operatorname{Deck}(p)$, which is the same as $\operatorname{Aut}(p)$.

Now suppose $p:C \to X$ is a covering map and $C$ (and therefore also $X$) is connected and locally path connected. The action of $\operatorname{Aut}(p)$ on each fiber is [free](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Group_action_%28mathematics%29#Notable_properties_of_actions). If this action is [transitive](wikipedia://en/12781#Transitivity_properties) on some fiber, then it is transitive on all fibers, and we call the cover **regular** (or **normal** or **Galois**). Every such regular cover is a [principal $G$-bundle](wikipedia://en/310950), where $G = \operatorname{Aut}(p)$ is considered as a discrete topological group.

Every universal cover $p:D \to X$ is regular, with deck transformation group being isomorphic to the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) $\pi_1(X)$.

:::

::: {#o43 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

- Let $q : S^1 \to S^1$ be the covering $q(z)=z^{n}$ for some $n \in \mathbb{N}$, then the map $d_k:S^1 \rightarrow S^1 : z \mapsto z \, e^{2\pi ik/n}$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ is a deck transformation and $\operatorname{Deck}(q)\cong \mathbb{Z}/ n\mathbb{Z}$.
- Let $r : \mathbb{R} \to S^1$ be the covering $r(t)=(\cos(2 \pi t), \sin(2 \pi t))$, then the map $d_k:\mathbb{R} \rightarrow \mathbb{R} : t \mapsto t + k$ for $k \in \mathbb{Z}$ is a deck transformation and $\operatorname{Deck}(r)\cong \mathbb{Z}$.
- As another important example, consider $\mathbb{C}$ the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) and $\mathbb{C}^{\times}$ the complex plane minus the origin. Then the map $p: \mathbb{C}^{\times} \to \mathbb{C}^{\times}$ with $p(z) = z^{n}$ is a regular cover. The deck transformations are multiplications with $n$-th [roots of unity](wikipedia://en/171950) and the deck transformation group is therefore isomorphic to the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) $\mathbb{Z}/n\mathbb{Z}$. Likewise, the map $\exp : \mathbb{C} \to \mathbb{C}^{\times}$ with $\exp(z) = e^{z}$ is the universal cover.

:::

::: {#o45 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

Let $X$ be a path-connected space and $p:E \rightarrow X$ be a connected covering. Since a deck transformation $d:E \rightarrow E$ is [bijective](wikipedia://en/3942), it permutes the elements of a fiber $p^{-1}(x)$ with $x \in X$ and is uniquely determined by where it sends a single point. In particular, only the identity map fixes a point in the fiber.p. 70 Because of this property every deck transformation defines a [group action](wikipedia://en/12781) on $E$, i.e. let $U \subset X$ be an open neighborhood of a $x \in X$ and $\tilde U \subset E$ an open neighborhood of an $e \in p^{-1}(x)$, then $\operatorname{Deck}(p) \times E \rightarrow E: (d,\tilde U)\mapsto d(\tilde U)$ is a [group action](wikipedia://en/12781).

:::

### Normal coverings {#Normal_coverings}

::: {#o48 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

#### Definition {#Definition}

A covering $p:E \rightarrow X$ is called normal, if $\operatorname{Deck}(p) \backslash E \cong X$. This means, that for every $x \in X$ and any two $e_0,e_1 \in p^{-1}(x)$ there exists a deck transformation $d:E \rightarrow E$, such that $d(e_0)=e_1$.

:::

::: {#o50 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

#### Properties {#Properties}

Let $X$ be a path-connected space and $p:E \rightarrow X$ be a connected covering. Let $H=p_{\#}(\pi_1(E))$ be a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of $\pi_1(X)$, then $p$ is a normal covering iff $H$ is a [normal subgroup](wikipedia://en/21918) of $\pi_1(X)$.

If $p:E \rightarrow X$ is a normal covering and $H=p_{\#}(\pi_1(E))$, then $\operatorname{Deck}(p) \cong \pi_1(X)/H$.

If $p:E \rightarrow X$ is a path-connected covering and $H=p_{\#}(\pi_1(E))$, then $\operatorname{Deck}(p) \cong N(H)/H$, whereby $N(H)$ is the [normaliser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normaliser) of $H$.p. 71

Let $E$ be a topological space. A group $\Gamma$ acts *discontinuously* on $E$, if every $e \in E$ has an open neighborhood $V \subset E$ with $V \neq \emptyset$, such that for every $d_1, d_2 \in \Gamma$ with $d_1 V \cap d_2 V \neq \emptyset$ one has $d_1 = d_2$.

If a group $\Gamma$ acts discontinuously on a topological space $E$, then the [quotient map](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quotient_map_%28topology%29) $q: E \rightarrow \Gamma \backslash E$ with $q(e)=\Gamma e$ is a normal covering.p. 72 Hereby $\Gamma \backslash E = \{\Gamma e: e \in E\}$ is the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/237213) and $\Gamma e = \{\gamma(e):\gamma \in \Gamma\}$ is the [orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) of the group action.

:::

::: {#o52 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

- The covering $q : S^1 \to S^1$ with $q(z)=z^{n}$ is a normal coverings for every $n \in \mathbb{N}$.
- Every simply connected covering is a normal covering.

:::

### Calculation {#Calculation}

Let $\Gamma$ be a group, which acts discontinuously on a topological space $E$ and let $q: E \rightarrow \Gamma \backslash E$ be the normal covering.

- If $E$ is path-connected, then $\operatorname{Deck}(q) \cong \Gamma$.p. 72
- If $E$ is simply connected, then $\operatorname{Deck}(q)\cong \pi_1(\Gamma \backslash E)$.p. 71

::: {#o55 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

- Let $n \in \mathbb{N}$. The antipodal map $g:S^n \rightarrow S^n$ with $g(x)=-x$ generates, together with the composition of maps, a group $D(g) \cong \mathbb{Z/2Z}$ and induces a group action $D(g) \times S^n \rightarrow S^n, (g,x)\mapsto g(x)$, which acts discontinuously on $S^n$. Because of $\mathbb{Z_2} \backslash S^n \cong \mathbb{R}P^n$ it follows, that the quotient map $q : S^n \rightarrow \mathbb{Z_2}\backslash S^n \cong \mathbb{R}P^n$ is a normal covering and for $n > 1$ a universal covering, hence $\operatorname{Deck}(q)\cong \mathbb{Z/2Z}\cong \pi_1({\mathbb{R}P^n})$ for $n > 1$.
- Let $\mathrm{SO}(3)$ be the [special orthogonal group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Special_orthogonal_group), then the map $f : \mathrm{SU}(2) \rightarrow \mathrm{SO}(3) \cong \mathbb{Z_2} \backslash \mathrm{SU}(2)$ is a normal covering and because of $\mathrm{SU}(2) \cong S^3$, it is the universal covering, hence $\operatorname{Deck}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z/2Z} \cong \pi_1(\mathrm{SO}(3))$.
- With the group action $(z_1,z_2)*(x,y)=(z_1+(-1)^{z_2}x,z_2+y)$ of $\mathbb{Z^2}$ on $\mathbb{R^2}$, whereby $(\mathbb{Z^2},*)$ is the [semidirect product](wikipedia://en/48246) $\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}$, one gets the universal covering $f: \mathbb{R^2} \rightarrow (\mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z}) \backslash \mathbb{R^2} \cong K$ of the [klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412) $K$, hence $\operatorname{Deck}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z} \rtimes \mathbb{Z} \cong \pi_1(K)$.
- Let $T = S^1 \times S^1$ be the [torus](wikipedia://en/74800#Topology) which is embedded in the $\mathbb{C^2}$. Then one gets a homeomorphism $\alpha: T \rightarrow T: (e^{ix},e^{iy}) \mapsto (e^{i(x+\pi)},e^{-iy})$, which induces a discontinuous group action $G_{\alpha} \times T \rightarrow T$, whereby $G_{\alpha} \cong \mathbb{Z/2Z}$. It follows, that the map $f: T \rightarrow G_{\alpha} \backslash T \cong K$ is a normal covering of the klein bottle, hence $\operatorname{Deck}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z/2Z}$.
- Let $S^3$ be embedded in the $\mathbb{C^2}$. Since the group action $S^3 \times \mathbb{Z/pZ} \rightarrow S^3: ((z_1,z_2),[k]) \mapsto (e^{2 \pi i k/p}z_1,e^{2 \pi i k q/p}z_2)$ is discontinuously, whereby $p,q \in \mathbb{N}$ are [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556), the map $f:S^3 \rightarrow \mathbb{Z_p} \backslash S^3 =: L_{p,q}$ is the universal covering of the [lens space](wikipedia://en/885725) $L_{p,q}$, hence $\operatorname{Deck}(f) \cong \mathbb{Z/pZ} \cong \pi_1(L_{p,q})$.

:::

## Galois correspondence {#Galois_correspondence}

Let $X$ be a connected and [locally simply connected](wikipedia://en/7541497) space, then for every [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) $H\subseteq \pi_1(X)$ there exists a path-connected covering $\alpha:X_H \rightarrow X$ with $\alpha_{\#}(\pi_1(X_H))=H$.p. 66

Let $p_1:E \rightarrow X$ and $p_2: E' \rightarrow X$ be two path-connected coverings, then they are equivalent iff the subgroups $H = p_{1\#}(\pi_1(E))$ and $H'=p_{2\#}(\pi_1(E'))$ are [conjugate](wikipedia://en/49176#Definition) to each other.p. 482

Let $X$ be a connected and locally simply connected space, then, up to equivalence between coverings, there is a bijection:

$$\begin{matrix} \qquad \displaystyle \{\text{Subgroup of }\pi_1(X)\} & \longleftrightarrow & \displaystyle \{\text{path-connected covering } p:E \rightarrow X\} \\ H & \longrightarrow & \alpha:X_H \rightarrow X \\ p_\#(\pi_1(E))&\longleftarrow & p \\ \displaystyle \{\text{normal subgroup of }\pi_1(X)\} & \longleftrightarrow & \displaystyle \{\text{normal covering } p:E \rightarrow X\} \end{matrix}$$

For a sequence of subgroups $\displaystyle \{\text{e}\} \subset H \subset G \subset \pi_1(X)$ one gets a sequence of coverings $\tilde X \longrightarrow X_H \cong H \backslash \tilde X \longrightarrow X_G \cong G \backslash \tilde X \longrightarrow X\cong \pi_1(X) \backslash \tilde X$. For a subgroup $H \subset \pi_1(X)$ with [index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Index_of_a_group) $\displaystyle[\pi_1(X):H] = d$, the covering $\alpha:X_H \rightarrow X$ has degree $d$.

### Equivalence {#Equivalence}

Let $X$ be a connected and locally simply connected space, $x \in X$ and $G = \pi_1(X,x)$ be the fundamental group of $X$. Since $G$ defines, by lifting of paths and evaluating at the endpoint of the lift, a group action on the fiber of a covering, the [functor](wikipedia://en/10987) $F:\boldsymbol{Cov(X)} \longrightarrow \boldsymbol{G-Set}: p \mapsto p^{-1}(x)$ is an [equivalence of categories](wikipedia://en/379051).p. 68-70

## Applications {#Applications}

*Image: Rotating gimbal-xyz.gif.* [Gimbal lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimbal_lock) occurs because any map *T*<sup>3</sup> → **RP**<sup>3</sup> is not a covering map. In particular, the relevant map carries any element of *T*<sup>3</sup>, that is, an ordered triple (a,b,c) of angles (real numbers mod 2$\pi$), to the composition of the three coordinate axis rotations R<sub>x</sub>(a)$\circ$R<sub>y</sub>(b)$\circ$R<sub>z</sub>(c) by those angles, respectively. Each of these rotations, and their composition, is an element of the rotation group SO(3), which is topologically **RP**<sup>3</sup>. This animation shows a set of three gimbals mounted together to allow *three* degrees of freedom. When all three gimbals are lined up (in the same plane), the system can only move in two dimensions from this configuration, not three, and is in *gimbal lock*. In this case it can pitch or yaw, but not roll (rotate in the plane that the axes all lie in).

An important practical application of covering spaces occurs in [charts on SO(3)](wikipedia://en/411226), the [rotation group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rotation_group_SO%283%29). This group occurs widely in engineering, due to 3-dimensional rotations being heavily used in [navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Navigation), [nautical engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nautical_engineering), and [aerospace engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aerospace_engineering), among many other uses. Topologically, SO(3) is the [real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694) **RP**<sup>3</sup>, with fundamental group **Z**/2, and only (non-trivial) covering space the hypersphere *S*<sup>3</sup>, which is the group [Spin(3)](wikipedia://en/411231), and represented by the unit [quaternions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quaternions). Thus quaternions are a preferred method for representing spatial rotations – see [quaternions and spatial rotation](wikipedia://en/186057).

However, it is often desirable to represent rotations by a set of three numbers, known as [Euler angles](wikipedia://en/411492) (in numerous variants), both because this is conceptually simpler for someone familiar with planar rotation, and because one can build a combination of three [gimbals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimbal) to produce rotations in three dimensions. Topologically this corresponds to a map from the 3-torus *T*<sup>3</sup> of three angles to the real projective space **RP**<sup>3</sup> of rotations, and the resulting map has imperfections due to this map being unable to be a covering map. Specifically, the failure of the map to be a local homeomorphism at certain points is referred to as [gimbal lock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gimbal_lock), and is demonstrated in the animation at the right – at some points (when the axes are coplanar) the [rank](wikipedia://en/7880215) of the map is 2, rather than 3, meaning that only 2 dimensions of rotations can be realized from that point by changing the angles. This causes problems in applications, and is formalized by the notion of a covering space.
