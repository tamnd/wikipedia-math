# Ergodic hypothesis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Ergodic hypothesis"}

*Image: Ergodic hypothesis w reflecting rays.jpg.* The question of ergodicity in a perfectly collisionless [ideal gas](wikipedia://en/65905) with [specular reflections](wikipedia://en/679297)

*Image: Fruit fly trap.jpg.* This device can trap fruit flies, but if it trapped atoms when placed in gas that already uniformly fills the available [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101), then both [Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312301) and the [second law of thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/54077#Classification) should be violated.

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939) and [thermodynamics](wikipedia://en/29952), the **ergodic hypothesis**[^o2] says that, over long periods of time, the time spent by a system in some region of the [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) of [microstates](wikipedia://en/3066350) with the same energy is proportional to the volume of this region; i.e., that over long periods of time all accessible microstates are [equiprobable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equiprobable).

:::

[Liouville's theorem](wikipedia://en/312301) states that, for a [Hamiltonian system](wikipedia://en/1197531), the local density of microstates following a particle path through phase space is constant as viewed by an observer moving with the ensemble—i.e., the [convective time derivative](wikipedia://en/668449) is zero. Thus, if the microstates are [uniformly distributed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_distribution_%28continuous%29) in [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) initially, they shall remain so at all times. But Liouville's theorem does *not* imply that the ergodic hypothesis holds for all Hamiltonian systems.

The ergodic hypothesis is often assumed in the [statistical analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_analysis) of [computational physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_physics). The analyst would assume that the [average](wikipedia://en/60167) of a process parameter over [time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time) and the average over the [statistical ensemble](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Statistical_ensemble) are the same. This assumption—that it is as good to simulate a system for long as it is to make many independent realizations of the same system—is not always correct. (See, for example, the [Fermi–Pasta–Ulam–Tsingou experiment](wikipedia://en/2864857) of 1953.)

Assumption of the ergodic hypothesis allows proof that certain types of perpetual motion machines of the [second kind](wikipedia://en/54077#Classification) are impossible.

Systems that are ergodic are said to have the property of [ergodicity](wikipedia://en/5456824); a broad range of systems in [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), and [probability](wikipedia://en/47895) are ergodic. Ergodic systems are studied in [ergodic theory](wikipedia://en/258986).

## Phenomenology {#Phenomenology}

In macroscopic systems, the timescales over which a system can explore the entirety of its own [phase space](wikipedia://en/191101) can be sufficiently large that the thermodynamic equilibrium state exhibits some form of **ergodicity breaking**. A common example is that of spontaneous magnetization in [ferromagnetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ferromagnetic) systems, whereby below the [Curie temperature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curie_temperature) the system preferentially adopts a non-zero magnetization even though the ergodic hypothesis implies that no net magnetisation should exist by virtue of the system exploring all states whose time-averaged magnetization are zero. The fact that macroscopic systems often violate the literal form of the ergodic hypothesis is an example of [spontaneous symmetry breaking](wikipedia://en/297466).

However, complex disordered systems such as a [spin glass](wikipedia://en/148555) show an even more complicated form of ergodicity breaking where the properties of the thermodynamic equilibrium state seen in practice are much more difficult to predict only by symmetry arguments. Also conventional glasses (e.g. window glasses) violate ergodicity in a complicated manner. In practice this means that on sufficiently short time scales (e.g. those of parts of seconds, minutes, or a few hours) the systems may behave as *solids*—with a positive shear modulus—but on extremely long scales, e.g. over millennia or eons, as *liquids*, or with two or more time scales and plateaus in between.[^o4]

## Ergodic hypothesis in finance {#Ergodic_hypothesis_in_finance}

The ergodic hypothesis is prevalent in [modern portfolio theory](wikipedia://en/734434), [discounted cash flow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discounted_cash_flow) (DCF) models, and [aggregate indicator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomic_indicators) models that infuse [macroeconomics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Macroeconomics), among others.

The situations modeled by these theories can be useful. But often they are only useful during much, but not all, of any particular time period under study. They can therefore miss some of the largest deviations from the standard model, such as [financial crises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Financial_crises), [debt crises](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Debt_crises), and [systemic risk](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Systemic_risk) in the banking system that occur only infrequently.

[Nassim Nicholas Taleb](wikipedia://en/1093496) has argued that a very important part of empirical reality in finance and investment is non-ergodic. An even statistical distribution of probabilities, where the system returns to every possible state an infinite number of times, is simply not the case we observe in situations where "absorbing states" are reached, a state where *ruin* is seen. The death of an individual, or total loss of everything, or the devolution or dismemberment of a [nation state](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nation_state) and the [legal regime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regime_change) that accompanied it, are all absorbing states. Thus, in finance, [path dependence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Path_dependence) matters. A path where an individual, firm, or country hits a "stop"—an **absorbing barrier** ("anything that prevents people with skin in the game from emerging from it, and to which the system will invariably tend. Let us call these situations *ruin*, as the entity cannot emerge from the condition. The central problem is that if there is a possibility of ruin, [cost benefit analyses](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cost–benefit_analysis) are no longer possible."[^o7]) shall be non-ergodic. All traditional models based on standard probabilistic statistics break down in these extreme situations.

The emerging field of [ergodicity economics](wikipedia://en/66241271) is beginning to show how including non-ergodic dynamics addresses some of the criticisms of neoclassical and pluralist economics;[^o8][^o9] and, practically, what investors and entrepreneurs can do[^o10] to correct for the typical outcome of a business or investment fund (under non-ergodic capital dynamics) being less than the expectation value. This correction is necessary for the [regenerative economy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regenerative_economy_theory) to work in practice.

## Ergodic hypothesis in social science {#Ergodic_hypothesis_in_social_science}

In the [social sciences](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_science), the ergodic hypothesis corresponds to the assumption that individuals are representative of groups and vice versa that group averages can adequately characterize what might be seen in an individual. This appears to not be the case: group-level data often give a poor indication of individual variation,[^o13][^o14] as individual standard deviations (SDs) tend to be eight times more than group SDs of the same people.[^o14] Subsequently, a third of the individual observations fall outside a 99.9% confidence interval of group-level data.
