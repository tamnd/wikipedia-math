# Radical of an ideal

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Radical of an ideal"}

In [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **radical** of an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) $I$ of a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) is another ideal defined by the property that an element $x$ is in the radical [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) some power of $x$ is in $I$. Taking the radical of an ideal is called *radicalization*. A **radical ideal** (or **semiprime ideal** or **reduced ideal**) is an ideal that is equal to its radical. The radical of a [primary ideal](wikipedia://en/3021435) is a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928).

:::

This concept is generalized to [non-commutative rings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_ring) in the [semiprime ring](wikipedia://en/7739701) article.

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The **radical** (occasionally also called the **nilradical**[^o4]) of an ideal $I$ in a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) $R$, denoted by $\operatorname{rad}(I)$ or $\sqrt{I}$, is defined as

$$\sqrt{I} = \left\{r\in R \mid r^n\in I\ \hbox{for some}\ n \in \mathbb{Z}^{+}\!\right\},$$

(note that $I \subseteq \sqrt{I}$). Intuitively, $\sqrt{I}$ is obtained by taking all roots of elements of $I$ within the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) $R$. Equivalently, $\sqrt{I}$ is the [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of the ideal of [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235) elements (the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636) of the ring) of the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $R/I$ (via the natural map $\pi\colon R\to R/I$). The latter proves that $\sqrt{I}$ is an ideal.[^o5]

If the radical of $I$ is [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/25977#Types_of_ideals), then some power of $\sqrt{I}$ is contained in $I$.[^o6] In particular, if $I$ and $J$ are ideals of a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129), then $I$ and $J$ have the same radical if and only if $I$ contains some power of $J$ and $J$ contains some power of $I$.

If an ideal $I$ coincides with its own radical, then $I$ is called a *radical ideal* or *[semiprime ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiprime_ideal)*.

:::

::: {#o8 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

- Consider the ring $\mathbb{Z}$ of [integers](wikipedia://en/14563#Algebraic_properties).
  1. The radical of the ideal $4\mathbb{Z}$ of integer multiples of $4$ is $2\mathbb{Z}$ (the [evens](wikipedia://en/143135)).
  1. The radical of $5\mathbb{Z}$ is $5\mathbb{Z}$.
  1. The radical of $12\mathbb{Z}$ is $6\mathbb{Z}$.
  1. In general, the radical of $m\mathbb{Z}$ is $r\mathbb{Z}$, where $r$ is the product of all distinct [prime factors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) of $m$, the largest [square-free](wikipedia://en/29525) factor of $m$ (see [Radical of an integer](wikipedia://en/1634886)). In fact, this generalizes to an arbitrary ideal (see the Properties section).
- Consider the ideal $I = \left(y^4\right) \subseteq \mathbb{C}[x,y]$. It is trivial to show $\sqrt{I}=(y)$ (using the basic property $\sqrt{I^n} = \sqrt{I}$), but we give some alternative methods: The radical $\sqrt{I}$ corresponds to the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636) $\sqrt{0}$ of the quotient ring $R = \mathbb{C}[x,y]/\!\left(y^4\right)$, which is the [intersection](wikipedia://en/23476429) of all prime ideals of the quotient ring. This is contained in the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656), which is the intersection of all [maximal ideals](wikipedia://en/48164), which are the [kernels](wikipedia://en/45240) of [homomorphisms](wikipedia://en/26411) to [fields](wikipedia://en/10603). Any ring homomorphism $R \to \mathbb{C}$ must have $y$ in the kernel in order to have a well-defined homomorphism (if we said, for example, that the kernel should be $(x,y-1)$ the composition of $\mathbb{C}[x,y] \to R \to \mathbb{C}$ would be $\left(x, y^4, y-1\right)$, which is the same as trying to force $1=0$). Since $\mathbb{C}$ is [algebraically closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraically_closed), every homomorphism $R \to \mathbb{F}$ must factor through $\mathbb{C}$, so we only have to compute the intersection of $\{\ker(\Phi) : \Phi \in \operatorname{Hom}(R,\mathbb{C}) \}$ to compute the radical of $(0).$ We then find that $\sqrt{0} = (y) \subseteq R.$

:::

## Properties {#Properties}

This section will continue the convention that $I$ is an ideal of a commutative ring $R$:

- It is always true that $\sqrt{\sqrt{I}} = \sqrt{I}$, i.e. radicalization is an [idempotent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idempotent) operation. Moreover, $\sqrt{I}$ is the smallest radical ideal containing $I$.
- $\sqrt{I}$ is the intersection of all the [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) of $R$ that contain $I$ $$\sqrt{I}=\bigcap_{\stackrel{\mathfrak{p}\text{ prime}}{R\supsetneq\mathfrak{p}\supseteq I}}\mathfrak{p},$$ and thus the radical of a prime ideal is equal to itself.[^o11][^o12]
- Specializing the last point, the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636) (the set of all nilpotent elements) is equal to the intersection of all prime ideals of $R$[^o13] $$\sqrt{0} = \mathfrak{N}_R = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p}\subsetneq R\text{ prime}}\mathfrak{p}.$$ This property is seen to be equivalent to the former via the natural map $\pi\colon R\to R/I$, which yields a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) $u$: $$\left\lbrace\text{ideals }J\mid R\supseteq J\supseteq I\right\rbrace \quad {\overset{u}{\rightleftarrows}} \quad \left\lbrace\text{ideals }J\mid J\subseteq R/I\right\rbrace,$$ defined by $u \colon J\mapsto J/I=\lbrace r+I\mid r\in J\rbrace.$[^o14][^o15]
- An ideal $I$ in a ring $R$ is radical if and only if the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $R/I$ is [reduced](wikipedia://en/539749).
- The radical of a [homogeneous ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homogeneous_ideal) is homogeneous.
- The radical of an intersection of ideals is equal to the intersection of their radicals: $\sqrt{I \cap J} = \sqrt{I} \cap \sqrt{J}$.
- The radical of a [primary ideal](wikipedia://en/3021435) is prime. If the radical of an ideal $I$ is maximal, then $I$ is primary.[^o16]
- If $I$ is an ideal, $\sqrt{I^n} = \sqrt{I}$. Since prime ideals are radical ideals, $\sqrt{\mathfrak{p}} = \mathfrak{p}$ for any prime ideal $\mathfrak{p}$.
- Let $I,J$ be ideals of a ring $R$. If $\sqrt{I}, \sqrt{J}$ are [comaximal](wikipedia://en/25977#Types_of_ideals), then $I, J$ are comaximal.[^o17]
- Let $M$ be a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) [module](wikipedia://en/276410) over a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) $R$. Then[^o18] $$\sqrt{\operatorname{ann}_R(M)} = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \,\in\, \operatorname{supp}M} \mathfrak{p} = \bigcap_{\mathfrak{p} \,\in\, \operatorname{ass}M} \mathfrak{p}$$ where $\operatorname{supp}M$ is the [support](wikipedia://en/16073360) of $M$ and $\operatorname{ass}M$ is the set of [associated primes](wikipedia://en/17286442) of $M$.

:::

## Applications to algebraic geometry {#Applications_to_algebraic_geometry}

One of the primary motivations for studying radicals of ideals is to understand [algebraic sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebraic_set) and [varieties](wikipedia://en/248808) in algebraic geometry.

For a subset of polynomials $S\subset \mathbb{k}[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ and subset of points $X\subset \mathbb{k}^n,$ where $\mathbb{k}$ is an [algebraically closed field](wikipedia://en/1018), let

$$\operatorname{V}(S) := \left\{x \in \mathbb{k}^n \mid f(x)=0 \mbox{ for all } f \in S\right\}$$

and

$$\operatorname{I}(X) := \{f \in \mathbb{k}[x_1, \dots, x_n] \mid f(x)=0 \mbox{ for all } x \in X \}$$

be the *zero locus* (or '*variety*') of *S* and *vanishing ideal* (or **ideal*') of*X*, respectively.***

If $S\subset\mathbb{k}[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ is any subset of polynomials, $(S)$ is the ideal generated by the elements of *S*, and $\sqrt{(S)}$ is the radical of that ideal, then these collections of polynomials have the same zero loci:

$$\operatorname{V}(S)=\operatorname{V} ((S))=\operatorname{V}(\sqrt{(S)}).$$

Moreover, the vanishing ideal of any $X\subset \mathbb{k}^n$ is always a radical ideal:

$$\operatorname{I}(X)=\sqrt{\operatorname{I}(X)}.$$

The operations V and I are, in a sense made more precise below, inverses of each other:

For any subset of points $X\subset \mathbb{k}^n$,

$$\operatorname{V}(\operatorname{I}(X))=\overline{X},$$

where $\overline{X}$ is the closure of *X* in the [Zariski topology](wikipedia://en/241744). In particular, $\operatorname{V}(\operatorname{I}(X))=X$ if *X* is an algebraic set, since algebraic sets are closed in the Zariski topology.

[Hilbert's Nullstellensatz](wikipedia://en/149215) is a fundamental result in [commutative algebra](wikipedia://en/245990) and [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997) that addresses the composition of V and I in the opposite order. One version of this celebrated theorem states that for any subset of polynomials $S\subset \mathbb{k}[x_1,\dots,x_n],$ we have

$$\operatorname{I}(\operatorname{V}(S)) = \sqrt{(S)}.$$

Geometrically, this says that if an [affine algebraic set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Affine_algebraic_set) *W* is cut out by the [polynomial equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_equation) $f_1(x)=0,\dots,f_m(x)=0,$ then the polynomials *f* that vanish on *W* are precisely those such that one of the powers of *f* lies in the ideal generated by the polynomials $f_1,\dots,f_m;$ i.e., $f^r\in (f_1,\ldots,f_m)$ for some natural number *r*. As a corollary, $\operatorname{I}(\operatorname{V}(J)) = J$ if *J* is a radical ideal.

It is clear that both V and I reverse the direction of inclusions: $S_1\subset S_2 \implies \operatorname{V}(S_1)\supset\operatorname{V}(S_2)$ and $X_1\subset X_2 \implies \operatorname{I}(X_1)\supset\operatorname{I}(X_2).$ Consequently, $X \subset \operatorname{V}(S) \iff S \subset \operatorname{I}(X),$ and so V and I form an [antitone](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antitone) [Galois connection](wikipedia://en/156411) between subsets of polynomials in $\mathbb{k}[x_1,\dots,x_n]$ and subsets of points in $\mathbb{k}^n$ that gives rise to a bijective correspondence when restricted to radical ideals and algebraic sets:

$$\{\mathrm{radical\ ideals}\subset \mathbb{k}[x_1,\dots,x_n]\}\ \underset{\operatorname{I}}{\stackrel{\operatorname{V}}{\rightleftarrows}}\ \{\mathrm{algebraic\ sets}\subset \mathbb{k}^n\}.$$
