# Proof of Bertrand's postulate

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Proof of Bertrand's postulate"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [Bertrand's postulate](wikipedia://en/232526) (now a [theorem](wikipedia://en/30977)) states that, for each $n \ge 2$, there is a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666) $p$ such that $n<p<2n$. First [conjectured](wikipedia://en/6138) in 1845 by [Joseph Bertrand](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Joseph_Louis_François_Bertrand),[^o2] it was first [proven](wikipedia://en/82285) by [Chebyshev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chebyshev), and a shorter but also advanced proof was given by [Ramanujan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramanujan).[^o3]

:::

The following [elementary proof](wikipedia://en/9318685) was published by [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) in 1932, as one of his earliest mathematical publications.[^o4] The basic idea is to show that the [central binomial coefficients](wikipedia://en/2128244) must have a [prime factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_factor) within the [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) $(n, 2n)$ in order to be large enough. This is achieved through analysis of their factorizations.

The main steps of the proof are as follows. First, one shows that the contribution of every [prime power](wikipedia://en/1630483) factor $p^r$ in the prime decomposition of the central binomial coefficient $\textstyle\binom{2n}{n}=(2n)!/(n!)^2$ is at most $2n$; then, one shows that every prime larger than $\sqrt{2n}$ appears at most once.

The next step is to prove that $\tbinom{2n}{n}$ has no prime factors in the interval $(\tfrac{2n}{3}, n)$. As a consequence of these bounds, the contribution to the size of $\tbinom{2n}{n}$ coming from the prime factors that are at most $n$ grows [asymptotically](wikipedia://en/641995) as $\theta^{\!\;n}$ for some $\theta<4$. Since the asymptotic growth of the central binomial coefficient is at least $4^n\!/2n$, the conclusion is that, [by contradiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proof_by_contradiction) and for large enough $n$, the binomial coefficient must have another prime factor, which can only lie between $n$ and $2n$.

The argument given is valid for all $n\ge427$. The remaining values of $n$ are verified by direct inspection, which completes the proof.

::: {#o6 .proof cue=named-heading name="Lemmas in the proof"}

## Lemmas in the proof {#Lemmas_in_the_proof}

The proof uses the following four [lemmas](wikipedia://en/18634) to establish facts about the primes present in the central binomial coefficients.

::: {#o8 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Lemma 1"}

### Lemma 1 {#Lemma_1}

For any [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) $n>0$, we have

$$\frac{4^n}{2n} \le \binom{2n}{n}.$$

::: {#o9 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** Applying the [binomial theorem](wikipedia://en/4677),

$$4^n = (1 + 1)^{2n} = \sum_{k = 0}^{2n} \binom{2n}{k}=2+\sum_{k = 1}^{2n-1} \binom{2n}{k} \le 2n\binom{2n}{n},$$

since $\tbinom{2n}{n}$ is the largest term in the sum in the left-hand side, and the sum has $2n$ terms (including the initial $2$ outside the summation).

:::

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Lemma 2"}

### Lemma 2 {#Lemma_2}

For a fixed prime $p$, define $R(n,p)=\nu_p( \tbinom{2n}{n} )$ to be the [*p*-adic valuation](wikipedia://en/16047618) of the [central binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/2128244) $\tbinom{2n}{n}$, that is, the largest [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474) $r$ such that $p^r$ divides $\tbinom{2n}{n}$, or equivalently, the exponent to which $p$ appears in the prime factorization of $\tbinom{2n}{n}$.

For any prime $p$, $p^{R(n,p)}\le 2n$.

::: {#o12 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** The exponent of $p$ in $n!$ is given by [Legendre's formula](wikipedia://en/44533982)

$$\sum_{j = 1}^\infty \left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^j} \right\rfloor\!,$$

so

$$R(n,p)=\sum_{j = 1}^\infty \left\lfloor \frac{2n}{p^j} \right\rfloor - 2\sum_{j = 1}^\infty \left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^j} \right\rfloor=\sum_{j = 1}^\infty \left(\left\lfloor \frac{2n}{p^j} \right\rfloor - 2\!\left\lfloor \frac{n}{p^j} \right\rfloor\right)$$

But each term of the last summation must be either zero (if $n/p^j \bmod 1<1/2$) or one (if $n/p^j\bmod 1\ge1/2$), and all terms with $j>\log_p(2n)$ are zero. Therefore,

$$R(n,p) \le \log_p(2n),$$

and

$$p^{R(n,p)} \le p^{\log_p(2n)} = 2n.$$

:::

:::

::: {#o14 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Lemma 3"}

### Lemma 3 {#Lemma_3}

If $p$ is an [odd](wikipedia://en/143135) prime and $\frac{2n}{3} < p \leq n$, then $R(n,p) = 0.$

::: {#o15 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** There are exactly two factors of $p$ in the numerator of the expression $\tbinom{2n}{n}=(2n)!/(n!)^2$, coming from the two terms $p$ and $2p$ in $(2n)!$, and also two factors of $p$ in the denominator from one copy of the term $p$ in each of the two factors of $n!$. These factors all cancel, leaving no factors of $p$ in $\tbinom{2n}{n}$. (The bound on $p$ in the preconditions of the lemma ensures that $3p$ is too large to be a term of the numerator, and the assumption that $p$ is odd is needed to ensure that $2p$ contributes only one factor of $p$ to the numerator.)

:::

:::

::: {#o17 .statement kind=statement/lemma cue=named-heading name="Lemma 4"}

### Lemma 4 {#Lemma_4}

An upper bound is supplied for the [primorial](wikipedia://en/422786) function,

$$n\#=\prod_{p\,\le\,n}p,$$

where the product is taken over all *prime* numbers $p$ less than or equal to $n$.

For all $n\ge1$, $n\#<4^n$.

::: {#o18 .proof cue=inline-head name="Proof"}

**Proof:** We use [complete induction](wikipedia://en/18881#Complete_%28strong%29_induction).

For $n=1,2$ we have $1\#=1<4$ and $2\#=2<4^2=16$.

Let us assume that the inequality holds for all $1\le n\le2k-1$. Since $n=2k>2$ is composite, we have

$$(2k)\#=(2k-1)\#<4^{2k-1}<4^{2k}.$$

Now let us assume that the inequality holds for all $1\le n\le2k$. Since $\binom{2k+1}{k}=\frac{(2k+1)!}{k!(k+1)!}$ is an integer and all the primes $k+2\le p\le2k+1$ appear only in the numerator, we have

$$\frac{(2k+1)\#}{(k+1)\#}\le\binom{2k+1}{k}=\frac12\!\left[\binom{2k+1}{k}+\binom{2k+1}{k+1}\right]<\frac12(1+1)^{2k+1}=4^k .$$

Therefore,

$$(2k+1)\#=(k+1)\#\cdot\frac{(2k+1)\#}{(k+1)\#}\le4^{k+1}\binom{2k+1}{k}<4^{k+1}\cdot4^k=4^{2k+1}.$$

:::

:::

::: {#o20 .proof cue=heading name="Proof from the lemmas"}

## Proof from the lemmas {#Proof_from_the_lemmas}

Assume that there is a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151): an integer *n* ≥ 2 such that there is no prime *p* with *n* \< *p* \< 2*n*.

If 2 ≤ *n* \< 630, then *p* can be chosen from among the prime numbers 3, 5, 7, 13, 23, 43, 83, 163, 317, 631 (each being the largest prime less than twice its predecessor) such that *n* \< *p* \< 2*n*. Therefore, *n* ≥ 630.

There are no prime factors *p* of $\textstyle\binom{2n}{n}$ such that:

- 2*n* \< *p*, because every factor must divide (2*n*)!;
- *p* = 2*n*, because 2*n* is not prime;
- *n* \< *p* \< 2*n*, because we assumed there is no such prime number;
- 2*n*/3 \< *p* ≤ *n*: by Lemma 3.

Therefore, every prime factor *p* satisfies *p* ≤ 2*n*/3.

When $p > \sqrt{2n},$ the number $\textstyle {2n \choose n}$ has at most one factor of *p*. By Lemma 2, for any prime *p* we have *p*<sup>*R*(*p*,*n*)</sup> ≤ 2*n*, and the [number of primes less than or equal to *x*](wikipedia://en/331826), $\pi(x)\le x-1$ since 1 is neither prime nor composite. Then, starting with Lemma 1 and decomposing the right-hand side into its prime factorization, and finally using Lemma 4, these bounds give:

$$\frac{4^n}{2n}\le\binom{2n}{n}=\left(\,\prod_{p\,\le\,\sqrt{2n}}p^{R(p,n)}\right)\!\!\left(\prod_{\sqrt{2n}\,<\,p\,\le\,2n/3}\!\!\!\!\!\!\!p^{R(p,n)}\right)<\left(\,\prod_{p\,\le\,\sqrt{2n}}\!\!2n\right)\!\!\left(\prod_{p\,\le\,2n/3}\!\!p\right)\le(2n)^{\sqrt{2n}-1}4^{2n/3}.$$

Therefore

$4^{n/3}<(2n)^\sqrt{2n}$, which simplifies to $2^\sqrt{2n}<(2n)^3.$

Taking the base-2 [logarithm](wikipedia://en/17860) of and squaring both sides yields

$$2n<9\log_2^2(2n).$$

By [concavity](wikipedia://en/263321) of the right-hand side as a function of *n* for $n\ge 2$ and linearity of the left-hand side, the last inequality is necessarily verified on an interval. Since it holds true for $n=426$ and it does not for $n=427$, we obtain

$$n<427.$$

But these cases have already been settled, and we conclude that no counterexample to the postulate is possible.

### Addendum to proof {#Addendum_to_proof}

It is possible to reduce the bound to $n=50$.

For $n\ge17,$ we get $\pi(n)<\frac{n}{2}-1$, so we can say that the product $p^{R(n,p)}$ is at most $(2n)^{0.5\sqrt{2n}-1}$, which gives

$$\begin{aligned} &\frac{4^n}{2n}\le\binom{2n}{n}\le(2n)^{0.5\sqrt{2n}-1}4^{2n/3}\\ &4^{\sqrt{2n}}\le(2n)^3\\ &8n\le9\log_2^2(2n) \end{aligned}$$

which is true for $n=49$ and false for $n=50$.

:::
