# Almost

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Almost"}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553), when dealing with [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of [infinite size](wikipedia://en/142679), the term **almost** or **nearly** is used to refer to all but a negligible amount of elements in the set. The notion of "negligible" depends on the context, and may mean "of measure zero" (in a [measure space](wikipedia://en/45270)), "finite" (when infinite sets are involved), or "countable" (when [uncountably infinite sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountably_infinite_set) are involved).

:::

For example:

- The set $S = \{ n \in \mathbb{N}\,|\, n \ge k \}$ is almost $\mathbb{N}$ for any $k$ in **$\mathbb{N}$**, because only finitely many [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) are less than *$k$*.
- The set of [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666) is not almost **$\mathbb{N}$**, because there are infinitely many natural numbers that are not prime numbers.
- The set of [transcendental numbers](wikipedia://en/30325) are almost **$\mathbb{R}$**, because the [algebraic](wikipedia://en/1158) [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) numbers form a [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable) [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) of the set of real numbers (which is uncountable).[^o2]
- The [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) is uncountably infinite, but has [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) zero.[^o3] So almost all real numbers in (0, 1) are members of the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of the Cantor set.
