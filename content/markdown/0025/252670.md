# Absolute continuity

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Absolute continuity"}

In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) and [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478), **absolute continuity** is a regularity property of [functions](wikipedia://en/185427) that is stronger than [uniform continuity](wikipedia://en/32337), and hence [continuity](wikipedia://en/6122). The notion of absolute continuity allows one to obtain generalizations of the relationship between the two central operations of [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176)—[differentiation](wikipedia://en/7921) and [integration](wikipedia://en/15532). This relationship is commonly characterized (by the [fundamental theorem of calculus](wikipedia://en/19468696)) in the framework of [Riemann integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_integration), but with absolute continuity it may be formulated in terms of [Lebesgue integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration). For real-valued functions on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), two interrelated notions appear: **absolute continuity of functions** and **absolute continuity of measures**. These two notions are generalized in different directions. The usual derivative of a function is related to the *[Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative)*, or *density*, of a measure. We have the following chains of inclusions for functions over an interval:

:::

*[Lipschitz continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lipschitz_continuous)* ⊆ *absolutely continuous* ⊆ *[uniformly continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_continuous)* ⊆ *[continuous](wikipedia://en/6122)*

and, for a bounded interval,

*absolutely continuous* ⊆ *[bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673)* ⊆ *[differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688)*.

## Absolute continuity of functions {#Absolute_continuity_of_functions}

A continuous function fails to be absolutely continuous if it fails to be [uniformly continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniformly_continuous), which can happen if the domain of the function is not compact – examples are tan(*x*) over 0, *π*/2, *x*<sup>2</sup> over the entire real line, and sin(1/*x*) over (0, 1\]. But a continuous function *f* can fail to be absolutely continuous even on a compact interval. It may not be "differentiable almost everywhere" (like the [Weierstrass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_function), which is not differentiable anywhere). Or it may be [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) almost everywhere and its derivative *f* may be [Lebesgue integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration), but the integral of *f* differs from the increment of *f* (how much *f* changes over an interval). This happens for example with the [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819).

::: {#o4 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

Let $I$ be an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) in the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) $\mathbb{R}$. A function $f\colon I \to \mathbb{R}$ is **absolutely continuous** on $I$ if for every positive number $\varepsilon$, there is a positive number $\delta$ such that whenever a finite sequence of [pairwise disjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pairwise_disjoint) sub-intervals $(x_k, y_k)$ of $I$ with $x_k < y_k$ satisfies[^o5]

$$\sum_{k=1}^{N} (y_k - x_k) < \delta$$

then

$$\sum_{k=1}^{N} | f(y_k) - f(x_k) | < \varepsilon.$$

The collection of all absolutely continuous functions on $I$ is denoted $\operatorname{AC}(I)$.

:::

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Equivalent definitions"}

### Equivalent definitions {#Equivalent_definitions}

The following conditions on a real-valued function *f* on a compact interval \[*a*,*b*\] are equivalent:[^o8]

1. *f* is absolutely continuous;
1. *f* has a derivative *f* [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688), the derivative is Lebesgue integrable, and $$f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x f'(t) \, dt$$ for all *x* on \[*a*,*b*\];
1. there exists a Lebesgue integrable function *g* on \[*a*,*b*\] such that $$f(x) = f(a) + \int_a^x g(t) \, dt$$ for all *x* in \[*a*,*b*\].

If these equivalent conditions are satisfied, then necessarily any function *g* as in condition 3. satisfies *g* = *f* almost everywhere.

Equivalence between (1) and (3) is known as the **fundamental theorem of Lebesgue integral calculus**, due to [Lebesgue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue).[^o9]

For an equivalent definition in terms of measures see the section Relation between the two notions of absolute continuity.

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

### Properties {#Properties}

- The sum and difference of two absolutely continuous functions are also absolutely continuous. If the two functions are defined on a (bounded) [closed interval](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closed_interval), then their product is also absolutely continuous.[^o12] On unbounded intervals this may fail: for example on $\mathbb R$, the function $f(x)=x$ is absolutely continuous, but $f(x)^2$ is not even uniformly continuous.
- If an absolutely continuous function *f* is defined on a (bounded) closed interval and is nowhere zero then *1/f* is absolutely continuous.[^o13]
- Every absolutely continuous function (over an interval) is [uniformly continuous](wikipedia://en/32337) and, therefore, [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122). Every (globally) [Lipschitz-continuous](wikipedia://en/51731) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) is absolutely continuous.[^o14]
- If *f*: \[*a*,*b*\] → **R** is absolutely continuous, then it is [weakly differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weakly_differentiable); conversely if *f*: \[*a*,*b*\] → **R** is weakly differentiable, then it coincides almost everywhere with an absolutely continuous function;[^o15] this provides a characterization of [Sobolev spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sobolev_spaces) on intervals of the real line.
- If *f*: \[*a*,*b*\] → **R** is absolutely continuous, then it is of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673) on \[*a*,*b*\].[^o16]
- If *f*: \[*a*,*b*\] → **R** is absolutely continuous, then it can be written as the difference of two monotonic nondecreasing absolutely continuous functions on \[*a*,*b*\].
- If *f*: \[*a*,*b*\] → **R** is absolutely continuous, then it has the [Luzin *N* property](wikipedia://en/5756885) (that is, for any $N \subseteq [a,b]$ such that $\lambda(N) = 0$, it holds that $\lambda(f(N)) = 0$, where $\lambda$ stands for the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) on **R**).
- *f*: \[*a*,*b*\] → **R** is absolutely continuous if and only if it is continuous, is of bounded variation and has the Luzin *N* property. This statement is also known as the Banach-Zareckiǐ theorem.[^o17]
- If *f*: *I* → **R** is absolutely continuous and *g*: **R** → **R** is globally [Lipschitz-continuous](wikipedia://en/51731), then the composition *g $\circ$ f* is absolutely continuous. Conversely, for every function *g* that is not globally Lipschitz continuous there exists an absolutely continuous function *f* such that *g $\circ$ f* is not absolutely continuous.[^o18]

:::

::: {#o20 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

### Examples {#Examples}

The following functions are uniformly continuous but **not** absolutely continuous:

- The [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819) on \[0, 1\] (it is of bounded variation but not absolutely continuous);
- The function: $$f(x) = \begin{cases} 0, & \text{if }x =0 \\ x \sin(1/x), & \text{if } x \neq 0 \end{cases}$$ on a finite interval containing the origin.

The following functions are absolutely continuous but not α-Hölder continuous:

- The function *f*(*x*) = *x<sup>β</sup>* on \[0, *c*\], for any 0 \< *β* \< *α* \< 1

The following functions are absolutely continuous and [α-Hölder continuous](wikipedia://en/4005892) but not [Lipschitz continuous](wikipedia://en/51731):

- The function *f*(*x*) = $\sqrt{x}$ on \[0, *c*\], for *α* ≤ 1/2.

:::

::: {#o22 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

Let (*X*, *d*) be a [metric space](wikipedia://en/20018) and let *I* be an [interval](wikipedia://en/49172) in the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line) **R**. A function *f*: *I* → *X* is **absolutely continuous** on *I* if for every positive number $\varepsilon$, there is a positive number $\delta$ such that whenever a finite sequence of [pairwise disjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pairwise_disjoint) sub-intervals \[*x*<sub>*k*</sub>, *y*<sub>*k*</sub>\] of *I* satisfies:

$$\sum_{k} \left| y_k - x_k \right| < \delta$$

then:

$$\sum_{k} d \left( f(y_k), f(x_k) \right) < \varepsilon.$$

The collection of all absolutely continuous functions from *I* into *X* is denoted AC(*I*; *X*).

A further generalization is the space AC<sup>*p*</sup>(*I*; *X*) of curves *f*: *I* → *X* such that:[^o23]

$$d \left( f(s), f(t) \right) \leq \int_s^t m(\tau) \,d\tau \text{ for all } [s, t] \subseteq I$$

for some *m* in the [*L*<sup>*p*</sup> space](wikipedia://en/45194) *L*<sup>*p*</sup>(I).

:::

### Properties of these generalizations {#Properties_of_these_generalizations}

- Every absolutely continuous function (over a compact interval) is [uniformly continuous](wikipedia://en/32337) and, therefore, [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122). Every [Lipschitz-continuous](wikipedia://en/51731) [function](wikipedia://en/185427) is absolutely continuous.
- If *f*: \[*a*,*b*\] → *X* is absolutely continuous, then it is of [bounded variation](wikipedia://en/330673) on \[*a*,*b*\].
- For *f* ∈ AC<sup>*p*</sup>(*I*; *X*), the [metric derivative](wikipedia://en/6840149) of *f* exists for *λ*-[almost all](wikipedia://en/1309) times in *I*, and the metric derivative is the smallest *m* ∈ *L*<sup>*p*</sup>(*I*; **R**) such that:[^o25] $$d \left( f(s), f(t) \right) \leq \int_s^t m(\tau) \,d\tau \text{ for all } [s, t] \subseteq I.$$

## Absolute continuity of measures {#Absolute_continuity_of_measures}

::: {#o28 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

### Definition {#Definition}

A [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) $\mu$ on [Borel subsets](wikipedia://en/43327) of the real line is absolutely continuous with respect to the [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) $\lambda$ if for every Borel set $A,$ $\lambda(A) = 0$ implies $\mu(A) = 0$. Equivalently, $\mu(A) > 0$ implies $\lambda(A) > 0$. This condition is written as $\mu \ll \lambda.$ We say $\mu$ is *dominated* by $\lambda.$

In most applications, if a measure on the real line is simply said to be absolutely continuous — without specifying with respect to which other measure it is absolutely continuous — then absolute continuity with respect to the Lebesgue measure is meant.

The same principle holds for measures on Borel subsets of $\mathbb{R}^n, n \geq 2.$

:::

::: {#o30 .definition cue=heading name="Equivalent definitions"}

### Equivalent definitions {#Equivalent_definitions}

The following conditions on a finite measure $\mu$ on Borel subsets of the real line are equivalent:[^o31]

1. $\mu$ is absolutely continuous;
1. For every positive number $\varepsilon$ there is a positive number $\delta > 0$ such that $\mu(A) < \varepsilon$ for all Borel sets $A$ of Lebesgue measure less than $\delta;$
1. There exists a Lebesgue integrable function $g$ on the real line such that: $$\mu(A) = \int_A g \,d\lambda$$ for all Borel subsets $A$ of the real line.

For an equivalent definition in terms of functions see the section Relation between the two notions of absolute continuity.

Any other function satisfying (3) is equal to $g$ almost everywhere. Such a function is called [Radon–Nikodym derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radon–Nikodym_derivative), or density, of the absolutely continuous measure $\mu.$

Equivalence between (1), (2) and (3) holds also in $\mathbb{R}^n$ for all $n\in\mathbb{N}.$

Thus, the absolutely continuous measures on $\mathbb{R}^n$ are precisely those that have densities; as a special case, the absolutely continuous probability measures are precisely the ones that have [probability density functions](wikipedia://en/43487).

:::

::: {#o33 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

### Generalizations {#Generalizations}

If $\mu$ and $\nu$ are two [measures](wikipedia://en/19873) on the same [measurable space](wikipedia://en/43333) $(X, \mathcal{A}),$ $\mu$ is said to be **Absolutely continuous measure with respect to $\nu$** if $\mu(A) = 0$ for every set $A$ for which $\nu(A) = 0.$[^o34] This is written as "$\mu\ll\nu$". That is:

$$\mu \ll \nu \qquad \text{ if and only if } \qquad \text{ for all } A\in\mathcal{A}, \quad (\nu(A) = 0\ \text{ implies } \ \mu (A) = 0).$$

When $\mu\ll\nu,$ then $\nu$ is said to be **Domination (measure theory)** $\mu.$

Absolute continuity of measures is [reflexive](wikipedia://en/200458) and [transitive](wikipedia://en/200463), but is not [antisymmetric](wikipedia://en/1176), so it is a [preorder](wikipedia://en/23582) rather than a [partial order](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_order). Instead, if $\mu \ll \nu$ and $\nu \ll \mu,$ the measures $\mu$ and $\nu$ are said to be [equivalent](wikipedia://en/6612667). Thus absolute continuity induces a partial ordering of such [equivalence classes](wikipedia://en/9260).

If $\mu$ is a [signed](wikipedia://en/2985811) or [complex measure](wikipedia://en/3057532), it is said that $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\nu$ if its variation $|\mu|$ satisfies $|\mu| \ll \nu;$ equivalently, if every set $A$ for which $\nu(A) = 0$ is $\mu$-[null](wikipedia://en/21520).

The [Radon–Nikodym theorem](wikipedia://en/338746)[^o35] states that if $\mu$ is absolutely continuous with respect to $\nu,$ and both measures are [σ-finite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Σ-finite), then $\mu$ has a density, or "Radon-Nikodym derivative", with respect to $\nu,$ which means that there exists a $\nu$-measurable function $f$ taking values in $[0, +\infty),$ denoted by $f = d\mu / d\nu,$ such that for any $\nu$-measurable set $A$ we have:

$$\mu(A) = \int_A f \,d\nu.$$

:::

### Singular measures {#Singular_measures}

Via [Lebesgue's decomposition theorem](wikipedia://en/4316238),[^o37] every σ-finite measure can be decomposed into the sum of an absolutely continuous measure and a singular measure with respect to another σ-finite measure. See [singular measure](wikipedia://en/4304500) for examples of measures that are not absolutely continuous.

## Relation between the two notions of absolute continuity {#Relation_between_the_two_notions_of_absolute_continuity}

A finite measure *μ* on [Borel subsets](wikipedia://en/43327) of the real line is absolutely continuous with respect to [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198) if and only if the point function:

$$F(x)=\mu((-\infty,x])$$

is an absolutely continuous real function. More generally, a function has a locally (meaning on every bounded interval) absolutely continuous representative almost everywhere if and only if its [distributional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_derivative) is a measure that is absolutely continuous with respect to the Lebesgue measure.

If absolute continuity holds then the Radon–Nikodym derivative of *μ* is equal almost everywhere to the derivative of *F*.[^o39]

More generally, the measure *μ* is assumed to be locally finite (rather than finite) and *F*(*x*) is defined as *μ*((0,*x*\]) for *x* > 0, 0 for *x* = 0, and −*μ*((*x*,0\]) for *x* \< 0. In this case *μ* is the [Lebesgue–Stieltjes measure](wikipedia://en/368525) generated by *F*.[^o40] The relation between the two notions of absolute continuity still holds.[^o41]
