# Grade (slope)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Grade"}

*Image: Grade dimension.svg.* d = run Δh = rise l = slope length α = angle of inclination

The **grade** (US) or **gradient** (UK) (also called **slope**, **incline**, **mainfall**, **pitch** or **rise**) of a physical feature, landform or constructed line is either the [elevation angle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elevation_angle) of that surface to the [horizontal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_plane) or its tangent. It is a special case of the [slope](wikipedia://en/29368), where zero indicates [horizontality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_plane). A larger number indicates higher or steeper degree of "tilt". Often slope is calculated as a ratio of "rise" to "run", or as a fraction ("rise over run") in which *run* is the horizontal distance (not the distance along the slope) and *rise* is the vertical distance.

:::

Slopes of existing physical features such as [canyons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canyon) and hillsides, [stream and river banks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_%28geography%29), and [beds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stream_bed) are often described as grades, but typically the word "grade" is used for human-made surfaces such as roads, [landscape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape) grading, [roof pitches](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roof_pitch), [railroads](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rail_tracks), [aqueducts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aqueduct_%28watercourse%29), and pedestrian or bicycle routes. The grade may refer to the [longitudinal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_terms_of_location) slope or the [perpendicular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_terms_of_location) [cross slope](wikipedia://en/12520196).

## Nomenclature {#Nomenclature}

*Image: Slope quadrant.svg.* Illustration of grades (percentages), angles in degrees, and ratio

*Image: 50 per mille.png.* A tram and gradient sign in [Gdańsk, Poland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gdańsk,_Poland), indicating a 50[‰](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Permille) slope (50m/km, 5%).

There are several ways to express slope:

1. as an *angle* of inclination to the horizontal. (This is the angle $\alpha$ opposite the "rise" side of a triangle with a right angle between vertical rise and horizontal run.)
1. as a *[percentage](wikipedia://en/1712680)*, the formula for which is $100 \times \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}$, which is equivalent to the tangent of the angle of inclination times 100. At a slope angle of 45 degrees, the run is equal to the rise. Expressed as a percentage, the slope of this angle is 100 percent. As the slope approaches 90 degrees (vertical), the percentage slope approaches infinity.[^o3] In Europe and the U.S. percentage *grade* is the most commonly used figure for describing slopes.
1. as a *‰* figure (denoted with the symbol ‰), the formula for which is $1000 \times \frac{\text{rise}}{\text{run}}$, which could also be expressed as *the tangent of the angle of inclination times 1000*. This is commonly used in Europe to denote the incline of a railway. It is sometimes written using *mm/m* or *m/km* instead of the ‰ symbol.[^o4][^o5]
1. as a *ratio* of one part rise to so many parts run. For example, a slope that has a rise of 5 feet for every 1000 feet of run would have a slope ratio of 1 in 200. (The word *in* is normally used rather than the mathematical ratio notation of e.g. *1:200*.) This is generally the method used to describe railway grades in Australia and the UK. It is used for roads in Hong Kong, and was used for roads in the UK until the 1970s.
1. as a *ratio* of many parts run to one part rise, which is the inverse of the previous expression (depending on the country and the industry standards). For example, a slope expressed as *4:1* in this system means for a given vertical distance the horizontal distance travelled is four times as long.[^o6]

Any of these may be used. When the term *grade* is used, the slope is usually expressed as a percentage. If one looks at red numbers on the chart specifying grade, one can see the quirkiness of using the grade to specify slope; the numbers go from 0 for flat, to 100% at 45 degrees, to infinity at vertical.

Slope may still be expressed when the horizontal run is not known: the rise can be divided by the [hypotenuse](wikipedia://en/251366) (the slope length). This is not the usual way to specify slope; this nonstandard expression follows the [sine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sine) function rather than the tangent function, so it calls a 45 degree slope a 71 percent grade instead of a 100 percent. But in practice the usual way to calculate slope is to measure the distance along the slope and the vertical rise, and calculate the horizontal run from that, in order to calculate the grade (100% × rise/run) or standard slope (rise/run). When the angle of inclination is small, using the slope length rather than the horizontal displacement (i.e., using the sine of the angle rather than the tangent) makes only an insignificant difference and can then be used as an approximation. Railway gradients are often expressed in terms of the rise in relation to the distance along the track as a practical measure. In cases where the difference between sin and tan is significant, the tangent is used. In either case, the following identity holds for all inclinations up to 90 degrees: $\tan{\alpha} = \frac{\sin{\alpha}}{\sqrt{1-\sin^2{\alpha}}}$. Or more simply, one can calculate the horizontal run by using the Pythagorean theorem, after which it is trivial to calculate the (standard math) slope or the grade (percentage).

In Europe, road gradients are expressed in signage as percentage.[^o7]

### Equations {#Equations}

Grades are related using the following equations with symbols from the figure at top.

#### Tangent as a ratio {#Tangent_as_a_ratio}

$$\tan{\alpha} = \frac{\Delta h}{d}$$

The slope expressed as a percentage can similarly be determined from the tangent of the angle:

$$\%\,\text{slope} = 100 \tan{\alpha}$$

#### Angle from a tangent gradient {#Angle_from_a_tangent_gradient}

$$\alpha = \arctan{\frac{\Delta h}{d}}$$

If the tangent is expressed as a percentage, the angle can be determined as:

$$\alpha = \arctan{\frac{\%\,\text{slope}}{100}}$$

If the angle is expressed as a ratio *(1 in n)* then:

$$\alpha = \arctan{\frac{1}{n}}$$

## Roads {#Roads}

In [vehicular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vehicle) [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering), various [land](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landform)-based designs ([automobiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automobiles), [sport utility vehicles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sport_utility_vehicle), [trucks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truck), [trains](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Train), etc.) are rated for their ability to ascend [terrain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Terrain). Trains typically rate much lower than automobiles. The highest grade a vehicle can ascend while maintaining a particular speed is sometimes termed that vehicle's "gradeability" (or, less often, "grade ability"). The lateral slopes of a highway geometry are sometimes called [fills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_and_fill) or [cuts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cut_%28earthworks%29) where these techniques have been used to create them.

In the United States, the maximum grade for federally funded highways is specified in a design table based on terrain and design speeds,[^o16] with up to 6% generally allowed in mountainous areas and hilly urban areas with exceptions for up to 7% grades on mountainous roads with speed limits below 60 mph.

The steepest roads in the world according to the Guinness Book of World Records are [Baldwin Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_Street) in Dunedin, New Zealand, [Ffordd Pen Llech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ffordd_Pen_Llech) in Harlech, Wales[^o17] and [Canton Avenue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Canton_Avenue) in Pittsburgh, Pennsylvania.[^o18] The Guinness World Record once again lists [Baldwin Street](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baldwin_Street) as the steepest street in the world, with a 34.8% grade (1 in 2.87) after a successful appeal[^o19] against the ruling that handed the title, briefly, to [Ffordd Pen Llech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ffordd_Pen_Llech).

A number of streets elsewhere have steeper grades than those listed in the Guinness Book. Drawing on the U.S. National Elevation Dataset, [7x7 (magazine)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/7x7_%28magazine%29) identified ten blocks of public streets in San Francisco open to vehicular traffic in the city with grades over 30 percent. The steepest, at 41 percent, is the block of Bradford Street above Tompkins Avenue in the [Bernal Heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bernal_Heights) neighborhood.[^o20] The [San Francisco Municipal Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Francisco_Municipal_Railway) operates bus service among [the city's hills](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_hills_in_San_Francisco). The steepest grade for bus operations is 23.1% by the *[67 Bernal Heights](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_San_Francisco_Municipal_Railway_lines)* on Alabama Street between Ripley and Esmeralda Streets.[^o28]

Likewise, the Pittsburgh Department of Engineering and Construction recorded a grade of 37% (20°) for Canton Avenue.[^o21] The street has formed part of a bicycle race since 1983.[^o22]

## Environmental design {#Environmental_design}

Grade, pitch, and slope are important components in [landscape design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape_design), [garden design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Garden_design), [landscape architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Landscape_architecture), and [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) for engineering and aesthetic design factors. Drainage, slope stability, circulation of people and vehicles, complying with building codes, and design integration are all aspects of slope considerations in [environmental design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Environmental_design).

## Railways {#Railways}

*Image: Track Grade indicator 150-88.JPG.* Grade indicator near [Bellville, Western Cape](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bellville,_Western_Cape), [South Africa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/South_Africa), showing 1:150 and 1:88 grades

[Ruling gradients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruling_gradient) limit the load that a [locomotive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locomotive) can haul, including the weight of the locomotive itself. Pulling a heavily loaded train at 20 km/h may require ten times the force climbing a 1% slope than on level track.

Early railways in the United Kingdom were laid out with very gentle gradients, such as 0.07575% (1 in 1320) and 0.1515% (1 in 660) on the [Great Western main line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Western_main_line), nicknamed Brunel's Billiard Table, because the early locomotives (and their brakes) were feeble. Steep gradients were concentrated in short sections of lines where it was convenient to employ [assistant engines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bank_engine) or [cable haulage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cable_railway), such as the \1.2 km section from [Euston](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euston_railway_station) to [Camden Town](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camden_Town).

Extremely steep gradients require mechanical assistance. Cable systems are used in cases like the Scenic Railway at [Katoomba Scenic World](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Katoomba_Scenic_World) in Australia, which reaches a maximum grade of 122% (52°) and is claimed to be the world's steepest passenger-carrying [funicular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Funicular).[^o25] For somewhat gentler inclines, [rack railways](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rack_railways) are employed, such as the [Pilatus Railway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pilatus_Railway) in Switzerland, which has a maximum grade of 48% (26°) and is considered the steepest rack railway.[^o26]

Gradients can be expressed as an angle, as feet per mile, feet per [chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chain_%28unit%29), 1 in $n$, $x$% or $y$ per mille. Since designers like round figures, the method of expression can affect the gradients selected.

### Compensation for curvature {#Compensation_for_curvature}

Gradients on sharp curves are effectively a bit steeper than the same gradient on straight track, so to compensate for this and make the [ruling grade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ruling_grade) uniform throughout, the gradient on those sharp curves should be reduced slightly.

### Continuous brakes {#Continuous_brakes}

In the era before they were provided with [continuous brakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_brake#Continuous_brakes), whether [air brakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Railway_air_brake) or [vacuum brakes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vacuum_brake), steep gradients made it extremely difficult for trains to stop safely. In those days, for example, an [inspector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Her_Majesty's_Railway_Inspectorate) insisted that [Rudgwick railway station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudgwick_railway_station) in [West Sussex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/West_Sussex) be regraded. He would not allow it to open until the gradient through the platform was eased from 1 in 80 to 1 in 130.
