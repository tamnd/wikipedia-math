# Hypotenuse

*Image: Hypotenuse.svg.* A right-angled triangle and its hypotenuse

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hypotenuse"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **hypotenuse** is the side of a [right triangle](wikipedia://en/39420) that is opposite to the [right angle](wikipedia://en/76956).[^o2] It is always the longest side of the triangle. The other two sides of a right triangle are called [legs](wikipedia://en/2568071) or catheti.[^o3][^o4]

:::

The [length](wikipedia://en/18542) of the hypotenuse can be found using the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034), which states that the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) of the length of the hypotenuse equals the sum of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides. As an algebraic formula, this can be written as $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$, where $a$ is the length of one leg, $b$ is the length of the other leg, and $c$ is the length of the hypotenuse.[^o5] For example, if the two legs of a right triangle have lengths 3 and 4, respectively, then the hypotenuse has length 5, because $\textstyle 3^2 + 4^2 = 25 = 5^2$.[^o3]

## Etymology {#Etymology}

The English word *hypotenuse* is derived from the ὑποτείνουσα. It refers to how the hypotenuse "stretches under" the right angle.[^o7][^o8]

It was [loaned](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loanword) into [Latin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Late_Latin) as hypotenusa and later into [French](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/French_language) as hypoténuse.[^o9] It first appeared in English in the 1570s.[^o7]

The equivalent Latin verb, la, is still used in English in a more general sense to refer to any line or curve stretched across an angle.

### Pythagorean theorem {#Pythagorean_theorem}

*Image: Pythagorean.svg.* An illustration of the Pythagorean theorem

The [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) states that the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) of the length of the hypotenuse ($c$) is equal to the [sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sum_%28mathematics%29) of the squares of the lengths of the other two sides ($a$ and $b$). This can be written as the [equation](wikipedia://en/9284) $a^2 + b^2 = c^2$. To calculate the length of the hypotenuse, the equation can be rearranged to solve for $c$ by taking the [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) of both sides, yielding $c = \sqrt{a^2 + b^2}$. This is sometimes known as [Pythagorean addition](wikipedia://en/14597289).[^o14] For example, if the two legs of a right triangle have lengths 3 and 4, respectively, then the hypotenuse has length 5, because $\sqrt{3^2 + 4^2} = \sqrt{25} = 5$.[^o3]

The Pythagorean theorem is a special case of the [law of cosines](wikipedia://en/19480890), a more general theorem relating the lengths of sides in any triangle. It states that $a^2+b^2-2ab\cos{\theta}=c^2$ where $\theta$ is the angle between sides $a$ and $b$.[^o15] When $\theta$ is $\tfrac{\pi}{2}$ radians or 90°, then $\cos{\theta} = 0$ and the formula reduces to the usual Pythagorean theorem.

:::

### Sine and cosine {#Sine_and_cosine}

*Image: Trigono sine en2.svg.* For the angle $\alpha$, the sine function gives the ratio of the length of the opposite side to the length of the hypotenuse

The [sine and cosine](wikipedia://en/5010838) functions (sin and cos) describe the relationship of the hypotenuse to the lengths and angles of the other two sides. These, along with [tangent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tangent_function) (tan), are the most common [trigonometric functions](wikipedia://en/30367).[^o3]

The trig functions are usually described in terms of one of the [acute angles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Acute_angles) of a right triangle ($\angle A$), the [leg](wikipedia://en/2568071) that is adjacent to that angle, and the leg that is opposite that angle. The sine of the acute angle gives the ratio of the opposite leg to the hypotenuse, while the cosine of the angle gives the ratio of the adjacent leg to the hypotenuse. This can be written as the [equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equations):

$$\sin(\angle A) = \frac{\text{opposite}}{\text{hypotenuse}}, \qquad \cos(\angle A) = \frac{\text{adjacent}}{\text{hypotenuse}}.$$

The definitions of the sine, cosine and tangent functions are often remembered using the [mnemonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mnemonic) "[SOH-CAH-TOA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SOH-CAH-TOA)", where "SOH" stands for "sine = opposite / hypotenuse", "CAH" stands for "cosine = adjacent / hypotenuse", and "TOA" stands for "tangent = opposite / adjacent".[^o3]

:::

## In computer programming {#In_computer_programming}

Many [programming languages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Programming_languages) support a version of the [ISO C](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ISO_C) standard function `hypot(x, y)`, which calculates the hypotenuse of a right triangle using the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034).[^o18][^o19][^o20] The function is designed not to fail where the straightforward calculation might [overflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_overflow) or [underflow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arithmetic_underflow). It can often be more accurate and slower than the straightforward calculation.[^o14]

Some languages have extended the definition to higher dimensions. For example, [C++17](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C++17) supports `std::hypot(x, y, z)` which gives the length of the diagonal of a [rectangular cuboid](wikipedia://en/38848329) with edge lengths *x*, *y*, and *z*.[^o21] [Python](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Python_language) 3.8 includes a version of `math.hypot` which can handle an arbitrary number of arguments.[^o22]
