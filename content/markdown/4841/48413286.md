# Bregman–Minc inequality

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Bregman–Minc inequality"}

In [discrete mathematics](wikipedia://en/8492), the **Bregman–Minc inequality**, or **Bregman's theorem**, allows one to estimate the [permanent](wikipedia://en/215889) of a [binary matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_matrix) via its row or column sums. The inequality was conjectured in 1963 by [Henryk Minc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henryk_Minc) and first proved in 1973 by [Lev M. Bregman](wikipedia://en/39180704).[^o10][^o9] Further [entropy](wikipedia://en/15445)-based proofs have been given by [Alexander Schrijver](wikipedia://en/35280549) and [Jaikumar Radhakrishnan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jaikumar_Radhakrishnan).[^o12][^o11] The Bregman–Minc inequality is used, for example, in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401) to obtain upper bounds for the number of [perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/44031) in a [bipartite graph](wikipedia://en/244431).

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The permanent of a square binary matrix $A = (a_{ij})$ of size $n$ with row sums $r_i = a_{i1} + \cdots + a_{in}$ for $i=1, \ldots , n$ can be estimated by

$$\operatorname{per}A \leq \prod_{i=1}^n (r_i!)^{1/r_i}.$$

The permanent is therefore bounded by the product of the [geometric means](wikipedia://en/13046) of the numbers from $1$ to $r_i$ for $i=1, \ldots , n$. Equality holds if the matrix is a [block diagonal matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Block_diagonal_matrix) consisting of [matrices of ones](wikipedia://en/690246) or results from row and/or column permutations of such a block diagonal matrix. Since the permanent is invariant under [transposition](wikipedia://en/173844), the inequality also holds for the column sums of the matrix accordingly.[^o13][^o14]

:::

::: {#o5 .application cue=heading name="Application"}

## Application {#Application}

*Image: Perfect matching qtl1.svg.* A binary matrix and the corresponding bipartite graph with a possible perfect matching marked in red. According to the Bregman–Minc inequality, there are at most 18 perfect matchings in this graph.

There is a one-to-one correspondence between a square binary matrix $A$ of size $n$ and a [simple](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_graph) bipartite graph $G = (V \, \dot\cup \, W, E)$ with equal-sized [partitions](wikipedia://en/11973947) $V = \{ v_1, \ldots , v_n \}$ and $W = \{ w_1, \ldots , w_n \}$ by taking

$$a_{ij} = 1 \Leftrightarrow \{ v_i, w_j \} \in E.$$

This way, each nonzero entry of the matrix $A$ defines an [edge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edge_%28graph_theory%29) in the graph $G$ and vice versa. A perfect matching in $G$ is a selection of $n$ edges, such that each [vertex](wikipedia://en/638899) of the graph is an endpoint of one of these edges. Each nonzero summand of the permanent of $A$ satisfying

$$a_{1\sigma(1)} \cdots a_{n\sigma(n)} = 1$$

corresponds to a perfect matching $\{ \{ v_1, w_{\sigma(1)} \}, \ldots , \{ v_n, w_{\sigma(n)} \} \}$ of $G$. Therefore, if $\mathcal{M}(G)$ denotes the set of perfect matchings of $G$,

$$| \mathcal{M}(G) | = \operatorname{per}A$$

holds. The Bregman–Minc inequality now yields the estimate

$$| \mathcal{M}(G) | \leq \prod_{i=1}^n (d(v_i)!)^{1/d(v_i)},$$

where $d(v_i)$ is the [degree](wikipedia://en/1374448) of the vertex $v_i$. Due to symmetry, the corresponding estimate also holds for $d(w_i)$ instead of $d(v_i)$. The number of possible perfect matchings in a bipartite graph with equal-sized partitions can therefore be estimated via the degrees of the vertices of any of the two partitions.[^o15]

:::

## Related statements {#Related_statements}

Using the [inequality of arithmetic and geometric means](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inequality_of_arithmetic_and_geometric_means), the Bregman–Minc inequality directly implies the weaker estimate

$$\operatorname{per}A \leq \prod_{i=1}^n \frac{r_i+1}{2},$$

which was proven by Henryk Minc already in 1963. Another direct consequence of the Bregman–Minc inequality is a proof of the following conjecture of [Herbert Ryser](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Herbert_Ryser) from 1960. Let $k$ by a divisor of $n$ and let $\Lambda_{kn}$ denote the set of square binary matrices of size $n$ with row and column sums equal to $k$, then

$$\max_{A \in \Lambda_{kn}} \operatorname{per}A = (k!)^{n/k}.$$

The maximum is thereby attained for a block diagonal matrix whose diagonal blocks are square matrices of ones of size $k$. A corresponding statement for the case that $k$ is not a divisor of $n$ is an open mathematical problem.[^o13][^o14]
