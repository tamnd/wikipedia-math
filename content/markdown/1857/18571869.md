# Petrie polygon

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Petrie polygon"}

In [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a **Petrie polygon** for a [regular polytope](wikipedia://en/518693) of $n$ dimensions is a [skew polygon](wikipedia://en/6545461) in which every $n - 1$ consecutive [sides](wikipedia://en/12158034) (but no $n$) belongs to one of the [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647). The **Petrie polygon** of a [regular polygon](wikipedia://en/333306) is the regular polygon itself; that of a [regular polyhedron](wikipedia://en/333299) is a skew polygon such that every two consecutive sides (but no three) belongs to one of the [faces](wikipedia://en/149926).[^o2] Petrie polygons are named for mathematician [John Flinders Petrie](wikipedia://en/23915649).

:::

For every regular polytope there exists an [orthogonal projection](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection) onto a plane such that one Petrie polygon becomes a regular polygon with the remainder of the projection interior to it. The plane in question is the [Coxeter plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_plane) of the [symmetry group](wikipedia://en/28186) of the polygon, and the number of sides, $h$, is the [Coxeter number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coxeter_number) of the [Coxeter group](wikipedia://en/297004). These polygons and projected graphs are useful in visualizing symmetric structure of the higher-dimensional regular polytopes.

Petrie polygons can be defined more generally for any [embedded graph](wikipedia://en/8149170). They form the faces of another embedding of the same graph, usually on a different surface, called the [Petrie dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petrie_dual).[^o3]

## History {#History}

John Flinders Petrie (1907–1972) was the son of [Egyptologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptologist) [Hilda](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hilda_Petrie) and [Flinders Petrie](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sir_W._M._Flinders_Petrie). He was born in 1907 and as a schoolboy showed remarkable promise of mathematical ability. In periods of intense concentration he could answer questions about complicated four-dimensional objects by *visualizing* them.

He first noted the importance of the regular skew polygons which appear on the surface of regular polyhedra and higher polytopes. Coxeter explained in 1937 how he and Petrie began to expand the classical subject of regular polyhedra:

One day in 1926, J. F. Petrie told me with much excitement that he had discovered two new regular polyhedral; infinite but free of false vertices. When my incredulity had begun to subside, he described them to me: one consisting of squares, six at each vertex, and one consisting of hexagons, four at each vertex.[^o5]

In 1938 Petrie collaborated with Coxeter, [Patrick du Val](wikipedia://en/10637467), and H. T. Flather to produce *[The Fifty-Nine Icosahedra](wikipedia://en/13181245)* for publication.[^o6] Realizing the geometric facility of the skew polygons used by Petrie, Coxeter named them after his friend when he wrote *[Regular Polytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_Polytopes)*.

The idea of Petrie polygons was later extended to [semiregular polytopes](wikipedia://en/11684875).

## The Petrie polygon of regular polychora (4-polytopes) {#The_Petrie_polygon_of_regular_polychora_(4-polytopes)}

The Petrie polygon for the regular polychora {*p*, *q* ,*r*} can also be determined, such that every three consecutive sides (but no four) belong to one of the polychoron's cells. As the surface of a 4-polytope is a 3-dimensional space (the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792)), the Petrie polygon of a regular 4-polytope is a 3-dimensional helix in this surface.

## The Petrie polygon projections of regular and uniform polytopes {#The_Petrie_polygon_projections_of_regular_and_uniform_polytopes}

The Petrie polygon projections are useful for the visualization of polytopes of dimension four and higher.

### Hypercubes {#Hypercubes}

A [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783) of dimension *n* has a Petrie polygon of size 2*n*, which is also the number of its [facets](wikipedia://en/7186647). So each of the (*n* − 1)-cubes forming its [surface](wikipedia://en/525101) has *n* − 1 sides of the Petrie polygon among its edges.
