# Gould's sequence

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gould's sequence"}

*Image: Pascal triangle small.png.* Pascal's triangle, rows 0 through 7. The number of odd integers in row *i* is the *i*-th number in Gould's sequence.

*Image: Gould sawtooth.svg.* The [self-similar](wikipedia://en/28782) sawtooth shape of Gould's sequence

**Gould's sequence** is an [integer sequence](wikipedia://en/51421) named after [Henry W. Gould](wikipedia://en/38149527) that counts how many [odd numbers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Odd_number) are in each row of [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497). It consists only of [powers of two](wikipedia://en/376948), and begins:[^o3]

:::

1, 2, 2, 4, 2, 4, 4, 8, 2, 4, 4, 8, 4, 8, 8, 16, 2, 4, ...

For instance, the sixth number in the sequence is 4, because there are four odd numbers in the sixth row of Pascal's triangle (the four bold numbers in the sequence **1**, **5**, 10, 10, **5**, **1**). Gould's sequence is also a [fractal sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractal_sequence).

## Additional interpretations {#Additional_interpretations}

The $n$th value in the sequence (starting from $n = 0$) gives the highest power of 2 that divides the [central binomial coefficient](wikipedia://en/2128244) $\tbinom{2n}{n}$, and it gives the numerator of $2^n/n!$ (expressed as a fraction in lowest terms).[^o2]

*Image: PascalungeradeDreiecke.svg.* [Sierpinski triangle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sierpinski_triangle) generated by [Rule 90](wikipedia://en/16572515), or by marking the positions of odd numbers in [Pascal's triangle](wikipedia://en/49497). Gould's sequence counts the number of live cells in each row of this pattern.

Gould's sequence also gives the number of live cells in the $n$th generation of the [Rule 90](wikipedia://en/16572515) [cellular automaton](wikipedia://en/54342) starting from a single live cell.[^o2][^o5] It has a characteristic growing [sawtooth](wikipedia://en/52147) shape that can be used to recognize physical processes that behave similarly to Rule 90.[^o6]

## Related sequences {#Related_sequences}

The [binary logarithms](wikipedia://en/249992) (exponents in the powers of two) of Gould's sequence themselves form an integer sequence,

0, 1, 1, 2, 1, 2, 2, 3, 1, 2, 2, 3, 2, 3, 3, 4, ...

in which the $n$th value gives the [number of nonzero bits](wikipedia://en/1127884) in the [binary representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_representation) of the number $n$, sometimes written in [mathematical notation](wikipedia://en/277184) as $\#_1(n)$.[^o2][^o3] Equivalently, the $n$th value in Gould's sequence is

$$2^{\#_1(n)}.$$

Taking the sequence of exponents modulo two gives the [Thue–Morse sequence](wikipedia://en/491903).[^o8]

The [partial sums](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_sum) of Gould's sequence,

0, 1, 3, 5, 9, 11, 15, 19, 27, 29, 33, 37, 45, ...

count all odd numbers in the first $n$ rows of Pascal's triangle. These numbers grow proportionally to $n^{\log_2 3}\approx n^{1.585}$, but with a constant of proportionality that oscillates between 0.812556... and 1, periodically as a function of $\log n$.[^o9][^o10]

## Recursive construction and self-similarity {#Recursive_construction_and_self-similarity}

The first $2^{i}$ values in Gould's sequence may be constructed by recursively constructing the first $2^{i - 1}$ values, and then concatenating the doubles of the first $2^{i - 1}$ values. For instance, concatenating the first four values 1, 2, 2, 4 with their doubles 2, 4, 4, 8 produces the first eight values. Because of this doubling construction, the first occurrence of each power of two $2^{i}$ in this sequence is at position $2^{i} - 1$.[^o2]

Gould's sequence, the sequence of its exponents, and the Thue–Morse sequence are all [self-similar](wikipedia://en/28782): they have the property that the [subsequence](wikipedia://en/236098) of values at even positions in the whole sequence equals the original sequence, a property they also share with some other sequences such as [Stern's diatomic sequence](wikipedia://en/22672164).[^o5][^o12][^o13] In Gould's sequence, the values at odd positions are double their predecessors, while in the sequence of exponents, the values at odd positions are one plus their predecessors.

## History {#History}

The sequence is named after [Henry W. Gould](wikipedia://en/38149527), who studied it in the early 1960s. However, the fact that these numbers are powers of two, with the exponent of the $n$th number equal to the number of ones in the [binary representation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_representation) of $n$, was already known to [J. W. L. Glaisher](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Whitbread_Lee_Glaisher) in 1899.[^o15][^o16]

Proving that the numbers in Gould's sequence are powers of two was given as a problem in the 1956 [William Lowell Putnam Mathematical Competition](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lowell_Putnam_Mathematical_Competition).[^o17]
