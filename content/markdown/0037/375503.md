# Frieze group

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frieze group"}

*Image: Meander alagrek.svg.* Examples of frieze patterns

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **frieze** or **frieze pattern** is a two-dimensional design that repeats in one direction. The term is derived from [friezes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frieze) in [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture) and [decorative arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decorative_arts), where such repeating patterns are often used. Frieze patterns can be classified into seven types according to their symmetries. The set of [symmetries](wikipedia://en/53741) of a frieze pattern is called a **frieze group**.

:::

Frieze groups are two-dimensional [line groups](wikipedia://en/32186226), having repetition in only one direction. They are related to the more complex [wallpaper groups](wikipedia://en/386214), which classify patterns that are repetitive in two directions, and [crystallographic groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crystallographic_group), which classify patterns that are repetitive in three directions.

## History {#History}

Mathematical friezes have their origins in the formulas for the *[pentagramma mirificum](wikipedia://en/59503919)* found by [Carl Friedrich Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125) in 1843 and [Harold Scott MacDonald Coxeter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harold_Scott_MacDonald_Coxeter)'s study of symmetries in the mid-20th century.[^o4][^o3] Frieze patterns were formally introduced by Coxeter in 1971.[^o4] In the 1970s to 1980s, frieze patterns were the subject of mathematical works by Coxeter, [John Horton Conway](wikipedia://en/15807), [Geoffrey Colin Shephard](wikipedia://en/33354532), [Pierre Gabriel](wikipedia://en/30716698), and others in [combinatorics](wikipedia://en/5170) and the theory of [quiver representations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quiver_representation).[^o4]

In the 21st century, new relations were found between frieze patterns and [cluster algebras](wikipedia://en/27038423), [Grassmannians](wikipedia://en/373810), linear [difference equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Difference_equation), [moduli spaces](wikipedia://en/361609) of points in [projective spaces](wikipedia://en/242135), and transfer matrices in [solvable lattice models](wikipedia://en/291472).[^o4]

## Definition {#Definition}

*The seven frieze groups*

::: {#o7 .table cue=table name="The seven frieze groups"}

| p1: T (translation only, in the horizontal direction) p1m1: TV (translation and vertical line reflection) p11m: THG (translation, horizontal line reflection, and glide reflection) p11g: TG (translation and glide reflection) p2: TR (translation and 180° rotation) p2mg: TRVG (translation, 180° rotation, vertical line reflection, and glide reflection) p2mm: TRHVG (translation, 180° rotation, horizontal line reflection, vertical line reflection, and glide reflection) |
|---|

:::

Formally, a frieze group is a class of infinite discrete [symmetry groups](wikipedia://en/28186) of patterns on a strip (infinitely wide rectangle), hence a class of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447) of [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777) of the plane, or of a strip. A symmetry group of a frieze group necessarily contains [translations](wikipedia://en/204682) and may contain [glide reflections](wikipedia://en/412984), [reflections](wikipedia://en/295829) along the long axis of the strip, reflections along the narrow axis of the strip, and 180° [rotations](wikipedia://en/39789). There are seven frieze groups, listed in the summary table. Many authors present the frieze groups in a different order.[^o8][^o9]

:::

## Symmetry groups and generators {#Symmetry_groups_and_generators}

The actual symmetry groups within a frieze group are characterized by the smallest translation distance, and, for the frieze groups with vertical line reflection or 180° rotation (groups 2, 5, 6, and 7), by a shift parameter locating the reflection axis or point of rotation. In the case of symmetry groups in the plane, additional parameters are the direction of the translation vector, and, for the frieze groups with horizontal line reflection, glide reflection, or 180° rotation (groups 3–7), the position of the reflection axis or rotation point in the direction perpendicular to the translation vector. Thus there are two [degrees of freedom](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Degrees_of_freedom_%28physics_and_chemistry%29) for group 1, three for groups 2, 3, and 4, and four for groups 5, 6, and 7.

For two of the seven frieze groups (groups 1 and 4) the symmetry groups are [singly generated](wikipedia://en/99945), for four (groups 2, 3, 5, and 6) they have a pair of generators, and for group 7 the symmetry groups require three generators. A symmetry group in frieze group 1, 2, 3, or 5 is a [subgroup](wikipedia://en/28011) of a symmetry group in the last frieze group with the same translational distance. A symmetry group in frieze group 4 or 6 is a subgroup of a symmetry group in the last frieze group with *half* the translational distance. This last frieze group contains the symmetry groups of the simplest periodic patterns in the strip (or the plane), a row of dots. Any transformation of the plane leaving this pattern invariant can be decomposed into a translation, (*x*, *y*) ↦ (*n* + *x*, *y*), optionally followed by a reflection in either the horizontal axis, (*x*, *y*) ↦ (*x*, −*y*), or the vertical axis, (*x*, *y*) ↦ (−*x*, *y*), provided that this axis is chosen through or midway between two dots, or a rotation by 180°, (*x*, *y*) ↦ (−*x*, −*y*) (ditto). Therefore, in a way, this frieze group contains the "largest" symmetry groups, which consist of all such transformations.

The inclusion of the *discrete* condition is to exclude the group containing all translations, and groups containing arbitrarily small translations (e.g. the group of horizontal translations by rational distances). Even apart from scaling and shifting, there are infinitely many cases, e.g. by considering rational numbers of which the denominators are powers of a given prime number.

The inclusion of the *infinite* condition is to exclude groups that have no translations:

- the group with the identity only (isomorphic to C<sub>1</sub>, the [trivial group](wikipedia://en/193059) of order 1).
- the group consisting of the identity and reflection in the horizontal axis (isomorphic to C<sub>2</sub>, the [cyclic group](wikipedia://en/52327) of order 2).
- the groups each consisting of the identity and reflection in a vertical axis (ditto)
- the groups each consisting of the identity and 180° rotation about a point on the horizontal axis (ditto)
- the groups each consisting of the identity, reflection in a vertical axis, reflection in the horizontal axis, and 180° rotation about the point of intersection (isomorphic to the [Klein four-group](wikipedia://en/41928))

## Descriptions of the seven frieze groups {#Descriptions_of_the_seven_frieze_groups}

There are seven distinct subgroups (up to scaling and shifting of patterns) in the discrete frieze group (called TRHVG) generated by a translation, reflection (along the same axis) and a 180° rotation. Each of these subgroups is the symmetry group of a frieze pattern, and sample patterns are shown in the table further down. The seven different groups correspond to [the 7 infinite series of axial point groups in three dimensions](wikipedia://en/2694525#The_seven_infinite_series_of_axial_groups), but with *n* equal, as it were, to infinity.[^o12] (Think of a dome with a frieze going around its base. It will belong to one of the point groups, with *n* being the number of repeats as one goes all the way around the dome. The frieze groups are similar, but translations go on infinitely without coming back to the starting point.)

It is easy to see why there are seven groups. Regarding reflexions and glides on the axis of the frieze, there are three possibilities: there may be glides (G), glides and a reflexion (H), or neither. Regarding reflexions on "vertical" lines (V), there are two possibilities: having them or not. This gives six groups:

::: {#o13 .table cue=table}

|  | No glide | Glides only | Glides and reflexion around horizontal axis |
|---|---|---|---|
| No reflexion around vertical lines | T (C${}_{\infty}$) | TG (S${}_{\infty}$) | THG (C${}_{\infty h}$) |
| Presence of reflexions around vertical lines | TV (C${}_{\infty v}$) | TRVG (D${}_{\infty d}$) | TRHVG (D${}_{\infty h}$) |

:::

When both V and glides are present, then there are two-fold rotations R. But R can exist by itself, giving the seventh group, TR (D${}_{\infty}$).

The groups are identified in the table below using [Hermann–Mauguin notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann–Mauguin_notation), [Coxeter notation](wikipedia://en/32088502), [Schönflies notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schönflies_notation), [orbifold notation](wikipedia://en/2915678), nicknames created by mathematician [John H. Conway](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_H._Conway), and finally a description in terms of translation, reflections and rotations.

Of the seven frieze groups, there are only four up to [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/12397). Two are singly generated and isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}$; four of them are doubly generated, among which one is [abelian](wikipedia://en/2974) and three are nonabelian and isomorphic to $D_\infty$, the [infinite dihedral group](wikipedia://en/4296050); and one of them has three generators.[^o14]

### Lattice types: Oblique and rectangular {#Lattice_types:_Oblique_and_rectangular}

The groups can be classified by their type of two-dimensional grid or lattice.[^o16] The lattice being oblique means that the second direction *need not be orthogonal* to the direction of repeat.

::: {#o17 .table cue=table}

| Lattice type | Groups |
|---|---|
| Oblique | p1, p2 |
| Rectangular | p1m1, p11m, p11g, p2mm, p2mg |

:::

## Web demo and software {#Web_demo_and_software}

There exist software graphic tools that create 2D patterns using frieze groups. Usually, the entire pattern is updated automatically in response to edits of the original strip.

- [EscherSketch](https://eschersket.ch/) A free online program for drawing, saving, and exporting tessellations. Supports all wallpaper groups.
- [Kali](http://www.geom.uiuc.edu/java/Kali/welcome.html), a [free and open source software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Free_and_open_source_software) application for wallpaper, frieze and other patterns.
- [Kali](http://www.geometrygames.org/Kali/index.html) (2020-11-21). <https://web.archive.org/web/20201121143626/http://www.geometrygames.org/Kali/index.html>, free downloadable Kali for Windows and Mac Classic.
- [Tess](http://www.peda.com/tess/), a [nagware](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nagware) tessellation program for multiple platforms, supports all wallpaper, frieze, and rosette groups, as well as Heesch tilings.
- [FriezingWorkz](http://apronyms.com/software/friezingworkz.html), a freeware Hypercard stack for the Classic Mac platform that supports all frieze groups.
