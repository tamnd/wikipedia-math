# Frequency domain

*Image: Fourier transform time and frequency domains (small).gif.* The [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) converts the function's time-domain representation, shown in red, to the function's frequency-domain representation, shown in blue. The component frequencies, spread across the frequency spectrum, are represented as peaks in the frequency domain.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Frequency domain"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), [electronics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronics), [control systems engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_systems_engineering), and [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the **frequency domain** refers to the analysis of [mathematical functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_function) or [signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_%28information_theory%29) with respect to [frequency](wikipedia://en/10779) (and possibly phase), rather than time, as in [time series](wikipedia://en/406624).[^o2] While a [time-domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time-domain) graph shows how a signal changes over time, a frequency-domain graph shows how the signal is distributed within different frequency bands over a range of frequencies. A complex valued frequency-domain representation consists of both the magnitude and the [phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29) of a set of [sinusoids](wikipedia://en/324749) (or other basis waveforms) at the frequency components of the signal. Although it is common to refer to the magnitude portion (the real valued frequency-domain) as the frequency response of a signal, the phase portion is required to uniquely define the signal.

:::

A given function or signal can be converted between the time and frequency domains with a pair of mathematical [operators](wikipedia://en/22213) called [transforms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transform_%28mathematics%29). An example is the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247), which converts a time function into a complex valued sum or integral of [sine waves](wikipedia://en/324749) of different frequencies, with amplitudes and phases, each of which represents a frequency component. The "[spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum)" of frequency components is the frequency-domain representation of the signal. The [inverse Fourier transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_Fourier_transform) converts the frequency-domain function back to the time-domain function. A [spectrum analyzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_analyzer) is a tool commonly used to visualize [electronic signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signal_%28electrical_engineering%29) in the frequency domain.

A frequency-domain representation may describe either a static function or a particular time period of a dynamic function (signal or system). The frequency transform of a dynamic function is performed over a finite time period of that function and assumes the function repeats infinitely outside of that time period. Some specialized signal processing techniques for dynamic functions use transforms that result in a joint [time–frequency domain](wikipedia://en/857710), with the [instantaneous frequency](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instantaneous_frequency) response being a key link between the time domain and the frequency domain.

## Advantages {#Advantages}

One of the main reasons for using a frequency-domain representation of a problem is to simplify the mathematical analysis. For mathematical systems governed by [linear differential equations](wikipedia://en/379868), a very important class of systems with many real-world applications, converting the description of the system from the time domain to a frequency domain converts the [differential equations](wikipedia://en/1424309) to [algebraic equations](wikipedia://en/404001), which are much easier to solve.

In addition, looking at a system from the point of view of frequency can often give an intuitive understanding of the qualitative behavior of the system, and a revealing scientific nomenclature has grown up to describe it, characterizing the behavior of physical systems to time varying inputs using terms such as [bandwidth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bandwidth_%28signal_processing%29), [frequency response](wikipedia://en/302033), [gain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gain_%28electronics%29), [phase shift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_%28waves%29), [resonant frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonant_frequency), [time constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Time_constant), [resonance width](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Resonance_width), [damping factor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Damping_factor), [Q factor](wikipedia://en/229160), [harmonics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Harmonic), [spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum), [power spectral density](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Power_spectral_density), [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue), [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29), and [zeros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28complex_analysis%29).

An example of a field in which frequency-domain analysis gives a better understanding than time domain is [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music); the theory of operation of musical instruments and the [musical notation](wikipedia://en/20201) used to record and discuss pieces of music is implicitly based on the breaking down of complex sounds into their separate component frequencies ([musical notes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Musical_note)).

## Magnitude and phase {#Magnitude_and_phase}

In using the [Laplace](wikipedia://en/18610), [Z-](wikipedia://en/171589), or Fourier transforms, a signal is described by a [complex function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_function) of frequency: the component of the signal at any given frequency is given by a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826). The [modulus](wikipedia://en/5826#Modulus_and_argument) of the number is the [amplitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amplitude) of that component, and the [argument](wikipedia://en/5826#Modulus_and_argument) is the relative phase of the wave. For example, using the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247), a [sound wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sound_wave), such as human speech, can be broken down into its component tones of different frequencies, each represented by a sine wave of a different amplitude and phase. The response of a system, as a function of frequency, can also be described by a complex function. In many applications, phase information is not important. By discarding the phase information, it is possible to simplify the information in a frequency-domain representation to generate a [frequency spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frequency_spectrum) or [spectral density](wikipedia://en/202672). A [spectrum analyzer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spectrum_analyzer) is a device that displays the spectrum, while the time-domain signal can be seen on an [oscilloscope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscilloscope).

## Types {#Types}

Although "*the*" frequency domain is spoken of in the singular, there are a number of different mathematical transforms which are used to analyze time-domain functions and are referred to as "frequency domain" methods. These are the most common transforms, and the fields in which they are used:

- [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038) – periodic signals, [oscillating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oscillation) systems.
- [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247) – aperiodic signals, transients.
- [Laplace transform](wikipedia://en/18610) – [electronic circuits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_circuits) and [control systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Control_system).
- [Z transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Z_transform) – [discrete-time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time) signals, [digital signal processing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_signal_processing).
- [Wavelet transform](wikipedia://en/3352292) — image analysis, [data compression](wikipedia://en/8013).

More generally, one can speak of the **transform domain** with respect to any transform. The above transforms can be interpreted as capturing some form of frequency, and hence the transform domain is referred to as a frequency domain.

## Discrete frequency domain {#Discrete_frequency_domain}

A **discrete frequency domain** is a frequency domain that is [discrete](wikipedia://en/56061) rather than [continuous](wikipedia://en/24758520). For example, the [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811) maps a function having a [discrete time domain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete_time) into one having a discrete frequency domain. The [discrete-time Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/1216914), on the other hand, maps functions with discrete time ([discrete-time signals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time_signal)) to functions that have a continuous frequency domain.[^o7][^o8]

A [periodic signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_signal) has energy only at a base frequency and its harmonics; thus it can be analyzed using a discrete frequency domain. A [discrete-time signal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time_signal) gives rise to a periodic frequency spectrum. In a situation where both these conditions occur, a signal which is discrete and periodic results in a frequency spectrum which is also discrete and periodic; this is the usual context for a [discrete Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/8811).

## History of term {#History_of_term}

The use of the terms "frequency domain" and "[time domain](wikipedia://en/173419)" arose in communication engineering in the 1950s and early 1960s, with "frequency domain" appearing in 1953.[^o10] See [time domain: origin of term](wikipedia://en/173419#Origin_of_term) for details.[^o11]
