# Pathological (mathematics)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Pathological"}

*Image: WeierstrassFunction.svg.* The [Weierstrass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_function) is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) everywhere but [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) nowhere.

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), when a mathematical phenomenon runs counter to some [intuition](wikipedia://en/154170), then the phenomenon is sometimes called **pathological**. On the other hand, if a phenomenon does not run counter to intuition, it is sometimes called **well-behaved** or **nice**. These terms are sometimes useful in mathematical research and teaching, but there is no strict mathematical definition of pathological or well-behaved.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Pathological function"}

## In analysis {#In_analysis}

A classic example of a pathology is the [Weierstrass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_function), a function that is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) everywhere but [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) nowhere.[^o2] The sum of a differentiable [function](wikipedia://en/185427) and the Weierstrass function is again continuous but nowhere differentiable; so there are at least as many such functions as differentiable functions. In fact, using the [Baire category theorem](wikipedia://en/55227), one can show that continuous functions are [generically](wikipedia://en/12886758) nowhere differentiable.[^o5]

Such examples were deemed pathological when they were first discovered. To quote [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740):[^o6]

Logic sometimes breeds monsters. For half a century there has been springing up a host of weird functions, which seem to strive to have as little resemblance as possible to honest functions that are of some use. No more continuity, or else continuity but no derivatives, etc. More than this, from the point of view of logic, it is these strange functions that are the most general; those that are met without being looked for no longer appear as more than a particular case, and they have only quite a little corner left them.

Formerly, when a new function was invented, it was in view of some practical end. To-day they are invented on purpose to show our ancestors' reasonings at fault, and we shall never get anything more than that out of them.

If logic were the teacher's only guide, he would have to begin with the most general, that is to say, with the most weird, functions. He would have to set the beginner to wrestle with this collection of monstrosities. If you don't do so, the logicians might say, you will only reach exactness by stages.

Since Poincaré, nowhere differentiable functions have been shown to appear in basic physical and biological processes such as [Brownian motion](wikipedia://en/4436) and in applications such as the [Black-Scholes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Black-Scholes) model in finance.

*Counterexamples in Analysis* is a whole book of such counterexamples.[^o7]

Another example of pathological function is [Du-Bois Reymond](wikipedia://en/7457861) [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122), that can't be represented as a [Fourier series](wikipedia://en/59038).[^o8]

:::

## In topology {#In_topology}

One famous counterexample in topology is the [Alexander horned sphere](wikipedia://en/685665), showing that topologically embedding the sphere *S*<sup>2</sup> in **R**<sup>3</sup> may fail to separate the space cleanly. As a counterexample, it motivated mathematicians to define the *tameness* property, which suppresses the kind of *wild* behavior exhibited by the horned sphere, [wild knot](wikipedia://en/19377159), and other similar examples.[^o10]

Like many other pathologies, the horned sphere in a sense plays on infinitely fine, recursively generated structure, which in the limit violates ordinary intuition. In this case, the topology of an ever-descending chain of interlocking loops of continuous pieces of the sphere in the limit fully reflects that of the common sphere, and one would expect the outside of it, after an embedding, to work the same. Yet it does not: it fails to be [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected).

For the underlying theory, see [Jordan–Schönflies theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan–Schönflies_theorem).

*[Counterexamples in Topology](wikipedia://en/915449)* is a whole book of such counterexamples.[^o11]

## In algebraic geometry {#In_algebraic_geometry}

In a sequence of four papers published in the *[American Journal of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Journal_of_Mathematics)* between 1961 and 1975, [David Mumford](wikipedia://en/394594) explored "pathological" behavior in algebraic geometry.[^o13][^o14][^o15][^o16] His examples fall into two types: (a) bad behavior in [characteristic p](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Characteristic_p) and (b) bad behavior in [moduli spaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_spaces).

Mumford wrote in the 1962 paper *Further Pathologies in Algebraic Geometry*:

> A non-singular characteristic *p* variety is analogous to a general non-Kähler complex manifold; in particular, a projective embedding of such a variety is not as strong as a [Kähler metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kähler_metric) on a complex manifold, and the Hodge–Lefschetz–Dolbeault theorems on [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357) break down in every possible way.[^o17]

1. In *Pathologies* I (*Pathologies of Modular Algebraic Surfaces*), Mumford (a) finds an everywhere regular differential form on a smooth projective surface that is not closed, and shows that [Hodge symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hodge_symmetry) fails for classical [Enriques surfaces](wikipedia://en/3094760) in characteristic two. This example is developed further in Mumford's third paper on classification of surfaces in characteristic *p* (written with [Enrico Bombieri](wikipedia://en/10475)). It can be explained in terms of the [Picard scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Picard_scheme) of the surface, and in particular, its failure to be a [reduced scheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reduced_scheme), a theme developed in Mumford's book *Lectures on Curves on an Algebraic Surface*. Pathologies related to p-torsion in [crystalline cohomology](wikipedia://en/10220067) were later explored by [Luc Illusie](wikipedia://en/27206965).[^o18]
1. In *Pathologies* II (*Further Pathologies in Algebraic Geometry*), Mumford (a) gives a simple example of a surface in characteristic *p* where the [geometric genus](wikipedia://en/3115807) is non-zero, but the second [Betti number](wikipedia://en/346262) is equal to the rank of the [Néron–Severi group](wikipedia://en/1873706). Further such examples arise in [Zariski surface](wikipedia://en/3096979) theory. He also conjectured that the [Kodaira vanishing theorem](wikipedia://en/2988093) is false for surfaces in characteristic *p*. Mumford also (b) finds that the [Hilbert scheme](wikipedia://en/6612581) parametrizing space curves of degree 14 and genus 24 has a multiple component. In 2006 [Ravi Vakil](wikipedia://en/5981037) in his paper "Murphy's law in algebraic geometry" showed that Hilbert schemes can be arbitrarily "bad", with unlimited numbers of components and with arbitrarily large multiplicities.[^o19]
1. In *Pathologies* III, he (a) gives an example of a [normal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_variety) surface for which Kodaira vanishing fails. The first example of a smooth surface for which Kodaira vanishing fails was given by [Michel Raynaud](wikipedia://en/4760928) in 1978.
1. In *Pathologies* IV, he (b) finds reduced and irreducible complete curves which are not specializations of non-singular curves.

## Well-behaved {#Well-behaved}

[Mathematicians](wikipedia://en/18902) (and those in related sciences) very frequently speak of whether a [mathematical](wikipedia://en/18831) object—a [function](wikipedia://en/185427), a [set](wikipedia://en/26691), a [space](wikipedia://en/5308894) of one sort or another—is **"well-behaved"**. While the term has no fixed formal definition, it generally refers to the quality of satisfying a list of prevailing conditions, which might be dependent on context, mathematical interests, fashion, and taste. To ensure that an object is "well-behaved", mathematicians introduce further axioms to narrow down the domain of study. This has the benefit of making analysis easier, but produces a [loss of generality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Loss_of_generality) of any conclusions reached.

In both pure and applied mathematics (e.g., [optimization](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optimization_%28mathematics%29), [numerical integration](wikipedia://en/170089), [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416)), *well-behaved* also means not violating any assumptions needed to successfully apply whatever analysis is being discussed.

The opposite case is usually labeled "pathological". It is not unusual to have situations in which most cases (in terms of [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) or [measure](wikipedia://en/19873)) are pathological, but the pathological cases will not arise in practice—unless constructed deliberately.

The term "well-behaved" is generally applied in an absolute sense—either something is well-behaved or it is not. For example:

- In [algorithmic inference](wikipedia://en/20890511), a [well-behaved statistic](wikipedia://en/20890519) is monotonic, well-defined, and [sufficient](wikipedia://en/140841).
- In [Bézout's theorem](wikipedia://en/149217), two [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000) are well-behaved, and thus the formula given by the theorem for the number of their intersections is valid, if their [polynomial greatest common divisor](wikipedia://en/9927028) is a constant.
- A [meromorphic function](wikipedia://en/80857) is a ratio of two well-behaved functions, in the sense of those two functions being [holomorphic](wikipedia://en/14110).
- The [Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions](wikipedia://en/2397362) are first-order necessary conditions for a solution in a well-behaved [nonlinear programming](wikipedia://en/679709) problem to be optimal; a problem is referred to as well-behaved if some regularity conditions are satisfied.
- In [probability](wikipedia://en/22934), events contained in the [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325)'s corresponding [sigma-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sigma-algebra) are well-behaved, as are [measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Measurable) functions.

Unusually, the term could also be applied in a comparative sense:

- In [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176):
  - [Analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) are better-behaved than general [smooth functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function).
  - Smooth functions are better-behaved than general differentiable functions.
  - Continuous [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) functions are better-behaved than general continuous functions. The larger the number of times the function can be differentiated, the more well-behaved it is.
  - [Continuous functions](wikipedia://en/6122) are better-behaved than [Riemann-integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_integration) functions on compact sets.
  - Riemann-integrable functions are better-behaved than [Lebesgue-integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_integration) functions.
  - Lebesgue-integrable functions are better-behaved than general functions.
- In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954):
  - [Continuous](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_function_%28topology%29) functions are better-behaved than discontinuous ones.
  - [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) is better-behaved than [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610).
  - Attractive [fixed points](wikipedia://en/449738) are better-behaved than repulsive fixed points.
  - [Hausdorff topologies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hausdorff_topology) are better-behaved than those in arbitrary [general topology](wikipedia://en/178649).
  - [Borel sets](wikipedia://en/43327) are better-behaved than arbitrary [sets](wikipedia://en/26691) of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).
  - Spaces with [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) dimension are better-behaved than spaces with [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907).
- In [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384):
  - [Groups](wikipedia://en/19447) are better-behaved than [magmas](wikipedia://en/141916) and [semigroups](wikipedia://en/27799).
  - [Abelian groups](wikipedia://en/2974) are better-behaved than non-Abelian groups.
  - [Finitely-generated Abelian groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely-generated_abelian_group) are better-behaved than non-finitely-generated Abelian groups.
  - Finite-[dimensional](wikipedia://en/38267) [vector spaces](wikipedia://en/32370) are better-behaved than [infinite](wikipedia://en/20110824)-dimensional ones.
  - [Fields](wikipedia://en/10603) are better-behaved than [skew fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Skew_field) or general [rings](wikipedia://en/48404).
  - Separable [field extensions](wikipedia://en/11634) are better-behaved than non-separable ones.
  - [Normed division algebras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_division_algebra) are better-behaved than general composition algebras.

## Pathological examples {#Pathological_examples}

Pathological examples often have some undesirable or unusual properties that make it difficult to contain or explain within a theory. Such pathological behaviors often prompt new investigation and research, which leads to new theory and more general results. Some important historical examples of this are:

- [Ranked-choice voting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instant-runoff_voting) is commonly described as a pathological [social choice function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Social_choice_function), because of its tendency to eliminate candidates for [winning too many votes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonicity_criterion).[^o23]
- The discovery of [irrational numbers](wikipedia://en/20647689) by the school of [Pythagoras](wikipedia://en/23275) in ancient Greece; for example, the length of the diagonal of a [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301), that is $\sqrt{2}$.
- The discovery of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) in the 16th century in order to find the roots of [cubic](wikipedia://en/243709) and [quartic](wikipedia://en/308152) [polynomial functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial_function).
- Some [number fields](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) have [rings of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) that do not form a [unique factorization domain](wikipedia://en/60546), for example the [extended field](wikipedia://en/11634) $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{-5})$.
- The discovery of [fractals](wikipedia://en/10913) and other "rough" geometric objects (see [Hausdorff dimension](wikipedia://en/14294)).
- [Weierstrass function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weierstrass_function), a [real](wikipedia://en/20646438)-valued function on the [real line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_line), that is [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) everywhere but [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) nowhere.[^o2]
- [Test functions](wikipedia://en/815799) in [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478) and distribution theory, which are [infinitely differentiable functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infinitely_differentiable_function) on the real line that are 0 everywhere outside of a given limited [interval](wikipedia://en/49172). An example of such a function is the test function, $$\varphi(t) = \begin{cases} e^{-1/(1-t^2)}, & -1<t<1, \\ 0, & \text{otherwise}. \end{cases}$$
- The [Cantor set](wikipedia://en/6172) is a subset of the interval $[0,1]$ that has [measure](wikipedia://en/19873) zero but is [uncountable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uncountable).
- The [fat Cantor set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fat_Cantor_set) is [nowhere dense](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nowhere_dense) but has positive [measure](wikipedia://en/19873).
- The [Fabius function](wikipedia://en/28192039) is everywhere [smooth](wikipedia://en/1408000) but nowhere [analytic](wikipedia://en/61478).
- [Volterra's function](wikipedia://en/924193) is [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) with [bounded](wikipedia://en/311509) derivative everywhere, but the derivative is not [Riemann-integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann-integrable).
- The Peano [space-filling curve](wikipedia://en/352398) is a continuous [surjective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surjective) function that maps the unit interval $[0,1]$ onto $[0,1]\times[0,1]$.
- The [Dirichlet function](wikipedia://en/630005), which is the [indicator function](wikipedia://en/240790) for rationals, is a bounded function that is not [Riemann integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_integrable).
- The [Cantor function](wikipedia://en/320819) is a [monotonic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotonic) continuous surjective function that maps $[0,1]$ onto $[0,1]$, but has zero derivative [almost everywhere](wikipedia://en/44688).
- The [Minkowski question-mark function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minkowski_question-mark_function) is continuous and *strictly* increasing but has zero derivative almost everywhere.
- Satisfaction classes containing "intuitively false" arithmetical statements can be constructed for [countable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable), recursively saturated [models](wikipedia://en/19858) of [Peano arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peano_arithmetic).
- The [Osgood curve](wikipedia://en/26717031) is a [Jordan curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jordan_curve) (unlike most [space-filling curves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Space-filling_curves)) of positive [area](wikipedia://en/1209).
- An [exotic sphere](wikipedia://en/1114931) is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) but not [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic) to the standard Euclidean [n-sphere](wikipedia://en/39782).

At the time of their discovery, each of these was considered highly pathological; today, each has been assimilated into modern mathematical theory. These examples prompt their observers to correct their beliefs or intuitions, and in some cases necessitate a reassessment of foundational definitions and concepts. Over the course of history, they have led to more correct, more precise, and more powerful mathematics. For example, the Dirichlet function is Lebesgue integrable, and convolution with test functions is used to approximate any [locally integrable function](wikipedia://en/4258398) by smooth functions.[^o24]

Whether a behavior is pathological is by definition subject to personal intuition. Pathologies depend on context, training, and experience, and what is pathological to one researcher may very well be standard behavior to another.

Pathological examples can show the importance of the assumptions in a theorem. For example, in [statistics](wikipedia://en/26685), the [Cauchy distribution](wikipedia://en/7003) does not satisfy the [central limit theorem](wikipedia://en/39406), even though its symmetric [bell-shape](wikipedia://en/59505168) appears similar to many distributions which do; it fails the requirement to have a mean and standard deviation which exist and that are finite.

Some of the best-known [paradoxes](wikipedia://en/24390), such as [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220) and [Hausdorff paradox](wikipedia://en/634759), are based on the existence of [non-measurable sets](wikipedia://en/620134). Mathematicians, unless they take the minority position of denying the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), are in general resigned to living with such sets.

## Computer science {#Computer_science}

In [computer science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_science), *pathological* has a slightly different sense with regard to the study of [algorithms](wikipedia://en/775). Here, an input (or set of inputs) is said to be *pathological* if it causes atypical behavior from the algorithm, such as a violation of its average case [complexity](wikipedia://en/7543), or even its correctness. For example, [hash tables](wikipedia://en/13833) generally have pathological inputs: sets of keys that [collide](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hash_collision) on hash values. [Quicksort](wikipedia://en/3268249) normally has $O(n \log{n})$ time complexity, but deteriorates to $O(n^2)$ when it is given input that triggers suboptimal behavior.

The term is often used pejoratively, as a way of dismissing such inputs as being specially designed to break a routine that is otherwise sound in practice (compare with *[Byzantine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Byzantine_failure)*). On the other hand, awareness of pathological inputs is important, as they can be exploited to mount a [denial-of-service attack](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Denial-of-service_attack) on a computer system. Also, the term in this sense is a matter of subjective judgment as with its other senses. Given enough run time, a sufficiently large and diverse user community (or other factors), an input which may be dismissed as pathological could in fact occur (as seen in the [first test flight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5_Flight_501) of the [Ariane 5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ariane_5)).

## Exceptions {#Exceptions}

A similar but distinct phenomenon is that of [exceptional objects](wikipedia://en/5280515) (and [exceptional isomorphisms](wikipedia://en/25262819)), which occurs when there are a "small" number of exceptions to a general pattern (such as a finite set of exceptions to an otherwise infinite rule). By contrast, in cases of pathology, often most or almost all instances of a phenomenon are pathological (e.g., almost all real numbers are irrational).

Subjectively, exceptional objects (such as the [icosahedron](wikipedia://en/43856084) or [sporadic simple groups](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sporadic_simple_group)) are generally considered "beautiful", unexpected examples of a theory, while pathological phenomena are often considered "ugly", as the name implies. Accordingly, theories are usually expanded to include exceptional objects. For example, the [exceptional Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/6573043) are included in the theory of [semisimple Lie algebras](wikipedia://en/1337587): the axioms are seen as good, the exceptional objects as unexpected but valid.

By contrast, pathological examples are instead taken to point out a shortcoming in the axioms, requiring stronger axioms to rule them out. For example, requiring tameness of an embedding of a sphere in the [Schönflies problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Schönflies_problem). In general, one may study the more general theory, including the pathologies, which may provide its own simplifications (the real numbers have properties very different from the rationals, and likewise continuous maps have very different properties from smooth ones), but also the narrower theory, from which the original examples were drawn.
