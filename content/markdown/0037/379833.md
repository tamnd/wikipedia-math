# Double negation

::: {#o1 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=infobox-mathematical-statement name="Double negation"}

**Double negation.**

If a statement is true, then it is not the case that the statement is not true, and vice versa."

:::

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Double negation"}

In [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus), the **double negation** of a statement states that "it is not the case that the statement is not true". In [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), every statement is [logically equivalent](wikipedia://en/169324) to its double negation, but this is not true in [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262); this can be expressed by the formula A ≡ ~(~A) where the sign ≡ expresses logical equivalence and the sign **~** expresses [negation](wikipedia://en/161019).

:::

Like the [law of the excluded middle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_the_excluded_middle), this principle is considered to be a [law of thought](wikipedia://en/1252308) in classical logic,[^o3] but it is disallowed by [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262).[^o4] The principle was stated as a theorem of [propositional logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus) by [Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) and [Whitehead](wikipedia://en/43395) in *[Principia Mathematica](wikipedia://en/24133)* as:

$\mathbf{*4\cdot13}. \ \ \vdash.\ p \ \equiv \ \thicksim(\thicksim p)$[^o5]

"This is the principle of double negation, *i.e.* a proposition is equivalent of the falsehood of its negation."

::: {#o7 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Double negation elimination"}

## Elimination and introduction {#Elimination_and_introduction}

**Double negation elimination** and **double negation introduction** are two [valid](wikipedia://en/21304742) [rules of replacement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rules_of_replacement). They are the [inferences](wikipedia://en/317465) that, if *not not-A* is true, then *A* is true, and its [converse](wikipedia://en/155941), that, if *A* is true, then *not not-A* is true, respectively. The rule allows one to introduce or eliminate a [negation](wikipedia://en/161019) from a [formal proof](wikipedia://en/1197184). The rule is based on the equivalence of, for example, *It is false that it is not raining.* and *It is raining.*

The *double negation introduction* rule is:

*P $\Rightarrow$ P*

and the *double negation elimination* rule is:

*P $\Rightarrow$ P*

In logics that have both rules, negation is an [involution](wikipedia://en/293450).

### Formal notation {#Formal_notation}

The *double negation introduction* rule may be written in [sequent](wikipedia://en/252355) notation:

$$P \vdash \neg \neg P$$

The *double negation elimination* rule may be written as:

$$\neg \neg P \vdash P$$

In [rule form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inference_rule):

$$\frac{P}{\neg \neg P}$$

and

$$\frac{\neg \neg P}{P}$$

or as a [tautology](wikipedia://en/4495335) (plain propositional calculus sentence):

$$P \to \neg \neg P$$

and

$$\neg \neg P \to P$$

These can be combined into a single biconditional formula:

$$\neg \neg P \leftrightarrow P.$$

Since biconditionality is an [equivalence relation](wikipedia://en/9259), any instance of ¬¬*A* in a [well-formed formula](wikipedia://en/404582) can be replaced by *A*, leaving unchanged the [truth-value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Truth-value) of the well-formed formula.

Double negative elimination is a theorem of [classical logic](wikipedia://en/55868), but not of weaker logics such as [intuitionistic logic](wikipedia://en/169262) and [minimal logic](wikipedia://en/26267678). Double negation introduction is a theorem of both intuitionistic logic and minimal logic, as is $\neg \neg \neg A \vdash \neg A$.

Because of their constructive character, a statement such as *It's not the case that it's not raining* is weaker than *It's raining.* The latter requires a proof of rain, whereas the former merely requires a proof that rain would not be contradictory. This distinction also arises in natural language in the form of [litotes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Litotes).

## Proofs {#Proofs}

### In classical propositional calculus system {#In_classical_propositional_calculus_system}

In [Hilbert-style deductive systems](wikipedia://en/8529655) for propositional logic, double negation is not always taken as an axiom (see [list of Hilbert systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/List_of_Hilbert_systems)), and is rather a theorem. We describe a proof of this theorem in the system of three axioms proposed by [Jan Łukasiewicz](wikipedia://en/305064):

A1. $\phi \to \left( \psi \to \phi \right)$

A2. $\left( \phi \to \left( \psi \rightarrow \xi \right) \right) \to \left( \left( \phi \to \psi \right) \to \left( \phi \to \xi \right) \right)$

A3. $\left ( \lnot \phi \to \lnot \psi \right) \to \left( \psi \to \phi \right)$

We use the lemma $p \to p$ proved [here](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Propositional_calculus#Proof_example_in_P2), which we refer to as (L1), and use the following additional lemma, proved [here](wikipedia://en/8529655#Some_useful_theorems_and_their_proofs):

(L2) $p \to ((p \to q) \to q)$

We first prove $\neg \neg p \to p$. For shortness, we denote $q \to ( r \to q )$ by φ<sub>0</sub>. We also use repeatedly the method of the [hypothetical syllogism metatheorem](wikipedia://en/177633#As_a_metatheorem) as a shorthand for several proof steps.

(1) $\varphi_0$ (instance of (A1))

(2) $(\neg \neg \varphi_0 \to \neg \neg p ) \to (\neg p \to \neg \varphi_0)$ (instance of (A3))

(3) $(\neg p \to \neg \varphi_0) \to (\varphi_0 \to p )$ (instance of (A3))

(4) $(\neg \neg \varphi_0 \to \neg \neg p ) \to (\varphi_0 \to p )$ (from (2) and (3) by the hypothetical syllogism metatheorem)

(5) $\neg \neg p \to ( \neg \neg \varphi_0 \to \neg \neg p )$ (instance of (A1))

(6) $\neg \neg p \to (\varphi_0 \to p )$ (from (4) and (5) by the hypothetical syllogism metatheorem)

(7) $\varphi_0 \to ((\varphi_0 \to p) \to p)$ (instance of (L2))

(8) $(\varphi_0 \to p) \to p$ (from (1) and (7) by modus ponens)

(9) $\neg \neg p \to p$ (from (6) and (8) by the hypothetical syllogism metatheorem)

We now prove $p \to \neg \neg p$.

(1) $\neg\neg\neg p \to \neg p$ (instance of the first part of the theorem we have just proven)

(2) $(\neg\neg\neg p \to \neg p) \to (p \to \neg\neg p)$ (instance of (A3))

(3) $p \to \neg \neg p$ (from (1) and (2) by modus ponens)

And the proof is complete.
