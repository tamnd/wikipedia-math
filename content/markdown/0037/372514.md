# Schur decomposition

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Schur decomposition"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), the **Schur decomposition** or **Schur triangulation**, named after [Issai Schur](wikipedia://en/617522), is a [matrix decomposition](wikipedia://en/253873). It allows one to write an arbitrary complex square matrix as [unitarily similar](wikipedia://en/341149) to an [upper triangular matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper-triangular_matrix) whose diagonal elements are the eigenvalues of the original matrix.

:::

::: {#o3 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

The complex Schur decomposition reads as follows: if $A$ is an $n \times n$ [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) with [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_numbers) entries, then *A* can be expressed as[^o4][^o5][^o6]

$$A = Q U Q^{-1}$$

for some [unitary matrix](wikipedia://en/129222) *Q* (so that the inverse *Q*<sup>−1</sup> is also the [conjugate transpose](wikipedia://en/217548) *Q*\* of *Q*), and some [upper triangular matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular_matrix) *U*. This is called a **Schur form** of *A*. Since *U* is [similar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_%28linear_algebra%29) to *A*, it has the same [spectrum](wikipedia://en/206136), and since it is triangular, its [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) are the diagonal entries of *U*.

The Schur decomposition implies that there exists a nested sequence of *A*-invariant subspaces $\{0\} = V_{0} \subset V_{1} \subset \cdots \subset V_{n} = \mathbf{C}^{n}$, and that there exists an ordered [orthonormal basis](wikipedia://en/195063) (for the standard [Hermitian form](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermitian_form) of $\mathbf{C}^{n}$) such that the first *i* basis vectors span $V_{i}$ for each *i* occurring in the nested sequence. Phrased somewhat differently, the first part says that a [linear operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) *J* on a complex finite-dimensional vector space [stabilizes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit-stabilizer_theorem#Orbits_and_stabilizers) a complete [flag](wikipedia://en/390646) $(V_{1}, ..., V_{n})$.

There is also a real Schur decomposition. If $A$ is an $n \times n$ [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) with [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_numbers) entries, then *A* can be expressed as[^o7]

$$A = Q H Q^{-1}$$

where $Q$ is an [orthogonal matrix](wikipedia://en/105620) and $H$ is either upper or lower quasi-triangular. A quasi-triangular matrix is a matrix that when expressed as a block matrix of $2 \times 2$ and $1 \times 1$ blocks is triangular. This is a stronger property than being [Hessenberg](wikipedia://en/519204). Just as in the complex case, a family of commuting real matrices {*A<sub>i</sub>*} may be simultaneously brought to quasi-triangular form by an orthogonal matrix. There exists an orthogonal matrix *Q* such that, for every *A<sub>i</sub>* in the given family,

$$H_i = Q A_i Q^{-1}$$

is upper quasi-triangular.

:::

::: {#o9 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

A constructive proof for the Schur decomposition is as follows: every operator *A* on a complex finite-dimensional vector space has an eigenvalue *λ*, corresponding to some eigenspace *V<sub>λ</sub>*. Let *V<sub>λ</sub>*<sup>⊥</sup> be its orthogonal complement. It is clear that, with respect to this orthogonal decomposition, *A* has matrix representation (one can pick here any orthonormal bases *Z*<sub>1</sub> and *Z*<sub>2</sub> spanning *V<sub>λ</sub>* and *V<sub>λ</sub>*<sup>⊥</sup> respectively)

$$\begin{bmatrix} Z_1 & Z_2 \end{bmatrix}^{*} A \begin{bmatrix}Z_1 & Z_2\end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} \lambda \, I_{\lambda} & A_{12} \\ 0 & A_{22} \end{bmatrix}: \begin{matrix} V_{\lambda} \\ \oplus \\ V_{\lambda}^{\perp} \end{matrix} \rightarrow \begin{matrix} V_{\lambda} \\ \oplus \\ V_{\lambda}^{\perp} \end{matrix}$$

where *I<sub>λ</sub>* is the identity operator on *V<sub>λ</sub>*. The above matrix would be upper-triangular except for the *A*<sub>22</sub> block. But exactly the same procedure can be applied to the sub-matrix *A*<sub>22</sub>, viewed as an operator on *V<sub>λ</sub>*<sup>⊥</sup>, and its submatrices. Continue this way until the resulting matrix is upper triangular. Since each conjugation increases the dimension of the upper-triangular block by at least one, this process takes at most *n* steps. Thus the space **C**<sup>*n*</sup> will be exhausted and the procedure has yielded the desired result.[^o10]

The above argument can be slightly restated as follows: let *λ* be an eigenvalue of *A*, corresponding to some eigenspace *V<sub>λ</sub>*. *A* induces an operator *T* on the [quotient space](wikipedia://en/898784) **C**<sup>*n*</sup>/*V<sub>λ</sub>*. This operator is precisely the *A*<sub>22</sub> submatrix from above. As before, *T* would have an eigenspace, say *W<sub>μ</sub>* ⊂ **C**<sup>*n*</sup> modulo *V<sub>λ</sub>*. Notice the preimage of *W<sub>μ</sub>* under the quotient map is an [invariant subspace](wikipedia://en/585826) of *A* that contains *V<sub>λ</sub>*. Continue this way until the resulting quotient space has dimension 0. Then the successive preimages of the eigenspaces found at each step form a flag that *A* stabilizes.

:::

## Notes {#Notes}

Although every square matrix has a Schur decomposition, in general this decomposition is not unique. For example, the eigenspace *V<sub>λ</sub>* can have dimension > 1, in which case any orthonormal basis for *V<sub>λ</sub>* would lead to the desired result.

Write the triangular matrix *U* as *U* = *D* + *N*, where *D* is diagonal and *N* is strictly upper triangular (and thus a [nilpotent matrix](wikipedia://en/1167800)). The diagonal matrix *D* contains the eigenvalues of *A* in arbitrary order (hence its Frobenius norm, squared, is the sum of the squared moduli of the eigenvalues of *A*, while the Frobenius norm of *A*, squared, is the sum of the squared [singular values](wikipedia://en/709116) of *A*). The nilpotent part *N* is generally not unique either, but its [Frobenius norm](wikipedia://en/1543735#Frobenius_norm) is uniquely determined by *A* (just because the Frobenius norm of A is equal to the Frobenius norm of *U* = *D* + *N*).[^o12]

It is clear that if *A* is a [normal matrix](wikipedia://en/170366), then *U* from its Schur decomposition must be a [diagonal matrix](wikipedia://en/174080) and the column vectors of *Q* are the [eigenvectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvector) of *A*. Therefore, the Schur decomposition extends the [spectral decomposition](wikipedia://en/13576645). In particular, if *A* is [positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive-definite_matrix), the Schur decomposition of *A*, its spectral decomposition, and its [singular value decomposition](wikipedia://en/142207) coincide.

A [commuting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_operation) family {*A<sub>i</sub>*} of matrices can be simultaneously triangularized, i.e. there exists a unitary matrix *Q* such that, for every *A<sub>i</sub>* in the given family, *Q A<sub>i</sub> Q\** is upper triangular. This can be readily deduced from the above proof. Take element *A* from {*A<sub>i</sub>*} and again consider an eigenspace *V<sub>A</sub>*. Then *V<sub>A</sub>* is invariant under all matrices in {*A<sub>i</sub>*}. Therefore, all matrices in {*A<sub>i</sub>*} must share one common eigenvector in *V<sub>A</sub>*. Induction then proves the claim. As a corollary, we have that every commuting family of normal matrices can be simultaneously [diagonalized](wikipedia://en/246325).

In the infinite dimensional setting, not every [bounded operator](wikipedia://en/455961) on a [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) has an invariant subspace. However, the upper-triangularization of an arbitrary square matrix does generalize to [compact operators](wikipedia://en/577441). Every [compact operator](wikipedia://en/577441) on a complex Banach space has a [nest](wikipedia://en/390646#Subspace_nest) of closed invariant subspaces.

::: {#o14 .definition kind=definition/construction cue=heading name="Computation"}

## Computation {#Computation}

The Schur decomposition of a given matrix is numerically computed by the [QR algorithm](wikipedia://en/594072) or its variants. In other words, the roots of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) corresponding to the matrix are not necessarily computed ahead in order to obtain its Schur decomposition. Conversely, the QR algorithm can be used to compute the roots of any given [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268) by finding the Schur decomposition of its [companion matrix](wikipedia://en/532542). Similarly, the QR algorithm is used to compute the eigenvalues of any given matrix, which are the diagonal entries of the upper triangular matrix of the Schur decomposition. Its complexity is [$\mathcal{O}(n^3)$](wikipedia://en/44578).[^o15][^o16]

:::

## Applications {#Applications}

[Lie theory](wikipedia://en/1595681) applications include:

- Every invertible operator is contained in a [Borel group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Borel_group).
- Every operator fixes a point of the [flag manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Flag_manifold).

:::

## Generalized Schur decomposition {#Generalized_Schur_decomposition}

Given square matrices *A* and *B*, the **generalized Schur decomposition** factorizes both matrices as $A = QSZ^*$ and $B = QTZ^*$, where *Q* and *Z* are [unitary](wikipedia://en/129222), and *S* and *T* are [upper triangular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Upper_triangular). The generalized Schur decomposition is also sometimes called the **QZ decomposition**.[^o5]p. 375 [^o21]

The generalized [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) $\lambda$ that solve the [generalized eigenvalue problem](wikipedia://en/13576645#Additional_topics) $A\mathbf{x}=\lambda B\mathbf{x}$ (where **x** is an unknown nonzero vector) can be calculated as the ratio of the diagonal elements of *S* to those of *T*. That is, using subscripts to denote matrix elements, the *i*th generalized eigenvalue $\lambda_i$ satisfies $\lambda_i = S_{ii} / T_{ii}$.

:::
