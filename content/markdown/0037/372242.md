# Radical of a ring

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Radical of a ring"}

In [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **radical of a ring** is an [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977) of "not-good" elements of the [ring](wikipedia://en/48404).

:::

The first example of a radical was the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636) introduced by (Köthe 1930), based on a suggestion of (Wedderburn 1908). In the next few years several other radicals were discovered, of which the most important example is the [Jacobson radical](wikipedia://en/57656). The general theory of radicals was defined independently by () and (Kurosh 1953).

The study of radicals is called **torsion theory**.

::: {#o7 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

## Definitions {#Definitions}

In the theory of radicals, rings are usually assumed to be [associative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Associative), but need not be [commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative) and [need not have a multiplicative identity](wikipedia://en/3405004). In particular, every ideal in a ring is also a ring.

Let $\mathfrak{A}$ be a class of rings which is:

1. closed under [homomorphic](wikipedia://en/26411) [images](wikipedia://en/579311). That is, for all rings $A, B \in \mathfrak{A}$ and any ring homomorphism $f: A \rightarrow B$ (which may fail to preserve any left- or right-identities) then the image of $f$ is in $\mathfrak{A}$
1. closed under taking ideals (for all rings $A \in \mathfrak{A}$, and $I$ is an ideal on $A$, then $I \in \mathfrak{A}$).

In particular, $\mathfrak{A}$ could just be the class of all (non-unital) rings.

Let *r* be some abstract property of rings in $\mathfrak{A}$. A ring with property *r* is called an *r*-ring; an ideal of some ring with property *r* is called an *r*-ideal. In particular, the *r*-ideals are a subset of the *r*-rings. A ring $A$ is said to be a *r*-**semi-simple ring** if it has no non-zero r-ideals.

*r* is said to be a **radical property** if:

1. the class of *r*-rings is closed under homomorphic images
1. For every ring $A \in \mathfrak{A}$ there exists an associated *r*-ideal $r(A)$, which is maximal — $r(A)$ contains all the *r*-ideals of A. The ideal $r(A)$ is called the *r*- **radical of the ring** $A$.
1. $r( A / r(A) ) = 0$, which is true iff the quotient ring $A / r(A)$ is *r*-semi-simple.

Note that, for any *r*-ring $R$, $R$ is its own maximal *r*-ideal. One can say that $R$ is a **radical**, and the class of *r*-rings is the **radical class**. One can define a radical property by specifying a valid radical class as a subclass of $\mathfrak{A}$: for an ideal I of some arbitrary ring in $\mathfrak{A}$, I is an ''r''-ideal if it is isomorphic to some ring in the radical class.

For any class of rings $\delta \in \mathfrak{A}$, there is a smallest radical class $L \delta$ containing it, called the **lower radical** of $\delta$. The operator *L* is called the **lower radical operator**.

A class of rings is called **regular** if every [non-zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_ideal) ideal of a ring in the class has a non-zero image in the class. For every regular class δ of rings, there is a largest radical class *U*δ, called the upper radical of δ, having zero intersection with δ. The operator *U* is called the **upper radical operator**.

A radical property *r* is said to be **hereditary** if for any ring $A \in \mathfrak{A}$ and any ideal $I$ of ring $A$, $r(I)=r(A) \cap I$. An equivalent condition on the radical class is that any ideal of a radical is also a radical.

The definition readily extends to defining the radical of an [algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_%28ring_theory%29). In particular, rings are algebras over the ring of integers.

## Examples {#Examples}

### Jacobson radical {#Jacobson_radical}

Let *R* be any ring, not necessarily commutative. The **Jacobson radical of** ***R*** is the intersection of the [annihilators](wikipedia://en/328684) of all [simple](wikipedia://en/29430) right *R*-modules.

There are several equivalent characterizations of the Jacobson radical, such as:

- J(*R*) is the intersection of the regular [maximal](wikipedia://en/48164) right (or left) ideals of *R*.
- J(*R*) is the intersection of all the right (or left) [primitive ideals](wikipedia://en/485842) of *R*.
- J(*R*) is the maximal right (or left) quasi-regular right (resp. left) ideal of *R*.

As with the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636), one can extend this definition to arbitrary two-sided ideals *I* by defining J(*I*) to be the [preimage](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Preimage) of J(*R/I*) under the projection map *R* → *R/I*.

If *R* is commutative, the Jacobson radical always contains the nilradical. If the ring *R* is a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/11985661) **Z**-[algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algebra_over_a_ring), then the nilradical is equal to the Jacobson radical, and more generally: the radical of any ideal *I* will always be equal to the intersection of all the maximal ideals of *R* that contain *I*. This says that *R* is a [Jacobson ring](wikipedia://en/4607512).

### Baer radical {#Baer_radical}

The Baer radical of a ring is the intersection of the [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928) of the ring *R*. Equivalently it is the smallest [semiprime ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiprime_ideal) in *R*. The Baer radical is the lower radical of the class of nilpotent rings, and is also called the "lower nilradical" (and denoted Nil<sub>∗</sub>*R*), the "prime radical", and the "Baer-McCoy radical". Every element of the Baer radical is [nilpotent](wikipedia://en/252235), so it is a [nil ideal](wikipedia://en/3100140).

For commutative rings, this is just the [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636) and closely follows the definition of the [radical of an ideal](wikipedia://en/252009).

### Upper nil radical or Köthe radical {#Upper_nil_radical_or_Köthe_radical}

The sum of the [nil ideals](wikipedia://en/3100140) of a ring *R* is the upper nilradical Nil<sup>\*</sup>*R* or Köthe radical and is the unique largest nil ideal of *R*. [Köthe's conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Köthe's_conjecture) asks whether any left nil ideal is in the nilradical.

::: {#o14 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="Singular radical"}

### Singular radical {#Singular_radical}

An element of a (possibly [non-commutative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-commutative_ring) ring) is called left **singular** if it annihilates an [essential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Essential_submodule) left ideal, that is, *r* is left singular if *Ir* = 0 for some essential left ideal *I*. The set of left singular elements of a ring *R* is a two-sided ideal, called the [left singular ideal](wikipedia://en/32191263), and is denoted $\mathcal{Z}(_R R)$. The ideal *N* of *R* such that $N/\mathcal{Z}(_R R)=\mathcal{Z}(_{R/\mathcal{Z}(_R R)} R/\mathcal{Z}(_R R))\,$ is denoted by $\mathcal{Z}_2(_R R)$ and is called the **singular radical** or the **Goldie torsion** of *R*. The singular radical contains the prime radical (the nilradical in the case of commutative rings) but may properly contain it, even in the commutative case. However, the singular radical of a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129) is always nilpotent.

:::

### Levitzki radical {#Levitzki_radical}

The Levitzki radical is defined as the largest [locally nilpotent ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_nilpotent_ideal), analogous to the [Hirsch–Plotkin radical](wikipedia://en/22468906) in the theory of [groups](wikipedia://en/19447). If the ring is [Noetherian](wikipedia://en/56129), then the Levitzki radical is itself a nilpotent ideal, and so is the unique largest left, right, or two-sided nilpotent ideal.

### Brown–McCoy radical {#Brown–McCoy_radical}

The Brown–McCoy radical (called the **strong radical** in the theory of [Banach algebras](wikipedia://en/4665)) can be defined in any of the following ways:

- the intersection of the maximal two-sided ideals
- the intersection of all maximal modular ideals
- the upper radical of the class of all [simple rings](wikipedia://en/443387) with multiplicative identity

The Brown–McCoy radical is studied in much greater generality than associative rings with 1.

### von Neumann regular radical {#von_Neumann_regular_radical}

A [von Neumann regular ring](wikipedia://en/907524) is a ring *A* (possibly non-commutative without multiplicative identity) such that for every *a* there is some *b* with *a* = *aba*. The von Neumann regular rings form a radical class. It contains every [matrix ring](wikipedia://en/487541) over a [division algebra](wikipedia://en/26549625), but contains no nil rings.

### Artinian radical {#Artinian_radical}

The Artinian radical is usually defined for two-sided [Noetherian rings](wikipedia://en/56129) as the sum of all right ideals that are [Artinian modules](wikipedia://en/542336). The definition is left-right symmetric, and indeed produces a two-sided ideal of the ring. This radical is important in the study of Noetherian rings, as outlined by (Chatters Hajarnavis 1980).

:::
