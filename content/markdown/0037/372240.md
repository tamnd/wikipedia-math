# Tensor product of fields

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Tensor product of fields"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **tensor product** of two [fields](wikipedia://en/10603) is their [tensor product](wikipedia://en/372395) as [algebras](wikipedia://en/191788) over a common [subfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subfield_%28mathematics%29). If no subfield is explicitly specified, the two fields must have the same [characteristic](wikipedia://en/1066621) and the common subfield is their [prime subfield](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Prime_field).

:::

The tensor product of two fields is sometimes a field, and often a [direct product](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Product_ring) of fields; in some cases, it can contain non-zero [nilpotent elements](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nilpotent_element).

The tensor product of two fields expresses in a single structure the different way to embed the two fields in a common [extension field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Extension_field).

## Compositum of fields {#Compositum_of_fields}

First, one defines the notion of the compositum of fields. This construction occurs frequently in [field theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Field_theory_%28mathematics%29). The idea behind the compositum is to make the smallest field containing two other fields. In order to formally define the compositum, one must first specify a [tower of fields](wikipedia://en/28194169). Let *k* be a field and *L* and *K* be two extensions of *k*. The compositum, denoted *K.L*, is defined to be $K.L = k(K \cup L)$ where the right-hand side denotes the extension generated by *K* and *L*. This assumes *some* field containing both *K* and *L*. Either one starts in a situation where an ambient field is easy to identify (for example if *K* and *L* are both subfields of the [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826)), or one proves a result that allows one to place both *K* and *L* (as [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) copies) in some large enough field.

In many cases one can identify *K*.*L* as a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) [tensor product](wikipedia://en/30844), taken over the field *N* that is the intersection of *K* and *L*. For example, if one adjoins √2 to the [rational field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rational_numbers) $\mathbb{Q}$ to get *K*, and √3 to get *L*, it is true that the field *M* obtained as *K*.*L* inside the complex numbers $\mathbb{C}$ is ([up to](wikipedia://en/44787) isomorphism)

$$K\otimes_{\mathbb Q}L$$

as a vector space over $\mathbb{Q}$. (This type of result can be verified, in general, by using the [ramification](wikipedia://en/384644) theory of [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705).)

Subfields *K* and *L* of *M* are [linearly disjoint](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linearly_disjoint) (over a subfield *N*) when in this way the natural *N*-linear map of

$$K\otimes_NL$$

to *K*.*L* is [injective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Injective). Naturally enough this isn't always the case, for example when *K* = *L*. When the degrees are finite, injectivity is equivalent here to [bijectivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijectivity). Hence, when *K* and *L* are linearly disjoint finite-degree extension fields over *N*, $K.L \cong K \otimes_N L$, as with the aforementioned extensions of the rationals.

A significant case in the theory of [cyclotomic fields](wikipedia://en/30872462) is that for the *n*th [roots of unity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roots_of_unity), for *n* a [composite number](wikipedia://en/82289), the subfields generated by the *p*<sup>*k*</sup>th roots of unity for [prime powers](wikipedia://en/1630483) dividing *n* are linearly disjoint for distinct *p*.

## The tensor product as ring {#The_tensor_product_as_ring}

To get a general theory, one needs to consider a [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) structure on $K \otimes_N L$. One can define the product $(a\otimes b)(c\otimes d)$ to be $ac \otimes bd$ (see [Tensor product of algebras](wikipedia://en/372395)). This formula is multilinear over *N* in each variable; and so defines a ring structure on the tensor product, making $K \otimes_N L$ into a [commutative *N*-algebra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commutative_algebra_%28structure%29), called the **tensor product of fields**.

## Analysis of the ring structure {#Analysis_of_the_ring_structure}

The structure of the ring can be analysed by considering all ways of embedding both *K* and *L* in some field extension of *N*. The construction here assumes the common subfield *N*; but does not assume *[a priori](wikipedia://en/6679056)* that *K* and *L* are subfields of some field *M* (thus getting round the caveats about constructing a compositum field). Whenever one embeds *K* and *L* in such a field *M*, say using embeddings α of *K* and β of *L*, there results a [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) γ from $K \otimes_N L$ into *M* defined by:

$$\gamma(a\otimes b) = (\alpha(a)\otimes1)\star(1\otimes\beta(b)) = \alpha(a).\beta(b).$$

The [kernel](wikipedia://en/45240) of γ will be a [prime ideal](wikipedia://en/24928) of the tensor product; and [conversely](wikipedia://en/155941) any prime ideal of the tensor product will give a homomorphism of *N*-algebras to an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462) (inside a [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271)) and so provides embeddings of *K* and *L* in some field as extensions of (a copy of) *N*.

In this way one can analyse the structure of $K \otimes_N L$: there may in principle be a non-zero [nilradical](wikipedia://en/289636) (intersection of all prime ideals) – and after taking the quotient by that one can speak of the product of all embeddings of *K* and *L* in various *M*, *over* *N*.

In case *K* and *L* are finite extensions of *N*, the situation is particularly simple since the tensor product is of finite dimension as an *N*-algebra (and thus an [Artinian ring](wikipedia://en/489273)). One can then say that if *R* is the radical, one has $(K \otimes_N L) / R$ as a direct product of finitely many fields. Each such field is a representative of an [equivalence class](wikipedia://en/9260) of (essentially distinct) field embeddings for *K* and *L* in some extension *M*.

## Examples {#Examples}

To give an explicit example consider the fields $K=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-2)$ and $L=\mathbb{Q}[y]/(y^2-2)$. Clearly $\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\cong K\cong L\neq K$ are isomorphic but technically unequal fields with their (set theoretic) intersection being the prime field $N=\mathbb{Q}$. Their tensor product

$$K\otimes L=K\otimes_{N} L=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-2)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}[y]/(y^2-2) \cong \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})[z]/(z^2-2)\cong \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}}\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$$

is not a field, but a 4-dimensional $\mathbb{Q}$-algebra. Furthermore this algebra is isomorphic to a direct sum of fields

$$K\otimes L\cong \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})[z]/(z^2-2)\cong \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\oplus\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$$

via the map induced by $1\mapsto (1,1), z\mapsto (\sqrt{2},-\sqrt{2})$. Morally $\tilde{N}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})$ should be considered the *largest common subfield up to isomorphism* of *K* and *L* via the isomorphisms $\tilde{N}=\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})\cong K\cong L$. When one performs the tensor product over this better candidate for the largest common subfield we actually get a (rather trivial) field

$$K\otimes_{\tilde{N}} L=\mathbb{Q}[x]/(x^2-2)\otimes_{\mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})}\mathbb{Q}[y]/(y^2-2) \cong \mathbb{Q}(\sqrt{2})=\tilde{N}\cong K\cong L.$$

For another example, if *K* is generated over $\mathbb{Q}$ by the [cube root](wikipedia://en/292506) of 2, then $K \otimes_{\mathbb Q} K$ is the sum of (a copy of) *K*, and a [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of

*X*<sup>3</sup> − 2,

of degree 6 over $\mathbb{Q}$. One can prove this by calculating the dimension of the tensor product over $\mathbb{Q}$ as 9, and observing that the splitting field does contain two (indeed three) copies of *K*, and is the compositum of two of them. That incidentally shows that *R* = {0} in this case.

An example leading to a non-zero nilpotent: let

*P*(*X*) = *X*<sup>*p*</sup> − *T*

with *K* the field of [rational functions](wikipedia://en/361210) in the indeterminate *T* over the [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) with *p* elements (see [Separable polynomial](wikipedia://en/362400): the point here is that *P* is *not* separable). If *L* is the field extension *K*(*T*<sup>1/*p*</sup>) (the [splitting field](wikipedia://en/174440) of *P*) then *L*/*K* is an example of a [purely inseparable field extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Purely_inseparable_field_extension). In $L \otimes_K L$ the element

$$T^{1/p}\otimes1-1\otimes T^{1/p}$$

is nilpotent: by taking its *p*th power one gets 0 by using *K*-linearity.

:::

## Classical theory of real and complex embeddings {#Classical_theory_of_real_and_complex_embeddings}

In [algebraic number theory](wikipedia://en/174705), tensor products of fields are (implicitly, often) a basic tool. If *K* is an extension of $\mathbb{Q}$ of finite degree *n*, $K\otimes_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb R$ is always a product of fields isomorphic to $\mathbb{R}$ or $\mathbb{C}$. The [totally real number fields](wikipedia://en/312853) are those for which only [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) fields occur: in general there are *r*<sub>1</sub> real and *r*<sub>2</sub> complex fields, with *r*<sub>1</sub> + 2*r*<sub>2</sub> = *n* as one sees by counting dimensions. The field factors are in 1–1 correspondence with the *real embeddings*, and *pairs of complex conjugate embeddings*, described in the classical literature.

This idea applies also to $K\otimes_{\mathbb Q}\mathbb Q_p,$ where $\mathbb{Q}$<sub>*p*</sub> is the field of [*p*-adic numbers](wikipedia://en/51423). This is a product of finite extensions of $\mathbb{Q}$<sub>*p*</sub>, in 1–1 correspondence with the completions of *K* for extensions of the *p*-adic metric on $\mathbb{Q}$.

## Consequences for Galois theory {#Consequences_for_Galois_theory}

This gives a general picture, and indeed a way of developing [Galois theory](wikipedia://en/61316) (along lines exploited in [Grothendieck's Galois theory](wikipedia://en/372470)). It can be shown that for [separable extensions](wikipedia://en/363340) the radical is always {0}; therefore the Galois theory case is the *semisimple* one, of products of fields alone.
