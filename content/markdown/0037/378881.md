# Gauss map

*Image: Gauss map.svg.* The Gauss map provides a mapping from every point on a curve or a surface to a corresponding point on a unit sphere. In this example, the curvature of a 2D-surface is mapped onto a 1D unit circle.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Gauss map"}

In [differential geometry](wikipedia://en/8625), the **Gauss map** of a [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) is a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) that maps each point in the surface to its [normal direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normal_direction), a [unit vector](wikipedia://en/167053) that is [orthogonal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal) to the surface at that point. Namely, given a surface *X* in [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>3</sup>, the Gauss map is a map *N*: *X* → *S*<sup>2</sup> (where *S*<sup>2</sup> is the [unit sphere](wikipedia://en/27033090)) such that for each *p* in *X*, the function value *N*(*p*) is a unit vector orthogonal to *X* at *p*. The Gauss map is named after [Carl F. Gauss](wikipedia://en/6125).

:::

The Gauss map can be defined (globally) if and only if the surface is [orientable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientable), in which case its [degree](wikipedia://en/3094450) is half the [Euler characteristic](wikipedia://en/148420). The Gauss map can always be defined locally (i.e. on a small piece of the surface). The [Jacobian](wikipedia://en/195351) determinant of the Gauss map is equal to [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623), and the [differential](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differential_%28calculus%29) of the Gauss map is called the [shape operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shape_operator).

Gauss first wrote a draft on the topic in 1825 and published in 1827.[^o2]

There is also a Gauss map for a [link](wikipedia://en/1161784), which computes [linking number](wikipedia://en/451999).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalizations"}

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

The Gauss map can be defined for [hypersurfaces](wikipedia://en/525101) in **R**<sup>*n*</sup> as a map from a hypersurface to the unit sphere *S*<sup>*n* − 1</sup> ⊆ **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.

For a general oriented *k*-[submanifold](wikipedia://en/525095) of **R**<sup>*n*</sup> the Gauss map can also be defined, and its target space is the *oriented* [Grassmannian](wikipedia://en/373810) $\tilde{G}_{k,n}$, i.e. the set of all oriented *k*-planes in **R**<sup>*n*</sup>. In this case a point on the submanifold is mapped to its oriented tangent subspace. One can also map to its oriented *normal* subspace; these are equivalent as $\tilde{G}_{k,n} \cong \tilde{G}_{n-k,n}$ via orthogonal complement. In [Euclidean 3-space](wikipedia://en/9697), this says that an oriented 2-plane is characterized by an oriented 1-line, equivalently a unit normal vector (as $\tilde{G}_{1,n} \cong S^{n-1}$), hence this is consistent with the definition above.[^o5]

Finally, the notion of Gauss map can be generalized to an oriented submanifold *X* of dimension *k* in an oriented ambient [Riemannian manifold](wikipedia://en/144652) *M* of dimension *n*. In that case, the Gauss map then goes from *X* to the set of tangent *k*-planes in the [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794) *TM*. The target space for the Gauss map *N* is a [Grassmann bundle](wikipedia://en/50406093) built on the tangent bundle *TM*. In the case where $M=\mathbf{R}^n$, the tangent bundle is trivialized (so the Grassmann bundle becomes a map to the Grassmannian), and we recover the previous definition.

:::

## Total curvature {#Total_curvature}

The area of the image of the Gauss map is called the **total curvature** and is equivalent to the [surface integral](wikipedia://en/528867) of the [Gaussian curvature](wikipedia://en/285623) within regions where the sign of the curvature does not change. This is the original interpretation given by Gauss.

$$\iint_R \pm|N_u \times N_v| \ du\, dv = \iint_R K|X_u \times X_v| \ du\, dv = \iint_S K \ dA$$

The [Gauss–Bonnet theorem](wikipedia://en/139229) links total curvature of a surface to its [topological](wikipedia://en/29954) properties.

## Cusps of the Gauss map {#Cusps_of_the_Gauss_map}

*Image: Cusp of the Gauss map.png.* A surface with a parabolic line and its Gauss map. A ridge passes through the parabolic line giving rise to a cusp on the Gauss map.

The Gauss map reflects many properties of the surface: when the surface has zero Gaussian curvature (that is along a [parabolic line](wikipedia://en/59542102)), the Gauss map will have a [fold catastrophe](wikipedia://en/434288#Fold_catastrophe).[^o8] This fold may contain [cusps](wikipedia://en/6851758) and these cusps were studied in depth by [Thomas Banchoff](wikipedia://en/4708571), [Terence Gaffney](wikipedia://en/40737298) and [Clint McCrory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clint_McCrory). Both parabolic lines and cusp are stable phenomena and will remain under slight deformations of the surface. Cusps occur when:

1. The surface has a bi-tangent plane;
1. A [ridge](wikipedia://en/18896093) crosses a parabolic line;
1. At the closure of the set of inflection points of the [asymptotic curves](wikipedia://en/915472) of the surface.

There are two types of cusp: *elliptic cusp* and *hyperbolic cusp*.
