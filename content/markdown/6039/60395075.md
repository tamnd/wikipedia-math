# Domenico Montesano

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Domenico Montesano"}

**Domenico Alfonso Emmanuele Montesano** (Potenza, 22 December 1863 – Naples, 1 October 1930) was an Italian mathematician. He influenced and developed theory on linear congruences and on conic bilinear complexes.[^o3]

:::

## Family life {#Family_life}

Domenico was born in [Potenza, Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Potenza) in 1863 to Leonardo Antonio Montesano and the duchess Isabella Schiavone of [Aragona, Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aragona).[^o3] They had nine children, five boys and four girls. Two sons became lawyers, two became medical doctors, and Domenico studied mathematics. The daughters studied through to high school, learning music and painting. Domenico's brother, Giuseppe Ferruccio Montesano, became one of the founders of psychology and [child psychiatry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Child_psychiatry) in Italy.[^o3]

## Education and career {#Education_and_career}

Domenico was a disciple of [Luigi Cremona](wikipedia://en/1846002) and [Giuseppe Battaglini](wikipedia://en/34349418) and graduated in Rome in 1884; after a brief period of improvement and assistantship, in 1885, at the age of twenty-two, he became a professor of projective and descriptive geometry at the [University of Bologna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Bologna). In 1893, he became a full professor in the same chair at the [University of Naples Federico II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Naples_Federico_II) and later in 1895 to the teaching position in higher geometry. For many years he also held the chair of higher mathematics. In 1903 he was Dean of the Faculty of Mathematical Sciences.[^o3]

He was a member of the Royal Academy of Sciences of Naples, and in 1921 he became president of the Academy of Physical and Mathematical Sciences of Naples. He also served on the board of the [Accademia Pontaniana](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accademia_Pontaniana) and a member of the Palermo Mathematical Circle. He was honored as Commander of the Order of the Crown of Italy and Knight and Officer of the Order of Saints Maurice and Lazarus.[^o3]

He became a member of the [Freemasons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Freemasonry) at a [lodge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonic_lodge) in [Bologna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bologna), during Cremona's term there as [Venerable Master](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonic_lodge_officers#Worshipful_Master) (and thus could personally conduct the initiation ceremonies of his protégé), with [Giosuè Carducci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giosuè_Carducci) as [Secretary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masonic_lodge_officers#Secretary).[^o7]

He died in Naples on 1 October 1930.[^o3]

## Works {#Works}

*Image: Togliatti surface.png.* [Togliatti surface](wikipedia://en/20705037), a quintic surface with 31 [crunodes](wikipedia://en/3090324) (the maximum possible)

He made important contributions to the theory of [Cremona transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cremona_transformation) and handled the [linear congruences](wikipedia://en/7713) and [conical bilinear complexes](wikipedia://en/705158). He discovered 30 new [rational surfaces](wikipedia://en/2380148) of the 5th order. He was the author of over fifty scholarly publications relating to Cremonian address geometry. Montesano's studies that mostly interested the international academic environment concerned the [geometry of the straight line](wikipedia://en/946975) and the Cremonian transformations. Among his major works was a *Treatise on [Projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849)*. Some of his theories are still the object of interpretation and study by contemporary mathematicians.[^o9]

He also worked on fifth order [algebraic surfaces](wikipedia://en/648311), more commonly referred to as quintic surfaces.[^o10]
