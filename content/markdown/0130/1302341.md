# Luneburg lens

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Luneburg lens"}

*Image: Luneburg lens.svg.* Cross-section of the standard Luneburg lens, with blue shading proportional to the refractive index

A **Luneburg lens** (original German *L**ü**neburg-Linse*) is a spherically symmetric [gradient-index lens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gradient-index_lens). A typical Luneburg lens's refractive index *n* decreases radially from the center to the outer surface. They can be made for use with [electromagnetic radiation](wikipedia://en/9426) from [visible light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_light) to [radio waves](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radio_waves).

:::

For certain index profiles, the lens will form perfect geometrical [images](wikipedia://en/71925) of two given concentric spheres onto each other. There are an infinite number of refractive-index profiles that can produce this effect. The simplest such solution was proposed by [Rudolf Luneburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rudolf_Luneburg) in 1944.[^o23] Luneburg's solution for the refractive index creates two conjugate [foci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_%28optics%29) outside the lens. The solution takes a simple and explicit form if one [focal point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Focus_%28optics%29) lies at infinity, and the other on the opposite surface of the lens. J. Brown and A. S. Gutman subsequently proposed solutions which generate one internal focal point and one external focal point.[^o2][^o3] These solutions are not unique; the set of solutions are defined by a set of [definite integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Definite_integrals) which must be evaluated numerically.[^o4]

## Designs {#Designs}

### Luneburg's solution {#Luneburg's_solution}

*Image: Luneburg Lens.gif.* A Luneburg lens converts a point source into a [collimated beam](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collimated_beam) when the source is placed at its edge.

Each point on the surface of an ideal Luneburg lens is the focal point for parallel radiation incident on the opposite side. Ideally, the [dielectric constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dielectric_constant) $\epsilon_r$ of the material composing the lens falls from 2 at its center to 1 at its surface (or equivalently, the [refractive index](wikipedia://en/25880) $n$ falls from $\sqrt{2}$ to 1), according to

$$n = \sqrt{\epsilon_r} = \sqrt{2 - \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^2},$$

where $R$ is the radius of the lens. Because the refractive index at the surface is the same as that of the surrounding medium, no reflection occurs at the surface. Within the lens, the paths of the rays are arcs of [ellipses](wikipedia://en/9277).

### Maxwell's fish-eye lens {#Maxwell's_fish-eye_lens}

*Image: Maxwells fish-eye lens.svg.* Cross-section of Maxwell's fish-eye lens, with blue shading representing increasing refractive index

Maxwell's fish-eye lens is also an example of the generalized Luneburg lens. The fish-eye, which was first fully described by [Maxwell](wikipedia://en/28989696) in 1854[^o8] (and therefore pre-dates Luneburg's solution), has a refractive index varying according to

$$n(r) = \sqrt{\epsilon_r} = \frac{n_0}{1 + \left( \frac{r}{R} \right)^2},$$

where $n_0$ is the index of refraction at the center of the lens and $R$ is the radius of the lens's spherical surface.[^o9] The index of refraction at the lens's surface is $n_0/2$. The lens images each point on the spherical surface to the opposite point on the surface. Within the lens, the paths of the rays are arcs of circles.

#### Publication and attribution {#Publication_and_attribution}

The properties of this lens are described in one of a number of set problems or puzzles in the 1853 *[Cambridge and Dublin Mathematical Journal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_and_Dublin_Mathematical_Journal)*.[^o11] The challenge is to find the refractive index as a function of radius, given that a ray describes a circular path, and further to prove the focusing properties of the lens. The solution is given in the 1854 edition of the same journal.[^o8] The problems and solutions were originally published anonymously, but the solution of this problem (and one other) were included in Niven's *The Scientific Papers of James Clerk Maxwell*,[^o12] which was published 11 years after Maxwell's death.

## Applications {#Applications}

In practice, Luneburg lenses are normally layered structures of discrete concentric shells, each of a different refractive index. These shells form a stepped refractive index profile that differs slightly from Luneburg's solution. This kind of lens is usually employed for [microwave frequencies](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave), especially to construct efficient [microwave antennas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_antenna) and [radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar) calibration standards. Cylindrical analogues of the Luneburg lens are also used for [collimating](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Collimating) light from [laser diodes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laser_diode).

### Radar reflector {#Radar_reflector}

A [radar reflector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_reflector) can be made from a Luneburg lens by metallizing parts of its surface. Radiation from a distant radar transmitter is focussed onto the underside of the metallization on the opposite side of the lens; here it is reflected, and focussed back onto the radar station. A difficulty with this scheme is that metallized regions block the entry or exit of radiation on that part of the lens, but the non-metallized regions result in a blind-spot on the opposite side.

Removable Luneburg lens type radar reflectors are sometimes attached to military aircraft in order to make [stealth aircraft](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stealth_aircraft) visible during training operations, or to conceal their true [radar signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Radar_signature). Unlike other types of radar reflectors, their shape doesn't affect the handling of the aircraft.[^o16][^o17]

### Microwave antenna {#Microwave_antenna}

*Image: Type 984 radar, HMS Victorious 1961.jpg.* [Type 984](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Type_984_radar) [3D radar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3D_radar) on Victorious, 1961, using a Luneburg lens

In modern [millimeter wave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Millimeter_wave) and [microwave](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave) systems, specialized [microwave antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Microwave_antenna) equipment is often required. A custom-made Luneburg lens antenna can be designed to meet specific requirements, including different sizes, feeds, and operating frequencies. A Luneburg lens can be used as the basis of a high-gain radio antenna.

A Luneburg lens antenna is comparable to a [parabolic dish antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_antenna), but uses the lens rather than a parabolic reflector as the main focusing element. As with the dish antenna, a [*feed*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_feed) to the receiver or from the transmitter is placed at the focus, the feed typically consisting of a [horn antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horn_antenna). The [phase centre](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phase_centre) of the [feed horn](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Feed_horn) must coincide with the point of focus, but since the phase centre is invariably somewhat inside the mouth of the horn, it cannot be brought right up against the surface of the lens. Consequently it is necessary to use a variety of Luneburg lens that focusses somewhat beyond its surface,[^o24] rather than the classic lens with the focus lying on the surface.

A Luneburg lens antenna offers a number of advantages over a [parabolic dish antenna](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parabolic_antenna). Because the lens is spherically symmetric, the antenna can be steered by moving the [feed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Antenna_feed) around the lens, without having to bodily rotate the whole antenna. Again, because the lens is spherically symmetric, a single lens can be used with several feeds looking in widely different directions. In contrast, if multiple feeds are used with a parabolic reflector, all must be within a small angle of the [optical axis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_axis) to avoid suffering [coma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coma_%28optics%29) (a form of de-focussing). Apart from [offset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Offset_dish_antenna) systems, dish antennas suffer from the feed and its supporting structure partially obscuring the main element (*aperture blockage*); in common with other refracting systems, the Luneburg lens antenna avoids this problem.

A variation on the Luneburg lens antenna is the *hemispherical Luneburg lens antenna* or *Luneburg reflector antenna*. This uses just one hemisphere of a Luneburg lens, with the cut surface of the sphere resting on a reflecting metal [ground plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ground_plane). The arrangement halves the weight of the lens, and the ground plane provides a convenient means of support. However, the feed does partially obscure the lens when the [angle of incidence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angle_of_incidence_%28optics%29) on the reflector is less than about 45°.

Since 2015, U.S. [wireless network operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_network) [AT&T](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AT&T) has used Luneberg lens antennas called "Giant Eyeball Antennas" to increase cell network capacity at events where large crowds of people gather.[^o19]

## Path of a ray within the lens {#Path_of_a_ray_within_the_lens}

For any spherically symmetric lens, each ray lies entirely in a plane passing through the centre of the lens. The initial direction of the ray defines a line which together with the centre-point of the lens identifies a plane bisecting the lens. Being a plane of symmetry of the lens, the [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) of the refractive index has no component perpendicular to this plane to cause the ray to deviate either to one side of it or the other. In the plane, the [circular symmetry](wikipedia://en/2217599) of the system makes it convenient to use [polar coordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polar_coordinates) $(r, \theta)$ to describe the ray's trajectory.

Given any two points on a ray (such as the point of entry and exit from the lens), [Fermat's principle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermat's_principle) asserts that the path that the ray takes between them is that which it can traverse in the least possible time. Given that the speed of light at any point in the lens is inversely proportional to the refractive index, and by [Pythagoras](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pythagoras's_theorem), the time of transit between two points $(r_1, \theta_1)$ and $(r_2, \theta_2)$ is

$$T = \int _{(r_1, \theta_1)}^{(r_2, \theta_2)} \frac{n(r)}{c} \sqrt{(r \,d\theta)^2 + d r^2} = \frac{1}{c} \int _{\theta_1}^{\theta_2} n(r) \sqrt{r^2 + \left( \frac{d r}{d \theta}\right)^2} \,d\theta,$$

where $c$ is the speed of light in vacuum. Minimizing this $T$ yields a second-order [differential equation](wikipedia://en/1424309) determining the dependence of $r$ on $\theta$ along the path of the ray. This type of minimization problem has been extensively studied in [Lagrangian mechanics](wikipedia://en/23371726), and a ready-made solution exists in the form of the [Beltrami identity](wikipedia://en/17181013), which immediately supplies the [first integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/First_integral) of this second-order equation. Substituting $L(r, r') = n(r) \sqrt{r'^2 + r^2}$ (where $r'$ represents $\tfrac{d r}{d \theta}$), into this identity gives

$$n(r) \sqrt{r'^2 + r^2} - n(r) \frac{r'^2}{\sqrt{r'^2 + r^2}} = h,$$

where $h$ is a [constant of integration](wikipedia://en/147939). This first-order differential equation is [separable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Separable_ordinary_differential_equation), that is it can be re-arranged so that $r$ only appears on one side, and $\theta$ only on the other:[^o23]

$$d \theta = \frac{h}{r \sqrt{\big(n(r)\big)^2 r^2 - h^2}} \,dr.$$

The parameter $h$ is a constant for any given ray, but differs between rays passing at different distances from the centre of the lens. For rays passing through the centre, it is zero. In some special cases, such as for Maxwell's fish-eye, this first order equation can be further integrated to give a formula for $\theta$ as a function of $r$. In general it provides the relative rates of change of $\theta$ and $r$, which may be [integrated numerically](wikipedia://en/272829) to follow the path of the ray through the lens.
