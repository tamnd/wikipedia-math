# David E. Muller

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="David E. Muller"}

**David Eugene Muller** (November 2, 1924 – April 27, 2008) was an American [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [computer scientist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_scientist). He was a professor of mathematics and computer science at the [University of Illinois](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Illinois) (1953–92), after which he became an emeritus professor, and was an adjunct professor of mathematics at the [New Mexico State University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Mexico_State_University) (1995–2008). Muller received his BS in 1947 and his PhD in 1951 in physics from [Caltech](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caltech); an honorary PhD was conferred by the [University of Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_Paris) in 1989.[^o3] He was the inventor of the [Muller C-element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C-element) (or Muller C-gate), a device used to implement [asynchronous circuitry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Asynchronous_circuit) in digital computers. He also co-invented the [Reed–Muller codes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reed–Muller_codes). He discovered the codes, and [Irving S. Reed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irving_S._Reed) proposed the majority logic decoding for the first time. Furthermore, he invented [Muller automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muller_automaton), an automaton model for [infinite words](wikipedia://en/3983826).[^o4] In [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164) Muller is known for the [Muller–Schupp theorem](wikipedia://en/54414184), joint with [Paul Schupp](wikipedia://en/54677806), characterizing [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/963042) [virtually](wikipedia://en/10377971) [free groups](wikipedia://en/59735) as finitely generated groups with [context-free](wikipedia://en/6867) [word problem](wikipedia://en/33563).[^o5]

:::

## Family {#Family}

David E. Muller was the son of [Hermann Joseph Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Joseph_Muller) and [Jessie Jacobs Muller Offermann](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jessie_Marie_Jacobs_Muller_Offermann) (formerly Jesse Marie Jacobs). He was born in [Austin, Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Austin,_Texas), when his parents taught at [The University of Texas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_University_of_Texas). His mother was one of the first women to receive a Ph.D. in mathematics in the United States, and he credited her with inspiring his early interest in mathematics.[^o7] She lost her position as an instructor in pure mathematics at Texas because she became pregnant, and according to Hermann Joseph Muller's biographer, "her colleagues felt that a mother could not give full attention to classroom duties and remain a good mother."[^o8] As a child he was with his parents in [Berlin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Berlin) and [Leningrad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leningrad) in 1933–34. His family was dissolved in the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union). He returned to Austin with his mother in July 1934. His mother obtained a divorce in Texas in the summer of 1935. Sometime between October 1935 and January 1936, Jessie Muller married Carlos Alberto Offermann, who had been working in Muller's laboratory and was on a visit to Austin from the Soviet Union at that time.[^o7] [Hermann Joseph Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Joseph_Muller) left the Soviet Union in 1937 after the start of Stalin's political persecutions. After a brief stay in [Madrid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Madrid) and [Paris](wikipedia://en/22989), in September 1937, Hermann moved to [Edinburgh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edinburgh), where he married Dorothea Kantorowicz in May 1939. They had a daughter, Helen Juliette. [Hermann Joseph Muller](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hermann_Joseph_Muller) received the [Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nobel_Prize_in_Physiology_or_Medicine) in 1946.

David E. Muller died in 2008 in [Las Cruces, New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Las_Cruces,_New_Mexico). He is survived by his children, Chandra L. Muller and Kenneth J. Muller. His half-sister, [Helen J. Muller](http://www.unm.edu/~hmuller/), is a professor emerita at the [University of New Mexico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_of_New_Mexico). He was predeceased by his wife Alice Mimi Muller, who died in Urbana, Illinois, in 1989, and divorced (posthumously) in 2009 from his second wife, Denise Impens Muller, in Las Cruces, New Mexico.
