# Wirtinger derivatives

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Wirtinger derivatives"}

In [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) of one and [several complex variables](wikipedia://en/360243), **Wirtinger derivatives** (sometimes also called **Wirtinger operators**[^o2]), named after [Wilhelm Wirtinger](wikipedia://en/2044921) who introduced them in 1927 in the course of his studies on the [theory of functions of several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables), are [partial differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) of the first order which behave in a very similar manner to the ordinary [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) with respect to one [real variable](wikipedia://en/1268958), when applied to [holomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/14110), [antiholomorphic functions](wikipedia://en/464178) or simply [differentiable functions](wikipedia://en/330206) on [complex domains](wikipedia://en/16796643). These operators permit the construction of a [differential calculus](wikipedia://en/50416) for such functions that is entirely analogous to the ordinary differential calculus for [functions of real variables](wikipedia://en/1268958).[^o3]

:::

## Historical notes {#Historical_notes}

### Early days (1899–1911): the work of Henri Poincaré {#Early_days_(1899–1911):_the_work_of_Henri_Poincaré}

Wirtinger derivatives were used in [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759) at least as early as in the paper (Poincaré 1899), as briefly noted by (Cherry Ye 2001, 31) and by (Remmert 1991, 66–67).[^o9] In the third paragraph of his 1899 paper,[^o10] [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740) first defines the [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable) in $\mathbb{C}^n$ and its [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) as follows

$$\begin{cases} x_k+iy_k=z_k\\ x_k-iy_k=u_k \end{cases} \qquad 1 \leqslant k \leqslant n.$$

Then he writes the equation defining the functions $V$ he calls *biharmonique*,[^o11] previously written using [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565) with respect to the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [variables](wikipedia://en/3728109) $x_k, y_q$ with $k, q$ ranging from 1 to $n$, exactly in the following way[^o12]

$$\frac{d^2 V}{dz_k \, du_q}=0$$

This implies that he implicitly used (definition 2) below: to see this it is sufficient to compare equations 2 and 2' of (Poincaré 1899, 112). Apparently, this paper was not noticed by early researchers in the [theory of functions of several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables): in the papers of (Levi-Civita 1905), (Levi 1910) (and (Levi 1911)) and of (Amoroso 1912) all fundamental [partial differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) of the theory are expressed directly by using [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives) respect to the [real](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_part) and [imaginary parts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Imaginary_part) of the [complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable) involved. In the long survey paper by (Osgood 1966) (first published in 1913),[^o19] [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives) with respect to each [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable) of a [holomorphic function of several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables) seem to be meant as [formal derivatives](wikipedia://en/3832917): as a matter of fact when [Osgood](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Fogg_Osgood) expresses the [pluriharmonic operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pluriharmonic_operator)[^o20] and the [Levi operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_operator), he follows the established practice of [Amoroso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luigi_Amoroso), [Levi](wikipedia://en/11114879) and [Levi-Civita](wikipedia://en/499180).

### The work of Dimitrie Pompeiu in 1912 and 1913: a new formulation {#The_work_of_Dimitrie_Pompeiu_in_1912_and_1913:_a_new_formulation}

According to (Henrici 1993, 294), a new step in the definition of the concept was taken by [Dimitrie Pompeiu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimitrie_Pompeiu): in the paper (Pompeiu 1912), given a [complex valued](wikipedia://en/5826) [differentiable function](wikipedia://en/330206) (in the sense of [real analysis](wikipedia://en/26478)) of one [complex variable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_variable) $g(z)$ defined in the [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) of a given [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28mathematics%29) $z_0 \in \mathbb{C},$ he defines the areolar derivative as the following [limit](wikipedia://en/26551602)

$${\frac{\partial g}{\partial \bar{z}}(z_0)}\mathrel{\overset{\mathrm{def}}{=}}\lim_{r \to 0}\frac{1}{2\pi i r^2} \oint_{\Gamma(z_0,r)} g(z)\mathrm{d}z,$$

where $\Gamma(z_0,r)=\partial D(z_0,r)$ is the [boundary](wikipedia://en/72824) of a [disk](wikipedia://en/329542) of radius $r$ entirely contained in the [domain of definition](wikipedia://en/50263) of $g(z),$ i.e. his bounding [circle](wikipedia://en/6220).[^o24] This is evidently an alternative definition of Wirtinger derivative respect to the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) [variable](wikipedia://en/3728109):[^o25] it is a more general one, since, as noted a by (Henrici 1993, 294), the limit may exist for functions that are not even [differentiable](wikipedia://en/330206) at $z=z_0.$[^o27] According to (Fichera 1969, 28), the first to identify the areolar derivative as a [weak derivative](wikipedia://en/1411087) in the [sense of Sobolev](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Generalized_derivative) was [Ilia Vekua](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ilia_Vekua).[^o29] In his following paper, (Pompeiu 1913) uses this newly defined concept in order to introduce his generalization of [Cauchy's integral formula](wikipedia://en/72827), the now called [Cauchy–Pompeiu formula](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy–Pompeiu_formula).

### The work of Wilhelm Wirtinger {#The_work_of_Wilhelm_Wirtinger}

The first systematic introduction of Wirtinger derivatives seems due to [Wilhelm Wirtinger](wikipedia://en/2044921) in the paper (Wirtinger 1927) in order to simplify the calculations of quantities occurring in the [theory of functions of several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables): as a result of the introduction of these [differential operators](wikipedia://en/263886), the form of all the differential operators commonly used in the theory, like the [Levi operator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Levi_operator) and the [Cauchy–Riemann operator](wikipedia://en/7583), is considerably simplified and consequently easier to handle. The paper is deliberately written from a formal point of view, i.e. without giving a rigorous derivation of the properties deduced.

## Formal definition {#Formal_definition}

Despite their ubiquitous use,[^o35] it seems that there is no text listing all the properties of Wirtinger derivatives: however, fairly complete references are the short course on [multidimensional complex analysis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables) by (Andreotti 1976, 3–5),[^o37] the [monograph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monograph) of (Gunning Rossi 1965, 3–6),[^o39] and the monograph of (Kaup Kaup 1983, 2,4)[^o41] which are used as general references in this and the following sections.

### Functions of one complex variable {#Functions_of_one_complex_variable}

(Definition 1.) Consider the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) $\mathbb{C} \equiv \mathbb{R}^2 = \{(x,y) \mid x, y \in \mathbb{R} \}$ (in a sense of expressing a complex number $z = x + iy$ for real numbers $x$ and $y$). Let $\bar{z}$ represent the [complex conjugate](wikipedia://en/173918) of $z \ .$ Then the Wirtinger derivatives are defined as the following [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) [partial differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) of first order:

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial z} &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x} - i \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \right) \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}} &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial}{\partial y} \right) \end{aligned}$$

Clearly, the natural [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of definition of these partial differential operators is the space of [$C^1$ functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function#Differentiability_classes) on a [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643) $\Omega \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2,$ but, since these operators are [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear) and have [constant coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_coefficients), they can be readily extended to every [space](wikipedia://en/340630) of [generalized functions](wikipedia://en/1580246)

The operators can be derived by noting that $x = \frac{1}{2} (z + \bar{z}), y = \frac{1}{2i} (z - \bar{z})$, and taking the derivative with respect to $\bar{z}$ using the multivariable chain rule, treating $x, y$ as intermediate variables.

### Functions of *n* > 1 complex variables {#Functions_of_n_>_1_complex_variables}

(Definition 2.) Consider the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) on the [complex field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_field)

$$\mathbb{C}^n = \mathbb{R}^{2n} = \left\{\left( \mathbf{x}, \mathbf{y} \right) = \left(x_1,\ldots,x_n, y_1, \ldots, y_n\right) \mid \mathbf{x},\mathbf{y} \in \mathbb{R}^n \right\}.$$

The Wirtinger derivatives are defined as the following [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_operator) [partial differential operators](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_operator) of first order:

$$\begin{cases} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_1} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}- i \frac{\partial}{\partial y_1} \right) \\ \qquad \vdots \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial z_n} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x_n}- i \frac{\partial}{\partial y_n} \right) \\ \end{cases}, \qquad \begin{cases} \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_1} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x_1}+ i \frac{\partial}{\partial y_1} \right) \\ \qquad \vdots \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_n} = \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial}{\partial x_n}+ i \frac{\partial}{\partial y_n} \right) \\ \end{cases}.$$

As for Wirtinger derivatives for functions of one complex variable, the natural [domain](wikipedia://en/50263) of definition of these partial differential operators is again the space of [$C^1$ functions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function#Differentiability_classes) on a [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643) $\Omega \subset \mathbb{R}^{2n},$ and again, since these operators are [linear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear) and have [constant coefficients](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Constant_coefficients), they can be readily extended to every [space](wikipedia://en/340630) of [generalized functions](wikipedia://en/1580246).

:::

## Relation with complex differentiation {#Relation_with_complex_differentiation}

When a function $f$ is [complex differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_derivative) at a point, the Wirtinger derivative $\partial f/\partial z$ agrees with the complex derivative $df/dz$. This follows from the [Cauchy-Riemann equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cauchy-Riemann_equations). For the complex function $f(z) = u(z) + iv(z)$ which is complex differentiable

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} - i \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right) \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} -i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \right) \\ &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial z} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial z} = \frac{d f}{d z} \end{aligned}$$

where the third equality uses the first definition of Wirtinger's derivatives for $u$ and $v$.

It can also be done through actual application of the Cauchy-Riemann equations.

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial f}{\partial z} &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} - i \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \right) \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} -i \frac{\partial u}{\partial y} + \frac{\partial v}{\partial y} \right) \\ &= \frac{1}{2} \left( \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} + \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} \right) \\ &= \frac{\partial u}{\partial x} + i \frac{\partial v}{\partial x} = \frac{d f}{d z} \end{aligned}$$

The final equality comes from it being one of four equivalent formulations of the complex derivative through partial derivatives of the components.

The second Wirtinger derivative is also related with complex differentiation; $\frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{z}} = 0$ is equivalent to the Cauchy-Riemann equations in a complex form.

## Basic properties {#Basic_properties}

In the present section and in the following ones it is assumed that $z \in \mathbb{C}^n$ is a [complex vector](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector) and that $z \equiv (x,y) = (x_1,\ldots,x_n,y_1,\ldots,y_n)$ where $x,y$ are [real vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_vector), with *n* ≥ 1: also it is assumed that the [subset](wikipedia://en/27631) $\Omega$ can be thought of as a [domain](wikipedia://en/16796643) in the [real](wikipedia://en/20646438) [euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^{2n}$ or in its [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/14828) [complex](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_field) counterpart $\mathbb{C}^n.$ All the proofs are easy consequences of (definition 1) and (definition 2) and of the corresponding properties of the [derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivative_%28mathematics%29) (ordinary or [partial](wikipedia://en/52565)).

### Linearity {#Linearity}

(Lemma 1.) If $f,g \in C^1(\Omega)$ and $\alpha,\beta$ are [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), then for $i=1,\dots,n$ the following equalities hold

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i} \left(\alpha f+\beta g\right) &= \alpha\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_i} + \beta\frac{\partial g}{\partial z_i} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_i} \left(\alpha f+\beta g\right) &= \alpha\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}_i} + \beta\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bar{z}_i} \end{aligned}$$

### Product rule {#Product_rule}

(Lemma 2.) If $f,g \in C^1(\Omega),$ then for $i= 1,\dots,n$ the [product rule](wikipedia://en/248988) holds

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i} (f\cdot g) &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial z_i}\cdot g + f\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial z_i} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_i} (f\cdot g) &= \frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}_i}\cdot g + f\cdot\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bar{z}_i} \end{aligned}$$

This property implies that Wirtinger derivatives are [derivations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Derivation_%28abstract_algebra%29) from the [abstract algebra](wikipedia://en/19616384) point of view, exactly like ordinary [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) are.

### Chain rule {#Chain_rule}

This property takes two different forms respectively for functions of one and [several complex variables](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Several_complex_variables): for the *n* > 1 case, to express the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) in its full generality it is necessary to consider two [domains](wikipedia://en/16796643) $\Omega'\subseteq\mathbb{C}^m$ and $\Omega''\subseteq\mathbb{C}^p$ and two [maps](wikipedia://en/516931) $g: \Omega'\to\Omega$ and $f:\Omega \to \Omega''$ having natural [smoothness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_function) requirements.[^o50]

#### Functions of one complex variable {#Functions_of_one_complex_variable}

(Lemma 3.1) If $f,g \in C^1(\Omega),$ and $g(\Omega ) \subseteq \Omega,$ then the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) holds

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial z} (f\circ g) &= \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\circ g \right) \frac{\partial g}{\partial z} + \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}}\circ g \right) \frac{\partial\bar{g}}{\partial z} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}} (f\circ g) &= \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\circ g \right)\frac{\partial g}{\partial\bar{z}}+ \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}}\circ g \right) \frac{\partial\bar{g}}{\partial\bar{z}} \end{aligned}$$

#### Functions of *n* > 1 complex variables {#Functions_of_n_>_1_complex_variables}

(Lemma 3.2) If $g \in C^1(\Omega',\Omega)$ and $f \in C^1(\Omega,\Omega''),$ then for $i= 1,\dots,n$ the following form of the [chain rule](wikipedia://en/6113) holds

$$\begin{aligned} \frac{\partial}{\partial z_i} \left(f\circ g\right) &= \sum_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_j}\circ g \right) \frac{\partial g_j}{\partial z_i} + \sum_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}_j}\circ g \right) \frac{\partial \bar{g}_j}{\partial z_i} \\ \frac{\partial}{\partial\bar{z}_i} \left(f\circ g\right) &= \sum_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_j}\circ g \right) \frac{\partial g_j}{\partial\bar{z}_i} + \sum_{j=1}^n\left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}_j}\circ g \right)\frac{\partial \bar{g}_j}{\partial\bar{z}_i} \end{aligned}$$

### Conjugation {#Conjugation}

(Lemma 4.) If $f\in C^1(\Omega),$ then for $i=1,\dots,n$ the following equalities hold

$$\begin{aligned} \overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_i}} &= \overline{\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial z_i}\Big)} =\frac{\partial \bar{f}}{\partial \bar{z}_i} \\ \overline{\frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{z}_i}} &= \overline{\Big(\frac{\partial f}{\partial \bar{z}_i}\Big)} =\frac{\partial \bar{f}}{\partial z_i} \end{aligned}$$
