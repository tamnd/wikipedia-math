# Ya'qub ibn Tariq

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Ya'qub ibn Tariq"}

**Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq** (یعقوب ابن طارق; referred to by some sources as **Yaʿqūb**; [^n1] died 796) was a [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_people) astronomer and mathematician who lived in [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Baghdad).

:::

## Career {#Career}

*Image: Baghdad 150 to 300 AH.png.* [William Muir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Muir)'s depiction of the original [round city of Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_city_of_Baghdad) (1883), where Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq was active during his career

Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq was active in [Baghdad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Round_city_of_Baghdad) as an [astronomer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomer) during the rule of the second [Abbasid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abbasid) caliph, [al-Manṣūr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Manṣūr) (754).[^n2][^o6] He seems not to have been aware of [Ptolemaic astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Greek_astronomy#Ptolemaic_astronomy),[^n2] and used a [Zoroastrian calendar](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iranian_calendars#Zoroastrian_calendar), which consisted of 12 months of 30 days each, with any remaining days being added after the eighth month, [Ābān](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aban_%28month%29).[^n2]

Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq's [treatise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise) ar dealt with [cosmography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cosmography) (the placement and sizes of the heavenly bodies).[^n3] The estimations of their sizes and distances in ar were tabulated in the 11th century by the polymath [al-Bīrūnī](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Bīrūnī), in his [work on India](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alberuni's_India). According to al-Bīrūnī, Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq gave the [radius of the Earth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Earth_radius) as 1,050 ar, the diameter of the [Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moon) and [Mercury](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mercury_%28planet%29) as 5,000 ar (4.8 Earth radii), and the diameter of the other heavenly bodies (Venus, the Sun, Mars, Jupiter, and Saturn) as 20,000 ar (19.0 Earth radii).[^n4] He wrote that each of the planets had six associated spheres, that the Sun possessed two spheres, and the Moon three. He also spoke of planetary [epicycles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Epicycle) and speeds.[^n5] His values for the [longitudes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Longitude) and [apogees](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apogee) of celestial objects originated from a [Persian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Persian_language) set of astronomical tables, the fa written by [Yazdegerd III](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yazdegerd_III), although he used methods originating from the work of [Indian astronomers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Indian_astronomy) to calculate the [lunar phases](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase).[^n1]

The Christian [astrologer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astrologer) [Ibn Hibintā](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ibn_Hibintā) mentioned Yaʿqūb, noting that he used the positions of the Sun and the stars to determine the [latitude](wikipedia://en/17616) of places.[^n6]

## Works {#Works}

Works ascribed to Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq include:[^n3]

- ar (*"Astronomical Tables in the '[Sindhind](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zij_as-Sindhind)' Resolved for each Degree"*);
- ar ("*Arrangement of the Orbs*"). Part of this work, the earliest surviving description of the celestial sky by an Islamic astronomer, is preserved by Ibn Hibintā.[^n7]
- ar ("*Rationales*");
- ar ("*Distribution of the ar of the [Sine](wikipedia://en/5010838)*");
- ar ("*Elevation along the [Arc of the Meridian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meridian_arc)*").

An astrological work, ar ("*The Chapters*"), is ascribed to Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq by an unreliable source.[^n3]

Yaʿqūb ibn Ṭāriq's *[zij](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zij)*, written in around 770, was based on a [Sanskrit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanskrit) work,[^n3] thought to be similar to the *[Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta)*. It was brought to the court of [al-Mansūr](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Mansūr), the third [caliph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Caliph) of the [Fatimid Caliphate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fatimid_Caliphate), from [Sindh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sindh),[^n8] reportedly by the Sindhi astronomer [Kankah](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kankah).[^n9]
