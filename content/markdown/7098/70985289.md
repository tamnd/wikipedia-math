# Staircase paradox

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Staircase paradox"}

*Image: Quadrat Diagonale.svg.* Staircases converging pointwise to the diagonal of a unit square, but not converging to its length

In [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), the **staircase paradox** is a [pathological example](wikipedia://en/372742) showing that [limits](wikipedia://en/26551602) of curves do not necessarily preserve their [length](wikipedia://en/18542).

:::

## Description {#Description}

The paradox consists of a sequence of "staircase" [polygonal chains](wikipedia://en/12056032) in a [unit square](wikipedia://en/285301), formed from horizontal and vertical [line segments](wikipedia://en/22634860) of decreasing length, so that these staircases [converge uniformly](wikipedia://en/50652) to the diagonal of the square. However, each staircase has length two, while the length of the diagonal is the [square root of 2](wikipedia://en/341682) (approximately 1.4142), so the sequence of staircase lengths does not converge to the length of the diagonal.

[Martin Gardner](wikipedia://en/20435) calls this "an ancient geometrical paradox". The staircase paradox shows that, for curves under uniform convergence, the length of a curve is not a [continuous function](wikipedia://en/6122) of the curve.

## Explanation {#Explanation}

For any [smooth curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Smooth_curve), polygonal chains with segment lengths decreasing to zero, connecting consecutive [vertices](wikipedia://en/638899) along the curve, always converge to the [arc length](wikipedia://en/1898401). The failure of the staircase curves to converge to the correct length can be explained by the fact that some of their vertices do not lie on the diagonal.

## Usage {#Usage}

*Image: circle\_staircase\_paradox.svg.* A fallacious approach to calculating the circumference of a circle due to the staircase paradox

As well as highlighting the need for careful definitions of arc length in mathematics education, the staircase paradox has applications in [digital geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_geometry), where it motivates methods of estimating the perimeter of pixelated shapes that do not merely sum the lengths of boundaries between pixels.

In [higher dimensions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Higher_dimension), the [Schwarz lantern](wikipedia://en/58278053) provides an analogous example showing that polyhedral surfaces that [converge pointwise](wikipedia://en/340198) to a curved surface do not necessarily converge to its area, even when the vertices all lie on the surface.
