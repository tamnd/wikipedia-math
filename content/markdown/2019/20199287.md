# Graph canonization

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Graph canonization"}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a branch of mathematics, **graph canonization** is the problem of finding a [canonical form](wikipedia://en/515096) of a given graph *G*. A canonical form is a [labeled graph](wikipedia://en/1609861) Canon(*G*) that is [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577) to *G*, such that every graph that is isomorphic to *G* has the same canonical form as *G*. Thus, from a solution to the graph canonization problem, one could also solve the problem of [graph isomorphism](wikipedia://en/247577): to test whether two graphs *G* and *H* are isomorphic, compute their canonical forms Canon(*G*) and Canon(*H*), and test whether these two canonical forms are identical.

:::

The canonical form of a graph is an example of a [complete](wikipedia://en/9903220) [graph invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graph_invariant): every two isomorphic graphs have the same canonical form, and every two non-isomorphic graphs have different canonical forms.[^o2][^o3] Conversely, every complete invariant of graphs may be used to construct a canonical form.[^o4] The vertex set of an *n*-vertex graph may be identified with the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) from 1 to *n*, and using such an identification a canonical form of a graph may also be described as a [permutation](wikipedia://en/44027) of its vertices. Canonical forms of a graph are also called **canonical labelings**,[^o5] and graph canonization is also sometimes known as **graph canonicalization**.

## Computational complexity {#Computational_complexity}

::: {#o7 .problem cue=unsolved}

Is graph canonization polynomial-time equivalent to the graph isomorphism problem?

:::

The **[graph isomorphism problem](wikipedia://en/1950766)** is the [computational problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computational_problem) of determining whether two finite [graphs](wikipedia://en/325806) are [isomorphic](wikipedia://en/247577). Clearly, the graph canonization problem is at least as [computationally hard](wikipedia://en/7543) as the [graph isomorphism problem](wikipedia://en/1950766). In fact, graph isomorphism is even [AC<sup>0</sup>](wikipedia://en/7400698)-[reducible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Reducible_%28complexity%29) to graph canonization. However, it is still an open question whether the two problems are [polynomial-time equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynomial-time_equivalent).[^o3]

In 2019, [László Babai](wikipedia://en/3461604) announced a [quasi-polynomial-time](wikipedia://en/25269049) algorithm for graph canonization, that is, one with running time $2^{O((\log n)^c)}$ for some fixed $c > 0$.[^o8] While the existence of (deterministic) polynomial-time algorithms for graph isomorphism is still an open problem in [computational complexity theory](wikipedia://en/7543), in 1977 [László Babai](wikipedia://en/3461604) reported that with probability at least 1 − exp(−O(*n*)), a simple vertex-classification algorithm produces a canonical labeling of a graph chosen uniformly at random from the set of all *n*-vertex graphs after only two refinement steps. Small modifications and an added [depth-first search](wikipedia://en/97034) step produce canonical labelings of such uniformly-chosen random graphs in linear expected time. This result sheds some light on the question of why many reported graph isomorphism algorithms behave well in practice.[^o9][^o10] This was an important breakthrough in [probabilistic complexity theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Probabilistic_complexity_theory), which became widely known in its manuscript form and which was still cited as an "unpublished manuscript" long after it was reported at a symposium.

A commonly known canonical form is the [lexicographically smallest](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lexicographical_order) graph within the [isomorphism class](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphism_class), which is the graph of the class with lexicographically smallest [adjacency matrix](wikipedia://en/244463) considered as a linear string. However, the computation of the lexicographically smallest graph is [NP-hard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NP-hard). [^o11]

For trees, a concise polynomial-time canonization algorithm requiring *O*(*n*) space is presented by (Read 1972).[^o13] Begin by labeling each vertex with the string 01. Iteratively for each non-leaf *x*, remove the leading 0 and trailing 1 from *x*'s label; then, sort *x*'s label along with the labels of all adjacent leaves in lexicographic order. Concatenate these sorted labels, add back a leading 0 and trailing 1, make this the new label of *x*, and delete the adjacent leaves. If there are two vertices remaining, concatenate their labels in lexicographic order.

## Applications {#Applications}

Graph canonization is the essence of many graph isomorphism algorithms. One of the leading tools is Nauty.[^o16]

A common application of graph canonization is in graphical [data mining](wikipedia://en/42253), in particular in [chemical database](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_database) applications.[^o17]

A number of [identifiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Identifier) for [chemical substances](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chemical_substance), such as [SMILES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SMILES) and [InChI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InChI), use canonization steps in their computation, which is essentially the canonization of the graph which represents the molecule.[^o18][^o19][^o20] These identifiers are designed to provide a standard (and sometimes human-readable) way to encode molecular information and to facilitate the search for such information in databases and on the web.
