# Conel Hugh O'Donel Alexander

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conel Hugh O'Donel Alexander"}

**Conel Hugh O'Donel Alexander** (19 April 1909 – 15 February 1974), known as **Hugh Alexander** and **C. H. O'D. Alexander**, was an Irish-born British [cryptanalyst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cryptanalyst), chess player, and chess writer. He worked on the German [Enigma machine](wikipedia://en/9256) at [Bletchley Park](wikipedia://en/4037) during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War), and was later the head of the [cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715) division at [GCHQ](wikipedia://en/12884) for 25 years. He was twice [British chess champion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_chess_champion) and earned the title of [International Master](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Master).

:::

## Early life {#Early_life}

Hugh Alexander was born into an [Anglo-Irish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anglo-Irish) family on 19 April 1909 in [Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cork_%28city%29), [Ireland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ireland), the eldest child of Conel William Long Alexander, an [engineering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Engineering) professor at [University College, Cork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/University_College,_Cork) (UCC), and Hilda Barbara Bennett.[^o4] His father died in 1920 (during the [Irish War of Independence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Irish_War_of_Independence)), and the family moved to [Birmingham](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham), [England](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/England), where he attended [King Edward's School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King_Edward's_School,_Birmingham).[^o4] Alexander won a scholarship to study [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) at [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge), in 1928, graduating with a first in 1931.[^o4] He represented [Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University) in chess.

From 1932, Alexander taught mathematics at [Winchester College](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Winchester_College), and married Enid Constance Crichton Neate (1900–1982) on 22 December 1934.[^o4] Their elder son was [Sir Michael O'Donel Bjarne Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michael_Alexander_%28diplomat%29) (1936–2002), a diplomat. The Alexanders' other son was [Patrick Macgillicuddy Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Patrick_Alexander_%28poet%29) (20 March 1940 – 21 September 2005), a poet who settled in Australia in 1960. In 1938, Hugh Alexander left teaching and became head of research at the [John Lewis Partnership](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Lewis_Partnership).[^o4]

World War II began while Alexander was competing in the [8th Chess Olympiad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/8th_Chess_Olympiad) in Buenos Aires, Argentina, prompting him and the rest of the English team to abandon the competition and return to the UK.

## Bletchley Park and GCHQ {#Bletchley_Park_and_GCHQ}

In February 1940, Alexander arrived at [Bletchley Park](wikipedia://en/4037), the British codebreaking centre during the [Second World War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_World_War). He joined [Hut 6](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut_6), the section tasked with breaking [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germany) [Army](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_Army_%281935–1945%29) and [Air Force](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luftwaffe) [Enigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Enigma_%28machine%29) messages. In 1941, Alexander transferred to [Hut 8](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hut_8), the corresponding hut working on [Naval Enigma](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naval_Enigma).

He became deputy head of Hut 8 under [Alan Turing](wikipedia://en/1208). Alexander was more involved with the day-to-day operations of the hut than Turing, and, while Turing was visiting the [United States](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States), Alexander formally became the head of Hut 8 *circa* November 1942. Other senior colleagues included [Stuart Milner-Barry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stuart_Milner-Barry), [Gordon Welchman](wikipedia://en/2385693), and [Harry Golombek](wikipedia://en/1820134). In October 1944, Alexander was transferred to work on the [Japanese](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Japan) [JN-25](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/JN-25) code.

In mid-1946, Alexander joined [GCHQ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Communications_Headquarters) (under the control of the [Foreign Office](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Foreign_Office)), which was the post-war successor organisation to the [Government Code and Cypher School](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Government_Code_and_Cypher_School) at Bletchley Park. By 1949, he had been promoted to the head of "Section H" ([cryptanalysis](wikipedia://en/5715)), a post he retained until his retirement in 1971. He was appointed [Officer of the Order of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Officer_of_the_Order_of_the_British_Empire) in the [1946 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1946_New_Year_Honours), [Commander of the Order of the British Empire](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Commander_of_the_Order_of_the_British_Empire) in the [1955 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1955_New_Year_Honours) and [Companion of the Order of St Michael and St George](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Companion_of_the_Order_of_St_Michael_and_St_George) in the [1970 New Year Honours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/1970_New_Year_Honours).

[MI5](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MI5)'s [Peter Wright](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Wright_%28MI5_officer%29), in his 1987 best-selling book *[Spycatcher: The Candid Autobiography of a Senior Intelligence Officer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Spycatcher:_The_Candid_Autobiography_of_a_Senior_Intelligence_Officer)*, wrote about Alexander's assistance to MI5 in the ongoing [Venona project](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venona_project), as well as other important mutual cooperation between the two organizations, which broke down previous barriers to progress. "Any help is gratefully received in this department", Alexander told Wright, and that proved the case from then on. Wright also lauded Alexander's professionalism, and opined that the exceptional mental demands of his cryptanalytical career and chess hobby likely contributed to Alexander's early death at age 64, despite his healthy lifestyle.[^o9]

## Chess career {#Chess_career}

*Image: Conel Hugh O'Donel Alexander.jpg.* C. H. O'D. Alexander

Alexander represented [Cambridge University](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University) in the Varsity chess matches of 1929, 1930, 1931 and 1932 (he studied at [King's College, Cambridge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/King's_College,_Cambridge)). He was twice a winner of the [British Chess Championship](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/British_Chess_Championship), in 1938 and 1956. Alexander represented England in the [Chess Olympiad](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chess_Olympiad) six times, in 1933, 1935, 1937, 1939, 1954 and 1958. At the 1939 Olympiad in [Buenos Aires](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Buenos_Aires), [Argentina](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Argentina), Alexander had to leave part-way through the event, along with the rest of the English team, because of the declaration of [World War II](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_War_II), since he was required at home for codebreaking duties. He was also the non-playing captain of England from 1964 to 1970. Alexander was awarded the [International Master](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Master) title in 1950 and the International Master for [Correspondence Chess](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Correspondence_Chess) title in 1970. He won [Hastings](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hastings_International_Chess_Congress) 1946/47 with the score 7½/9, a point ahead of [Savielly Tartakower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Savielly_Tartakower).[^o11][^o12] Alexander's best tournament result may have been first equal (with [David Bronstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bronstein)) at Hastings 1953/54, where he went undefeated and beat Soviet grandmasters [David Bronstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Bronstein) and [Alexander Tolush](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexander_Tolush) in individual games. Alexander's opportunities to appear abroad were limited as he was not allowed to play chess in the [Soviet bloc](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_bloc) because of his secret work in cryptography.[^o13] He was also the chess columnist of *[The Sunday Times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sunday_Times_%28UK%29)* in the 1960s and 1970s.

Many knowledgeable chess people believe that Alexander had [Grandmaster](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grandmaster_%28chess%29) potential, had he been able to develop his chess abilities further.[^o14] Many top players peak in their late twenties and early thirties, but for Alexander this stretch coincided with World War II, when high-level competitive opportunities were unavailable. After this, his professional responsibilities as a senior cryptanalyst limited his top-class appearances. He defeated [Mikhail Botvinnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mikhail_Botvinnik) in one game of a team radio match against the [Soviet Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Soviet_Union) in 1946, at a time when Botvinnik was probably the world's top player. Alexander made important theoretical contributions to the [Dutch Defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dutch_Defence) and [Petroff Defence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Petroff_Defence).

## In popular culture {#In_popular_culture}

Alexander is portrayed by actor [Matthew Goode](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matthew_Goode) in the 2014 film *[The Imitation Game](wikipedia://en/40281611)*, which depicts the British attempts to crack the Enigma machine at Bletchley Park.[^o16]
