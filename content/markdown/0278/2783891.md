# Noetherian scheme

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Noetherian scheme"}

In [algebraic geometry](wikipedia://en/1997), a **Noetherian scheme** is a [scheme](wikipedia://en/364754) that admits a finite [covering](wikipedia://en/317552) by [open](wikipedia://en/39358) affine subsets [$\operatorname{Spec} A_i$](wikipedia://en/37291), where each $A_i$ is a [Noetherian ring](wikipedia://en/56129). More generally, a scheme is **locally Noetherian** if it is covered by spectra of Noetherian rings. Thus, a scheme is Noetherian if and only if it is locally Noetherian and [compact](wikipedia://en/6042). As with Noetherian rings, the concept is named after [Emmy Noether](wikipedia://en/149896).

:::

It can be shown that, in a locally Noetherian scheme, if $\operatorname{Spec} A$ is an open affine subset, then *A* is a Noetherian ring; in particular, $\operatorname{Spec} A$ is a Noetherian scheme if and only if *A* is a Noetherian ring. For a locally Noetherian scheme *X,* the [local rings](wikipedia://en/153391) $\mathcal{O}_{X, x}$ are also Noetherian rings.

A Noetherian scheme is a [Noetherian topological space](wikipedia://en/2936080). But the converse is false in general; consider, for example, the spectrum of a non-Noetherian [valuation ring](wikipedia://en/966856).

The definitions extend to [formal schemes](wikipedia://en/18829483).

## Properties and Noetherian hypotheses {#Properties_and_Noetherian_hypotheses}

Having a (locally) Noetherian hypothesis for a statement about schemes generally makes a lot of problems more accessible because they sufficiently rigidify many of its properties.

Any Noetherian scheme can only have finitely many [irreducible components](wikipedia://en/2652476).[^o3]

Every [morphism](wikipedia://en/7482797) from a Noetherian scheme $X \to S$ is [quasi-compact](wikipedia://en/12410777).[^o4]

### Dévissage {#Dévissage}

One of the most important structure theorems about Noetherian rings and Noetherian schemes is the [dévissage theorem](wikipedia://en/26377503). This makes it possible to decompose arguments about [coherent sheaves](wikipedia://en/603780) into inductive arguments. Given a [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence) of coherent sheaves

> $$0 \to \mathcal{E}' \to \mathcal{E} \to \mathcal{E}'' \to 0,$$

proving one of the sheaves has some property is equivalent to proving the other two have the property. In particular, given a fixed coherent sheaf $\mathcal{F}$ and a sub-coherent sheaf $\mathcal{F}'$, showing $\mathcal{F}$ has some property can be reduced to looking at $\mathcal{F}'$ and $\mathcal{F}/\mathcal{F}'$. Since this process can only be non-trivially applied only a finite number of times, this makes many induction arguments possible.

### Homological properties {#Homological_properties}

There are many nice [homological](wikipedia://en/142432) properties of Noetherian schemes.[^o7]

#### Čech and sheaf cohomology {#Čech_and_sheaf_cohomology}

[Čech cohomology](wikipedia://en/856614) and [sheaf cohomology](wikipedia://en/1055357) agree on an affine open cover. This makes it possible to compute the sheaf cohomology of $\mathbb{P}^n_S$ using Čech cohomology for the standard open cover.

#### Compatibility of colimits with cohomology {#Compatibility_of_colimits_with_cohomology}

Given a [direct system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Direct_system) $\{ \mathcal{F}_\alpha, \phi_{\alpha\beta} \}_{\alpha \in \Lambda}$ of sheaves of abelian groups on a Noetherian scheme, there is a canonical isomorphism

> $$\varinjlim H^i(X,\mathcal{F}_\alpha) \to H^i(X, \varinjlim \mathcal{F}_\alpha)$$

meaning the [functors](wikipedia://en/10987)

> $$H^i(X,-): \text{Ab}(X) \to \text{Ab}$$

preserve direct limits and [coproducts](wikipedia://en/310889).

#### Derived direct image {#Derived_direct_image}

Given a [locally finite type](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Locally_of_finite_type) morphism $f:X \to S$ to a Noetherian scheme $S$ and a complex of sheaves $\mathcal{E}^\bullet \in D^b_{Coh}(X)$ with bounded [coherent cohomology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coherent_cohomology) such that the sheaves $H^i(\mathcal{E}^\bullet)$ have proper support over $S$, then the derived pushforward $\mathbf{R}f_*(\mathcal{E}^\bullet)$ has bounded coherent cohomology over $S$, meaning it is an object in $D^b_{Coh}(S)$.[^o11]

## Examples {#Examples}

Most schemes of interest are Noetherian schemes.

### Locally of finite type over a Noetherian base {#Locally_of_finite_type_over_a_Noetherian_base}

Another class of examples of Noetherian schemes[^o15] are families of schemes $X \to S$ where the base $S$ is Noetherian and $X$ is of [finite type](wikipedia://en/70728366) over $S$. This includes many examples, such as the [connected components](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_component_%28topology%29) of a [Hilbert scheme](wikipedia://en/6612581), i.e. with a fixed Hilbert polynomial. This is important because it implies many [moduli spaces](wikipedia://en/361609) encountered in the wild are Noetherian, such as the [Moduli of algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/3010057) and [Moduli of stable vector bundles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Moduli_of_stable_vector_bundles). Also, this property can be used to show many schemes considered in algebraic geometry are in fact Noetherian.

### Quasi-projective varieties {#Quasi-projective_varieties}

In particular, quasi-projective varieties are Noetherian schemes. This class includes [algebraic curves](wikipedia://en/253260), [elliptic curves](wikipedia://en/10225), [abelian varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abelian_varieties), [Calabi-Yau schemes](wikipedia://en/188932), [Shimura varieties](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shimura_varieties), [K3 surfaces](wikipedia://en/646116), and [cubic surfaces](wikipedia://en/526126). Basically all of the objects from classical algebraic geometry fit into this class of examples.

### Infinitesimal deformations of Noetherian schemes {#Infinitesimal_deformations_of_Noetherian_schemes}

In particular, infinitesimal deformations of Noetherian schemes are again Noetherian. For example, given a curve $C / \text{Spec}(\mathbb{F}_q)$, any [deformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deformation_theory) $\mathcal{C}/\text{Spec}(\mathbb{F}_{q}[\varepsilon]/(\varepsilon^n))$ is also a Noetherian scheme. A tower of such deformations can be used to construct formal Noetherian schemes.

### Non-examples {#Non-examples}

#### Schemes over Adelic bases {#Schemes_over_Adelic_bases}

One of the natural rings which are non-Noetherian are the [ring of adeles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ring_of_adeles) $\mathbb{A}_K$ for an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822) $K$. In order to deal with such rings, a topology is considered, giving [topological rings](wikipedia://en/60097). There is a notion of algebraic geometry over such rings developed by [André Weil](wikipedia://en/2019) and [Alexander Grothendieck](wikipedia://en/2042).[^o21]

#### Rings of integers over infinite extensions {#Rings_of_integers_over_infinite_extensions}

Given an infinite [Galois field extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Galois_field_extension) $K/L$, such as $\mathbb{Q}(\zeta_\infty)/\mathbb{Q}$ (by adjoining all roots of unity), the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) $\mathcal{O}_K$ is a non-Noetherian ring which is dimension $1$. This breaks the intuition that finite dimensional schemes are necessarily Noetherian. Also, this example provides motivation for why studying schemes over a non-Noetherian base; that is, schemes $\text{Sch}/\text{Spec}(\mathcal{O}_E)$, can be an interesting and fruitful subject.

One special case[^o23]<sup>pg 93</sup> of such an extension is taking the maximal unramified extension $K^{ur}/K$ and considering the ring of integers $\mathcal{O}_{K^{ur}}$. The induced morphism

> $$\text{Spec}(\mathcal{O}_{K^{ur}}) \to \text{Spec}(\mathcal{O}_K)$$

forms the [universal covering](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_covering) of $\text{Spec}(\mathcal{O}_K)$.

#### Polynomial ring with infinitely many generators {#Polynomial_ring_with_infinitely_many_generators}

Another example of a non-Noetherian finite-dimensional scheme (in fact zero-dimensional) is given by the following quotient of a polynomial ring with infinitely many generators.

> $$\frac{\mathbb{Q}[x_1,x_2,x_3,\ldots]}{(x_1,x_2^2,x_3^3,\ldots)}$$
