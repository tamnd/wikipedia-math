# Poincaré–Bendixson theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Poincaré–Bendixson theorem"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Poincaré–Bendixson theorem** is a statement about the long-term behaviour of [orbits](wikipedia://en/252250) of [continuous dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_dynamical_system) on the plane, cylinder, or two-sphere.[^o2]

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Theorem"}

## Theorem {#Theorem}

Given a [differentiable real dynamical system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system_%28definition%29#Real_dynamical_system) defined on an [open](wikipedia://en/39358) subset of the plane, every [non-empty](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Non-empty) [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) [*ω*-limit set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ω-limit_set) of an [orbit](wikipedia://en/252250), which contains only finitely many fixed points, is either[^o5]

- a [fixed point](wikipedia://en/1951424),
- a [periodic orbit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_orbit), or
- a [connected set](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_set) composed of a finite number of fixed points together with [homoclinic](wikipedia://en/3948734) and [heteroclinic](wikipedia://en/3948758) orbits connecting these.

Moreover, there is at most one orbit connecting different fixed points in the same direction. However, there could be countably many homoclinic orbits connecting one fixed point.

:::

## Discussion {#Discussion}

A weaker version of the theorem was originally conceived by (txt), although he lacked a complete proof which was later given by (txt).

Continuous dynamical systems that are defined on two-dimensional manifolds other than the plane (or cylinder or two-sphere), as well as those defined on higher-dimensional manifolds, may exhibit [*ω*-limit sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ω-limit_set) that defy the three possible cases under the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem. On a [torus](wikipedia://en/74800), for example, it is possible to have a recurrent non-periodic orbit,[^o9] and three-dimensional systems may have [strange attractors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_attractors). Nevertheless, it is possible to classify the [minimal sets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Minimal_sets) of continuous dynamical systems on any two-dimensional [compact](wikipedia://en/6042) and [connected](wikipedia://en/6233) manifold due to a generalization of Arthur J. Schwartz.[^o10][^o11]

::: {#o13 .application cue=heading name="Applications"}

## Applications {#Applications}

One important implication is that a two-dimensional continuous dynamical system cannot give rise to a [strange attractor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strange_attractor). If a strange attractor **C** did exist in such a system, then it could be enclosed in a closed and bounded subset of the phase space. By making this subset small enough, any nearby stationary points could be excluded. But then the Poincaré–Bendixson theorem says that **C** is not a strange attractor at all—it is either a [limit cycle](wikipedia://en/452950) or it converges to a limit cycle.

The Poincaré–Bendixson theorem does not apply to [discrete dynamical systems](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dynamical_system_%28definition%29#Discrete_dynamical_system), where chaotic behaviour can arise in two- or even one-dimensional systems.

:::
