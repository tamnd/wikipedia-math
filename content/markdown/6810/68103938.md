# Aristotelian realist philosophy of mathematics

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Aristotelian realist philosophy of mathematics"}

In the [philosophy of mathematics](wikipedia://en/46439), **Aristotelian realism** holds that mathematics studies properties such as [symmetry](wikipedia://en/2714149), [continuity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuity_%28mathematics%29) and [order](wikipedia://en/361924) that can be [immanently realized](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Immanent_realism) in the physical world (or in any other world there might be). It contrasts with [Platonism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonism) in holding that the objects of mathematics, such as numbers, do not exist in an "abstract" world but can be physically realized.[^o2] It contrasts with [nominalism](wikipedia://en/21176), [fictionalism](wikipedia://en/1535731), and [logicism](wikipedia://en/350672) in holding that mathematics is not about mere names or methods of inference or calculation but about certain real aspects of the world.

:::

Aristotelian realists emphasize [applied mathematics](wikipedia://en/24295969), especially [mathematical modeling](wikipedia://en/20590), rather than [pure mathematics](wikipedia://en/457210) as philosophically most important. Marc Lange argues that "Aristotelian realism allows mathematical facts to be explainers in distinctively mathematical explanations" in science as mathematical facts are themselves about the physical world.[^o3] [Paul Thagard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Thagard) describes Aristotelian realism as "the current philosophy of mathematics that fits best with what is known about [minds and science](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cognitive_science)."[^o4]

## History {#History}

Although [Aristotle](wikipedia://en/308) did not write extensively on the philosophy of mathematics, his various remarks on the topic exhibit a coherent view of the subject as being both about abstractions and applicable to the real world of space and counting.[^o6] Until the eighteenth century, the most common philosophy of mathematics was the Aristotelian view that it is the "science of [quantity](wikipedia://en/691277)", with quantity divided into the continuous (studied by [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446)) and the discrete (studied by arithmetic).[^o7]

Aristotelian approaches to the philosophy of mathematics were rare in the twentieth century but were revived by [Penelope Maddy](wikipedia://en/4023241) in *Realism in Mathematics* (1990) and by a number of authors since 2000 such as [James Franklin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/James_Franklin_%28philosopher%29),[^o8] Anne Newstead,[^o9] [Donald Gillies](wikipedia://en/24874263), and others.

## Numbers and sets {#Numbers_and_sets}

Aristotelian views of ([cardinal](wikipedia://en/6173) or counting) numbers begin with Aristotle's observation that the number of a heap or collection is relative to the unit or measure chosen: "'number' means a measured plurality and a plurality of measures ... the measure must always be some identical thing predicable of all the things it measures, e.g. if the things are horses, the measure is 'horse'."[^o11] Glenn Kessler develops this into the view that a number is a relation between a heap and a [universal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_%28metaphysics%29) that divides it into units; for example, the number 4 is realized in the relation between a heap of parrots and the universal "being a parrot" that divides the heap into so many parrots.[^o12][^o13][^o7]

On an Aristotelian view, [ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ratios) are not closely connected to cardinal numbers. They are relations between quantities such as heights. A ratio of two heights may be the same as the relation between two masses or two time intervals.[^o7]

Aristotelians regard sets as well as numbers as instantiated in the physical world (rather than being Platonist entities). Maddy argued that when an egg carton is opened, a set of three eggs is perceived (that is, a mathematical entity realized in the physical world).[^o14] However not all mathematical discourse needs to be interpreted realistically; for example Aristotelians may regard the [empty set](wikipedia://en/9566) and [zero](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero) as fictions,[^o7] and possibly higher infinities.

## Structural properties {#Structural_properties}

*Image: Königsberg riddle.png.* The seven bridges of Königsberg, studied by Euler

Aristotelians regard non-numerical structural properties like symmetry, continuity and order as equally important as numbers. Such properties are realized in physical reality, and are the subject matter of parts of mathematics. For example [group theory](wikipedia://en/41890) classifies the different kinds of symmetry, while the [calculus](wikipedia://en/5176) studies continuous variation. Provable results about such structures can apply directly to physical reality. For example [Euler](wikipedia://en/17902) proved that it was impossible to walk once and once only over the [seven bridges of Königsberg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Seven_bridges_of_Königsberg).[^o7]

## Epistemology {#Epistemology}

Since mathematical properties are realized in the physical world, they can be directly perceived. For example, humans easily perceive [facial symmetry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facial_symmetry).

Aristotelians also accord a role to abstraction and idealisation in mathematical thinking. This view goes back to Aristotle's statement in his *[Physics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physics_%28Aristotle%29)* that the mind 'separates out' in thought the properties that it studies in mathematics, considering the timeless properties of bodies apart from the world of change (Physics II.2.193b31-35).

At the higher levels of mathematics, Aristotelians follow the theory of Aristotle's *[Posterior Analytics](wikipedia://en/1692809)*, according to which the [proof](wikipedia://en/82285) of a mathematical proposition ideally allows the reader to understand why the proposition must be true.[^o7]

## Criticism {#Criticism}

Franklin's Aristotelian realism has suffered criticism from people who disagree with it. In some instances, supporters have replied to the criticism. Some of these objections were anticipated by Franklin as possible problems for his theory without anyone specifically addressing them towards it.

### Idealization {#Idealization}

An objection anticipated at length in James Franklin's book (chapter 14), drawing on Pincock and Saatsi and Batterman, is the objection that mathematics deals with [idealizations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Idealization_%28philosophy_of_science%29) of the physical world, not with the physical world itself. Aristotle himself was aware of the argument that geometers study perfect circles but hoops in the real world are not perfect circles, so it seems that mathematics must be studying some non-physical (Platonic) world.[^o19] Aristotelians reply that applied mathematics studies [approximations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Approximations) rather than idealizations and that as a result modern mathematics can study the complex shapes and other mathematical structures of real things.[^o7][^o20]

### Balaguer's objection from higher infinities {#Balaguer's_objection_from_higher_infinities}

A problem for Aristotelian realism is what account to give of higher [infinities](wikipedia://en/20110824), which may not be realized or realizable in the physical world. [Mark Balaguer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mark_Balaguer) writes:

"Set theory is committed to the existence of infinite sets that are so huge that they simply dwarf garden variety infinite sets, like the set of all the natural numbers. There is just no plausible way to interpret this talk of gigantic infinite sets as being about physical objects."[^o22]

Aristotelians reply that sciences can deal with uninstantiated universals; for example the science of color can deal with a shade of blue that happens not to occur on any real object.[^o23] However that does require denying the [instantiation principle](wikipedia://en/2892645), held by most Aristotelians, which holds that all genuine properties are instantiated. One Aristotelian philosopher of mathematics who denies the instantiation principle on the basis of [Frege](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frege)’s distinction between [sense and reference](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sense_and_reference) is [Donald Gillies](wikipedia://en/24874263). He has used this approach to develop a method of dealing with very large [transfinite cardinals](wikipedia://en/189734) from an Aristotelian point of view.[^o24]

### William Lane Craig's theistic objections {#William_Lane_Craig's_theistic_objections}

In chapter 5 of his book on *God and Abstract Objects*, [William Lane Craig](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Lane_Craig) raises the following objections to Aristotelian ("concretist") realism as defended by James Franklin:[^o26]

- **Bootstrapping/creation problem for [theism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theism):** If universals or exemplifiables (whether abstract properties, concrete universals, tropes, etc.) must exist for God to create things possessing them, then God would require the relevant properties prior to creation (e.g. to create "powerful" God would already have to be powerful), producing a vicious circularity when Aristotelianism is combined with divine creation.
- **Self-creation worry under Aristotelianism:** On a view that properties are immanent aspects of concrete particulars, theist commitments appear to imply that God would have to create the properties that constitute him, raising the paradoxical consequence that God would create himself.
- **Multiple-instantiation problem:** Concrete universals must be able to be wholly present in distinct locations (multiply instantiated). Craig contends that concretists owe an account of how a single concrete object can exist wholly at separate places; he notes that proponents he cites (e.g. [Armstrong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Malet_Armstrong)) admit they cannot satisfactorily explain this.
- **Uninstantiated universals and mathematical infinities:** A concretist account struggles with uninstantiated universals and with the infinities of classical mathematics, since the finite physical world seems unable to realize all required universals. Craig highlights that Franklin moves toward a "semi-Platonist" stance to handle uninstantiated properties, which undermines strict this-worldly Aristotelianism.
- **Tension between ontic and alethic realism:** Craig argues Franklin largely defends the truth of mathematical statements (alethic realism) rather than making a strong case for ontological commitment to concrete universals. Absent an argument linking mathematical truth to such entities, Franklin’s position risks collapsing into various forms of anti-realism or fictionalism about mathematical objects.
- **Critical assessment of Franklin's critiques of modal structuralism:** Craig reports Franklin objects to [Hellman](wikipedia://en/24774661)’s modal structuralism, but Craig finds those objections misguided and suggests that, if modal structuralism adequately accounts for uninstantiated structures, the motivation for positing concrete universals is weakened.

Craig presents the package of metaphysical and theological problems (creation/bootstrapping, instantiation, and the treatment of uninstantiated/infinite mathematical objects), and he concludes that Franklin has not given a decisive defense of ontic realism that avoids these difficulties.

Franklin argues that Craig's views create a mystery about how mathematics could apply to the world, which is resolved by an Aristotelian approach.[^o27]
