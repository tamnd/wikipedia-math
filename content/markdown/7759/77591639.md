# 1

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="1"}

**1** (**one**, **unit**, **unity**) is a [number](wikipedia://en/21690), [numeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Numeral_%28linguistics%29), and [grapheme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grapheme). It is the first and smallest [positive integer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_number) of the infinite sequence of [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). This fundamental property has led to its unique uses in other fields, ranging from science to sports, where it commonly denotes the first, leading, or top thing in a group. 1 is the [unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_%28measurement%29) of [counting](wikipedia://en/275768) or [measurement](wikipedia://en/19022), and represents a single thing. The representation of 1 evolved from ancient Sumerian and Babylonian symbols to the modern Arabic numeral. Linguistically, in English, "one" is a [determiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Determiner) for singular nouns and a gender-neutral pronoun.

:::

In mathematics, 1 is the multiplicative identity, meaning that any number multiplied by 1 equals the same number. 1 is by convention not considered a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666). In [digital technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_electronics), 1 represents the "on" state in [binary code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_code), the foundation of [computing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computing). Philosophically, 1 symbolizes the ultimate reality or source of existence in various traditions.

::: {#o4 .pointer-item cue=pointer name="1 (numeral)"}

## In mathematics {#In_mathematics}

The number 1 is the first natural number after [0](wikipedia://en/34513). Each [natural number](wikipedia://en/21474), including 1, is constructed by [succession](wikipedia://en/325637), that is, by adding 1 to the previous natural number. Although 1 meets the naïve definition of a [prime number](wikipedia://en/23666), being evenly divisible only by 1 and itself (also 1), by modern convention it is regarded as neither a [prime](wikipedia://en/23666#Primality_of_one) nor a [composite number](wikipedia://en/82289).[^n1]

The number 1 is the [multiplicative identity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_identity) of the [integers](wikipedia://en/14563), [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438), and [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), that is, any number $n$ multiplied by 1 remains unchanged $1\times n = n\times 1 = n$. As a result, the [square](wikipedia://en/659942) $1^2=1$, [square root](wikipedia://en/29208) $\sqrt{1} = 1$, and any other power of 1 is always equal to 1 itself.[^n2] More generally, in [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), it denotes the multiplicative identity in any [unital ring](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unital_ring) or [field](wikipedia://en/10603). An element with a multiplicative inverse is called a [unit](wikipedia://en/289666), generalizing the role of 1. For any number $a$, the first power satisfies $a^1=a$, so that 1 is also the identity for any power semigroup.

1 is its own [factorial](wikipedia://en/10606) $1!=1$. Moreover, the [empty product](wikipedia://en/181289), that is the product of a set of zero numbers, is also 1. Thus any number raised to the zeroth power is one, and 0! is 1.[^n3]

Different mathematical constructions of the natural numbers represent 1 in various ways. In [Giuseppe Peano](wikipedia://en/13043)'s original formulation of the [Peano axioms](wikipedia://en/25005), a set of postulates to define the natural numbers in a precise and logical way, 1 was treated as the starting point of the sequence of natural numbers.[^n4][^n5] Peano later revised his axioms to begin the sequence with 0.[^n4][^n6] In the [Von Neumann cardinal assignment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Von_Neumann_cardinal_assignment) of natural numbers, where each number is defined as a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) that contains all numbers before it, 1 is represented as the [singleton](wikipedia://en/381798) $\{0\}$, a set containing only the element 0.[^n7] The [unary numeral system](wikipedia://en/32316), as used in [tallying](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tally_mark), is an example of a "base-1" number system, since only one mark – the tally itself – is needed. While this is the simplest way to represent the natural numbers, base-1 is rarely used as a practical base for [counting](wikipedia://en/275768) due to its difficult readability.[^n8][^n9]

In many mathematical and engineering problems, numeric values are typically [normalized](wikipedia://en/77361219) to fall within the [unit interval](wikipedia://en/49324) $[0,1]$, where 1 represents the maximum possible value. For example, by definition 1 is the [probability](wikipedia://en/22934) of an event that is absolutely or [almost certain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Almost_certain) to occur.[^n10] Likewise, [vectors](wikipedia://en/32370) are often normalized into [unit vectors](wikipedia://en/167053) (i.e., vectors of magnitude one), because these often have more desirable properties. Functions are often normalized by the condition that they have [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) one, maximum value one, or [square integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Square_integrable) one, depending on the application.[^n11]

1 is the most common leading digit in many sets of real-world numerical data. This is a consequence of [Benford’s law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Benford’s_law), which states that the probability for a specific leading digit $d$ is $\log_{10} \left(\frac{d+1}{d} \right)$. The tendency for real-world numbers to grow exponentially or logarithmically biases the distribution towards smaller leading digits, with 1 occurring approximately 30% of the time.[^n12]

1 is the value of [Legendre's constant](wikipedia://en/435261), introduced in 1808 by [Adrien-Marie Legendre](wikipedia://en/49695) to express the [asymptotic behavior](wikipedia://en/641995) of the [prime-counting function](wikipedia://en/331826).[^n13] The [Weil's conjecture on Tamagawa numbers](wikipedia://en/3036289) states that the [Tamagawa number](wikipedia://en/1168653) $\tau(G)$, a geometrical measure of a connected linear [algebraic group](wikipedia://en/247924) over a global [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field), is 1 for all simply connected groups (those that are [path-connected](wikipedia://en/6519310) with no '[holes](wikipedia://en/6519310#Definition_using_holes)').[^n14][^n15]

## As a word {#As_a_word}

*One* originates from the [Old English](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Old_English) word *an*, derived from the [Germanic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Germanic_languages) root gem-x-proto, from the [Proto-Indo-European root](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Indo-European_root) *\*oi-no-* (meaning "one, unique").[^o22] Linguistically, *one* is a [cardinal number](wikipedia://en/6173) used for counting and expressing the number of items in a collection of things.[^n16] *One* is most commonly a [determiner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_determiners) used with [singular](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grammatical_number) countable [nouns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/English_nouns), as in *one day at a time*.[^n17] The determiner has two senses: numerical one (*I have one apple*) and singulative one (*one day I'll do it*).[^n18] *One* is also a gender-neutral [pronoun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/One_%28pronoun%29) used to refer to an unspecified [person](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Person) or to people in general as in *one should take care of oneself*.[^n19]

Words that derive their meaning from *one* include *alone*, which signifies *all one* in the sense of being by oneself, *none* meaning *not one*, *once* denoting *one time*, and *atone* meaning to become *at one* with the someone. Combining *alone* with *only* (implying *one-like*) leads to *lonely*, conveying a sense of solitude.[^n20] Other common [numeral prefixes](wikipedia://en/1705121) for the number 1 include *uni-* (e.g., [unicycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unicycle), universe, unicorn), *sol-* (e.g., solo dance), derived from Latin, or *mono-* (e.g., [monorail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monorail), monogamy, monopoly) derived from Greek.[^o28][^n21]

## Symbols and representation {#Symbols_and_representation}

### History {#History}

Among the earliest known records of a numeral system, is the [Sumerian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sumer) decimal-[sexagesimal](wikipedia://en/135316) system on [clay tablets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clay_tablet) dating from the first half of the [third millennium BCE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/3rd_millennium_BC).[^n22] Archaic Sumerian numerals for 1 and 60 both consisted of horizontal semi-circular symbols, [^n23] by c. 2350 BCE, the older Sumerian curviform numerals were replaced with [cuneiform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cuneiform) symbols, with 1 and 60 both represented by the same mostly vertical symbol.

*Image: Babylonian 1.svg.*

The Sumerian cuneiform system is a direct ancestor to the [Eblaite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eblaite) and [Assyro-Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Assyro-Babylonian) [Semitic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semitic_languages) cuneiform [decimal](wikipedia://en/8214) systems.[^n24] Surviving [Babylonian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonia) documents date mostly from Old Babylonian (c. 1500 BCE) and the Seleucid (c. 300 BCE) eras.[^n22] The Babylonian cuneiform script notation for numbers used the same symbol for 1 and 60 as in the Sumerian system.[^n25]

The most common [representative glyph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representative_glyph) used in the modern [Western world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Western_world) for the number 1 is the [Arabic numeral](wikipedia://en/1786), a vertical line, often with a [serif](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Serif) at the top and sometimes a short horizontal line at the bottom. It can be traced back to the [Brahmic](wikipedia://en/1903587) script of ancient India, as represented by [Ashoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ashoka) as a simple vertical line in his [Edicts of Ashoka](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edicts_of_Ashoka) in c. 250 BCE.[^o37] This script's numeral shapes were transmitted to Europe via the [Maghreb](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maghreb) and [Al-Andalus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Al-Andalus) during the Middle Ages [^n26] The Arabic numerals, and other glyphs used to represent the number one (e.g., Roman numeral (I ), Chinese numeral ()) are [logograms](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logogram). These symbols directly represent the concept of 'one' without breaking it down into phonetic components.[^n27]

### Modern typefaces {#Modern_typefaces}

In modern [typefaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typeface), the shape of the character for the digit 1 is typically typeset as a *lining figure* with an [ascender](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascender_%28typography%29), such that the digit is the same height and width as a [capital letter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Capital_letter). However, in typefaces with [text figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Text_figures) (also known as *Old style numerals* or *non-lining figures*), the glyph usually is of [x-height](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-height) and designed to follow the rhythm of the lowercase, as, for example, in

*Image: TextFigs148.svg.*

.[^n28] In many typefaces with text figures, the numeral 1 features parallel serifs at the top and bottom, resembling a [small caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Small_caps) version of the [Roman numeral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Roman_numeral) [I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I).[^o42][^o43] Many older [typewriters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Typewriter) do not have a dedicated key for the numeral 1, requiring the use of the lowercase letter *[L](wikipedia://en/4860524)* or uppercase *[I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I)* as substitutes.[^o44][^n29][^n30][^n31]

*Image: Clock 24 J.jpg.* The 24-hour tower clock in [Venice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Venice), using *J* as a symbol for 1

The lower case "[j](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J)" can be considered a [swash](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Swash_%28typography%29) variant of a lower-case Roman numeral "[i](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/I)", often employed for the final i of a "lower-case" Roman numeral. It is also possible to find historic examples of the use of *j* or *[J](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J)* as a substitute for the Arabic numeral 1.[^o48][^o49][^o50][^o51] In [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_language), the serif at the top may be extended into a long upstroke as long as the vertical line. This variation can lead to confusion with the glyph used for [seven](wikipedia://en/208159) in other countries and so to provide a visual distinction between the two the digit 7 may be written with a horizontal stroke through the vertical line.[^n32]

## In other fields {#In_other_fields}

In digital technology, data is represented by [binary code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binary_code), i.e., a [base](wikipedia://en/701207)-2 numeral system with numbers represented by a sequence of 1s and [0s](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/0_%28number%29). Digitised data is represented in physical devices, such as [computers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer), as pulses of electricity through switching devices such as [transistors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transistor) or [logic gates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Logic_gate) where "1" represents the value for "on". As such, the numerical value of [true](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Boolean_data_type) is equal to 1 in many [programming languages](wikipedia://en/23015).[^n33][^n34] In [lambda calculus](wikipedia://en/18203) and [computability theory](wikipedia://en/155414), natural numbers are represented by [Church encoding](wikipedia://en/2989409) as functions, where the Church numeral for 1 is represented by the function $f$ applied to an argument $x$ once (1$fx=fx$).[^n35]

In [physics](wikipedia://en/22939), selected [physical constants](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Physical_constant) are set to 1 in [natural unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Natural_unit) systems in order to simplify the form of equations; for example, in [Planck units](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_units) the [speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) equals 1.[^n36] [Dimensionless quantities](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dimensionless_quantities) are also known as 'quantities of dimension one'.[^n37] In [quantum mechanics](wikipedia://en/25202), the normalization condition for [wavefunctions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wavefunction) requires the integral of a wavefunction's squared modulus to be equal to 1.[^n38] In chemistry, [hydrogen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hydrogen), the first element of the [periodic table](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Periodic_table) and the most [abundant element](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abundance_of_the_elements) in the known [universe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universe), has an [atomic number](wikipedia://en/673) of 1. Group 1 of the periodic table consists of hydrogen and the [alkali metals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alkali_metal).[^n39]

In philosophy, the number 1 is commonly regarded as a symbol of unity, often representing God or the universe in [monotheistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Monotheism) traditions.[^n40] The [Pythagoreans](wikipedia://en/291170) considered the numbers to be plural and therefore did not classify 1 itself as a number, but as the origin of all numbers. In their number philosophy, where odd numbers were considered male and even numbers female, 1 was considered neutral capable of transforming even numbers to odd and vice versa by addition.[^n40] The [Neopythagorean](wikipedia://en/1128528) philosopher [Nicomachus of Gerasa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nicomachus)'s number treatise, as recovered by [Boethius](wikipedia://en/195684) in the Latin translation *[Introduction to Arithmetic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Introduction_to_Arithmetic)*, affirmed that one is not a number, but the source of number.[^o63] In the philosophy of [Plotinus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plotinus) (and that of other [neoplatonists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neoplatonist)), 'The One' is the ultimate reality and source of all existence.[^n41] [Philo of Alexandria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philo#Numbers) (20 BC – AD 50) regarded the number one as God's number, and the basis for all numbers.[^o65]
