# Nilradical of a Lie algebra

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Nilradical of a Lie algebra"}

In [algebra](wikipedia://en/18716923), the **nilradical** of a [Lie algebra](wikipedia://en/17944) is a nilpotent [ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ideal_%28Lie_algebra%29), which is as large as possible.

:::

The nilradical $\mathfrak{nil}(\mathfrak g)$ of a finite-dimensional Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$ is its maximal [nilpotent ideal](wikipedia://en/6389159), which exists because the sum of any two nilpotent ideals is nilpotent. It is an ideal in the [radical](wikipedia://en/11484689) $\mathfrak{rad}(\mathfrak{g})$ of the Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}$. The quotient of a Lie algebra by its nilradical is a [reductive](wikipedia://en/863720) Lie algebra $\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{red}}$. However, the corresponding [short exact sequence](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Short_exact_sequence)

$$0 \to \mathfrak{nil}(\mathfrak g)\to \mathfrak g\to \mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{red}}\to 0$$

does not split in general (i.e., there isn't always a *subalgebra* complementary to $\mathfrak{nil}(\mathfrak g)$ in $\mathfrak{g}$). This is in contrast to the [Levi decomposition](wikipedia://en/2774198): the short exact sequence

$$0 \to \mathfrak{rad}(\mathfrak g)\to \mathfrak g\to \mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{ss}}\to 0$$

does split (essentially because the quotient $\mathfrak{g}^{\mathrm{ss}}$ is semisimple).
