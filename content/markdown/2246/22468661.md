# Mathematics and art

*Image: Dürer Melancholia I.jpg.* Mathematics in art: [Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer)'s copper plate engraving *[Melencolia I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melencolia_I)*, 1514. Mathematical references include a compass for [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446), a [magic square](wikipedia://en/52202) and a [truncated rhombohedron](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dürer's_solid), while measurement is indicated by the scales and [hourglass](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hourglass).[^o86]

*Image: Paolo uccello, studio di vaso in prospettiva 02.jpg.* [Wireframe drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wire-frame_model)[^o1] of a vase as a [solid of revolution](wikipedia://en/329400)[^o1] by [Paolo Uccello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Uccello), 15th century

::: {#o2 .subject cue=lead name="Mathematics and art"}

**Mathematics and art** are related in a variety of ways. [Mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) has itself been described as an [art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art) [motivated by beauty](wikipedia://en/18974136). Mathematics can be discerned in arts such as [music](wikipedia://en/5643937), [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance), [painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painting), [architecture](wikipedia://en/279293), [sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture), and [textiles](wikipedia://en/13410380). This article focuses, however, on mathematics in the visual arts.

:::

Mathematics and art have a long historical relationship. [Artists have used mathematics](wikipedia://en/46914411) since the 4th century BC when the Greek [sculptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture) [Polykleitos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polykleitos) wrote [his *Canon*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polykleitos#Canon_of_Polykleitos), prescribing proportions [conjectured to have been based](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polykleitos#Conjectured_reconstruction) on the ratio 1:$\sqrt{2}$ for the ideal male nude. Persistent popular claims have been made for the use of the [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) in ancient art and architecture, without reliable evidence. In the Italian [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance), [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473) wrote the influential treatise *[De divina proportione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_divina_proportione)* (1509), illustrated with woodcuts by [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci), on the use of the golden ratio in art. Another Italian painter, [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca), developed [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s ideas on [perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perspective_%28visual%29) in treatises such as *De Prospectiva Pingendi*, and in his paintings. The engraver [Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer) made many references to mathematics in his work *[Melencolia I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melencolia_I)*. In modern times, the [graphic artist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphic_artist) [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127) made intensive use of [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671) and [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), with the help of the mathematician [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328), while the [De Stijl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Stijl) movement led by [Theo van Doesburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theo_van_Doesburg) and [Piet Mondrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piet_Mondrian) explicitly embraced geometrical forms. Mathematics has inspired textile arts such as [quilting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quilting), [knitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knitting), [cross-stitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-stitch), [crochet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crochet), [embroidery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embroidery), [weaving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weaving), [Turkish](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turkish_carpet) and other [carpet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet)-making, as well as [kilim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilim). In [Islamic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_art), symmetries are evident in forms as varied as Persian [girih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girih) and Moroccan [zellige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zellige) tilework, [Mughal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mughal_architecture) [jali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jali) pierced stone screens, and widespread [muqarnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqarnas) vaulting.

Mathematics has directly influenced art with conceptual tools such as [linear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_perspective), the analysis of [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741), and mathematical objects such as [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) and the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817). [Magnus Wenninger](wikipedia://en/8864685) creates colourful [stellated polyhedra](wikipedia://en/180253), originally as models for teaching. Mathematical concepts such as [recursion](wikipedia://en/25407) and logical paradox can be seen in paintings by [René Magritte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Magritte) and in engravings by M. C. Escher. [Computer art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_art) often makes use of [fractals](wikipedia://en/10913) including the [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562), and sometimes explores other mathematical objects such as [cellular automata](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cellular_automata). Controversially, the artist [David Hockney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hockney) has argued that artists from the Renaissance onwards made use of the [camera lucida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_lucida) to draw precise representations of scenes; the architect Philip Steadman similarly argued that [Vermeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermeer) used the [camera obscura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura) in his distinctively observed paintings.

Other relationships include the algorithmic analysis of artworks by [X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_fluorescence_spectroscopy), the finding that traditional [batiks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batik) from different regions of [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java) have distinct [fractal dimensions](wikipedia://en/285907), and stimuli to mathematics research, especially [Filippo Brunelleschi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filippo_Brunelleschi)'s theory of perspective, which eventually led to [Girard Desargues](wikipedia://en/358484)'s [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849). A persistent view, based ultimately on the [Pythagorean](wikipedia://en/291170) notion of harmony in music, holds that everything was arranged by Number, that God is the geometer of the world, and that therefore the world's [geometry is sacred](wikipedia://en/46754).

## Origins: from ancient Greece to the Renaissance {#Origins:_from_ancient_Greece_to_the_Renaissance}

### Polykleitos's *Canon* and *symmetria* {#Polykleitos's_Canon_and_symmetria}

*Image: Doryphoros MAN Napoli Inv6011-2.jpg.* Roman copy in marble of *[Doryphoros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doryphoros)*, originally a bronze by [Polykleitos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polykleitos)

[Polykleitos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polykleitos) the elder (c. 450–420 BC) was a [Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Greeks) [sculptor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculptor) from the school of [Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Argos), and a contemporary of [Phidias](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phidias). His works and statues consisted mainly of bronze and were of athletes. According to the philosopher and mathematician [Xenocrates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xenocrates), Polykleitos is ranked as one of the most important sculptors of [classical antiquity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity) for his work on the *[Doryphorus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doryphorus)* and the statue of [Hera](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hera) in the [Heraion of Argos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heraion_of_Argos).[^o5] While his sculptures may not be as famous as those of Phidias, they are much admired. In [his *Canon*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polykleitos#Canon_of_Polykleitos), a treatise he wrote designed to document the "perfect" [body proportions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Body_proportions) of the male nude, Polykleitos gives us a mathematical approach towards sculpturing the human body.[^o5]

The *Canon* itself has been lost but it is conjectured that Polykleitos used a sequence of proportions where each length is that of the diagonal of a square drawn on its predecessor, 1:$\sqrt{2}$ (about 1:1.4142).[^o6]

The influence of the *Canon* of Polykleitos is immense in [Classical Greek](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_Greek), [Roman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Rome), and [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) sculpture, with many sculptors following Polykleitos's prescription. While none of Polykleitos's original works survive, Roman copies demonstrate his ideal of physical perfection and mathematical precision. Some scholars argue that [Pythagorean](wikipedia://en/291170) thought influenced the *Canon* of Polykleitos.[^o7] The *Canon* applies the basic mathematical concepts of Greek geometry, such as the ratio, proportion, and *symmetria* (Greek for "harmonious proportions") and turns it into a system capable of describing the human form through a series of continuous [geometric progressions](wikipedia://en/12630).[^o6]

### Perspective and proportion {#Perspective_and_proportion}

*Image: Brunelleschi's perspective experiment.jpg.* [Brunelleschi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filippo_Brunelleschi)'s experiment with [linear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_perspective)

In classical times, rather than making distant figures smaller with [linear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_perspective), painters sized objects and figures according to their thematic importance. In the Middle Ages, some artists used [reverse perspective](wikipedia://en/538240) for special emphasis. The Muslim mathematician [Alhazen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alhazen) (Ibn al-Haytham) described a theory of optics in his *[Book of Optics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Optics)* in 1021, but never applied it to art.[^o107] The Renaissance saw a rebirth of Classical Greek and Roman culture and ideas, among them the study of mathematics to understand [nature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nature) and the [arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arts). Two major motives drove artists in the late Middle Ages and the Renaissance towards mathematics. First, painters needed to figure out how to depict three-dimensional scenes on a two-dimensional canvas. Second, philosophers and artists alike were convinced that mathematics was the true essence of the physical world and that the entire universe, including the arts, could be explained in geometric terms.[^o9]

The rudiments of perspective arrived with [Giotto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giotto_di_Bondone) (1266/7 – 1337), who attempted to draw in perspective using an algebraic method to determine the placement of distant lines. In 1415, the Italian [architect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architect) [Filippo Brunelleschi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Filippo_Brunelleschi) and his friend [Leon Battista Alberti](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leon_Battista_Alberti) demonstrated the geometrical method of applying perspective in Florence, using [similar triangles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Similar_triangles) as formulated by Euclid, to find the apparent height of distant objects.[^o10][^o11] Brunelleschi's own perspective paintings are lost, but [Masaccio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Masaccio)'s painting of the Holy Trinity shows his principles at work.[^o107][^o12][^o13]

*Image: San Romano Battle (Paolo Uccello, London) 01.jpg.* [Paolo Uccello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Uccello) made innovative use of [perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_perspective) in *[The Battle of San Romano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Battle_of_San_Romano)* (c. 1435–1460).

The Italian painter [Paolo Uccello](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paolo_Uccello) (1397–1475) was fascinated by perspective, as shown in his paintings of *[The Battle of San Romano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Battle_of_San_Romano)* (c. 1435–1460): broken lances lie conveniently along perspective lines.[^o14][^o15]

The painter [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca) (c. 1415–1492) exemplified this new shift in Italian Renaissance thinking. He was an expert [mathematician](wikipedia://en/18902) and [geometer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometer), writing books on [solid geometry](wikipedia://en/507960) and [perspective](wikipedia://en/380405), including *[De prospectiva pingendi](wikipedia://en/26914791) (On Perspective for Painting)*, *Trattato d'Abaco (Abacus Treatise)*, and *[De quinque corporibus regularibus](wikipedia://en/65240274) (On the Five Regular Solids)*.[^o16][^o17][^o18] The historian [Vasari](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vasari) in his *[Lives of the Painters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lives_of_the_Painters)* calls Piero the "greatest geometer of his time, or perhaps of any time."[^o19] Piero's interest in perspective can be seen in his paintings including the [Polyptych of Perugia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyptych_of_Perugia),[^o20] the *San Agostino altarpiece* and *[The Flagellation of Christ](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Flagellation_of_Christ)*. His work on geometry influenced later mathematicians and artists including [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473) in his *De divina proportione* and [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci). Piero studied classical mathematics and the works of [Archimedes](wikipedia://en/1844).[^o21] He was taught commercial arithmetic in "abacus schools"; his writings are formatted like abacus school textbooks,[^o22] perhaps including Leonardo Pisano ([Fibonacci](wikipedia://en/17949))'s 1202 *[Liber Abaci](wikipedia://en/167699)*. [Linear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_perspective) was just being introduced into the artistic world. Alberti explained in his 1435 *[De pictura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_pictura)*: "light rays travel in straight lines from points in the observed scene to the eye, forming a kind of [pyramid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramid) with the eye as vertex." A painting constructed with linear perspective is a [cross-section](wikipedia://en/1337370) of that pyramid.[^o23]

In *De Prospectiva Pingendi*, Piero transforms his empirical observations of the way aspects of a figure change with point of view into mathematical proofs. His treatise starts in the vein of Euclid: he defines the point as "the tiniest thing that is possible for the eye to comprehend".[^o9] He uses [deductive logic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deductive_logic) to lead the reader to the perspective representation of a three-dimensional body.[^o24]

The artist [David Hockney](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/David_Hockney) [argued](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hockney-Falco_thesis) in his book *[Secret Knowledge: Rediscovering the Lost Techniques of the Old Masters](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Secret_Knowledge:_Rediscovering_the_Lost_Techniques_of_the_Old_Masters)* that artists started using a [camera lucida](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_lucida) from the 1420s, resulting in a sudden change in precision and realism, and that this practice was continued by major artists including [Ingres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean-Auguste-Dominique_Ingres), [Van Eyck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_van_Eyck), and [Caravaggio](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Michelangelo_Merisi).[^o25] Critics disagree on whether Hockney was correct.[^o26][^o27] Similarly, the architect Philip Steadman argued controversially[^o28] that [Vermeer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vermeer) had used a different device, the [camera obscura](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Camera_obscura), to help him create his distinctively observed paintings.[^o29]

In 1509, [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473) (c. 1447–1517) published *[De divina proportione](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_divina_proportione)* on [mathematical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical) and [artistic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Artistic) [proportion](wikipedia://en/81863), including in the human face. [Leonardo da Vinci](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Leonardo_da_Vinci) (1452–1519) illustrated the text with woodcuts of regular solids while he studied under Pacioli in the 1490s. Leonardo's drawings are probably the first illustrations of skeletonic solids.[^o30] These, such as the [rhombicuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/26213), were among the first to be drawn to demonstrate perspective by being overlaid on top of each other. The work discusses perspective in the works of [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca), [Melozzo da Forlì](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melozzo_da_Forlì), and [Marco Palmezzano](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marco_Palmezzano).[^o31] Leonardo studied Pacioli's *Summa*, from which he copied tables of proportions.[^o32] In *[Mona Lisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa)* and *[The Last Supper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Last_Supper_%28Leonardo%29)*, Leonardo's work incorporated linear perspective with a [vanishing point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanishing_point) to provide apparent depth.[^o33] *The Last Supper* is constructed in a tight ratio of 12:6:4:3, as is [Raphael](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Raphael)'s *[The School of Athens](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_School_of_Athens)*, which includes Pythagoras with a tablet of ideal ratios, sacred to the Pythagoreans.[^o34][^o35] In *[Vitruvian Man](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvian_Man)*, Leonardo expressed the ideas of the Roman architect [Vitruvius](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vitruvius), innovatively showing the male figure twice, and centring him in both a circle and a square.[^o36]

As early as the 15th century, [curvilinear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curvilinear_perspective) found its way into paintings by artists interested in image distortions. [Jan van Eyck](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jan_van_Eyck)'s 1434 *[Arnolfini Portrait](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Arnolfini_Portrait)* contains a convex mirror with reflections of the people in the scene,[^o37] while [Parmigianino](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parmigianino)'s *Self-portrait in a Convex Mirror*, c. 1523–1524, shows the artist's largely undistorted face at the centre, with a strongly curved background and artist's hand around the edge.[^o38]

Three-dimensional space can be represented convincingly in art, as in [technical drawing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Technical_drawing), by means other than perspective. [Oblique projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Oblique_projection), including cavalier perspective (used by French military artists to depict fortifications in the 18th century), were used continuously and ubiquitously by Chinese artists from the first or second centuries until the 18th century. The Chinese acquired the technique from India, which acquired it from Ancient Rome. Oblique projection is seen in Japanese art, such as in the [Ukiyo-e](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ukiyo-e) paintings of [Torii Kiyonaga](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Torii_Kiyonaga) (1752–1815).[^o39]

### Golden ratio {#Golden_ratio}

The [golden ratio](wikipedia://en/12386) (roughly equal to 1.618) was known to [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331).[^o42] The golden ratio has persistently been claimed[^o43][^o44][^o45][^o46] in modern times to have been used in art and [architecture](wikipedia://en/279293) by the ancients in Egypt, Greece and elsewhere, without reliable evidence.[^o47] The claim may derive from confusion with "golden mean", which to the Ancient Greeks meant "avoidance of excess in either direction", not a ratio.[^o47] [Pyramidologists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pyramidology) since the 19th century have argued on dubious mathematical grounds for the golden ratio in pyramid design. The [Parthenon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parthenon), a 5th-century BC temple in Athens, has been claimed to use the golden ratio in its [façade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Façade) and floor plan,[^o48][^o49][^o50] but these claims too are disproved by measurement.[^o47] The [Great Mosque of Kairouan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Great_Mosque_of_Kairouan) in Tunisia has similarly been claimed to use the golden ratio in its design,[^o51] but the ratio does not appear in the original parts of the mosque.[^o52] The historian of architecture [Frederik Macody Lund](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frederik_Macody_Lund) argued in 1919 that the [Cathedral of Chartres](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cathedral_of_Chartres) (12th century), [Notre-Dame of Laon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre-Dame_of_Laon) (1157–1205) and [Notre Dame de Paris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Notre_Dame_de_Paris) (1160) are designed according to the golden ratio,[^o53] drawing regulator lines to make his case. Other scholars argue that until Pacioli's work in 1509, the golden ratio was unknown to artists and architects.[^o54] For example, the height and width of the front of Notre-Dame of Laon have the ratio 8/5 or 1.6, not 1.618. Such [Fibonacci ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fibonacci_ratio) quickly become hard to distinguish from the golden ratio.[^o55] After Pacioli, the golden ratio is more definitely discernible in artworks including Leonardo's *[Mona Lisa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mona_Lisa)*.[^o56]

Another ratio, the only other morphic number,[^o57] was named the [plastic number](wikipedia://en/2148532) in 1928 by the Dutch architect [Hans van der Laan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_van_der_Laan) (originally named *le nombre radiant* in French).[^o58] Its value is the solution of the [cubic equation](wikipedia://en/243709)

$$x^3=x+1\,,$$

an irrational number which is approximately 1.325. According to the architect [Richard Padovan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Richard_Padovan), this has characteristic ratios $\tfrac{3}{4}$ and $\tfrac{1}{7}$, which govern the limits of human perception in relating one physical size to another. Van der Laan used these ratios when designing the 1967 [St. Benedictusberg Abbey](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/St._Benedictusberg_Abbey) church in the Netherlands.[^o58]

### Planar symmetries {#Planar_symmetries}

*Image: Carpet with Double Medallion.jpg.* Powerful presence:[^o61] carpet with double medallion. Central [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia) (Konya – Karapınar), circa 1600. [Alâeddin Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alâeddin_Mosque)

[Planar symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planar_symmetry) have for millennia been exploited in artworks such as [carpets](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Carpet), lattices, textiles and tilings.[^o60][^o62][^o65][^o75]

Many traditional rugs, whether pile carpets or flatweave [kilims](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kilim), are divided into a central field and a framing border; both can have symmetries, though in handwoven carpets these are often slightly broken by small details, variations of pattern and shifts in colour introduced by the weaver.[^o60] In kilims from [Anatolia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anatolia), the [motifs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Motif_%28textile_arts%29) used are themselves usually symmetrical. The general layout, too, is usually present, with arrangements such as stripes, stripes alternating with rows of motifs, and packed arrays of roughly hexagonal motifs. The field is commonly laid out as a wallpaper with a [wallpaper group](wikipedia://en/386214) such as pmm, while the border may be laid out as a frieze of [frieze group](wikipedia://en/375503) pm11, pmm2 or pma2. Turkish and Central Asian kilims often have three or more borders in different frieze groups. Weavers certainly had the intention of symmetry, without explicit knowledge of its mathematics.[^o60] The mathematician and architectural theorist [Nikos Salingaros](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nikos_Salingaros) suggests that the "powerful presence"[^o61] (aesthetic effect) of a "great carpet"[^o61] such as the best Konya two-medallion carpets of the 17th century is created by mathematical techniques related to the theories of the architect [Christopher Alexander](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Christopher_Alexander). These techniques include making opposites couple; opposing colour values; differentiating areas geometrically, whether by using complementary shapes or balancing the directionality of sharp angles; providing small-scale complexity (from the knot level upwards) and both small- and large-scale symmetry; repeating elements at a hierarchy of different scales (with a ratio of about 2.7 from each level to the next). Salingaros argues that "all successful carpets satisfy at least nine of the above ten rules", and suggests that it might be possible to create a metric from these rules.[^o61]

Elaborate lattices are found in Indian [Jali](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jali) work, carved in marble to adorn tombs and palaces.[^o62] Chinese lattices, always with some symmetry, exist in 14 of the 17 wallpaper groups; they often have mirror, double mirror, or rotational symmetry. Some have a central medallion, and some have a border in a frieze group.[^o63] Many Chinese lattices have been analysed mathematically by Daniel S. Dye; he identifies [Sichuan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sichuan) as the centre of the craft.[^o64]

*Image: Girih tiles.svg.* [Girih tiles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girih_tiles)

Symmetries are prominent in [textile arts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Textile_arts) including [quilting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quilting),[^o65] [knitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knitting),[^o66] [cross-stitch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross-stitch), [crochet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crochet),[^o67] [embroidery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Embroidery)[^o68][^o69] and [weaving](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weaving),[^o70] where they may be purely decorative or may be marks of status.[^o71] [Rotational symmetry](wikipedia://en/701096) is found in circular structures such as [domes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dome); these are sometimes elaborately decorated with symmetric patterns inside and out, as at the 1619 [Sheikh Lotfollah Mosque](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sheikh_Lotfollah_Mosque) in [Isfahan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isfahan).[^o72] Items of embroidery and [lace](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lace) work such as tablecloths and table mats, made using bobbins or by [tatting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tatting), can have a wide variety of reflectional and rotational symmetries which are being explored mathematically.[^o73]

[Islamic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_art) [exploits symmetries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Islamic_geometric_patterns) in many of its artforms, notably in [girih](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Girih_tiles) tilings. These are formed using a set of five tile shapes, namely a regular decagon, an elongated hexagon, a bow tie, a rhombus, and a regular pentagon. All the sides of these tiles have the same length; and all their angles are multiples of 36° (π/5 [radians](wikipedia://en/26003)), offering fivefold and tenfold symmetries. The tiles are decorated with [strapwork](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Strapwork) lines (girih), generally more visible than the tile boundaries. In 2007, the physicists [Peter Lu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Peter_Lu) and [Paul Steinhardt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Paul_Steinhardt) argued that girih resembled [quasicrystalline](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quasicrystalline) [Penrose tilings](wikipedia://en/26611936).[^o74] Elaborate geometric [zellige](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zellige) tilework is a distinctive element in [Moroccan](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morocco) architecture.[^o75] [Muqarnas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Muqarnas) vaults are three-dimensional but were designed in two dimensions with drawings of geometrical cells.[^o76]

### Polyhedra {#Polyhedra}

The [Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) and other [polyhedra](wikipedia://en/23470) are a recurring theme in Western art. They are found, for instance, in a marble mosaic featuring the [small stellated dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/3029843), attributed to Paolo Uccello, in the floor of the [San Marco Basilica](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/San_Marco_di_Venezia) in Venice;[^o14] in Leonardo da Vinci's diagrams of regular polyhedra drawn as illustrations for [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473)'s 1509 book *The Divine Proportion*;[^o14] as a glass [rhombicuboctahedron](wikipedia://en/26213) in Jacopo de Barbari's portrait of Pacioli, painted in 1495;[^o14] in the truncated polyhedron (and various other mathematical objects) in [Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer)'s engraving [Melencolia I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melencolia_I);[^o14] and in [Salvador Dalí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Dalí)'s painting *The Last Supper* in which Christ and his disciples are pictured inside a giant [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407).[^o79]

[Albrecht Dürer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albrecht_Dürer) (1471–1528) was a [German](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/German_people) Renaissance [printmaker](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Printmaker) who made important contributions to polyhedral literature in his 1525 book, *Underweysung der Messung (Education on Measurement)*, meant to teach the subjects of [linear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_perspective), [geometry](wikipedia://en/18973446) in [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), [Platonic solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_solids), and [regular polygons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regular_polygons). Dürer was likely influenced by the works of [Luca Pacioli](wikipedia://en/18473) and [Piero della Francesca](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piero_della_Francesca) during his trips to [Italy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Italy).[^o80] While the examples of perspective in *Underweysung der Messung* are underdeveloped and contain inaccuracies, there is a detailed discussion of polyhedra. Dürer is also the first to introduce in text the idea of [polyhedral nets](wikipedia://en/1148129), polyhedra unfolded to lie flat for printing.[^o81] Dürer published another influential book on [human proportions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Human_proportions) called *Vier Bücher von Menschlicher Proportion (Four Books on Human Proportion)* in 1528.[^o82]

Dürer's well-known engraving *[Melencolia I](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Melencolia_I)* depicts a frustrated thinker sitting by a [truncated triangular trapezohedron](wikipedia://en/41031846) and a [magic square](wikipedia://en/52202).[^o86] These two objects, and the engraving as a whole, have been the subject of more modern interpretation than the contents of almost any other print,[^o86][^o83][^o84] including a two-volume book by Peter-Klaus Schuster,[^o85] and an influential discussion in [Erwin Panofsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Erwin_Panofsky)'s monograph of Dürer.[^o86][^o87]

[Salvador Dalí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Dalí)'s 1954 painting *[Corpus Hypercubus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Corpus_Hypercubus)* uniquely depicts the cross of Christ as an unfolded three-dimensional net for a [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783), also known as a [tesseract](wikipedia://en/31112): the unfolding of a tesseract into these eight cubes is analogous to unfolding the sides of a cube into a cross shape of six squares, here representing the divine perspective with a four-dimensional regular polyhedron.[^o88][^o89] The painting shows the figure of Christ in front of the tessaract; he would normally be shown fixed with nails to the cross, but there are no nails in the painting. Instead, there are four small cubes in front of his body, at the corners of the frontmost of the eight tessaract cubes. The mathematician [Thomas Banchoff](wikipedia://en/4708571) states that Dalí was trying to go beyond the three-dimensional world, while the poet and art critic [Kelly Grovier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kelly_Grovier) says that "The painting seems to have cracked the link between the spirituality of Christ's salvation and the materiality of geometric and physical forces. It appears to bridge the divide that many feel separates science from religion."[^o90]

### Fractal dimensions {#Fractal_dimensions}

*Image: Batik pedalaman - parang klithik.JPG.* [Batiks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batik) from [Surakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surakarta), Java, like this *parang klithik* sword pattern, have a [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) between 1.2 and 1.5.

Traditional Indonesian wax-resist [batik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Batik) designs on cloth combine [representational](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Representation_%28arts%29) motifs (such as floral and vegetal elements) with abstract and somewhat chaotic elements, including imprecision in applying the wax resist, and random variation introduced by cracking of the wax. Batik designs have a [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907) between 1 and 2, varying in different regional styles. For example, the batik of [Cirebon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cirebon) has a fractal dimension of 1.1; the batiks of [Yogyakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Yogyakarta) and [Surakarta](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surakarta) (Solo) in Central [Java](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Java) have a fractal dimension of 1.2 to 1.5; and the batiks of [Lasem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rembang_Regency) on the north coast of Java and of [Tasikmalaya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tasikmalaya) in West Java have a fractal dimension between 1.5 and 1.7.[^o92]

The [drip painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drip_painting) works of the modern artist [Jackson Pollock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson_Pollock) are similarly distinctive in their fractal dimension. His 1948 *Number 14* has a coastline-like dimension of 1.45, while his later paintings had successively higher fractal dimensions and accordingly more elaborate patterns. One of his last works, *Blue Poles*, took six months to create, and has the fractal dimension of 1.72.[^o93]

## A complex relationship {#A_complex_relationship}

The astronomer [Galileo Galilei](wikipedia://en/29688374) in his *[Il Saggiatore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Il_Saggiatore)* wrote that "\[The universe\] is written in [the language of mathematics](wikipedia://en/35659147), and its characters are triangles, circles, and other geometric figures."[^o95] Artists who strive and seek to study nature must first, in Galileo's view, fully understand mathematics. Mathematicians, conversely, have sought to interpret and analyse art through the lens of geometry and rationality. The mathematician [Felipe Cucker](wikipedia://en/65477623) suggests that mathematics, and especially geometry, is a source of rules for "rule-driven artistic creation", though not the only one.[^o96] Some of the many strands of the resulting complex relationship[^o97] are described below.

*Image: Ghhardy@72.jpg.* The mathematician [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) defined a set of criteria for [mathematical beauty](wikipedia://en/18974136).

### Mathematics as an art {#Mathematics_as_an_art}

The mathematician Jerry P. King describes mathematics as an art, stating that "the keys to mathematics are beauty and elegance and not dullness and technicality", and that beauty is the motivating force for mathematical research.[^o99] King cites the mathematician [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599)'s 1940 essay *[A Mathematician's Apology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/A_Mathematician's_Apology)*. In it, Hardy discusses why he finds two theorems of [classical times](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Classical_antiquity) as first rate, namely [Euclid](wikipedia://en/9331)'s proof there are infinitely many [prime numbers](wikipedia://en/23666), and the proof that the square root of 2 is [irrational](wikipedia://en/20647689). King evaluates this last against Hardy's criteria for [mathematical elegance](wikipedia://en/18974136): "*seriousness, depth, generality, unexpectedness, inevitability*, and *economy*" (King's italics), and describes the proof as "aesthetically pleasing".[^o100] The Hungarian mathematician [Paul Erdős](wikipedia://en/601284) agreed that mathematics possessed beauty but considered the reasons beyond explanation: "Why are numbers beautiful? It's like asking why is [Beethoven's Ninth Symphony](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Symphony_No._9_%28Beethoven%29) beautiful. If you don't see why, someone can't tell you. I *know* numbers are beautiful."[^o101]

### Mathematical tools for art {#Mathematical_tools_for_art}

Mathematics can be discerned in many of the arts, such as [music](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Music), [dance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dance),[^o103] [painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Painting), [architecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Architecture), and [sculpture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sculpture). Each of these is richly associated with mathematics.[^o104] Among the connections to the visual arts, mathematics can provide tools for artists, such as the rules of [linear perspective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Linear_perspective) as described by [Brook Taylor](wikipedia://en/210429) and [Johann Lambert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Lambert), or the methods of [descriptive geometry](wikipedia://en/484891), now applied in software modelling of solids, dating back to Albrecht Dürer and [Gaspard Monge](wikipedia://en/77840).[^o105] Artists from Luca Pacioli in the [Middle Ages](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Middle_Ages) and Leonardo da Vinci and Albrecht Dürer in the [Renaissance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Renaissance) have made use of and developed mathematical ideas in the pursuit of their artistic work.[^o104][^o106] The use of perspective began, despite some embryonic usages in the architecture of Ancient Greece, with Italian painters such as [Giotto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giotto) in the 13th century; rules such as the [vanishing point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vanishing_point) were first formulated by [Brunelleschi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brunelleschi) in about 1413,[^o107] his theory influencing Leonardo and Dürer. [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627)'s work on the [optical spectrum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Visible_spectrum) influenced [Goethe](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Johann_Wolfgang_Goethe)'s *[Theory of Colours](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theory_of_Colours)* and in turn artists such as [Philipp Otto Runge](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Philipp_Otto_Runge), [J. M. W. Turner](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._M._W._Turner),[^o108] the [Pre-Raphaelites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pre-Raphaelites) and [Wassily Kandinsky](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wassily_Kandinsky).[^o109][^o110] Artists may also choose to analyse the [symmetry](wikipedia://en/53741) of a scene.[^o111] Tools may be applied by mathematicians who are exploring art, or artists inspired by mathematics, such as [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127) (inspired by [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328)) and the architect [Frank Gehry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frank_Gehry), who more tenuously argued that [computer aided design](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_aided_design) enabled him to express himself in a wholly new way.[^o112]

*Image: Octopod by syntopia.jpg.* *Octopod* by Mikael Hvidtfeldt Christensen. [Algorithmic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_art) produced with the software *Structure Synth*

The artist Richard Wright argues that mathematical objects that can be constructed can be seen either "as processes to simulate phenomena" or as works of "[computer art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_art)". He considers the nature of mathematical thought, observing that [fractals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractals) were known to mathematicians for a century before they were recognised as such. Wright concludes by stating that it is appropriate to subject mathematical objects to any methods used to "come to terms with cultural artifacts like art, the tension between objectivity and subjectivity, their metaphorical meanings and the character of representational systems." He gives as instances an image from the [Mandelbrot set](wikipedia://en/19562), an image generated by a [cellular automaton](wikipedia://en/54342) algorithm, and a [computer-rendered](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rendering_%28computer_graphics%29) image, and discusses, with reference to the [Turing test](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Turing_test), whether [algorithmic](wikipedia://en/775) products can be art.[^o113] Sasho Kalajdzievski's *Math and Art: An Introduction to Visual Mathematics* takes a similar approach, looking at suitably visual mathematics topics such as tilings, fractals and hyperbolic geometry.[^o114]

Some of the first works of computer art were created by [Desmond Paul Henry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Desmond_Paul_Henry)'s "Drawing Machine 1", an [analogue machine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Analogue_computer) based on a [bombsight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bombsight) computer and exhibited in 1962.[^o115][^o116] The machine was capable of creating complex, abstract, asymmetrical, curvilinear, but repetitive line drawings.[^o115][^o117] More recently, [Hamid Naderi Yeganeh](wikipedia://en/45717431) has created shapes suggestive of real world objects such as fish and birds, using formulae that are successively varied to draw families of curves or angled lines.[^o118][^o119][^o120] Artists such as Mikael Hvidtfeldt Christensen create works of [generative](wikipedia://en/250466) or [algorithmic art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algorithmic_art) by writing scripts for a software system such as *Structure Synth*: the artist effectively directs the system to apply a desired combination of mathematical operations to a chosen set of data.[^o121][^o122]

### From mathematics to art {#From_mathematics_to_art}

*Image: Les Demoiselles d'Avignon.jpg.* [Proto-Cubism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proto-Cubism): [Pablo Picasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Picasso)'s 1907 painting *[Les Demoiselles d'Avignon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Les_Demoiselles_d'Avignon)* uses a [fourth dimension](wikipedia://en/38907546) projection to show a figure both full face and in profile.[^o124]

The mathematician and [theoretical physicist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theoretical_physicist) [Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/48740)'s *[Science and Hypothesis](wikipedia://en/43292507)* was widely read by the [Cubists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cubism), including [Pablo Picasso](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Picasso) and [Jean Metzinger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jean_Metzinger).[^n1][^o125] Being thoroughly familiar with [Bernhard Riemann](wikipedia://en/41980)'s work on non-Euclidean geometry, Poincaré was more than aware that [Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/9417) is just one of many possible geometric configurations, rather than as an absolute objective truth. The possible existence of a [fourth dimension](wikipedia://en/38907546) inspired artists to question classical [Renaissance perspective](wikipedia://en/380405#Renaissance&nbsp;:_Mathematical_basis): [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) became a valid alternative.[^o126][^o127][^o128] The concept that painting could be expressed mathematically, in colour and form, contributed to Cubism, the art movement that led to [abstract art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abstract_art).[^o129] Metzinger, in 1910, wrote that: "\[Picasso\] lays out a free, mobile perspective, from which that ingenious mathematician [Maurice Princet](wikipedia://en/15636049) has deduced a whole geometry".[^o130] Later, Metzinger wrote in his memoirs:

> Maurice Princet joined us often ... it was as an artist that he conceptualized mathematics, as an aesthetician that he invoked *n*-dimensional continuums. He loved to get the artists interested in the [new views on space](wikipedia://en/7201595) that had been opened up by [Schlegel](wikipedia://en/34212179) and some others. He succeeded at that.[^o131]

The impulse to make teaching or research models of mathematical forms naturally creates objects that have symmetries and surprising or pleasing shapes. Some of these have inspired artists such as the [Dadaists](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadaism) [Man Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_Ray),[^o132] [Marcel Duchamp](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Marcel_Duchamp)[^o133] and [Max Ernst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Ernst),[^o134][^o135] and following Man Ray, [Hiroshi Sugimoto](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hiroshi_Sugimoto).[^o136]

*Image: Objet mathematique by Man Ray.jpg.* [Enneper surfaces](wikipedia://en/4688830) as [Dadaism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dadaism): [Man Ray](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Man_Ray)'s 1934 *Objet mathematique*

Man Ray photographed some of the mathematical models in the [Institut Henri Poincaré](wikipedia://en/10513595) in Paris, including *Objet mathematique* (Mathematical object). He noted that this represented [Enneper surfaces](wikipedia://en/4688830) with constant [negative curvature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Negative_curvature), derived from the [pseudo-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-sphere). This mathematical foundation was important to him, as it allowed him to deny that the object was "abstract", instead claiming that it was as real as the urinal that Duchamp made into a work of art. Man Ray admitted that the object's \[Enneper surface\] formula "meant nothing to me, but the forms themselves were as varied and authentic as any in nature." He used his photographs of the mathematical models as figures in his series he did on [Shakespeare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shakespeare)'s plays, such as his 1934 painting *Antony and Cleopatra*.[^o137] The art reporter Jonathan Keats, writing in *ForbesLife*, argues that Man Ray photographed "the elliptic paraboloids and conic points in the same sensual light as his pictures of [Kiki de Montparnasse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kiki_de_Montparnasse)", and "ingeniously repurposes the cool calculations of mathematics to reveal the topology of desire".[^o138] Twentieth century sculptors such as [Henry Moore](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Henry_Moore), [Barbara Hepworth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Barbara_Hepworth) and [Naum Gabo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Naum_Gabo) took inspiration from mathematical models.[^o139] Moore wrote of his 1938 *Stringed Mother and Child*: "Undoubtedly the source of my stringed figures was the [Science Museum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Science_Museum,_London) ... I was fascinated by the mathematical models I saw there ... It wasn't the scientific study of these models but the ability to look through the strings as with a bird cage and to see one form within another which excited me."[^o140]

*Image: Theo van Doesburg 122.jpg.* [Theo van Doesburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theo_van_Doesburg)'s *Six Moments in the Development of Plane to Space*, 1926 or 1929

The artists [Theo van Doesburg](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Theo_van_Doesburg) and [Piet Mondrian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piet_Mondrian) founded the [De Stijl](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/De_Stijl) movement, which they wanted to "establish a visual vocabulary comprised elementary geometrical forms comprehensible by all and adaptable to any discipline".[^o141][^o142] Many of their artworks visibly consist of ruled squares and triangles, sometimes also with circles. De Stijl artists worked in painting, furniture, interior design and architecture.[^o141] After the breakup of De Stijl, Van Doesburg founded the [Avant-garde](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Avant-garde) [Art Concret](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_Concret) movement, describing his 1929–1930 *[Arithmetic Composition](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Theo_van_Doesburg_218.jpg)*, a series of four black squares on the diagonal of a squared background, as "a structure that can be controlled, a *definite* surface without chance elements or individual caprice", yet "not lacking in spirit, not lacking the universal and not ... empty as there is *everything* which fits the internal rhythm". The art critic Gladys Fabre observes that two progressions are at work in the painting, namely the growing black squares and the alternating backgrounds.[^o143]

The mathematics of [tessellation](wikipedia://en/321671), polyhedra, shaping of space, and self-reference provided the graphic artist [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127) (1898—1972) with a lifetime's worth of materials for his woodcuts.[^o144][^o145] In the *Alhambra Sketch*, Escher showed that art can be created with polygons or regular shapes such as triangles, squares, and hexagons. Escher used irregular polygons when tiling the plane and often used reflections, [glide reflections](wikipedia://en/412984), and [translations](wikipedia://en/204682) to obtain further patterns. Many of his works contain impossible constructions, made using geometrical objects which set up a contradiction between perspective projection and three dimensions, but are pleasant to the human sight. Escher's *[Ascending and Descending](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ascending_and_Descending)* is based on the "[impossible staircase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Penrose_stairs)" created by the medical scientist [Lionel Penrose](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lionel_Penrose) and his son the mathematician [Roger Penrose](wikipedia://en/26193).[^o146][^o147][^o148]

Some of Escher's many tessellation drawings were inspired by conversations with the mathematician [H. S. M. Coxeter](wikipedia://en/216328) on [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291).[^o149] Escher was especially interested in five specific polyhedra, which appear many times in his work. The [Platonic solids](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Platonic_solids)—tetrahedrons, cubes, octahedrons, dodecahedrons, and icosahedrons—are especially prominent in *Order and Chaos* and *Four Regular Solids*.[^o150] These stellated figures often reside within another figure which further distorts the viewing angle and conformation of the polyhedrons and provides a multifaceted perspective artwork.[^o151]

The visual intricacy of mathematical structures such as tessellations and polyhedra have inspired a variety of mathematical artworks. [Stewart Coffin](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stewart_Coffin) makes polyhedral puzzles in rare and beautiful woods; [George W. Hart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_W._Hart) works on the theory of [polyhedra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polyhedra) and sculpts objects inspired by them; [Magnus Wenninger](wikipedia://en/8864685) makes "especially beautiful" models of [complex stellated polyhedra](wikipedia://en/2915428).[^o152]

The distorted perspectives of [anamorphosis](wikipedia://en/2489512) have been explored in art since the sixteenth century, when [Hans Holbein the Younger](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hans_Holbein_the_Younger) incorporated a severely distorted skull in his 1533 painting *[The Ambassadors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Ambassadors_%28Holbein%29)*. Many artists since then, including Escher, have make use of anamorphic tricks.[^o153]

The mathematics of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) has inspired several artists in modern times. The sculptor [John Robinson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/John_Robinson_%28sculptor%29) (1935–2007) created works such as *Gordian Knot* and *Bands of Friendship*, displaying [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008) in polished bronze.[^o9] Other works by Robinson explore the topology of [toruses](wikipedia://en/74800). *Genesis* is based on [Borromean rings](wikipedia://en/873118) – a set of three circles, no two of which link but in which the whole structure cannot be taken apart without breaking.[^o154] The sculptor [Helaman Ferguson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Helaman_Ferguson) creates complex [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865) and other [topological objects](wikipedia://en/30450).[^o155] His works are visual representations of mathematical objects; *The Eightfold Way* is based on the [projective special linear group](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_special_linear_group) [PSL(2,7)](wikipedia://en/390426), a finite group of 168 elements.[^o156][^o157] The sculptor [Bathsheba Grossman](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bathsheba_Grossman) similarly bases her work on mathematical structures.[^o158][^o159] The artist [Nelson Saiers](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nelson_Saiers) incorporates mathematical concepts and theorems in his art from [toposes](wikipedia://en/21391464) and [schemes](wikipedia://en/364754) to the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949) and the irrationality of [π](wikipedia://en/23601).[^o160]

A liberal arts inquiry project examines connections between mathematics and art through the [Möbius strip](wikipedia://en/37817), [flexagons](wikipedia://en/98316), origami and [panorama](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Panorama) photography.[^o161]

Mathematical objects including the [Lorenz manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lorenz_attractor) and the [hyperbolic plane](wikipedia://en/1589135) have been crafted using [fiber arts](wikipedia://en/13410380) including crochet.[^o162] The American weaver [Ada Dietz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ada_Dietz) wrote a 1949 monograph *Algebraic Expressions in Handwoven Textiles*, defining weaving patterns based on the expansion of multivariate [polynomials](wikipedia://en/23000).[^o163] The mathematician [Daina Taimiņa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Daina_Taimiņa) demonstrated features of the hyperbolic plane by crocheting in 2001.[^o164] This led [Margaret and Christine Wertheim](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Margaret_Wertheim) to crochet a [coral reef](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coral_reef), consisting of many marine animals such as [nudibranchs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nudibranch) whose shapes are based on hyperbolic planes.[^o165] The mathematician [J. C. P. Miller](wikipedia://en/20706909) used the [Rule 90](wikipedia://en/16572515) [cellular automaton](wikipedia://en/54342) to design [tapestries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tapestry) depicting both trees and abstract patterns of triangles.[^o166] The "mathekniticians"[^o167] Pat Ashforth and Steve Plummer use knitted versions of mathematical objects such as [hexaflexagons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hexaflexagon) in their teaching, though their [Menger sponge](wikipedia://en/208811) proved too troublesome to knit and was made of plastic canvas instead.[^o168][^o169] Their "mathghans" (Afghans for Schools) project introduced [knitting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Knitting) into the British mathematics and technology curriculum.[^o170][^o171]

### Illustrating mathematics {#Illustrating_mathematics}

Modelling is far from the only possible way to illustrate mathematical concepts. Giotto's *[Stefaneschi Triptych](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Stefaneschi_Triptych)*, 1320, illustrates [recursion](wikipedia://en/25407) in the form of *[mise en abyme](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mise_en_abyme)*; the central panel of the triptych contains, lower left, the kneeling figure of Cardinal Stefaneschi, holding up the triptych as an offering.[^o175] [Giorgio de Chirico](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giorgio_de_Chirico)'s [metaphysical](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Metaphysics) paintings such as his 1917 *Great Metaphysical Interior* explore the question of levels of representation in art by depicting paintings within his paintings.[^o176]

Art can exemplify logical paradoxes, as in some paintings by the [surrealist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Surrealist) [René Magritte](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/René_Magritte), which can be read as [semiotic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Semiotic) jokes about confusion between levels. In *[La condition humaine](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Human_Condition_%28painting%29)* (1933), Magritte depicts an easel (on the real canvas), seamlessly supporting a view through a window which is framed by "real" curtains in the painting.[^o179] Earlier artists such as [Rembrandt](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rembrandt) had already explored the question of framing, in multiple paintings such as his 1646 *The Holy Family with a Curtain*. That work depicts both the scene and its frame (both wood and curtain), introducing a level confusion between reality and depiction of reality.[^o178] Alberti, in his discussion of perspective, had likened paintings to open windows on to the scenes depicted.[^o177][^o178] In *La condition humaine*, Magritte, in the words of the art historian András Rényi, "escalates the subversive power of the iconic difference that is already manifest in Rembrandt, and heightens it to an open paradox."[^o178] In Rényi's view, the resulting visual joke is the entire purpose of the painting, subverting the art of painting. He comments that *La condition humaine* is "more of a painted, meta-painterly philosopheme than a painterly work".[^o178]

Another approach to mathematical paradox is taken in Escher's *[Print Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Print_Gallery_%28M._C._Escher%29)* (1956); this is a print which depicts a distorted city which contains a gallery which [recursively](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Recursive) contains the picture, and so *[ad infinitum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ad_infinitum)*.[^o179] Magritte made use of spheres and cuboids to distort reality in a different way, painting them alongside an assortment of houses in his 1931 *Mental Arithmetic* as if they were children's building blocks, but house-sized.[^o180] *[The Guardian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Guardian)* observed that the "eerie toytown image" prophesied [Modernism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modernism)'s usurpation of "cosy traditional forms", but also plays with the human tendency to seek [patterns in nature](wikipedia://en/35659147).[^o181]

*Image: Escher Paradox Diagram.png.* Diagram of the apparent paradox embodied in M. C. Escher's 1956 lithograph *Print Gallery*, as discussed by [Douglas Hofstadter](wikipedia://en/8758) in his 1980 book *[Gödel, Escher, Bach](wikipedia://en/48103)*[^o184]

Salvador Dalí's last painting, *[The Swallow's Tail](wikipedia://en/1827768)* (1983), was part of a series inspired by [René Thom](wikipedia://en/61633)'s [catastrophe theory](wikipedia://en/434288).[^o182] The Spanish painter and sculptor [Pablo Palazuelo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pablo_Palazuelo) (1916–2007) focused on the investigation of form. He developed a style that he described as the geometry of life and the geometry of all nature. Consisting of simple geometric shapes with detailed patterning and coloring, in works such as *Angular I* and *Automnes*, Palazuelo expressed himself in geometric transformations.[^o9]

The artist Adrian Gray practises [stone balancing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rock_balancing), exploiting [friction](wikipedia://en/11062) and the [centre of gravity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Centre_of_gravity) to create striking and seemingly impossible compositions.[^o183]

*Image: Print Gallery by M. C. Escher.jpg.* Lithograph *[Print Gallery](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Print_Gallery_%28M._C._Escher%29)* by [M. C. Escher](wikipedia://en/20127), 1956

Artists, however, do not necessarily take geometry literally. As [Douglas Hofstadter](wikipedia://en/8758) writes in his 1980 reflection on human thought, *[Gödel, Escher, Bach](wikipedia://en/48103)*, by way of (among other things) the mathematics of art: "The difference between an Escher drawing and [non-Euclidean geometry](wikipedia://en/58610) is that in the latter, comprehensible interpretations can be found for the undefined terms, resulting in a comprehensible total system, whereas for the former, the end result is not reconcilable with one's conception of the world, no matter how long one stares at the pictures." Hofstadter discusses the seemingly [paradoxical](wikipedia://en/24390) lithograph *Print Gallery* by M. C. Escher; it depicts a seaside town containing an art gallery which seems to contain a painting of the seaside town, there being a "strange loop, or tangled hierarchy" to the levels of reality in the image. The artist himself, Hofstadter observes, is not seen; his reality and his relation to the lithograph are not paradoxical.[^o184] The image's central void has also attracted the interest of mathematicians Bart de Smit and [Hendrik Lenstra](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hendrik_Lenstra), who propose that it could contain a [Droste effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Droste_effect) copy of itself, rotated and shrunk; this would be a further illustration of recursion beyond that noted by Hofstadter.[^o185][^o186]

### Analysis of art history {#Analysis_of_art_history}

Algorithmic analysis of images of artworks, for example using [X-ray fluorescence spectroscopy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/X-ray_fluorescence_spectroscopy), can reveal information about art. Such techniques can uncover images in layers of paint later covered over by an artist; help art historians to visualize an artwork before it cracked or faded; help to tell a copy from an original, or distinguish the brushstroke style of a master from those of his apprentices.[^o188][^o189]

*Image: Max Ernst making Lissajous Figures 1942.jpg.* [Max Ernst](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Max_Ernst) was photographed in the act of making [Lissajous figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lissajous_figures) in New York, 1942. He used a conical dispenser suspended by a string from a second string, which was in turn attached at two points to a rod. The dispenser was allowed to swing freely over a square sheet to create the artwork, while the artist sat nearby, watching the process.[^o190]

The 20th century [Dadaist](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dada) artist Max Ernst painted [Lissajous figures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lissajous_figures) directly by swinging a punctured bucket of paint over a canvas. He had himself photographed in the act of making the mathematical figures in New York in 1942. He suspended the paint container by a string from a second string, which was in turn attached at two points to a rod. He allowed the container to swing freely over a square sheet to create the artwork, while he sat nearby, dressed in a suit and tie, watching the process.[^o190] Ernst was the first to introduce the use of this semi-automatic[^o191] form of [drip painting](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Drip_painting) (also called "oscillation"[^o191]), which he popularised.[^o192] He applied the technique to multiple paintings during his artistic career, including his 1942 works *The Bewildered Planet*, *Surrealism and Painting*, and *Young Man Intrigued by the Flight of a Non-Euclidean Fly*, and his 1970 work *Green Zone*.[^o193][^o194] Lissajous figures later featured repeatedly in early [computer art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Computer_art).[^o193] Ernst's use of the mathematical technique likely influenced [Jackson Pollock](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jackson_Pollock)'s drip painting style.[^o195] Pollock's paintings have a definite [fractal dimension](wikipedia://en/285907)[^o196] of controlled [chaos](wikipedia://en/6295).[^o197]

The computer scientist [Neil Dodgson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Neil_Dodgson) investigated whether [Bridget Riley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridget_Riley)'s stripe paintings could be characterised mathematically, concluding that while separation distance could "provide some characterisation" and global [entropy](wikipedia://en/9891) worked on some paintings, [autocorrelation](wikipedia://en/2724) failed as Riley's patterns were irregular. Local entropy worked best, and correlated well with the description given by the art critic Robert Kudielka.[^o198]

The American mathematician [George Birkhoff](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_Birkhoff)'s 1933 *Aesthetic Measure* proposes a quantitative metric of the [aesthetic quality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aesthetics) of an artwork. It does not attempt to measure the connotations of a work, such as what a painting might mean, but is limited to the "elements of order" of a polygonal figure. Birkhoff first combines (as a sum) five such elements: whether there is a vertical axis of symmetry; whether there is optical equilibrium; how many rotational symmetries it has; how wallpaper-like the figure is; and whether there are unsatisfactory features such as having two vertices too close together. This metric, *O*, takes a value between −3 and 7. The second metric, *C*, counts elements of the figure, which for a polygon is the number of different straight lines containing at least one of its sides. Birkhoff then defines his aesthetic measure of an object's beauty as *O/C*. This can be interpreted as a balance between the pleasure looking at the object gives, and the amount of effort needed to take it in. Birkhoff's proposal has been criticized in various ways, not least for trying to put beauty in a formula, but he never claimed to have done that.[^o199]

### Stimuli to mathematical research {#Stimuli_to_mathematical_research}

Art has sometimes stimulated the development of mathematics, as when Brunelleschi's theory of perspective in architecture and painting started a cycle of research that led to the work of [Brook Taylor](wikipedia://en/210429) and [Johann Heinrich Lambert](wikipedia://en/93085) on the mathematical foundations of perspective drawing,[^o201] and ultimately to the mathematics of [projective geometry](wikipedia://en/243849) of [Girard Desargues](wikipedia://en/358484) and [Jean-Victor Poncelet](wikipedia://en/764818).[^o202]

The Japanese paper-folding art of [origami](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Origami) has been reworked mathematically by [Tomoko Fusé](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tomoko_Fusé) [using modules](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Modular_origami), congruent pieces of paper such as squares, and making them into polyhedra or tilings.[^o203] Paper-folding was used in 1893 by T. Sundara Rao in his *[Geometric Exercises in Paper Folding](wikipedia://en/63640292)* to demonstrate geometrical proofs.[^o204] The [mathematics of paper folding](wikipedia://en/232840) has been explored in [Maekawa's theorem](wikipedia://en/39459296),[^o205] [Kawasaki's theorem](wikipedia://en/14511776),[^o206] and the [Huzita–Hatori axioms](wikipedia://en/232844).[^o207]

### Illusion to op art {#Illusion_to_op_art}

*Image: Fraser spiral.svg.* The [Fraser spiral illusion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraser_spiral_illusion), named for Sir James Fraser who discovered it in 1908.

[Optical illusions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Optical_illusion) such as the [Fraser spiral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fraser_spiral_illusion) strikingly demonstrate limitations in human visual perception, creating what the [art historian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Art_historian) [Ernst Gombrich](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ernst_Gombrich) called a "baffling trick." The black and white ropes that appear to form [spirals](wikipedia://en/61563) are in fact [concentric circles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Concentric_circle). The mid-twentieth century [op art or optical art](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Op_art) style of painting and graphics exploited such effects to create the impression of movement and flashing or vibrating patterns seen in the work of artists such as [Bridget Riley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridget_Riley), Spyros Horemis,[^o210] and [Victor Vasarely](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Victor_Vasarely).[^o211]

### Sacred geometry {#Sacred_geometry}

A strand of art from Ancient Greece onwards sees God as the geometer of the world, and the world's geometry therefore as sacred. The belief that God created the universe according to a geometric plan has ancient origins. [Plutarch](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plutarch) attributed the belief to [Plato](wikipedia://en/22954), writing that "Plato said God geometrizes continually" (*Convivialium disputationum*, liber 8,2). This image has influenced Western thought ever since. The Platonic concept derived in its turn from a [Pythagorean](wikipedia://en/23275) notion of harmony in music, where the notes were spaced in perfect proportions, corresponding to the lengths of the lyre's strings; indeed, the Pythagoreans held that everything was arranged by Number. In the same way, in Platonic thought, the [regular or Platonic solids](wikipedia://en/23905) dictate the proportions found in nature, and in art.[^o213][^o214] An illumination in the 13th-century *[Codex Vindobonensis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_Vindobonensis)* shows God drawing out the universe with a pair of compasses, which may refer to a verse in the Old Testament: "When he established the heavens I was there: when he set a compass upon the face of the deep" (Proverbs 8:27).[^o215] In 1596, the mathematical astronomer [Johannes Kepler](wikipedia://en/15736) modelled the universe as a set of nested Platonic solids, determining the relative sizes of the orbits of the planets.[^o215] [William Blake](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/William_Blake)'s *Ancient of Days* (depicting [Urizen](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Urizen), Blake's embodiment of reason and law) and his painting of the physicist [Isaac Newton](wikipedia://en/14627), naked, hunched and drawing with a compass, use the symbolism of compasses to critique conventional reason and materialism as narrow-minded.[^o216][^o217] [Salvador Dalí](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Salvador_Dalí)'s 1954 *[Crucifixion (Corpus Hypercubus)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crucifixion_%28Corpus_Hypercubus%29)* depicts the cross as a [hypercube](wikipedia://en/39783), representing the divine perspective with four dimensions rather than the usual three.[^o89] In Dalí's *[The Sacrament of the Last Supper](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Sacrament_of_the_Last_Supper)* (1955) Christ and his disciples are pictured inside a giant [dodecahedron](wikipedia://en/8407).[^o218]
