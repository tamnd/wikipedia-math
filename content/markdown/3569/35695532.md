# McMullen problem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="McMullen problem"}

::: {#o2 .problem cue=unsolved}

For how many points is it always possible to projectively transform the points into convex position?

:::

The **McMullen problem** is an open problem in [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468) named after [Peter McMullen](wikipedia://en/40964822).

:::

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

In 1972, David G. Larman wrote about the following problem: Determine the largest number $\nu(d)$ such that for any given $\nu(d)$ points in [general position](wikipedia://en/383736) in the $d$-dimensional affine space $\mathbb{R}^d$ there is a [projective transformation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation) mapping these points into [convex position](wikipedia://en/40079283) (so they form the vertices of a [convex polytope](wikipedia://en/1817885)). Larman credited the problem to a private communication by Peter McMullen.

:::

## Equivalent formulations {#Equivalent_formulations}

### Gale transform {#Gale_transform}

Using the [Gale transform](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gale_transform), this problem can be reformulated as: Determine the smallest number $\mu(d)$ such that for every set of $\mu(d)$ points $X=\{x_1,x_2,\dots,x_{\mu(d)}\}$ in linearly general position on the sphere $S^{d-1}$ it is possible to choose a set $Y=\{\varepsilon_1x_1,\varepsilon_2x_2,\dots,\varepsilon_{\mu(d)}x_{\mu(d)}\}$ where $\varepsilon_i=\pm 1$ for $i=1,2,\dots,\mu(d)$, such that every open hemisphere of $S^{d-1}$ contains at least two members of $Y$.

The numbers $\nu$ of the original formulation of the McMullen problem and $\mu$ of the Gale transform formulation are connected by the relationships

$$\begin{aligned} \mu(k)&=\min\{w \mid w\leq\nu(w-k-1)\} \\ \nu(d)&=\max\{w \mid w\geq\mu(w-d-1)\} \end{aligned}$$

### Partition into nearly-disjoint hulls {#Partition_into_nearly-disjoint_hulls}

Also, by simple geometric observation, it can be reformulated as: Determine the smallest number $\lambda(d)$ such that for every set $X$ of $\lambda(d)$ points in $\mathbb{R}^d$ there exists a [partition](wikipedia://en/340240) of $X$ into two sets $A$ and $B$ with

$$\operatorname{conv}(A\backslash \{x\})\cap \operatorname{conv}(B\backslash \{x\})\not=\varnothing,\forall x\in X. \,$$

The relation between $\mu$ and $\lambda$ is

$$\mu(d+1)=\lambda(d),\qquad d\geq1 \,$$

### Projective duality {#Projective_duality}

*Image: Pentagon dual arrangement.svg.* An [arrangement of lines](wikipedia://en/864149) dual to the regular pentagon. Every five-line projective arrangement, like this one, has a cell touched by all five lines. However, adding the [line at infinity](wikipedia://en/403142) produces a six-line arrangement with six pentagon faces and ten triangle faces; no face is touched by all of the lines. Therefore, the solution to the McMullen problem for *d* = 2 is *ν* = 5.

The equivalent [projective dual](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_dual) statement to the McMullen problem is to determine the largest number $\nu(d)$ such that every set of $\nu(d)$ [hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/99862) in general position in *d*-dimensional [real projective space](wikipedia://en/1014694) form an [arrangement of hyperplanes](wikipedia://en/864438) in which one of the cells is bounded by all of the hyperplanes.

::: {#o10 .statement cue=heading name="Results"}

## Results {#Results}

This problem is still open. However, the bounds of $\nu(d)$ are in the following results:

- David Larman proved in 1972 that $$2d+1\leq\nu(d)\leq(d+1)^2.$$
- [Michel Las Vergnas](wikipedia://en/40965514) proved in 1986 that $$\nu(d)\leq\frac{(d+1)(d+2)}{2}.$$
- Jorge Luis Ramírez Alfonsín proved in 2001 that $$\nu(d)\leq2d+\left\lceil\frac{d+1}{2}\right\rceil.$$

The conjecture of this problem is that $\nu(d)=2d+1$. This has been proven for $d=2,3,4$.
