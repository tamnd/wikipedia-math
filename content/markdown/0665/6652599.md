# Quasinorm

seminorm

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasinorm"}

In [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), [functional analysis](wikipedia://en/11180) and related areas of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **quasinorm** is similar to a [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) in that it satisfies the norm axioms, except that the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941) is replaced by

$$\|x + y\| \leq K(\|x\| + \|y\|)$$

for some $K > 1.$

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

A quasi-seminorm[^n1] on a vector space $X$ is a real-valued map $p$ on $X$ that satisfies the following conditions:

Non-negativity: $p \geq 0;$ [Absolute homogeneity](wikipedia://en/622844): $p(s x) = |s| p(x)$ for all $x \in X$ and all scalars $s;$ there exists a real $k \geq 1$ such that $p(x + y) \leq k [p(x) + p(y)]$ for all $x, y \in X.$

If $k = 1$ then this inequality reduces to the [triangle inequality](wikipedia://en/53941). It is in this sense that this condition generalizes the usual triangle inequality.

A quasi-norm[^n1] is a quasi-seminorm that also satisfies:

[Positive definite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_definite)/Point-separating: if $x \in X$ satisfies $p(x) = 0,$ then $x = 0.$

A pair $(X, p)$ consisting of a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370) $X$ and an associated quasi-seminorm $p$ is called a quasi-seminormed vector space. If the quasi-seminorm is a quasinorm then it is also called a quasi-normed vector space.

**Multiplier**

The [infimum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Infimum) of all values of $k$ that satisfy condition (3) is called the multiplier of $p.$ The multiplier itself will also satisfy condition (3) and so it is the unique smallest real number that satisfies this condition. The term $k$-quasi-seminorm is sometimes used to describe a quasi-seminorm whose multiplier is equal to $k.$

A [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) (respectively, a [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067)) is just a quasinorm (respectively, a quasi-seminorm) whose multiplier is $1.$ Thus every [seminorm](wikipedia://en/292067) is a quasi-seminorm and every [norm](wikipedia://en/990534) is a quasinorm (and a quasi-seminorm).

### Topology {#Topology}

If $p$ is a quasinorm on $X$ then $p$ induces a vector topology on $X$ whose neighborhood basis at the origin is given by the sets:[^n2]

$$\{x \in X : p(x) < 1/n\}$$

as $n$ ranges over the positive integers. A [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) with such a topology is called a quasinormed topological vector space or just a quasinormed space.

Every quasinormed topological vector space is [pseudometrizable](wikipedia://en/64278078).

A [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) quasinormed space is called a quasi-Banach space. Every [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) is a quasi-Banach space, although not conversely.

### Related definitions {#Related_definitions}

A quasinormed space $(A, \| \,\cdot\, \|)$ is called a quasinormed algebra if the vector space $A$ is an [algebra](wikipedia://en/191788) and there is a constant $K > 0$ such that

$$\|x y\| \leq K \|x\| \cdot \|y\|$$

for all $x, y \in A.$

A [complete](wikipedia://en/53695856) quasinormed algebra is called a quasi-Banach algebra.

:::

::: {#o10 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterizations"}

## Characterizations {#Characterizations}

A [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) (TVS) is a quasinormed space if and only if it has a bounded neighborhood of the origin.[^n2]

:::

::: {#o13 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

## Examples {#Examples}

Since every norm is a quasinorm, every [normed space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normed_space) is also a quasinormed space.

**$L^p$ spaces with $0 < p < 1$**

The [$L^p$ spaces](wikipedia://en/45194) for $0 < p < 1$ are quasinormed spaces (indeed, they are even [F-spaces](wikipedia://en/200440)) but they are not, in general, [normable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Normable_space) (meaning that there might not exist any norm that defines their topology). For $0 < p < 1,$ the [Lebesgue space](wikipedia://en/45194) $L^p([0, 1])$ is a [complete](wikipedia://en/7832) [metrizable TVS](wikipedia://en/64278078) (an [F-space](wikipedia://en/200440)) that is not [locally convex](wikipedia://en/518844) (in fact, its only [convex](wikipedia://en/6292) open subsets are itself $L^p([0, 1])$ and the empty set) and the only [continuous linear functional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuous_linear_functional) on $L^p([0, 1])$ is the constant $0$ function (Rudin 1991, §1.47). In particular, the [Hahn-Banach theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hahn-Banach_theorem) does not hold for $L^p([0, 1])$ when $0 < p < 1.$
