# The Petersen Graph

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="The Petersen Graph"}

***The Petersen Graph*** is a [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831) book about the [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926) and its applications in [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401). It was written by Derek Holton and John Sheehan, and published in 1993 by the [Cambridge University Press](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cambridge_University_Press) as volume 7 in their Australian Mathematical Society Lecture Series.

:::

## Topics {#Topics}

*Image: Petersen graph blue.svg.* The [Petersen graph](wikipedia://en/194926)

The Petersen graph is an [undirected graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Undirected_graph) with ten vertices and fifteen edges, commonly drawn as a [pentagram](wikipedia://en/197544) within a [pentagon](wikipedia://en/20741014), with corresponding vertices attached to each other. It has many unusual mathematical properties, and has frequently been used as a [counterexample](wikipedia://en/143151) to [conjectures](wikipedia://en/6138) in graph theory. The book uses these properties as an excuse to cover several advanced topics in graph theory where this graph plays an important role. It is heavily illustrated, and includes both [open problems](wikipedia://en/1333335) on the topics it discusses and detailed references to the literature on these problems.

After an introductory chapter, the second and third chapters concern [graph coloring](wikipedia://en/426743), the history of the [four color theorem](wikipedia://en/10949) for [planar graphs](wikipedia://en/24314), its equivalence to [3-edge-coloring](wikipedia://en/690647) of planar [cubic graphs](wikipedia://en/980508), the [snarks](wikipedia://en/727811) (cubic graphs that have no such colorings), and the conjecture of [W. T. Tutte](wikipedia://en/52021) that every snark has the Petersen graph as a [graph minor](wikipedia://en/353042). Two more chapters concern closely related topics, [perfect matchings](wikipedia://en/44031) (the sets of edges that can have a single color in a 3-edge-coloring) and [nowhere-zero flows](wikipedia://en/9909979) (the [dual concept](wikipedia://en/2536864) to planar graph coloring). The Petersen graph shows up again in another conjecture of Tutte, that when a [bridgeless graph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bridgeless_graph) does not have the Petersen graph as a minor, it must have a nowhere-zero 4-flow.

Chapter six of the book concerns [cages](wikipedia://en/7735022), the smallest [regular graphs](wikipedia://en/85821) with no [cycles](wikipedia://en/168609) shorter than a given length. The Petersen graph is an example: it is the smallest 3-regular graph with no cycles of length shorter than 5. Chapter seven is on [hypohamiltonian graphs](wikipedia://en/18301304), the graphs that do not have a [Hamiltonian cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hamiltonian_cycle) through all vertices but that do have cycles through every set of all but one vertices; the Petersen graph is the smallest example. The next chapter concerns the [symmetries of graphs](wikipedia://en/15094186), and types of graphs defined by their symmetries, including the [distance-transitive graphs](wikipedia://en/7768222) and [strongly regular graphs](wikipedia://en/1399856) (of which the Petersen graph is an example) and the [Cayley graphs](wikipedia://en/358277) (of which it is not). The book concludes with a final chapter of miscellaneous topics too small for their own chapters.

## Audience and reception {#Audience_and_reception}

The book assumes that its readers already have some familiarity with graph theory. It can be used as a reference work for researchers in this area, or as the basis of an advanced course in graph theory.

Although [Carsten Thomassen](wikipedia://en/7460839) describes the book as "elegant", and [Robin Wilson](wikipedia://en/2715277) evaluates its exposition as "generally good", reviewer Charles H. C. Little takes the opposite view, finding fault with its copyediting, with some of its mathematical notation, and with its failure to discuss the lattice of integer combinations of perfect matchings, in which the number of copies of the Petersen graph in the "bricks" of a certain graph decomposition plays a key role in computing the dimension. Reviewer Ian Anderson notes the superficiality of some of its coverage, but concludes that the book "succeeds in giving an exciting and enthusiastic glimpse" of graph theory.
