# Hartley function

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Hartley function"}

The **Hartley function** is a measure of [uncertainty](wikipedia://en/63778), introduced by [Ralph Hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ralph_Hartley) in 1928. If a sample from a [finite set](wikipedia://en/11742) *A* uniformly at random is picked, the amount of information revealed after the outcome is known is given by the Hartley function

:::

$$H_0(A) := \mathrm{log}_b \vert A \vert ,$$

where $\lvert A\rvert$ denotes the [cardinality](wikipedia://en/6174) of *A*.

If the [base of the logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base_of_a_logarithm) is 2, then the unit of uncertainty is the [shannon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_%28unit%29) (more commonly known as [bit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bit)). If it is the [natural logarithm](wikipedia://en/21476), then the unit is the [nat](wikipedia://en/3070481). Hartley used a [base-ten logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Base-ten_logarithm), and with this base, the unit of information is called the [hartley](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_%28unit%29) (aka [ban](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_%28unit%29) or [dit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hartley_%28unit%29)) in his honor. It is also known as the Hartley entropy or max-entropy.

## Hartley function, Shannon entropy, and Rényi entropy {#Hartley_function,_Shannon_entropy,_and_Rényi_entropy}

The Hartley function coincides with the [Shannon entropy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shannon_entropy) (as well as with the Rényi entropies of all orders) in the case of a uniform [probability distribution](wikipedia://en/23543). It is a special case of the [Rényi entropy](wikipedia://en/1731689) since:

$$H_0(X) = \frac 1 {1-0} \log \sum_{i=1}^{|\mathcal{X}|} p_i^0 = \log |\mathcal{X}|.$$

But it can also be viewed as a primitive construction, since, as emphasized by Kolmogorov and Rényi, the Hartley function can be defined without introducing any notions of probability (see *Uncertainty and information* by [George J. Klir](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/George_J._Klir), p. 423).

::: {#o4 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Characterization of the Hartley function"}

## Characterization of the Hartley function {#Characterization_of_the_Hartley_function}

The Hartley function only depends on the number of elements in a set, and hence can be viewed as a function on natural numbers. Rényi showed that the Hartley function in base 2 is the only function mapping natural numbers to real numbers that satisfies

1. $H(mn) = H(m)+H(n)$ (additivity)
1. $H(m) \leq H(m+1)$ (monotonicity)
1. $H(2)=1$ (normalization)

Condition 1 says that the uncertainty of the [Cartesian product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two finite sets *A* and *B* is the sum of uncertainties of *A* and *B*. Condition 2 says that a larger set has larger uncertainty.

:::

## Derivation of the Hartley function {#Derivation_of_the_Hartley_function}

We want to show that the Hartley function, log<sub>2</sub>(*n*), is the only function mapping natural numbers to real numbers that satisfies

1. $H(mn) = H(m)+H(n)\,$ (additivity)
1. $H(m) \leq H(m+1)\,$ (monotonicity)
1. $H(2)=1\,$ (normalization)

Let *f* be a function on positive [integers](wikipedia://en/14563) that satisfies the above three properties. From the additive property, we can show that for any integer *n* and *k*,

$$f(n^k) = kf(n).\,$$

Let *a*, *b*, and *t* be any positive integers. There is a unique integer *s* determined by

$$a^s \leq b^t \leq a^{s+1}. \qquad(1)$$

Therefore,

$$s \log_2 a\leq t \log_2 b \leq (s+1) \log_2 a \,$$

and

$$\frac{s}{t} \leq \frac{\log_2 b}{\log_2 a} \leq \frac{s+1}{t}.$$

On the other hand, by monotonicity,

$$f(a^s) \leq f(b^t) \leq f(a^{s+1}). \,$$

Using equation (1), one gets

$$s f(a) \leq t f(b) \leq (s+1) f(a),\,$$

and

$$\frac{s}{t} \leq \frac{f(b)}{f(a)} \leq \frac{s+1}{t}.$$

Hence,

$$\left\vert \frac{f(b)}{f(a)} - \frac{\log_2(b)}{\log_2(a)} \right\vert \leq \frac{1}{t}.$$

Since *t* can be arbitrarily large, the difference on the left hand side of the above inequality must be zero,

$$\frac{f(b)}{f(a)} = \frac{\log_2(b)}{\log_2(a)}.$$

So,

$$f(a) = \mu \log_2(a)\,$$

for some constant *μ*, which must be equal to 1 by the normalization property.
