# Serial relation

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Serial relation"}

In [set theory](wikipedia://en/27553) a **serial relation** is a [homogeneous relation](wikipedia://en/12987179) expressing the connection of an element of a [sequence](wikipedia://en/27838) to the following element. The [successor function](wikipedia://en/325637) used by [Peano](wikipedia://en/13043) to define [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474) is the prototype for a serial relation.

:::

[Bertrand Russell](wikipedia://en/4163) used serial relations in *[The Principles of Mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Principles_of_Mathematics)*[^o2] (1903) as he explored the foundations of [order theory](wikipedia://en/361924) and its applications. The term *serial relation* was also used by [B. A. Bernstein](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/B._A._Bernstein) for an article showing that particular common axioms in order theory are nearly incompatible: [connectedness](wikipedia://en/57495962), irreflexivity, and [transitivity](wikipedia://en/200463).[^o3]

A serial relation *R* is an [endorelation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Endorelation) on a set *U*. As stated by Russell, $\forall x \exists y \ xRy ,$ where the universal and existential quantifiers refer to *U*. In contemporary language of [relations](wikipedia://en/22517558), this property defines a [total relation](wikipedia://en/1482218). But a total relation may be heterogeneous. Serial relations are of historic interest.

For a relation *R*, let $\{y: xRy\}$ denote the "successor neighborhood" of *x*. A serial relation can be equivalently characterized as a relation for which every element has a non-empty successor neighborhood. Similarly, an **inverse serial** relation is a relation in which every element has non-empty "predecessor neighborhood".[^o4]

In [normal modal logic](wikipedia://en/911820), the extension of fundamental axiom set **K** by the serial property results in axiom set **D**.[^o5]

## Russell's series {#Russell's_series}

Relations are used to develop series in *The Principles of Mathematics*. The prototype is Peano's [successor function](wikipedia://en/325637) as a one-one relation on the [natural numbers](wikipedia://en/21474). Russell's series may be finite or generated by a relation giving [cyclic order](wikipedia://en/434673). In that case, the [point-pair separation](wikipedia://en/68571139) relation is used for description. To define a progression, he requires the generating relation to be a [connected relation](wikipedia://en/57495962). Then [ordinal numbers](wikipedia://en/26547932) are derived from progressions, the finite ones are finite ordinals.[^o2] Distinguishing open and closed series[^o2]p. 234 results in four total orders: finite, one end, no end and open, and no end and closed.[^o2]p. 202

Contrary to other writers, Russell admits negative ordinals. For motivation, consider the scales of [measurement](wikipedia://en/19022) using [scientific notation](wikipedia://en/59715), where a power of ten represents a [decade](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decade_%28log_scale%29) of measure. Informally, this parameter corresponds to [orders of magnitude](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orders_of_magnitude) used to quantify physical units. The parameter takes on negative as well as positive values.

### Stretch {#Stretch}

Russell adopted the term *stretch* from [Meinong](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Alexius_Meinong), who had contributed to the theory of distance.[^o8] Stretch refers to the intermediate terms between two points in a series, and the "number of terms measures the distance and divisibility of the whole."[^o2]p. 181 To explain Meinong, Russell refers to the [Cayley–Klein metric](wikipedia://en/30794512), which uses stretch coordinates in [anharmonic ratios](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cross_ratio) which determine distance by using logarithm.[^o2]p. 255[^o9]
