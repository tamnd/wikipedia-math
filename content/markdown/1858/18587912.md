# Interpolation space

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Interpolation space"}

In the field of [mathematical analysis](wikipedia://en/48396), an **interpolation space** is a space which lies "in between" two other [Banach spaces](wikipedia://en/3989). The main applications are in [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964), where spaces of functions that have a noninteger number of [derivatives](wikipedia://en/7921) are interpolated from the spaces of functions with integer number of derivatives.

:::

## History {#History}

The theory of interpolation of vector spaces began by an observation of [Józef Marcinkiewicz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Józef_Marcinkiewicz), later generalized and now known as the [Riesz-Thorin theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riesz-Thorin_theorem). In simple terms, if a linear function is continuous on a certain [space $L^{p}$](wikipedia://en/45194) and also on a certain space $L^{q}$, then it is also continuous on the space $L^{r}$, for any intermediate $r$ between $p$ and $q$. In other words, $L^{r}$ is a space which is intermediate between $L^{p}$ and $L^{q}$.

In the development of Sobolev spaces, it became clear that the [trace](wikipedia://en/11199199) spaces were not any of the usual function spaces (with integer number of derivatives), and [Jacques-Louis Lions](wikipedia://en/3735500) discovered that indeed these trace spaces were constituted of functions that have a noninteger degree of differentiability.

Many methods were designed to generate such spaces of functions, including the [Fourier transform](wikipedia://en/52247), complex interpolation,[^o3] real interpolation,[^o4] as well as other tools (see e.g. [fractional derivative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fractional_derivative)).

## The setting of interpolation {#The_setting_of_interpolation}

A [Banach space](wikipedia://en/3989) $X$ is said to be *continuously embedded* in a [Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/13637) [topological vector space](wikipedia://en/45752) $Z$ when $X$ is a linear subspace of $Z$ such that the inclusion map from $X$ into $Z$ is continuous. A **compatible couple** $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ of Banach spaces consists of two Banach spaces $X_{0}$ and $X_{1}$ that are continuously embedded in the same Hausdorff topological vector space $Z$.[^o6] The embedding in a linear space $Z$ allows to consider the two linear subspaces

$$X_0 \cap X_1$$

and

$$X_0 + X_1 = \left \{ z \in Z : z = x_0 + x_1, \ x_0 \in X_0, \, x_1 \in X_1 \right \}.$$

Interpolation does not depend only upon the isomorphic (nor isometric) equivalence classes of $X_{0}$ and $X_{1}$. It depends in an essential way from the specific *relative position* that $X_{0}$ and $X_{1}$ occupy in a larger space $Z$.

One can define norms on $X_{0} \cap X_{1}$ and $X_{0} + X_{1}$ by

$$\|x\|_{X_0 \cap X_1} := \max \left ( \left \|x \right \|_{X_0}, \left \|x \right \|_{X_1} \right ),$$

$$\|x\|_{X_0 + X_1} := \inf \left \{ \left \|x_0 \right \|_{X_0} + \left \|x_1 \right \|_{X_1} \ : \ x = x_0 + x_1, \; x_0 \in X_0, \; x_1 \in X_1 \right \}.$$

Equipped with these norms, the intersection and the sum are Banach spaces. The following inclusions are all continuous:

$$X_0 \cap X_1 \subset X_0, \ X_1 \subset X_0 + X_1.$$

Interpolation studies the family of spaces $X$ that are **intermediate spaces** between $X_{0}$ and $X_{1}$ in the sense that

$$X_0 \cap X_1 \subset X \subset X_0 + X_1,$$

where the two inclusions maps are continuous.

An example of this situation is the pair $(L^{1}(\mathbf{R}), L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}))$, where the two Banach spaces are continuously embedded in the space $Z$ of measurable functions on the real line, equipped with the topology of convergence in measure. In this situation, the spaces $L^{p}(\mathbf{R})$, for $1 \le p \le \infty$ are intermediate between $L^{1}(\mathbf{R})$ and $L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R})$. More generally,

$$L^{p_0}(\mathbf{R}) \cap L^{p_1}(\mathbf{R}) \subset L^p(\mathbf{R}) \subset L^{p_0}(\mathbf{R}) + L^{p_1}(\mathbf{R}), \ \ \text{when} \ \ 1 \le p_0 \le p \le p_1 \le \infty,$$

with continuous injections, so that, under the given condition, $L^{p}(\mathbf{R})$ is intermediate between $L^{p_{0}}(\mathbf{R})$ and $L^{p_{1}}(\mathbf{R})$.

**Definition.** Given two compatible couples $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ and $(Y_{0}, Y_{1})$, an **interpolation pair** is a couple $(X, Y)$ of Banach spaces with the two following properties:

- The space *X* is intermediate between $X_{0}$ and $X_{1}$, and *Y* is intermediate between $Y_{0}$ and $Y_{1}$.
- If $L$ is any linear operator from $X_{0} + X_{1}$ to $Y_{0} + Y_{1}$, which maps continuously $X_{0}$ to $Y_{0}$ and $X_{1}$ to $Y_{1}$, then it also maps continuously $X$ to $Y$.

The interpolation pair $(X, Y)$ is said to be of **exponent $\theta$** (with $0 < \theta < 1$) if there exists a constant $C$ such that

$$\|L\|_{X,Y} \leq C \|L\|_{X_0,Y_0}^{1-\theta} \; \|L\|_{X_1,Y_1}^{\theta}$$

for all operators $L$ as above. The notation $||L||_{X,Y}$ is for the norm of $L$ as a map from $X$ to $Y$. If $C = 1$, we say that $(X, Y)$ is an **exact interpolation pair of exponent $\theta$**.

## Complex interpolation {#Complex_interpolation}

If the scalars are [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826), properties of complex [analytic functions](wikipedia://en/61478) are used to define an interpolation space. Given a compatible couple (*X*<sub>0</sub>, *X*<sub>1</sub>) of Banach spaces, the linear space $\mathcal{F}(X_0, X_1)$ consists of all functions $\,f\, : \mathbf{C} \to X_{0} + X_{1}$, that are analytic on $S = \{z : 0 < \operatorname{Re}(z) < 1\},$ continuous on $\overline{S} = \{z : 0 \le \operatorname{Re}(z) \le 1\},$ and for which all the following subsets are bounded:

$$\{\,f\,(z) : z \in S\} \subset X_{0} + X_{1},$$

$$\{\,f\,(it) : t \in \mathbf{R}\} \subset X_{0},$$

$$\{\,f\,(1 + it) : t \in \mathbf{R}\} \subset X_{1}.$$

$\mathcal{F}(X_0, X_1)$ is a Banach space under the norm

$$\|f\|_{\mathcal{F}(X_0, X_1)} = \max \left\{ \sup_{t \in \mathbf{R}} \|f(it)\|_{X_0}, \; \sup_{t \in \mathbf{R}}\|f(1 + it)\|_{X_1} \right\}.$$

::: {#o8 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition.**[^o9] For $0 < \theta < 1$, the **complex interpolation space** $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta}$ is the linear subspace of $X_{0} + X_{1}$ consisting of all values *f*(*θ*) when *f* varies in the preceding space of functions,

$$(X_0, X_1)_\theta = \left \{ x \in X_0 + X_1 : x = f(\theta), \; f \in \mathcal{F}(X_0, X_1) \right \}.$$

The norm on the complex interpolation space $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta}$ is defined by

$$\ \|x\|_\theta = \inf \left \{ \|f\|_{\mathcal{F}(X_0, X_1)} \ :\ f(\theta) = x, \; f \in \mathcal{F}(X_0, X_1) \right \}.$$

Equipped with this norm, the complex interpolation space $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta}$ is a Banach space.

**Theorem.**[^o10] Given two compatible couples of Banach spaces $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ and $(Y_{0}, Y_{1})$, the pair $((X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta}, (Y_{0}, Y_{1})_{\theta})$ is an exact interpolation pair of exponent $\theta$, i.e., if $T : X_{0} + X_{1} \to Y_{0} + Y_{1}$, is a linear operator bounded from $X_{j}$ to $Y_{j}, j = 0, 1$, then $T$ is bounded from $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta}$ to $(Y_{0}, Y_{1})_{\theta}$ and

$$\|T\|_\theta \le \|T\|_0^{1 - \theta} \|T\|_1^\theta.$$

The family of $L^{p}$ spaces (consisting of complex valued functions) behaves well under complex interpolation.[^o11] If $(R, \Sigma, \mu)$ is an arbitrary [measure space](wikipedia://en/19873), if $1 \le p_{0}, p_{1} \le \infty$ and $0 < \theta < 1$, then

$$\left( L^{p_0}(R, \Sigma, \mu), L^{p_1}(R, \Sigma, \mu) \right)_\theta = L^p(R, \Sigma, \mu), \qquad \frac{1}{p} = \frac{1 - \theta}{p_0} + \frac{\theta}{p_1},$$

with equality of norms. This fact is closely related to the [Riesz–Thorin theorem](wikipedia://en/967440).

:::

## Real interpolation {#Real_interpolation}

There are two ways for introducing the **real interpolation method**. The first and most commonly used when actually identifying examples of interpolation spaces is the K-method. The second method, the J-method, gives the same interpolation spaces as the K-method when the parameter $\theta$ is in $(0, 1)$. That the J- and K-methods agree is important for the study of duals of interpolation spaces: basically, the dual of an interpolation space constructed by the K-method appears to be a space constructed from the dual couple by the J-method; [see below](wikipedia://en/18587912#Discrete_definitions).

### K-method {#K-method}

The K-method of real interpolation[^o14] can be used for Banach spaces over the field $\mathbf{R}$ of [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438).

::: {#o15 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition.** Let $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ be a compatible couple of Banach spaces. For $t > 0$ and every $x \in X_{0} + X_{1}$, let

$$K(x, t; X_0, X_1) = \inf \left \{ \left \|x_0 \right \|_{X_0} + t \left \|x_1 \right \|_{X_1} \ :\ x = x_0 + x_1, \; x_0 \in X_0, \, x_1 \in X_1 \right \}.$$

Changing the order of the two spaces results in:[^o16]

$$K(x, t; X_0, X_1) = t K \left (x, t^{-1}; X_1, X_0 \right).$$

Let

$$\begin{aligned} \|x\|_{\theta,q; K} &= \left( \int_0^\infty \left( t^{-\theta} K(x, t; X_0, X_1) \right)^q \, \tfrac{dt}{t} \right)^{\frac{1}{q}}, && 0 < \theta < 1, 1 \leq q < \infty, \\ \|x\|_{\theta,\infty; K} &= \sup_{t > 0} \; t^{-\theta} K(x, t; X_0, X_1), && 0 \le \theta \le 1. \end{aligned}$$

The K-method of real interpolation consists in taking $K_{\theta,q}(X_{0}, X_{1})$ to be the linear subspace of $X_{0} + X_{1}$ consisting of all $x$ such that $||x||_{\theta,q;K} < \infty$.

:::

::: {#o18 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

#### Example {#Example}

An important example is that of the couple $(L^{1}(\mathbf{R}, \Sigma, \mu), L^{\infty}(\mathbf{R}, \Sigma, \mu))$, where the functional $K(t, f\,; L^{1}, L^{\infty})$ can be computed explicitly. The measure $\mu$ is supposed [$\sigma$-finite](wikipedia://en/4316174). In this context, the best way of cutting the function $\,f\, \in L^{1} + L^{\infty}$ as sum of two functions $\,f_{0} \in L^{1}\,$ and $\,f_{1} \in L^{\infty}\,$ is, for some $s > 0$ to be chosen as function of $t$, to let $\,f_{1}(x)$ be given for all $x \in \mathbf{R}$ by

$$f_1(x) = \begin{cases} f(x) & |f(x)| < s, \\ \frac{s f(x)}{|f(x)|} & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}$$

The optimal choice of $s$ leads to the formula[^o19]

$$K \left (f, t; L^1, L^\infty \right ) = \int_0^t f^*(u) \, d u,$$

where $\,f^{\ast}$ is the [decreasing rearrangement](wikipedia://en/25943800#Decreasing_rearrangements) of $\,f\,$.

:::

### J-method {#J-method}

As with the K-method, the J-method can be used for real Banach spaces.

::: {#o21 .definition cue=inline-head name="Definition"}

**Definition.** Let $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ be a compatible couple of Banach spaces. For $t > 0$ and for every vector $x \in X_{0} \cap X_{1}$, let

$$J(x, t; X_0, X_1) = \max \left ( \|x\|_{X_0}, t \|x\|_{X_1} \right ).$$

A vector $x$ in $X_{0} + X_{1}$ belongs to the interpolation space $J_{\theta,q}(X_{0}, X_{1})$ if and only if it can be written as

$$x = \int_0^\infty v(t) \, \frac{dt}{t},$$

where $v(t)$ is measurable with values in $X_{0} \cap X_{1}$ and such that

$$\Phi(v) = \left( \int_0^\infty \left( t^{-\theta} J(v(t), t; X_0, X_1) \right)^q \, \tfrac{dt}{t} \right)^{\frac{1}{q}} < \infty.$$

The norm of $x$ in $J_{\theta,q}(X_{0}, X_{1})$ is given by the formula

$$\|x\|_{\theta,q;J} := \inf_v \left\{ \Phi(v) \ :\ x = \int_0^\infty v(t) \, \tfrac{dt}{t} \right\}.$$

:::

### Relations between the interpolation methods {#Relations_between_the_interpolation_methods}

The two real interpolation methods are equivalent when $0 < \theta < 1$.[^o23]

**Theorem.** Let $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ be a compatible couple of Banach spaces. If $0 < \theta < 1$ and $1 \le q \le \infty$, then

$$J_{\theta,q}(X_0, X_1) = K_{\theta,q}(X_0, X_1),$$

with [equivalence of norms](wikipedia://en/990534#Definition).

The theorem covers degenerate cases that have not been excluded: for example if $X_{0}$ and $X_{1}$ form a direct sum, then the intersection and the J-spaces are the null space, and a simple computation shows that the K-spaces are also null.

When $0 < \theta < 1$, one can speak, up to an equivalent renorming, about *the* Banach space obtained by the real interpolation method with parameters $\theta$ and $q$. The notation for this real interpolation space is $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta,q}$. One has that

$$(X_0, X_1)_{\theta, q} = (X_1, X_0)_{1 - \theta, q}, \qquad 0 < \theta < 1, 1 \le q \le \infty.$$

For a given value of $\theta$, the real interpolation spaces increase with $q$:[^o24] if $0 < \theta < 1$ and $1 \le q \le r \le \infty$, the following continuous inclusion holds true:

$$(X_0, X_1)_{\theta, q} \subset (X_0, X_1)_{\theta, r}.$$

**Theorem.** Given $0 < \theta < 1$, $1 \le q \le \infty$ and two compatible couples $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ and $(Y_{0}, Y_{1})$, the pair $((X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta,q}, (Y_{0}, Y_{1})_{\theta,q})$ is an exact interpolation pair of exponent $\theta$.[^o25]

A complex interpolation space is usually not isomorphic to one of the spaces given by the real interpolation method. However, there is a general relationship.

**Theorem.** Let $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ be a compatible couple of Banach spaces. If $0 < \theta < 1$, then

$$(X_0, X_1)_{\theta, 1} \subset (X_0, X_1)_\theta \subset (X_0, X_1)_{\theta, \infty}.$$

::: {#o27 .example cue=heading name="Examples"}

#### Examples {#Examples}

When $X_{0} = C([0, 1])$ and $X_{1} = C^{1}([0, 1])$, the space of continuously differentiable functions on $[0, 1]$, the $(\theta, \infty)$ interpolation method, for $0 < \theta < 1$, gives the [Hölder space](wikipedia://en/4005892) $C^{0,\theta}$ of exponent $\theta$. This is because the K-functional $K(f, t; X_{0}, X_{1})$ of this couple is equivalent to

$$\sup \left\{ |f(u)|, \, \frac{|f(u) - f(v)|}{1 + t^{-1} |u - v|} \ : \ u, v \in [0, 1] \right\}.$$

Only values $0 < t < 1$ are interesting here.

Real interpolation between $L^{p}$ spaces gives[^o28] the family of [Lorentz spaces](wikipedia://en/25943800). Assuming $0 < \theta < 1$ and $1 \le q \le \infty$, one has:

$$\left ( L^1(\mathbf{R}, \Sigma, \mu), L^\infty(\mathbf{R}, \Sigma, \mu) \right)_{\theta, q} = L^{p, q}(\mathbf{R}, \Sigma, \mu), \qquad \text{where } \tfrac{1}{p} = 1 - \theta,$$

with equivalent norms. This follows from an [inequality of Hardy](wikipedia://en/7015763) and from the value given above of the K-functional for this compatible couple. When $q = p$, the Lorentz space $L^{p,p}$ is equal to $L^{p}$, up to renorming. When $q = \infty$, the Lorentz space $L^{p,\infty}$ is equal to [weak-$L^{p}$](wikipedia://en/45194#Weak_Lp).

:::

::: {#o30 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="The reiteration theorem"}

## The reiteration theorem {#The_reiteration_theorem}

An intermediate space $X$ of the compatible couple $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ is said to be of **class *θ*** if [^o31]

$$(X_0, X_1)_{\theta,1} \subset X \subset (X_0, X_1)_{\theta,\infty},$$

with continuous injections. Beside all real interpolation spaces $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta,q}$ with parameter $\theta$ and $1 \le q \le \infty$, the complex interpolation space $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta}$ is an intermediate space of class $\theta$ of the compatible couple $(X_{0}, X_{1})$.

The reiteration theorems says, in essence, that interpolating with a parameter $\theta$ behaves, in some way, like forming a [convex combination](wikipedia://en/794534) $a = (1 - \theta)x_{0} + \theta x_{1}$: taking a further convex combination of two convex combinations gives another convex combination.

**Theorem.**[^o32] Let $A_{0}, A_{1}$ be intermediate spaces of the compatible couple $(X_{0}, X_{1})$, of class $\theta_{0}$ and $\theta_{1}$ respectively, with $0 < \theta_{0} \ne \theta_{1} < 1$. When $0 < \theta < 1$ and $1 \le q \le \infty$, one has

$$(A_0, A_1)_{\theta, q} = (X_0, X_1)_{\eta, q}, \qquad \eta = (1 - \theta) \theta_0 + \theta \theta_1.$$

It is notable that when interpolating with the real method between $A_{0} = (X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta_{0},q_{0}}$ and $A_{1} = (X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta_{1},q_{1}}$, only the values of $\theta_{0}$ and $\theta_{1}$ matter. Also, $A_{0}$ and $A_{1}$ can be complex interpolation spaces between $X_{0}$ and $X_{1}$, with parameters $\theta_{0}$ and $\theta_{1}$ respectively.

There is also a reiteration theorem for the complex method.

**Theorem.**[^o33] Let $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ be a compatible couple of complex Banach spaces, and assume that $X_{0} \cap X_{1}$ is dense in $X_{0}$ and in $X_{1}$. Let $A_{0} = (X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta_{0}}$ and $A_{1} = (X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta_{1}}$, where $0 \le \theta_{0} \le \theta_{1} \le 1$. Assume further that $X_{0} \cap X_{1}$ is dense in $A_{0} \cap A_{1}$. Then, for every $0 \le \theta \le 1$,

$$\left( \left (X_0, X_1 \right )_{\theta_0}, \left (X_0, X_1 \right )_{\theta_1} \right)_\theta = (X_0, X_1)_\eta, \qquad \eta = (1 - \theta) \theta_0 + \theta \theta_1.$$

The density condition is always satisfied when $X_{0} \subset X_{1}$ or $X_{1} \subset X_{0}$.

:::

## Duality {#Duality}

Let $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ be a compatible couple, and assume that $X_{0} \cap X_{1}$ is dense in *X*<sub>0</sub> and in *X*<sub>1</sub>. In this case, the restriction map from the (continuous) [dual](wikipedia://en/7988#Continuous_dual_space) $X'_j$ of $X_{j}$, $j = 0, 1,$ to the dual of $X_{0} \cap X_{1}$ is one-to-one. It follows that the pair of duals $\left (X'_0, X'_1 \right )$ is a compatible couple continuously embedded in the dual $(X_{0} \cap X_{1})'$.

For the complex interpolation method, the following duality result holds:

**Theorem.**[^o35] Let $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ be a compatible couple of complex Banach spaces, and assume that $X_{0} \cap X_{1}$ is dense in $X_{0}$ and in $X_{1}$. If $X_{0}$ and $X_{1}$ are [reflexive](wikipedia://en/210091), then the dual of the complex interpolation space is obtained by interpolating the duals,

$$( (X_0, X_1)_\theta )' = \left(X'_0, X'_1 \right )_\theta, \qquad 0 < \theta < 1.$$

In general, the dual of the space $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta}$ is equal[^o35] to $\left (X'_0, X'_1 \right )^{\theta},$ a space defined by a variant of the complex method.[^o36] The upper-θ and lower-θ methods do not coincide in general, but they do if at least one of *X*<sub>0</sub>, *X*<sub>1</sub> is a reflexive space.[^o37]

For the real interpolation method, the duality holds provided that the parameter *q* is finite:

**Theorem.**[^o38] Let $0 < \theta < 1, 1 \le q < \infty$ and $(X_{0}, X_{1})$ a compatible couple of real Banach spaces. Assume that $X_{0} \cap X_{1}$ is dense in $X_{0}$ and in $X_{1}$. Then

$$\left ( \left (X_0, X_1 \right )_{\theta, q} \right )' = \left (X'_0, X'_1 \right )_{\theta, q'},$$

where $\tfrac{1}{q'} = 1 - \tfrac{1}{q}.$

## Discrete definitions {#Discrete_definitions}

Since the function $t \to K(x, t)$ varies regularly (it is increasing, but $\tfrac{1}{t}K(x, t)$ is decreasing), the definition of the $K_{\theta,q}$-norm of a vector $n$, previously given by an integral, is equivalent to a definition given by a series.[^o40] This series is obtained by breaking $(0, \infty)$ into pieces $(2^{n}, 2^{n+1})$ of equal mass for the measure $\tfrac{dt}{t}$,

$$\|x\|_{\theta, q; K} \simeq \left( \sum_{n \in \mathbf{Z}} \left( 2^{-\theta n} K \left (x, 2^n; X_0, X_1 \right ) \right)^q \right)^{\frac{1}{q}}.$$

In the special case where $X_{0}$ is continuously embedded in $X_{1}$, one can omit the part of the series with negative indices $n$. In this case, each of the functions $x \to K(x, 2^{n}; X_{0}, X_{1})$ defines an equivalent norm on $X_{1}$.

The interpolation space $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta,q}$ is a "diagonal subspace" of an $\ell^{\,q}$-sum of a sequence of Banach spaces (each one being isomorphic to $X_{0} + X_{1}$). Therefore, when $q$ is finite, the dual of $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta,q}$ is a [quotient](wikipedia://en/3989#General_theory) of the $\ell^{\,p}$-sum of the duals, $\tfrac{1}{p} + \tfrac{1}{q} = 1$, which leads to the following formula for the discrete $J_{\theta,p}$-norm of a functional *x**in the dual of $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta,q}$:***

$$\|x'\|_{\theta, p; J} \simeq \inf \left\{ \left( \sum_{n \in \mathbf{Z}} \left( 2^{\theta n} \max \left (\left \|x'_n \right \|_{X'_0}, 2^{-n} \left \|x'_n \right\|_{X'_1} \right ) \right)^p \right)^{\frac{1}{p}} \ : \ x' = \sum_{n \in \mathbf{Z}} x'_n \right\}.$$

The usual formula for the discrete $J_{\theta,p}$-norm is obtained by changing $n$ to $-n$.

The discrete definition makes several questions easier to study, among which the already mentioned identification of the dual. Other such questions are compactness or weak-compactness of linear operators. Lions and Peetre have proved that:

**Theorem.**[^o41] If the linear operator $T$ is [compact](wikipedia://en/577441) from $X_{0}$ to a Banach space $Y$ and bounded from $X_{1}$ to $Y$, then $T$ is compact from $(X_{0}, X_{1})_{\theta,q}$ to $Y$ when $0 < \theta < 1$, $1 \le q \le \infty$.

Davis, Figiel, Johnson and Pełczyński have used interpolation in their proof of the following result:

**Theorem.**[^o42] A bounded linear operator between two Banach spaces is [weakly compact](wikipedia://en/33662) if and only if it factors through a [reflexive space](wikipedia://en/210091).

### A general interpolation method {#A_general_interpolation_method}

The space $\ell^{\,q}$ used for the discrete definition can be replaced by an arbitrary [sequence space](wikipedia://en/1701075) *Y* with [unconditional basis](wikipedia://en/3460662#Unconditionality), and the weights $a_{n} = 2^{-\theta n}$, $b_{n} = 2^{(1-\theta)n}$, that are used for the $K_{\theta,q}$-norm, can be replaced by general weights

$$a_n, b_n > 0, \ \ \sum_{n=1}^\infty \min(a_n, b_n) < \infty.$$

The interpolation space $K(X_{0}, X_{1}, Y, \{a_{n}\}, \{b_{n}\})$ consists of the vectors $x$ in $X_{0} + X_{1}$ such that[^o44]

$$\|x\|_{K(X_0, X_1)} = \sup_{m \ge 1} \left \| \sum_{n=1}^m a_n K \left (x, \tfrac{b_n}{a_n}; X_0, X_1 \right) \, y_n \right\|_Y < \infty,$$

where {*y<sub>n</sub>*} is the unconditional basis of $Y$. This abstract method can be used, for example, for the proof of the following result:

::: {#o45 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=inline-head name="Theorem"}

**Theorem.**[^o46] A Banach space with unconditional basis is isomorphic to a complemented subspace of a space with [symmetric basis](wikipedia://en/3460662#Unconditionality).

:::

## Interpolation of Sobolev and Besov spaces {#Interpolation_of_Sobolev_and_Besov_spaces}

Several interpolation results are available for [Sobolev spaces](wikipedia://en/611964) and [Besov spaces](wikipedia://en/18542594) on **R**<sup>*n*</sup>,[^o48]

$$\begin{aligned} &H^s_p && s \in \mathbf{R}, 1 \le p \le \infty \\ &B^s_{p, q} && s \in \mathbf{R}, 1 \le p, q \le \infty \end{aligned}$$

These spaces are spaces of [measurable functions](wikipedia://en/44055) on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ when $s \ge 0$, and of [tempered distributions](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distribution_%28mathematics%29) on $\mathbf{R}^{n}$ when $s < 0$. For the rest of the section, the following setting and notation will be used:

$$\begin{aligned} 0 &< \theta < 1, \\ 1 &\le p, p_0, p_1, q, q_0, q_1 \le \infty, \\ s, &s_0, s_1 \in \mathbf{R}, \\ s_\theta &= (1 - \theta) s_0 + \theta s_1, \\[4pt] \frac 1 {p_\theta} &= \frac{1 - \theta}{p_0} + \frac{\theta}{p_1}, \\[4pt] \frac 1 {q_\theta} &= \frac{1 - \theta}{q_0} + \frac{\theta}{q_1}. \end{aligned}$$

Complex interpolation works well on the class of Sobolev spaces $H^{s}_{p}$ (the [Bessel potential spaces](wikipedia://en/611964#Bessel_potential_spaces)) as well as Besov spaces:

$$\begin{aligned} \left (H^{s_0}_{p_0}, H^{s_1}_{p_1} \right )_\theta &= H^{s_\theta}_{p_\theta}, && s_0 \ne s_1, 1 < p_0, p_1 < \infty. \\ \left (B^{s_0}_{p_0,q_0}, B^{s_1}_{p_1,q_1} \right)_\theta &= B^{s_\theta}_{p_\theta, q_\theta}, && s_0 \ne s_1. \end{aligned}$$

Real interpolation between Sobolev spaces may give Besov spaces, except when $s_{0} = s_{1}$,

$$\left (H^{s}_{p_0}, H^{s}_{p_1} \right)_{\theta, p_\theta} = H^{s}_{p_\theta}.$$

When $s_{0} \ne s_{1}$ but $p_{0} = p_{1}$, real interpolation between Sobolev spaces gives a Besov space:

$$\left (H^{s_0}_p, H^{s_1}_p \right)_{\theta, q} = B^{s_\theta}_{p, q}, \qquad s_0 \ne s_1.$$

Also,

$$\begin{aligned} \left (B^{s_0}_{p,q_0}, B^{s_1}_{p,q_1} \right)_{\theta, q} &= B^{s_\theta}_{p,q}, && s_0 \ne s_1. \\ \left (B^s_{p,q_0}, B^s_{p, q_1} \right )_{\theta, q} &= B^{s}_{p, q_\theta}. \\ \left (B^{s_0}_{p_0,q_0}, B^{s_1}_{p_1,q_1} \right )_{\theta, q_\theta} &= B^{s_\theta}_{p_\theta, q_\theta}, && s_0 \ne s_1, p_\theta =q_\theta. \end{aligned}$$
