# Conductor (ring theory)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Conductor"}

In [ring theory](wikipedia://en/250424), a branch of [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **conductor** is a measurement of how far apart a [commutative ring](wikipedia://en/61346) and an [extension ring](wikipedia://en/24873145#Ring_extensions) are. Most often, the larger [ring](wikipedia://en/48404) is a [domain](wikipedia://en/487627) [integrally closed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integral_closure) in its [field of fractions](wikipedia://en/25271), and then the conductor measures the failure of the smaller ring to be integrally closed.

:::

The conductor is of great importance in the study of non-maximal [orders](wikipedia://en/1114171) in the [ring of integers](wikipedia://en/460613) of an [algebraic number field](wikipedia://en/28730822). One interpretation of the conductor is that it measures the failure of unique factorization into [prime ideals](wikipedia://en/24928).

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Let *A* and *B* be commutative rings, and assume $A \subseteq B$. The **conductor**[^o4] of *A* in *B* is the [ideal](wikipedia://en/25977)

$$\mathfrak{f}(B/A) = \operatorname{Ann}_A(B/A).$$

Here $B\,/A$ is viewed as a [quotient](wikipedia://en/364820) of *A*-[modules](wikipedia://en/276410), and $\operatorname{Ann}$ denotes the [annihilator](wikipedia://en/328684). More concretely, the conductor is the set

$$\mathfrak{f}(B/A) = \{ a \in A \colon aB \subseteq A \}.$$

Because the conductor is defined as an annihilator, it is an ideal of *A*.

If *B* is an [integral domain](wikipedia://en/15462), then the conductor may be rewritten as

$$\{0\} \cup \left\{ a \in A \setminus \{0\} : B \subseteq \textstyle\frac{1}{a}A \right\},$$

where $\textstyle\frac{1}{a}A$ is considered as a subset of the fraction field of *B*. That is, if *a* is non-zero and in the conductor, then every element of *B* may be written as a fraction whose numerator is in *A* and whose denominator is *a*. Therefore the non-zero elements of the conductor are those that suffice as common denominators when writing elements of *B* as quotients of elements of *A*.

Suppose *R* is a ring containing *B*. For example, *R* might equal *B*, or *B* might be a domain and *R* its field of fractions. Then, because $1 \in B$, the conductor is also equal to

$$\{ r \in R : rB \subseteq A \}.$$

:::

::: {#o6 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Elementary properties"}

## Elementary properties {#Elementary_properties}

The conductor is the whole ring *A* [if and only if](wikipedia://en/14922) it contains $1 \in A$ and, therefore, if and only if $A = B$. Otherwise, the conductor is a [proper ideal](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Proper_ideal) of *A*.

If the index $m = [B : A]$ is finite, then $mB \subseteq A$, so $m \in \mathfrak{f}(B/A)$. In this case, the conductor is non-zero. This applies in particular when *B* is the ring of integers in an algebraic number field and *A* is an order (a subring for which $B\,/A$ is finite).

The conductor is also an ideal of *B*, because, for any $b$ in $B$ and any $a$ in $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$, $baB \subseteq aB \subseteq A$. In fact, an ideal *J* of *B* is contained in *A* if and only if *J* is contained in the conductor. Indeed, for such a *J*, $JB \subseteq J \subseteq A$, so by definition *J* is contained in $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$. [Conversely](wikipedia://en/155941), the conductor is an ideal of *A*, so any ideal contained in it is contained in *A*. This fact implies that $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$ is the largest ideal of *A* which is also an ideal of *B*. (It can happen that there are ideals of *A* contained in the conductor which are not ideals of *B*.)

Suppose that *S* is a [multiplicative subset](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Multiplicative_subset) of *A*. Then

$$S^{-1}\mathfrak{f}(B/A) \subseteq \mathfrak{f}(S^{-1}B/S^{-1}A),$$

with equality in the case that *B* is a [finitely generated](wikipedia://en/326430) *A*-module.

:::

## Conductors of Dedekind domains {#Conductors_of_Dedekind_domains}

Some of the most important applications of the conductor arise when *B* is a [Dedekind domain](wikipedia://en/145132) and $B\,/A$ is finite. For example, *B* can be the ring of integers of a [number field](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Number_field) and *A* a non-maximal order. Or, *B* can be the affine coordinate ring of a smooth projective curve over a [finite field](wikipedia://en/11615) and *A* the affine coordinate ring of a singular model. The ring *A* does not have unique factorization into prime ideals, and the failure of unique factorization is measured by the conductor $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$.

Ideals [coprime](wikipedia://en/6556#Coprimality_in_ring_ideals) to the conductor share many of pleasant properties of ideals in Dedekind domains. Furthermore, for these ideals there is a tight correspondence between ideals of *B* and ideals of *A*:

- The ideals of *A* that are relatively prime to $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$ have unique factorization into products of invertible prime ideals that are coprime to the conductor. In particular, all such ideals are invertible.
- If *I* is an ideal of *B* that is relatively prime to $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$, then $I \cap A$ is an ideal of *A* that is relatively prime to $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$ and the natural [ring homomorphism](wikipedia://en/26411) $A / (I \cap A) \to B / I$ is an [isomorphism](wikipedia://en/14828). In particular, *I* is prime if and only if $I \cap A$ is prime.
- If *J* is an ideal of *A* that is relatively prime to $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$, then $JB$ is an ideal of *B* that is relatively prime to $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$ and the natural ring homomorphism $A / J \to B / JB$ is an isomorphism. In particular, *J* is prime if and only if *JB* is prime.
- The functions $I \mapsto I \cap A$ and $J \mapsto JB$ define a [bijection](wikipedia://en/3942) between ideals of *A* relatively prime to $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$ and ideals of *B* relatively prime to $\mathfrak{f}(B/A)$. This bijection preserves the property of being prime. It is also multiplicative, that is, $(I \cap A)(I' \cap A) = II' \cap A$ and $(JB)(J'B) = JJ'B$.

All of these properties fail in general for ideals not coprime to the conductor. To see some of the difficulties that may arise, assume that *J* is a non-zero ideal of both *A* and *B* (in particular, it is contained in, hence not coprime to, the conductor). Then *J* cannot be an invertible [fractional ideal](wikipedia://en/460700) of *A* unless $A = B$. Because *B* is a Dedekind domain, *J* is invertible in *B*, and therefore

$$\{ x \in K \colon xJ \subseteq J \} = B,$$

since we may multiply both sides of the equation $xJ \subseteq J$ by *J*<sup>−1</sup>. If *J* is also invertible in *A*, then the same reasoning applies. But the left-hand side of the above equation makes no reference to *A* or *B*, only to their shared fraction field, and therefore $A = B$. Therefore being an ideal of both *A* and *B* implies non-invertibility in *A*.

## Conductors of quadratic number fields {#Conductors_of_quadratic_number_fields}

Let *K* be a [quadratic extension](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quadratic_extension) of **Q**, and let $O_{K}$ be its ring of integers. By extending $1 \in O_{K}$ to a **Z**-basis, we see that every order *O* in *K* has the form $\mathbf{Z} + cO_{K}$ for some positive [integer](wikipedia://en/14563) *c*. The conductor of this order equals the ideal *cO*<sub>*K*</sub>. Indeed, it is clear that *cO*<sub>*K*</sub> is an ideal of *O*<sub>*K*</sub> contained in *O*, so it is contained in the conductor. On the other hand, the ideals of *O* containing *cO*<sub>*K*</sub> are the same as ideals of the [quotient ring](wikipedia://en/184635) $(\mathbf{Z} + cO_{K})\,\,/\,cO_{K}$. The latter ring is isomorphic to $\mathbf{Z}\,/\,c\mathbf{Z}$ by the [second isomorphism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Second_isomorphism_theorem), so all such ideals of *O* are the sum of *cO*<sub>*K*</sub> with an ideal of **Z**. Under this isomorphism, the conductor annihilates $\mathbf{Z}\,/\,c\mathbf{Z}$, so it must be $c\mathbf{Z}$.

In this case, the index $[O_{K} : O]$ is also equal to *c*, so for orders of quadratic number fields, the index may be identified with the conductor. This identification fails for higher degree number fields.
