# Doob decomposition theorem

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Doob decomposition theorem"}

In the theory of [stochastic processes](wikipedia://en/47895) in [discrete time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discrete-time_stochastic_process), a part of the mathematical theory of [probability](wikipedia://en/23542), the **Doob decomposition theorem** gives a unique decomposition of every [adapted](wikipedia://en/6583420) and [integrable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Integrable_stochastic_process) stochastic process as the sum of a [martingale](wikipedia://en/274536) and a [predictable process](wikipedia://en/33414015) (or "drift") starting at zero. The theorem was proved by and is named for [Joseph L. Doob](wikipedia://en/1547599).[^o2]

:::

The analogous theorem in the continuous-time case is the [Doob–Meyer decomposition theorem](wikipedia://en/3651135).

::: {#o4 .statement cue=heading name="Statement"}

## Statement {#Statement}

Let $(\Omega, \mathcal{F}, \mathbb{P})$ be a [probability space](wikipedia://en/43325), $I = \{0, 1, 2, ..., N\}$ with $N \in \mathbb{N}$ or $I = \mathbb{N}_0$ a finite or countably infinite index set, $(\mathcal{F}_n)_{n \in I}$ a [filtration](wikipedia://en/57854817) of $\mathcal{F}$, and $X = (X_{n})_{n\in I}$ an adapted stochastic process with $E[|X_{n}|] < \infty$ for all $n \in I$. Then there exist a martingale $M = (M_{n})_{n\in I}$ and an integrable predictable process $A = (A_{n})_{n\in I}$ starting with $A_{0} = 0$ such that $X_{n} = M_{n} + A_{n}$ for every $n \in I$. Here predictable means that $A_{n}$ is $\mathcal{F}_{n-1}$-[measurable](wikipedia://en/44055) for every $n \in I \backslash \{0\}$. This decomposition is [almost surely](wikipedia://en/351908) unique.[^o5][^o6][^o7]

### Remark {#Remark}

The theorem is valid word for word also for stochastic processes $X$ taking values in the $d$-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) $\mathbb{R}^d$ or the [complex vector space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complex_vector_space) $\mathbb{C}^d$. This follows from the one-dimensional version by considering the components individually.

:::

::: {#o10 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

## Proof {#Proof}

### Existence {#Existence}

Using [conditional expectations](wikipedia://en/435099), define the processes $A$ and $M$, for every $n \in I$, explicitly by

::: {#o12 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$A_n=\sum_{k=1}^n\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X_k\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]-X_{k-1}\bigr) \tag{1}$$

:::

and

::: {#o13 .equation cue=numblk name="2"}

$$M_n=X_0+\sum_{k=1}^n\bigl(X_k-\mathbb{E}[X_k\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]\bigr), \tag{2}$$

:::

where the sums for $n = 0$ are [empty](wikipedia://en/209748) and defined as zero. Here $A$ adds up the expected increments of $X$, and $M$ adds up the surprises, i.e., the part of every $X_{k}$ that is not known one time step before. Due to these definitions, $A_{n+1}$ (if $n + 1 \in I$) and $M_{n}$ are $F_{n}$-measurable because the process $X$ is adapted, $E[|A_{n}|] < \infty$ and $E[|M_{n}|] < \infty$ because the process $X$ is integrable, and the decomposition $X_{n} = M_{n} + A_{n}$ is valid for every $n \in I$. The martingale property

$\mathbb{E}[M_n-M_{n-1}\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=0$ [a.s.](wikipedia://en/351908)

also follows from the above definition ((2)), for every $n \in I \backslash \{0$}.

### Uniqueness {#Uniqueness}

To prove uniqueness, let $X = M + A$ be an additional decomposition. Then the process $Y := M - M = A - A$ is a martingale, implying that

$\mathbb{E}[Y_n\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]=Y_{n-1}$ a.s.,

and also predictable, implying that

$\mathbb{E}[Y_n\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{n-1}]= Y_n$ a.s.

for any $n \in I \backslash \{0$}. Since $Y_{0} = A_{0} - A_{0} = 0$ by the convention about the starting point of the predictable processes, this implies iteratively that $Y_{n} = 0$ almost surely for all $n \in I$, hence the decomposition is almost surely unique.

:::

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/corollary cue=named-heading name="Corollary"}

## Corollary {#Corollary}

A real-valued stochastic process $X$ is a [submartingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Submartingale) if and only if it has a Doob decomposition into a martingale $M$ and an integrable predictable process $A$ that is almost surely [increasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Increasing).[^o17] It is a [supermartingale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supermartingale), if and only if $A$ is almost surely [decreasing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decreasing).

::: {#o19 .proof cue=heading name="Proof"}

### Proof {#Proof}

If $X$ is a submartingale, then

$\mathbb{E}[X_k\,|\,\mathcal{F}_{k-1}]\ge X_{k-1}$ a.s.

for all $k \in I \backslash \{0$}, which is equivalent to saying that every term in definition ((1)) of $A$ is almost surely positive, hence $A$ is almost surely increasing. The equivalence for supermartingales is proved similarly.

:::

:::

::: {#o21 .example cue=heading name="Example"}

## Example {#Example}

Let $X = (X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_0}$ be a sequence in independent, integrable, real-valued random variables. They are adapted to the filtration generated by the sequence, i.e. $F_{n} = \sigma(X_{0}, . . . , X_{n})$ for all $n \in \mathbb{N}_0.$ By ((1)) and ((2)), the Doob decomposition is given by

$$A_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(\mathbb{E}[X_k]-X_{k-1}\bigr),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_0,$$

and

$$M_n=X_0+\sum_{k=1}^{n}\bigl(X_k-\mathbb{E}[X_k]\bigr),\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_0.$$

If the random variables of the original sequence $X$ have mean zero, this simplifies to

$A_n=-\sum_{k=0}^{n-1}X_k$ and $M_n=\sum_{k=0}^{n}X_k,\quad n\in\mathbb{N}_0,$

hence both processes are (possibly time-inhomogeneous) [random walks](wikipedia://en/235451). If the sequence $X = (X_{n})_{n\in\mathbb{N}_0}$ consists of symmetric random variables taking the values $+1$ and $-1$, then $X$ is bounded, but the martingale $M$ and the predictable process $A$ are unbounded [simple random walks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_random_walk) (and not [uniformly integrable](wikipedia://en/9610107)), and [Doob's optional stopping theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doob's_optional_stopping_theorem) might not be applicable to the martingale $M$ unless the stopping time has a finite expectation.

:::

::: {#o23 .application cue=heading name="Application"}

## Application {#Application}

In [mathematical finance](wikipedia://en/33589680), the Doob decomposition theorem can be used to determine the largest optimal exercise time of an [American option](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_option).[^o24][^o25] Let $X = (X_{0}, X_{1}, . . . , X_{N})$ denote the non-negative, [discounted](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Discounted_cash_flow) payoffs of an American option in a $N$-period financial market model, adapted to a filtration $(F_{0}, F_{1}, . . . , F_{N})$, and let $\mathbb{Q}$g denote an [equivalent](wikipedia://en/6612667) [martingale measure](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martingale_measure). Let $U = (U_{0}, U_{1}, . . . , U_{N})$ denote the [Snell envelope](wikipedia://en/35652136) of $X$ with respect to $\mathbb{Q}$. The Snell envelope is the smallest $\mathbb{Q}$-supermartingale dominating $X$[^o26] and in a complete financial market it represents the minimal amount of capital necessary to hedge the American option up to maturity.[^o27] Let $U = M + A$ denote the Doob decomposition with respect to $\mathbb{Q}$ of the Snell envelope $U$ into a martingale $M = (M_{0}, M_{1}, . . . , M_{N})$ and a decreasing predictable process $A = (A_{0}, A_{1}, . . . , A_{N})$ with $A_{0} = 0$. Then the largest [stopping time](wikipedia://en/1202193) to exercise the American option in an optimal way[^o28][^o29] is

$$\tau_{\text{max}}:=\begin{cases}N&\text{if }A_N=0,\\\min\{n\in\{0,\dots,N-1\}\mid A_{n+1}<0\}&\text{if } A_N<0.\end{cases}$$

Since $A$ is predictable, the [event](wikipedia://en/22961) $\{\tau_{\max} = n\} = \{A_{n} = 0, A_{n+1} < 0$} is in $F_{n}$ for every $n \in \{0, 1, . . . , N - 1$}, hence $\tau_{\max}$ is indeed a stopping time. It gives the last moment before the discounted value of the American option will drop in expectation; up to time $\tau_{\max}$ the discounted value process $U$ is a martingale with respect to $\mathbb{Q}$.

:::

::: {#o31 .statement kind=statement/generalization cue=heading name="Generalization"}

## Generalization {#Generalization}

The Doob decomposition theorem can be generalized from probability spaces to [σ-finite measure spaces](wikipedia://en/4316174).[^o32]

:::
