# Euclidean plane

*Image: Cartesian-coordinate-system.svg.* Bi-dimensional [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706)

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Euclidean plane"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **Euclidean plane** is a [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) of [dimension two](wikipedia://en/70691232), denoted $\textbf{E}^2$ or $\mathbb{E}^2$. It is a [geometric space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geometric_space) in which two [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) are required to determine the [position](wikipedia://en/1345771) of each [point](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_%28mathematics%29). It is an [affine space](wikipedia://en/298834), which includes in particular the concept of [parallel lines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parallel_lines). It has also [metrical properties](wikipedia://en/19022) induced by a [distance](wikipedia://en/53932), which allows to define [circles](wikipedia://en/6220), and [angle measurement](wikipedia://en/1196).

:::

A Euclidean plane with a chosen [Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706) is called a *[Cartesian plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cartesian_plane)*. The set $\mathbb{R}^2$ of the ordered pairs of real numbers (the [real coordinate plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Real_coordinate_plane)), equipped with the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093), is often called *the* Euclidean plane or *standard Euclidean plane*, since every Euclidean plane is [isomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isomorphic) to it.

## History {#History}

Books I through IV and VI of [Euclid's Elements](wikipedia://en/244107) dealt with two-dimensional geometry, developing such notions as similarity of shapes, the [Pythagorean theorem](wikipedia://en/26513034) (Proposition 47), equality of angles and [areas](wikipedia://en/1209), parallelism, the sum of the angles in a triangle, and the three cases in which triangles are "equal" (have the same area), among many other topics.

Later, the plane was described in a so-called *[Cartesian coordinate system](wikipedia://en/7706)*, a [coordinate system](wikipedia://en/81931) that specifies each [point](wikipedia://en/593693) uniquely in a [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) by a pair of [numerical](wikipedia://en/21690) *coordinates*, which are the [signed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Positive_and_negative_numbers) distances from the point to two fixed [perpendicular](wikipedia://en/76944) directed lines, measured in the same [unit of length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Unit_length). Each reference line is called a *coordinate axis* or just *axis* of the system, and the point where they meet is its *[origin](wikipedia://en/1313432)*, usually at ordered pair (0, 0). The coordinates can also be defined as the positions of the [perpendicular projections](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orthogonal_projection) of the point onto the two axes, expressed as signed distances from the origin.

The idea of this system was developed in 1637 in writings by Descartes and independently by [Pierre de Fermat](wikipedia://en/7576966), although Fermat also worked in three dimensions, and did not publish the discovery.[^o3] Both authors used a single ([abscissa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abscissa)) axis in their treatments, with the lengths of [ordinates](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ordinate) measured along lines not-necessarily-perpendicular to that axis.[^o4] The concept of using a pair of fixed axes was introduced later, after Descartes' *[La Géométrie](wikipedia://en/239981)* was translated into Latin in 1649 by [Frans van Schooten](wikipedia://en/66858) and his students. These commentators introduced several concepts while trying to clarify the ideas contained in Descartes' work.[^o5]

Later, the plane was thought of as a [field](wikipedia://en/10603), where any two points could be multiplied and, except for 0, divided. This was known as the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628). The complex plane is sometimes called the Argand plane because it is used in Argand diagrams. These are named after [Jean-Robert Argand](wikipedia://en/543727) (1768–1822), although they were first described by Danish-Norwegian land surveyor and mathematician [Caspar Wessel](wikipedia://en/21418268) (1745–1818).[^o6] Argand diagrams are frequently used to plot the positions of the [poles](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pole_%28complex_analysis%29) and [zeroes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zero_%28complex_analysis%29) of a [function](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mathematical_function) in the complex plane.

### Coordinate systems {#Coordinate_systems}

In mathematics, [analytic geometry](wikipedia://en/2202) (also called Cartesian geometry) describes every point in two-dimensional space by means of two coordinates. Two perpendicular [coordinate axes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coordinate_axis) are given which cross each other at the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432). They are usually labeled *x* and *y*. Relative to these axes, the position of any point in two-dimensional space is given by an ordered pair of real numbers, each number giving the distance of that point from the [origin](wikipedia://en/1313432) measured along the given axis, which is equal to the distance of that point from the other axis.

Another widely used coordinate system is the [polar coordinate system](wikipedia://en/25120), which specifies a point in terms of its distance from the origin and its angle relative to a rightward reference ray.

#### Non-convex {#Non-convex}

There exist infinitely many non-convex regular polytopes in two dimensions, whose Schläfli symbols consist of rational numbers {n/m}. They are called [star polygons](wikipedia://en/313303) and share the same [vertex arrangements](wikipedia://en/14308644) of the convex regular polygons.

In general, for any natural number n, there are n-pointed non-convex regular polygonal stars with Schläfli symbols {*n*/*m*} for all *m* such that *m* \< *n*/2 (strictly speaking {*n*/*m*} = {*n*/(*n* − *m*)}) and *m* and *n* are [coprime](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coprime).

### Circle {#Circle}

*Image: CIRCLE 1.svg.*

The [hypersphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hypersphere) in 2 dimensions is a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220), sometimes called a 1-sphere (*S*<sup>1</sup>) because it is a one-dimensional [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470). In a Euclidean plane, it has the length 2π*r* and the [area](wikipedia://en/1209) of its [interior](wikipedia://en/55610) is

$$A = \pi r^{2}$$

where $r$ is the radius.

### Other shapes {#Other_shapes}

There are an infinitude of other curved shapes in two dimensions, notably including the [conic sections](wikipedia://en/19008673): the [ellipse](wikipedia://en/9277), the [parabola](wikipedia://en/23231), and the [hyperbola](wikipedia://en/14052).

## In linear algebra {#In_linear_algebra}

Another mathematical way of viewing two-dimensional space is found in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422), where the idea of independence is crucial. The plane has two dimensions because the length of a [rectangle](wikipedia://en/70048) is independent of its width. In the technical language of linear algebra, the plane is two-dimensional because every point in the plane can be described by a linear combination of two independent [vectors](wikipedia://en/879358).

### Dot product, angle, and length {#Dot_product,_angle,_and_length}

The dot product of two vectors **A** = \[*A*<sub>1</sub>, *A*<sub>2</sub>\] and **B** = \[*B*<sub>1</sub>, *B*<sub>2</sub>\] is defined as:[^o25]

$$\mathbf{A}\cdot \mathbf{B} = A_1B_1 + A_2B_2$$

A vector can be pictured as an arrow. Its magnitude is its length, and its direction is the direction the arrow points. The magnitude of a vector **A** is denoted by $\|\mathbf{A}\|$. In this viewpoint, the dot product of two Euclidean vectors **A** and **B** is defined by[^o26]

$$\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf B = \|\mathbf A\|\,\|\mathbf B\|\cos\theta,$$

where θ is the [angle](wikipedia://en/1196) between **A** and **B**.

The dot product of a vector **A** by itself is

$$\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A = \|\mathbf A\|^2,$$

which gives

$$\|\mathbf A\| = \sqrt{\mathbf A\cdot\mathbf A},$$

the formula for the [Euclidean length](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_length) of the vector.

## In calculus {#In_calculus}

### Gradient {#Gradient}

In a rectangular coordinate system, the gradient is given by

$$\nabla f = \frac{\partial f}{\partial x} \mathbf{i} + \frac{\partial f}{\partial y} \mathbf{j} \,.$$

### Line integrals and double integrals {#Line_integrals_and_double_integrals}

For some [scalar field](wikipedia://en/161879) *f* : *U* ⊆ **R**<sup>*2*</sup> → **R**, the line integral along a [piecewise smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_smooth) [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) *C* ⊂ *U* is defined as

$$\int\limits_C f\, ds = \int_a^b f(\mathbf{r}(t)) |\mathbf{r}'(t)|\,dt,$$

where **r**: \[a, b\] → *C* is an arbitrary [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) [parametrization](wikipedia://en/576108) of the curve *C* such that **r**(*a*) and **r**(*b*) give the endpoints of *C* and $a < b$.

For a [vector field](wikipedia://en/62641) **F** : *U* ⊆ **R**<sup>*2*</sup> → **R**<sup>*2*</sup>, the line integral along a [piecewise smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_smooth) [curve](wikipedia://en/89246) *C* ⊂ *U*, in the direction of **r**, is defined as

$$\int\limits_C \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r})\cdot\,d\mathbf{r} = \int_a^b \mathbf{F}(\mathbf{r}(t))\cdot\mathbf{r}'(t)\,dt,$$

where · is the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) and **r**: \[a, b\] → *C* is a [bijective](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bijective) [parametrization](wikipedia://en/576108) of the curve *C* such that **r**(*a*) and **r**(*b*) give the endpoints of *C*.

A [double integral](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double_integral) refers to an [integral](wikipedia://en/15532) within a region *D* in **R**<sup>2</sup> of a [function](wikipedia://en/185427) $f(x,y),$ and is usually written as:

$$\iint\limits_D f(x,y)\,dx\,dy.$$

::: {#o31 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Fundamental theorem of line integrals"}

### Fundamental theorem of line integrals {#Fundamental_theorem_of_line_integrals}

The [fundamental theorem of line integrals](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fundamental_theorem_of_line_integrals) says that a [line integral](wikipedia://en/21245414) through a [gradient](wikipedia://en/12461) field can be evaluated by evaluating the original scalar field at the endpoints of the curve.

Let $\varphi : U \subseteq \mathbb{R}^2 \to \mathbb{R}$. Then

$$\varphi\left(\mathbf{q}\right)-\varphi\left(\mathbf{p}\right) = \int_{\gamma[\mathbf{p},\mathbf{q}]} \nabla\varphi(\mathbf{r})\cdot d\mathbf{r} ,$$

with **p**, **q** the endpoints of the curve γ.

:::

::: {#o33 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Green's theorem"}

### Green's theorem {#Green's_theorem}

Let *C* be a positively [oriented](wikipedia://en/3633706), [piecewise smooth](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Piecewise_smooth), [simple closed curve](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simple_closed_curve) in a [plane](wikipedia://en/19469852), and let *D* be the region bounded by *C*. If *L* and *M* are functions of (*x*, *y*) defined on an [open region](wikipedia://en/39358) containing *D* and have [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [partial derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_derivatives) there, then[^o34][^o35]

$$\oint_{C} (L\, dx + M\, dy) = \iint_{D} \left(\frac{\partial M}{\partial x} - \frac{\partial L}{\partial y}\right)\, dx\, dy$$

where the path of integration along C is [counterclockwise](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counterclockwise).

:::

## In topology {#In_topology}

In [topology](wikipedia://en/29954), the plane is characterized as being the unique [contractible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contractible) [2-manifold](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2-manifold).

Its dimension is characterized by the fact that removing a point from the plane leaves a space that is connected, but not [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected).

## In graph theory {#In_graph_theory}

In [graph theory](wikipedia://en/12401), a [planar graph](wikipedia://en/24314) is a [graph](wikipedia://en/325806) that can be [embedded](wikipedia://en/8149170) in the plane, i.e., it can be drawn on the plane in such a way that its edges intersect only at their endpoints. In other words, it can be drawn in such a way that no edges cross each other.[^o38] Such a drawing is called a *plane graph* or *planar embedding of the graph*. A plane graph can be defined as a planar graph with a mapping from every node to a point on a plane, and from every edge to a [plane curve](wikipedia://en/971691) on that plane, such that the extreme points of each curve are the points mapped from its end nodes, and all curves are disjoint except on their extreme points.
