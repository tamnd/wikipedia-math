# Divisor summatory function

*Image: Divisor-summatory.svg.* The summatory function, with leading terms removed, for $x < 10^4$

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Divisor summatory function"}

In [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), the **divisor summatory function** is a function that is a sum over the [divisor function](wikipedia://en/407249). It frequently occurs in the study of the asymptotic behaviour of the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809). The various studies of the behaviour of the divisor function are sometimes called **divisor problems**.

:::

::: {#o3 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

The divisor summatory function is defined as

$$D(x)=\sum_{n\le x} d(n) = \sum_{j,k \atop jk\le x} 1$$

where

$$d(n)=\sigma_0(n) = \sum_{j,k \atop jk=n} 1$$

is the [divisor function](wikipedia://en/407249). The divisor function counts the number of ways that the integer *n* can be written as a product of two integers. More generally, one defines

$$D_k(x)=\sum_{n\le x} d_k(n)= \sum_{m\le x}\sum_{mn\le x} d_{k-1}(n)$$

where *d*<sub>*k*</sub>(*n*) counts the number of ways that *n* can be written as a product of *k* numbers. This quantity can be visualized as the count of the number of lattice points fenced off by a hyperbolic surface in *k* dimensions. Thus, for *k* = 2, *D*(*x*) = *D*<sub>2</sub>(*x*) counts the number of points on a [square lattice](wikipedia://en/2874853) bounded on the left by the vertical-axis, on the bottom by the horizontal-axis, and to the upper-right by the hyperbola *jk* = *x*. Roughly, this shape may be envisioned as a hyperbolic [simplex](wikipedia://en/39781). This allows us to provide an alternative expression for *D*(*x*), and a simple way to compute it in $O(\sqrt{x})$ time:

$D(x)=\sum_{k=1}^x \left\lfloor\frac{x}{k}\right\rfloor = 2 \sum_{k=1}^u \left\lfloor\frac{x}{k}\right\rfloor - u^2$, where $u = \left\lfloor \sqrt{x}\right\rfloor$

If the hyperbola in this context is replaced by a circle then determining the value of the resulting function is known as the [Gauss circle problem](wikipedia://en/19474055).

Sequence of *D*(*n*) : 0, 1, 3, 5, 8, 10, 14, 16, 20, 23, 27, 29, 35, 37, 41, 45, 50, 52, 58, 60, 66, 70, 74, 76, 84, 87, 91, 95, 101, 103, 111, ...

:::

## Dirichlet's divisor problem {#Dirichlet's_divisor_problem}

Finding a closed form for this summed expression seems to be beyond the techniques available, but it is possible to give approximations. The leading behavior of the series is given by

$$D(x) = x\log x + x(2\gamma-1) + \Delta(x)\$$

where $\gamma$ is the [Euler–Mascheroni constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euler–Mascheroni_constant), and the error term is

$$\Delta(x) = O\left(\sqrt{x}\right).$$

Here, $O$ denotes [Big-O notation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Big-O_notation). This estimate can be proven using the [Dirichlet hyperbola method](wikipedia://en/57667287), and was first established by [Dirichlet](wikipedia://en/240002) in 1849.[^o5] The **Dirichlet divisor problem**, precisely stated, is to improve this error bound by finding the smallest value of $\theta$ for which

$$\Delta(x) = O\left(x^{\theta+\epsilon}\right)$$

holds true for all $\epsilon > 0$. As of today, this problem remains unsolved. Progress has been slow. Many of the same methods work for this problem and for [Gauss's circle problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gauss's_circle_problem), another lattice-point [counting problem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Counting_problem_%28complexity%29). Section F1 of *Unsolved Problems in Number Theory* [^o6] surveys what is known and not known about these problems.

- In 1904, [G. Voronoi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/G._Voronoi) proved that the error term can be improved to $O(x^{1/3}\log x).$ [^o7]
- In 1916, [G. H. Hardy](wikipedia://en/87599) showed that $\inf \theta \ge 1/4$. In particular, he demonstrated that for some constant $K$, there exist arbitrarily large values of *x* for which $\Delta(x) > Kx^{1/4}$ and arbitrarily large values of *x* for which $\Delta(x) < -Kx^{1/4}$.[^o5]
- In 1922, [J. van der Corput](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/J._van_der_Corput) improved Dirichlet's bound to $\inf \theta \le 33/100 = 0.33$.[^o7]
- In 1928, van der Corput proved that $\inf \theta \le 27/82 = 0.3\overline{29268}$.[^o7]
- In 1950, [Chih Tsung-tao](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Chih_Tsung-tao) and independently in 1953 [H. E. Richert](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._E._Richert) proved that $\inf \theta \le 15/46 = 0.32608695652...$.[^o7]
- In 1969, [Grigori Kolesnik](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grigori_Kolesnik_%28mathematician%29) demonstrated that $\inf \theta \le 12/37 = 0.\overline{324}$.[^o7]
- In 1973, Kolesnik demonstrated that $\inf \theta \le 346/1067 = 0.32427366448...$.[^o7]
- In 1982, Kolesnik demonstrated that $\inf \theta \le 35/108 = 0.32\overline{407}$.[^o7]
- In 1988, [H. Iwaniec](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H._Iwaniec) and [C. J. Mozzochi](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/C._J._Mozzochi) proved that $\inf \theta \leq 7/22 = 0.3\overline{18}$.[^o8]
- In 2003, [M.N. Huxley](wikipedia://en/5385232) improved this to show that $\inf \theta \leq 131/416 = 0.31490384615...$.[^o9]

So, $\inf \theta$ lies somewhere between 1/4 and 131/416 (approx. 0.3149); it is widely conjectured to be 1/4. Theoretical evidence lends credence to this conjecture, since $\Delta(x)/x^{1/4}$ has a (non-Gaussian) limiting distribution.[^o10] The value of 1/4 would also follow from a conjecture on [exponent pairs](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exponent_pairs).[^o11]

## Piltz divisor problem {#Piltz_divisor_problem}

In the generalized case, one has

$$D_k(x) = xP_k(\log x)+\Delta_k(x) \,$$

where $P_k$ is a [polynomial of degree](wikipedia://en/5930652) $k-1$. Using simple estimates, it is readily shown that

$$\Delta_k(x)=O\left(x^{1-1/k} \log^{k-2} x\right)$$

for integer $k\ge 2$. As in the $k=2$ case, the infimum of the bound is not known for any value of $k$. Computing these infima is known as the Piltz divisor problem, after the name of the German mathematician [Adolf Piltz](wikipedia://en/37748922) (also see his German page). Defining the order $\alpha_k$ as the smallest value for which $\Delta_k(x)=O\left(x^{\alpha_k+\varepsilon}\right)$ holds, for any $\varepsilon>0$, one has the following results (note that $\alpha_2$ is the $\theta$ of the previous section):

$\alpha_2\le\frac{131}{416}\ ,$[^o9]

$\alpha_3 \le\frac{43}{96}\ ,$[^o13] and[^o14]

$$\begin{aligned} \alpha_k & \le \frac{3k-4}{4k}\quad(4\le k\le 8) \\[6pt] \alpha_9 & \le\frac{35}{54}\ ,\quad \alpha_{10}\le\frac{41}{60}\ ,\quad \alpha_{11}\le\frac{7}{10} \\[6pt] \alpha_k & \le \frac{k-2}{k+2}\quad(12\le k\le 25) \\[6pt] \alpha_k & \le \frac{k-1}{k+4}\quad(26\le k\le 50) \\[6pt] \alpha_k & \le \frac{31k-98}{32k}\quad(51\le k\le 57) \\[6pt] \alpha_k & \le \frac{7k-34}{7k}\quad(k\ge 58) \end{aligned}$$

- [E. C. Titchmarsh](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E._C._Titchmarsh) conjectures that $\alpha_k =\frac{k-1}{2k}\ .$

## Mellin transform {#Mellin_transform}

Both portions may be expressed as [Mellin transforms](wikipedia://en/387297):

$$D(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} \zeta^2(w) \frac {x^w}{w}\, dw$$

for $c>1$. Here, $\zeta(s)$ is the [Riemann zeta function](wikipedia://en/25809). Similarly, one has

$$\Delta(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{c^\prime-i\infty}^{c^\prime+i\infty} \zeta^2(w) \frac {x^w}{w} \,dw$$

with $0<c^\prime<1$. The leading term of $D(x)$ is obtained by shifting the contour past the double pole at $w=1$: the leading term is just the [residue](wikipedia://en/73102), by [Cauchy's integral formula](wikipedia://en/72827). In general, one has

$$D_k(x)=\frac{1}{2\pi i} \int_{c-i\infty}^{c+i\infty} \zeta^k(w) \frac {x^w}{w} \,dw$$

and likewise for $\Delta_k(x)$, for $k\ge 2$.
