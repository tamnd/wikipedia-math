# Quasiconformal mapping

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Quasiconformal mapping"}

In mathematical [complex analysis](wikipedia://en/5759), a **quasiconformal mapping** is a (weakly differentiable) [homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/13660) between [plane](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Plane_%28geometry%29) domains which to first order takes small circles to small ellipses of bounded [eccentricity](wikipedia://en/9277#Eccentricity). Quasiconformal mappings are a generalization of [conformal mappings](wikipedia://en/50627) that permit the bounded distortion of angles locally. Quasiconformal mappings were introduced by (Grötzsch 1928) and named by (Ahlfors 1935),

:::

Intuitively, let *f* : *D* → *D*′ be an [orientation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orientation_%28mathematics%29)-preserving homeomorphism between [open sets](wikipedia://en/39358) in the plane. If *f* is [continuously differentiable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuously_differentiable), it is *K*-quasiconformal if, at every point, its derivative maps circles to ellipses with the ratio of the major to minor axis bounded by *K*.

::: {#o5 .definition cue=heading name="Definition"}

## Definition {#Definition}

Suppose *f* : *D* → *D*′ where *D* and *D*′ are two domains in **C**. There are a variety of equivalent definitions, depending on the required smoothness of *f*. If *f* is assumed to have [continuous](wikipedia://en/6122) [partial derivatives](wikipedia://en/52565), then *f* is quasiconformal provided it satisfies the [Beltrami equation](wikipedia://en/31356616)

::: {#o6 .equation cue=numblk name="1"}

$$\frac{\partial f}{\partial\bar{z}} = \mu(z)\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}, \tag{1}$$

:::

for some complex valued [Lebesgue measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_measurable) *μ* satisfying $\sup |\mu| < 1$ (Bers 1977). This equation admits a geometrical interpretation. Equip *D* with the [metric tensor](wikipedia://en/195795)

$$ds^2 = \Omega(z)^2\left| dz + \mu(z) \, d\bar{z}\right|^2,$$

where Ω(*z*) > 0. Then *f* satisfies ((1)) precisely when it is a conformal transformation from *D* equipped with this metric to the domain *D*′ equipped with the standard Euclidean metric. The function *f* is then called ***μ*-conformal**. More generally, the continuous differentiability of *f* can be replaced by the weaker condition that *f* be in the [Sobolev space](wikipedia://en/611964) *W*<sup>1,2</sup>(*D*) of functions whose first-order [distributional derivatives](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Distributional_derivative) are in [L<sup>2</sup>(*D*)](wikipedia://en/45194). In this case, *f* is required to be a [weak solution](wikipedia://en/3555416) of ((1)). When *μ* is zero almost everywhere, any homeomorphism in *W*<sup>1,2</sup>(*D*) that is a weak solution of ((1)) is conformal.

Without appeal to an auxiliary metric, consider the effect of the [pullback](wikipedia://en/494995) under *f* of the usual Euclidean metric. The resulting metric is then given by

$$\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|^2\left|dz+\mu(z)\,d\bar{z}\right|^2$$

which, relative to the background Euclidean metric $dz \, d\bar{z}$, has [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue)

$$(1+|\mu|)^2\left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|^2,\qquad (1-|\mu|)^2 \left|\frac{\partial f}{\partial z}\right|^2.$$

The eigenvalues represent, respectively, the squared length of the major and minor axes of the ellipse obtained by pulling back along *f* the unit circle in the tangent plane.

Accordingly, the *dilatation* of *f* at a point *z* is defined by

$$K(z) = \frac{1+|\mu(z)|}{1-|\mu(z)|}.$$

The (essential) [supremum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supremum) of *K*(*z*) is given by

$$K = \sup_{z\in D} |K(z)| = \frac{1+\|\mu\|_\infty}{1-\|\mu\|_\infty}$$

and is called the dilatation of *f*. If *f* is *K*-quasiconformal for some finite *K*, then *f* is quasiconformal.

A definition based on the notion of [extremal length](wikipedia://en/16819101) is as follows. If there is a finite *K* such that for every collection **Γ** of curves in *D* the extremal length of **Γ** is at most *K* times the extremal length of {*f* o γ : γ ∈ **Γ**}. Then *f* is *K*-quasiconformal.

:::

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/fact cue=heading name="Properties"}

## Properties {#Properties}

If *K* > 1 then the maps *x* + *iy* ↦ *Kx* + *iy* and *x* + *iy* ↦ *x* + *iKy* are both quasiconformal and have constant dilatation *K*.

If *s* > −1 then the map $z\mapsto z\,|z|^{s}$ is quasiconformal (here *z* is a [complex number](wikipedia://en/5826)) and has constant dilatation $\max(1+s, \frac{1}{1+s})$. When *s* ≠ 0, this is an example of a quasiconformal homeomorphism that is not smooth. If *s* = 0, this is simply the identity map.

A homeomorphism is 1-quasiconformal if and only if it is conformal. If *f* : *D* → *D*′ is *K*-quasiconformal and *g* : *D*′ → *D*′′ is *K*′-quasiconformal, then *g* o *f* is *KK*′-quasiconformal. The inverse of a *K*-quasiconformal homeomorphism is *K*-quasiconformal. Since *K*-quasiconformal maps are preserved under composition with conformal maps, one can extend the notion of *K*-quasiconformal maps to general [Riemann surfaces](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_surfaces).

The space of K-quasiconformal mappings from the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628) to itself mapping three distinct points to three given points is compact.

:::

::: {#o11 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Measurable Riemann mapping theorem"}

## Measurable Riemann mapping theorem {#Measurable_Riemann_mapping_theorem}

Of central importance in the theory of quasiconformal mappings in two dimensions is the [measurable Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/30947139), proved by Lars Ahlfors and Lipman Bers. The theorem generalizes the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215) from conformal to quasiconformal homeomorphisms, and is stated as follows. Suppose that *D* is a simply connected domain in **C** that is not equal to **C**, and suppose that *μ* : *D* → **C** is [Lebesgue measurable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lebesgue_measurable) and satisfies $\|\mu\|_\infty<1$. Then there is a quasiconformal homeomorphism *f* from *D* to the unit disk which is in the Sobolev space *W*<sup>1,2</sup>(*D*) and satisfies the corresponding Beltrami equation ((1)) in the [distributional sense](wikipedia://en/3555416). As with Riemann's mapping theorem, this *f* is unique up to 3 real parameters.

:::

## Computational quasi-conformal geometry {#Computational_quasi-conformal_geometry}

Recently, quasi-conformal geometry has attracted attention from different fields, such as applied mathematics, computer vision and medical imaging. Computational quasi-conformal geometry has been developed, which extends the quasi-conformal theory into a discrete setting. It has found various important applications in medical image analysis, computer vision and graphics.[^n1]
