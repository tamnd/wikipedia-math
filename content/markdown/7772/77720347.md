# Projectively unique polytope

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Projectively unique polytope"}

In [discrete geometry](wikipedia://en/386468), a [polytope](wikipedia://en/1211056) is **projectively unique** (or **projectively stable**) if it has a unique [convex realization](wikipedia://en/1817885) up to [projective transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation).

:::

The study of projectively unique polytopes was initiated by [Geoffrey C. Shephard](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Geoffrey_C._Shephard), [Micha Perles](wikipedia://en/35294296), [Peter McMullen](wikipedia://en/40964822) and [Branko Grünbaum](wikipedia://en/1731976). Later significant contributions are also due to [Karim Adiprasito](wikipedia://en/47874333) and [Günter M. Ziegler](wikipedia://en/14628647).

## Introduction {#Introduction}

Two convex polytope $P,Q\subset\mathbb R^n$ are *combinatorially equivalent* if they have the same number of faces with the same incidence relations between them. Formally this means that $P$ and $Q$ have isomorphic [face lattices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_lattice), or equivalently, isomorphic underlying [abstract polytopes](wikipedia://en/1211056). One also says that both $P$ and $Q$ are *realizations* of the face lattice (or abstract polytope).

*Image: Equivalence types 2.png.* Different realizations of the cube. From left to right: 1) the standard cube; 2) an affine transformation of the standard cube; 3) a projective transformation of the standard cube that is not an affine transformation; 4) a realization of the cube that is not a projective transformation of the standard cube.

Given a convex polytope $P\subset\mathbb R^n$ many combinatorially equivalent polytopes can be generated through transformation of the ambient space, such as [isometries](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Isometries) or [affine transformations](wikipedia://en/38449). The largest class of transformations of Euclidean space that preserves coplanarity, and hence maps polytopes onto combinatorially equivalent polytopes, is the class of [projective transformations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Projective_transformation). A polytope $P$ is **projectively unique** if all other realizations are obtained through projective transformations; or in other words, if $P$ has a *unique* convex realization up to projective transformations.

For example, [simplices](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simplex_%28geometry%29), such as [triangles](wikipedia://en/30654) and [tetrahedra](wikipedia://en/30606), are projectively unique. In contrast, the [cube](wikipedia://en/6285) is not projectively unique, as it has realizations that are not projectively equivalent to the standard cube (see the figure).

## Known projectively unique polytopes {#Known_projectively_unique_polytopes}

Projective transformations on $n$-dimensional Euclidean space can map any $n+2$ points onto any other $n+2$ points. Hence, any $n$-dimensional polytope with at most $n+2$ vertices is projectively unique. Being projectively unique is moreover closed under [polar duality](wikipedia://en/8815). Since duality swaps vertices and facets, any $n$-dimensional polytope with at most $n+2$ facets is projectively unique as well. This shows that, for example, the [product](wikipedia://en/24104095) of two simplices is projectively unique (such as the [(3,3)-duoprism](wikipedia://en/41745711)). In dimension up to three, these are the only polytopes that are projectively unique, but in dimensions $n\ge 4$ there are examples with more than $n+2$ vertices and facets.

### Dimension two {#Dimension_two}

The following is the complete list of projectively unique polytopes in dimension two:

- [triangle](wikipedia://en/30654)
- [quadrangle](wikipedia://en/25278)

### Dimension three {#Dimension_three}

A [3-polytope](wikipedia://en/23470) is projectively unique if and only if it has at most nine edges.[^o17] The following is therefore the complete list of projectively unique polytopes in dimension three:

- [tetrahedron](wikipedia://en/30606)
- [square pyramid](wikipedia://en/1128455)
- [triangular prism](wikipedia://en/2859584)
- [triangular bipyramid](wikipedia://en/646933)

### Operations {#Operations}

McMullen showed that projectively unique polytopes are closed under the following operations:[^o19][^o18]

- The [join](wikipedia://en/3127114) $P\star Q$ of two polytopes is projectively unique if and only if the $P$ and $Q$ are projectively unique.
- The [vertex sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Vertex_sum) $(P,v)\oplus (Q,w)$ of two projectively unique polytopes is projectively unique.
- If $P$ is projectively unique and $v$ is a vertex of $P$ so that $P$ is *not* a vertex sum with distinguished vertex $v$, then the [subdirect sum](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Subdirect_sum) $(P,v)\oplus\Delta_1$ is projectively unique (this is also known as *splitting* the vertex $v$).

These operations are sufficient to generate Shephard's list of projectively unique 4-polytopes. But not all projectively unique polytopes can be generated using these operations, for example, Perles' non-rational polytope (see below).

### Other examples {#Other_examples}

Perles & Shephard (1974) used [Gale diagrams](wikipedia://en/64979699) to construct many projectively unique polytopes and showed that their number increases at least exponentially with the dimension.[^o20] In general, one can start from a projectively unique point-line configuration, for example, obtained by encoding a polynomial with a single real zero. After doubling all points this can be interpreted as the Gale diagram of a projectively unique polytope. Starting from a suitable configuration (for example, the [Perles configuration](wikipedia://en/35289232)) this can be used to construct projectively unique polytopes that have no realization with rational coordinates.[^o22]

Faces of projectively unique polytopes need not be themselves projectively unique. In 2013 Adiprasito & Padrol showed that every polytope with algebraic vertex coordinates appears as a face of a projectively unique polytope.[^o21] This shows that projectively unique polytopes satisfy a form of [universality](wikipedia://en/24318162) and that no simple structure theory can be expected.

In 2015 Adiprasito & Ziegler constructed infinitely many distinct projectively unique polytopes in dimension 69.[^o17] There are only finitely many projectively unique polytopes in dimension two and three, and it is an open question whether this is also the case in dimension four.

## Linearly unique polytopes {#Linearly_unique_polytopes}

A [centrally symmetric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Central_symmetry) polytope $P$ is **linearly unique** (or **linearly stable**) if it has a unique origin symmetric realization up to linear transformations. The class of linear unique polytopes is also closed under polar duality. Examples of linearly unique polytopes are [cubes](wikipedia://en/39783) and [crosspolytopes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Crosspolytope) in all dimensions, and more generally, all [Hanner polytopes](wikipedia://en/39408400). In 1969 [Peter McMullen](wikipedia://en/40964822) showed that every centrally symmetric [0/1-polytope](wikipedia://en/73074954) is linearly unique.[^o15] In the same paper McMullen sketches an argument that this gives a complete characterization of linearly unique polytopes. Later, David Assaf (1976) constructs a counterexample, showing that McMullen's argument must be incomplete.[^o16] The counterexample provided is a centrally symmetric 0/1-polytope whose dual cannot be realized as a 0/1-polytope.

## Generalizations {#Generalizations}

For each natural number $k\ge 1$ there are polytopes with precisely $k$ distinct convex realizations up to projective transformations. One way to construct such polytopes is using [Gale diagrams](wikipedia://en/64979699). One starts from a point-line configurations with precisely $k$ distinct realizations (up to projective transformations). After doubling all points one can interpret it as the Gale diagram of a convex polytope.[^o13]
