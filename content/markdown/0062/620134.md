# Non-measurable set

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Non-measurable set"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), a **non-measurable set** is a [set](wikipedia://en/26691) which cannot be assigned a meaningful size, such as a length, area, or volume. The term is normally applied to [Lebesgue measure](wikipedia://en/18198), which assigns a length to a very large family of subsets of the real line but which cannot be extended to all subsets. In [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) with the [axiom of choice](wikipedia://en/840), non-measurable subsets of $\mathbb{R}$ exist. The first example of one was constructed by [Giuseppe Vitali](wikipedia://en/634990) in 1905. The existence of non-measurable sets marks a limit on how far the intuitive notion of size can be extended, and it is one of the best-known consequences of the axiom of choice.

:::

Whether such sets can be avoided depends on the required additivity and on the dimension of the space; but in every case the set itself can be obtained only by applying some form of a choice axiom. If the sizes of a countable sequence of disjoint sets are required to sum to the size of their union ([countable additivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_additivity), which the usual axioms of probability demand), then Vitali's construction already rules out assigning a length to every subset of the line. Further, the same obstruction arises in every dimension. If the sizes are required to add up only over finitely many pieces, the line and the plane are free of the obstruction: each admits a measure that is defined on all of its subsets, agrees with ordinary length or area, and is unchanged by rigid motion. Three-dimensional space is not. The [Hausdorff paradox](wikipedia://en/634759) of 1914 and the [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220) of 1924 show that the surface of a sphere and a solid ball can each be divided into finitely many pieces and reassembled into something of a different size, so that in three dimensions no [finitely additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_additive) notion of volume can apply to every subset and still be unaffected by rigid motion.

In 1970, [Robert M. Solovay](wikipedia://en/3160110) showed that existence of a non-measurable set cannot be proved from the Zermelo–Fraenkel axioms alone: assuming an inaccessible cardinal, he constructed a model of ZF in which the weaker axiom of dependent choice holds and every set of real numbers is Lebesgue measurable.

## Background {#Background}

The measure of the union of two disjoint sets is naturally expected to equal the sum of the measures of the two sets. A measure with this property is called [finitely additive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Finitely_additive).[^o3] Finite additivity matches the intuitive notion of area and provides the basis of [Riemann integration](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemann_integration).[^o4] [Countable additivity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countable_additivity), which applies to a [countably infinite](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Countably_infinite) collection of sets, is a stronger axiom that underlies Lebesgue's theory, and with it the convergence theorems and the standard axioms of probability.[^o4][^o5]

## Historical constructions {#Historical_constructions}

*Image: Giuseppe Vitali.jpg.* Giuseppe Vitali (1875–1932)

The first indication that length cannot be defined for arbitrary sets came in 1905, when [Giuseppe Vitali](wikipedia://en/634990) used the axiom of choice to construct a set of real numbers that is not [Lebesgue measurable](wikipedia://en/18198).[^o7][^o8] The [Vitali set](wikipedia://en/424189) shows that no countably additive, translation-invariant measure can be defined on *every* subset of the real line while assigning a length to each interval.

[Stefan Banach](wikipedia://en/29646) proved in 1923 that the line and the plane each admit a measure defined on all of their subsets, not just the Lebesgue-measurable ones. Such a measure is now called a [Banach measure](wikipedia://en/1339598): it is invariant under all [isometries](wikipedia://en/254777); agrees with Lebesgue measure wherever Lebesgue measure is defined; and it is additive over finite unions, though not over countable ones.[^o9][^o10] Therefore, no bounded planar region can be cut into finitely many pieces and reassembled by rigid motions into two copies of itself.

*Image: Stefana Banach - ستيفان بناخ.jpg.* Stefan Banach (1892–1945)

Rotations in three dimensions combine more freely than motions of the plane: about two different axes they can be strung together in endlessly many ways that never bring a point back where it began, leaving room to shuffle points between pieces with none lost or gained. In 1914 [Felix Hausdorff](wikipedia://en/10989) used this freedom to divide the surface of a sphere, apart from a countable set of points, into three disjoint subsets that are [congruent](wikipedia://en/39330) to one another and each also congruent to the union of the other two.[^o11][^o12] No reasonable notion of area survives the [Hausdorff paradox](wikipedia://en/634759): each piece ought to be a third of the sphere, yet one matches the other two combined, and so ought to be two-thirds.

In 1924, Banach and [Alfred Tarski](wikipedia://en/59920) carried the construction into solid bodies.[^o13] The [Banach–Tarski paradox](wikipedia://en/19759220) states that a [ball](wikipedia://en/160556) can be divided into finitely many pieces that, when merely moved and rotated, reassemble into two balls of the original size. The pieces are not chunks of the kind a knife would cut, but infinitely intricate clouds of points obtained using the axiom of choice. No consistent volume can be assigned to all of them, so at least some must be non-measurable sets.[^n1] In 1947, [Raphael M. Robinson](wikipedia://en/693495) showed that the minimum number of pieces needed is five (and only four if the center point of the ball is omitted).[^o15] This paradox applies not simply to spheres, but to arbitrary bounded volumes.[^o11]

Some form of choice is indispensable to all of these constructions. In 1970, [Solovay](wikipedia://en/3160110) showed that the existence of a set of real numbers that is not Lebesgue measurable is not provable within [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](wikipedia://en/152214) alone. Assuming the consistency of an [inaccessible cardinal](wikipedia://en/248076), he constructed a model of ZF, called the [Solovay model](wikipedia://en/25274967), in which the [axiom of dependent choice](wikipedia://en/441950) holds yet every set of real numbers is Lebesgue measurable and the full axiom of choice fails.[^o16] In 1984, [Saharon Shelah](wikipedia://en/331211) proved that the inaccessible cardinal is necessary.[^o17]

## Construction of a non-measurable set {#Construction_of_a_non-measurable_set}

The following construction, which is essentially Vitali's, produces a set to which no notion of length can be assigned. Let $S$ be the unit circle in the complex plane, and let $G$ be the group of rotations through angles which are rational multiples of $\pi$. Then $G$ is countable, being isomorphic to $\mathbb{Q}/\mathbb{Z}$, while $S$ is uncountable. Hence $S$ splits into uncountably many [orbits](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_%28group_theory%29) under $G$; the orbit of $s$ is the countable set $\{ s e^{i q \pi} : q \in \mathbb{Q} \}$. Using the axiom of choice, pick one point from each orbit to form a set $X$. Its rotated copies $gX$, one for each $g \in G$, are then pairwise disjoint, mutually congruent, countable, and together make up the whole circle. No rotation-invariant, countably additive probability measure on $S$ can give $X$ a value. The copies are congruent, so each would have to have the same measure as $X$; were that measure zero the circle would measure zero rather than one, and were it positive the circle would measure infinity (a countable sum of equal positive numbers diverges).[^n2] No such measure can give $X$ a value; $X$ is therefore non-measurable.

The analogous construction on the line, grouping real numbers that differ by a rational number (the classes of $\mathbb{R}/\mathbb{Q}$) and picking one from each, yields the Vitali set.[^o21]

:::

## The measure problem {#The_measure_problem}

[Lebesgue](wikipedia://en/49514)'s *measure problem* seeks a function $\mu$ that assigns every bounded subset of $\mathbb{R}^n$ a non-negative real number such that: $\mu([0,1]^n)=1$; $\mu$ is unchanged by isometries; and the measures of disjoint sets add up.[^n3] Normalization, invariance, and additivity make $\mu$ a concept of volume. However, all three conditions cannot apply to all subsets. If additivity is required over countable families, Vitali's construction rules out a solution in every dimension.[^o24] If it is required only over finite families, the line and the plane do admit solutions (the Banach measures), but the Hausdorff and Banach–Tarski paradoxes show that none exists for $n \ge 3$.[^n3]

To overcome these barriers, standard measure theory abandons the requirement that $\mu$ be defined on all subsets. Instead, Lebesgue measurable sets are used, retaining countable additivity as required by convergence theorems and the [axioms of probability](wikipedia://en/23539).[^o26]

Alternatively, the axiom of choice may be weakened: in the Solovay model every set of real numbers is measurable.[^o16] This second route demands more than dropping choice alone, since the [Hahn–Banach theorem](wikipedia://en/13860), which does not need the full axiom, already yields a non-measurable set and even the Banach–Tarski paradox.[^o27][^o28]
