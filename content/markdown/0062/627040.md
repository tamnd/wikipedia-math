# Electronic signature

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Electronic signature"}

An **electronic signature**, or **e-signature**, is [data](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data) that is logically associated with other data and which is used by the [signatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature) to sign the associated data.[^o54][^o56][^o2] This type of signature has the same [legal standing](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Legal_standing) as a handwritten signature as long as it adheres to the requirements of the specific regulation under which it was created (e.g., [eIDAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EIDAS) in the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union), [NIST-DSS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digital_Signature_Standard) in the [USA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_States) or [ZertES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZertES) in [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland)).[^o4][^o3]

:::

Electronic signatures are a legal concept distinct from [digital signatures](wikipedia://en/59644), a cryptographic mechanism often used to implement electronic signatures. While an electronic signature can be as simple as a name entered in an electronic document, digital signatures are increasingly used in [e-commerce](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/E-commerce) and in regulatory filings to implement electronic signatures in a [cryptographically protected](wikipedia://en/18934432) way. Standardization agencies like [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST) or [ETSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETSI) provide standards for their implementation (e.g., [NIST-DSS](wikipedia://en/59470), [XAdES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XAdES) or [PAdES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PAdES)).[^o4][^o5] The concept itself is not new, with [common law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Common_law) jurisdictions having recognized [telegraph](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraph) signatures as far back as the mid-19th century and [faxed](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fax) signatures since the 1980s.

## Description {#Description}

The USA's [E-Sign Act](wikipedia://en/1855696),[^o7][^o8] signed June 30, 2000 by [President Clinton](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bill_Clinton) was described months later as "more like a seal than a signature."[^o9]

An electronic signature is intended to provide a secure and accurate identification method for the signatory during a transaction. Definitions of electronic signatures vary depending on the applicable [jurisdiction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jurisdiction_%28area%29). A common denominator in most countries is the level of an [advanced electronic signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_electronic_signature) requiring that:

1. The [signatory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Signature) can be uniquely identified and linked to the signature
1. The signatory must have sole control of the [private key](wikipedia://en/24222) that was used to create the electronic signature
1. The signature must be capable of identifying if its accompanying data has been tampered with after the message was signed
1. In the event that the accompanying data has been changed, the signature must be invalidated[^o26]

Electronic signatures may be created with increasing levels of security, with each having its own set of requirements and means of creation on various levels that prove the validity of the signature. To provide an even stronger [probative value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relevance_%28law%29) than the above described advanced electronic signature, some countries like member states of the European Union or Switzerland introduced the qualified electronic signature. It is difficult to challenge the authorship of a statement signed with a [qualified electronic signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualified_electronic_signature) - the statement is [non-repudiable](wikipedia://en/52491).[^o10] Technically, a qualified electronic signature is implemented through an advanced electronic signature that utilizes a digital certificate, which has been encrypted through a security signature-creating device[^o11] and which has been authenticated by a [qualified trust service provider](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualified_electronic_signature#Qualified_trust_service_providers).[^o12]

## In contract law {#In_contract_law}

Since well before the [American Civil War](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Civil_War) began in 1861, [morse code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_code) was used to send messages electrically via the telegraph. Some of these messages were agreements to terms that were intended as enforceable [contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contract). An early acceptance of the enforceability of telegraphic messages as electronic signatures came from a [New Hampshire Supreme Court](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/New_Hampshire_Supreme_Court) case, Howley v. Whipple, in 1869.[^o15][^o16]

In the 1980s, many companies and even some individuals began using fax machines for high-priority or time-sensitive delivery of documents. Although the original signature on the original document was on paper, the image of the signature and its transmission was electronic.[^o17]

Courts in various jurisdictions have decided that enforceable legality of electronic signatures can include agreements made by email, entering a [personal identification number](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_identification_number) (PIN) into a bank [ATM](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automated_teller_machine), signing a credit or debit slip with a digital pen pad device (an application of [graphics tablet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Graphics_tablet) technology) at a [point of sale](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Point_of_sale), installing software with a [clickwrap](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Clickwrap) [software license agreement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Software_license_agreement) on the package, and signing electronic documents online.

The first agreement signed electronically by two sovereign nations was a Joint Communiqué recognizing the growing importance of the promotion of electronic commerce, signed by the United States and Ireland in 1998.[^o18]

### Enforceability {#Enforceability}

In 1996 the [United Nations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations) published the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce.[^o20] Article 7 of the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Commerce was highly influential in the development of electronic signature laws around the world, including in the US.[^o21] In 2001, UNCITRAL concluded work on a dedicated text, the UNCITRAL Model Law on Electronic Signatures,[^o22] which has been adopted in some 30 jurisdictions.[^o23] Article 9, paragraph 3 of the [United Nations Convention on the Use of Electronic Communications in International Contracts](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/United_Nations_Convention_on_the_Use_of_Electronic_Communications_in_International_Contracts), 2005, which establishes a mechanism for functional equivalence between electronic and handwritten signatures at the international level as well as for the cross-border recognition. The latest UNCITRAL text dealing with electronic signatures is article 16 of the UNCITRAL Model Law on the Use and Cross-border Recognition of Identity Management and Trust Services (2022).

Canadian law ([PIPEDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Personal_Information_Protection_and_Electronic_Documents_Act)) attempts to clarify the situation by first defining a generic electronic signature as "a signature that consists of one or more letters, characters, numbers or other symbols in digital form incorporated in, attached to or associated with an electronic document," then defining a secure electronic signature as an electronic signature with specific properties. PIPEDA's secure electronic signature regulations refine the definition as being a digital signature applied and verified in a specific manner.[^o24]

In the [European Union](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Union), EU [Regulation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Regulation_%28European_Union%29) No 910/2014 on electronic identification and trust services for electronic transactions in the European [internal market](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Single_Market) ([eIDAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EIDAS)) sets the legal frame for electronic signatures. It repeals [Directive](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Directive_%28European_Union%29) 1999/93/EC.[^o56] The current and applicable version of eIDAS was published by the [European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Parliament) and the [European Council](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Council) on July 23, 2014. Following Article 25 (1) of the eIDAS regulation, an [advanced electronic signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Advanced_electronic_signature) shall “not be denied legal effect and admissibility as evidence in legal proceedings". However it will reach a higher [probative value](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Relevance_%28law%29) when enhanced to the level of a [qualified electronic signature](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Qualified_electronic_signature). By requiring the use of a [qualified electronic signature creation device](wikipedia://en/52313606)[^o25] and being based on a certificate that has been issued by a qualified trust service provider, the upgraded advanced signature then carries according to Article 25 (2) of the eIDAS Regulation the same legal value as a handwritten signature.[^o56][^o26] However, this is only regulated in the European Union and similarly through [ZertES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ZertES) in [Switzerland](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Switzerland). A qualified electronic signature is not defined in the United States.[^o27][^o28]

The U.S. Code defines an electronic signature for the purpose of US law as "an electronic sound, symbol, or process, attached to or logically associated with a contract or other record and executed or adopted by a person with the intent to sign the record."[^o29] It may be an electronic transmission of the document which contains the signature, as in the case of [facsimile](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Facsimile) transmissions, or it may be encoded message, such as [telegraphy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Telegraphy) using [Morse code](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Morse_code).

In the United States, the definition of what qualifies as an electronic signature is wide and is set out in the [Uniform Electronic Transactions Act](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uniform_Electronic_Transactions_Act) ("UETA") released by the National Conference of Commissioners on Uniform State Laws (NCCUSL) in 1999.[^o30] It was influenced by [ABA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/American_Bar_Association) committee white papers and the uniform law promulgated by NCCUSL. Under UETA, the term means "an electronic sound, symbol, or process, attached to or logically associated with a record and executed or adopted by a person with the intent to sign the record." This definition and many other core concepts of UETA are echoed in the U.S. [ESign Act](wikipedia://en/1855696) of 2000.[^o29] 48 US states, the District of Columbia, and the US Virgin Islands have enacted UETA.[^o31] Only New York and Illinois have not enacted UETA,[^o31] but each of those states has adopted its own electronic signatures statute.[^o32][^o33][^o34] As of June 11, 2020, Washington State Office of CIO adopted UETA.[^o35]

In Australia, an electronic signature is recognised as "not necessarily the writing in of a name, but maybe any mark which identifies it as the act of the party.”[^o36] Under the Electronic Transactions Acts in each Federal, State and Territory jurisdiction, an electronic signature may be considered enforceable if (a) there was a method used to identify the person and to indicate that person’s intention in respect of the information communicated and the method was either: (i) as reliable as appropriate for the purpose for which the electronic communication was generated or communicated, in light of all the circumstances, including the relevant agreement; or (ii) proven in fact to have fulfilled the functions above by itself or together with further evidence and the person to whom the signature is required to be given consents to that method.[^o37]

### Legal definitions {#Legal_definitions}

Various laws have been passed internationally to facilitate commerce by using electronic records and signatures in interstate and foreign commerce. The intent is to ensure the validity and legal effect of contracts entered electronically. For instance,

**[PIPEDA](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PIPEDA) (Canadian federal law)**

(1) An electronic signature is "a signature that consists of one or more letters, characters, numbers or other symbols in digital form incorporated in, attached to or associated with an [electronic document](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Electronic_document)";

(2) A secure electronic signature is an electronic signature that

(a) is unique to the person making the signature;

(b) the technology or process used to make the signature is under the sole control of the person making the signature;

(c) the technology or process can be used to identify the person using the technology or process; and

(d) the electronic signature can be linked with an electronic document in such a way that it can be used to determine whether the electronic document has been changed since the electronic signature was incorporated in, attached to, or associated with the electronic document.

(4) ELECTRONIC RECORD- The term 'electronic record' means a contract or other record created, generated, sent, communicated, received, or stored by electronic means.

(5) ELECTRONIC SIGNATURE- The term 'electronic signature' means an electronic sound, symbol, or process, attached to or logically associated with a contract or other record and executed or adopted by a person with the intent to sign the record.

### Usage {#Usage}

In 2016, [Aberdeen Strategy and Research](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberdeen_Strategy_and_Research) reported that 73% of "best-in-class" and 34% of all other respondents surveyed made use of electronic signature processes in [supply chain](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Supply_chain) and [procurement](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Procurement), delivering benefits in the speed and efficiency of key procurement activities. The percentages of their survey respondents using electronic signatures in [accounts payable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accounts_payable) and [accounts receivable](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Accounts_receivable) processes were a little lower, 53% of "best-in-class" respondents in each case.[^o49]

## Technological implementations (underlying technology) {#Technological_implementations_(underlying_technology)}

### Digital signature {#Digital_signature}

*Image: Private key signing.svg.* A diagram showing how a digital signature is applied and then verified

Digital signatures are [cryptographic](wikipedia://en/18934432) implementations of **electronic signatures** used as a proof of [authenticity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Authenticity_%28philosophy%29), [data integrity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Data_integrity) and [non-repudiation](wikipedia://en/52491) of communications conducted over the [Internet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Internet). When implemented in compliance to digital signature standards, digital signing should offer end-to-end privacy with the signing process being user-friendly and secure. Digital signatures are generated and verified through standardized frameworks such as the [Digital Signature Algorithm](wikipedia://en/59470) (DSA)[^o5][^o55] by [NIST](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/NIST) or in compliance to the [XAdES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/XAdES), [PAdES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PAdES) or [CAdES](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/CAdES_%28computing%29) standards, specified by the [ETSI](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/ETSI).[^o52]

There are typically three algorithms involved with the digital signature process:

- [Key generation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Key_generation) – This algorithm provides a private key along with its corresponding public key.
- Signing – This algorithm produces a signature upon receiving a private key and the message that is being signed.
- Verification – This algorithm checks for the message's authenticity by verifying it along with the signature and public key.[^o53]

The process of digital signing requires that its accompanying public key can then authenticate the signature generated by both the fixed message and private key. Using these cryptographic algorithms, the user's signature cannot be replicated without having access to their private key.[^o53] A [secure channel](wikipedia://en/592935) is not typically required. By applying asymmetric cryptography methods, the digital signature process prevents several common attacks where the attacker attempts to gain access through the following attack methods.[^o54]

The most relevant standards on digital signatures with respect to size of domestic markets are the [Digital Signature Standard (DSS)](wikipedia://en/59470)[^o55] by the [National Institute of Standards and Technology](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_Institute_of_Standards_and_Technology) (NIST) and the [eIDAS](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/EIDAS) Regulation[^o56] enacted by the [European Parliament](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/European_Parliament).[^o4] [OpenPGP](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/OpenPGP) is a non-proprietary protocol for [email encryption](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Email_encryption) through [public key cryptography](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Public_key_cryptography). It is supported by [PGP](wikipedia://en/23080) and [GnuPG](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GnuPG), and some of the [S/MIME](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/S/MIME) [IETF](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IETF) standards and has evolved into the most popular email encryption standard in the world.[^o57]

### Biometric signature {#Biometric_signature}

An electronic signature may also refer to electronic forms of processing or verifying identity through the use of biometric "signatures" or biologically identifying qualities of an individual. Such signatures use the approach of attaching some biometric measurement to a document as evidence. Biometric signatures include fingerprints, [hand geometry](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hand_geometry) (finger lengths and palm size), [iris patterns](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Iris_recognition), [voice characteristics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speaker_recognition), retinal patterns, or any other human body property. All of these are collected using electronic sensors of some kind.

Biometric measurements of this type are useless as [passwords](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Passwords) because they can't be changed if compromised. However, they might be serviceable, except that to date, they have been so easily deceived that they can carry little assurance that the person who purportedly signed a document was actually the person who did. For example, a replay of the electronic signal produced and submitted to the computer system responsible for 'affixing' a signature to a document can be collected via [wiretapping](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wiretapping) techniques. Many commercially available fingerprint sensors have low resolution and can be deceived with inexpensive household items (for example, [gummy bear](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gummy_bear) candy gel).[^o59] In the case of a user's face image, researchers in Vietnam successfully demonstrated in late 2017 how a specially crafted mask could beat [Apple's](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apple_Inc.) [Face ID](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Face_ID) on [iPhone X](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/IPhone_X).[^o60]
