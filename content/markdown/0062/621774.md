# Low-dimensional topology

*Image: Trefoil knot arb.png.* A three-dimensional depiction of a thickened [trefoil knot](wikipedia://en/404773), the simplest non-[trivial knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trivial_knot). [Knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008) is an important part of low-dimensional topology.

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Low-dimensional topology"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), **low-dimensional topology** is the branch of [topology](wikipedia://en/29954) that studies [manifolds](wikipedia://en/2073470), or more generally topological spaces, of four or fewer [dimensions](wikipedia://en/8398). Representative topics are the theory of [3-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1018257) and [4-manifolds](wikipedia://en/1362795), [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008), and [braid groups](wikipedia://en/550138). This can be regarded as a part of [geometric topology](wikipedia://en/621732). It may also be used to refer to the study of topological spaces of dimension 1, though this is more typically considered part of [continuum theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Continuum_theory).

:::

## History {#History}

A number of advances starting in the 1960s had the effect of emphasising low dimensions in topology. The solution by [Stephen Smale](wikipedia://en/40294), in 1961, of the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798) in five or more dimensions made dimensions three and four seem the hardest; and indeed they required new methods, while the freedom of higher dimensions meant that questions could be reduced to computational methods available in [surgery theory](wikipedia://en/5961115). [Thurston's](wikipedia://en/39190) [geometrization conjecture](wikipedia://en/220642), formulated in the late 1970s, offered a framework that suggested geometry and topology were closely intertwined in low dimensions, and Thurston's proof of geometrization for [Haken manifolds](wikipedia://en/236567) utilized a variety of tools from previously only weakly linked areas of mathematics. [Vaughan Jones](wikipedia://en/1227628)' discovery of the [Jones polynomial](wikipedia://en/1107642) in the early 1980s not only led knot theory in new directions but gave rise to still mysterious connections between low-dimensional topology and [mathematical physics](wikipedia://en/173416). In 2002, [Grigori Perelman](wikipedia://en/225266) announced a proof of the three-dimensional Poincaré conjecture, using [Richard S. Hamilton](wikipedia://en/758911)'s [Ricci flow](wikipedia://en/288291), an idea belonging to the field of [geometric analysis](wikipedia://en/4325474).

Overall, this progress has led to better integration of the field into the rest of mathematics.

## Two dimensions {#Two_dimensions}

A [surface](wikipedia://en/27865) is a [two-dimensional](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Two-dimensional), [topological manifold](wikipedia://en/2119193). The most familiar examples are those that arise as the boundaries of solid objects in ordinary three-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>3</sup>—for example, the surface of a [ball](wikipedia://en/160556). On the other hand, there are surfaces, such as the [Klein bottle](wikipedia://en/17412), that cannot be [embedded](wikipedia://en/52119) in three-dimensional Euclidean space without introducing [singularities](wikipedia://en/581763) or self-intersections.

### Classification of surfaces {#Classification_of_surfaces}

The *[classification theorem](wikipedia://en/4576710) of closed surfaces* states that any [connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Connected_%28topology%29) [closed](wikipedia://en/669475) surface is homeomorphic to some member of one of these three families:

1. the sphere;
1. the [connected sum](wikipedia://en/490134) of *g* [tori](wikipedia://en/74800), for $g \geq 1$;
1. the connected sum of *k* [real projective planes](wikipedia://en/411325), for $k \geq 1$.

The surfaces in the first two families are [orientable](wikipedia://en/187446). It is convenient to combine the two families by regarding the sphere as the connected sum of 0 tori. The number *g* of tori involved is called the *genus* of the surface. The sphere and the torus have [Euler characteristics](wikipedia://en/148420) 2 and 0, respectively, and in general the Euler characteristic of the connected sum of *g* tori is 2 − 2*g*.

The surfaces in the third family are nonorientable. The Euler characteristic of the real projective plane is 1, and in general the Euler characteristic of the connected sum of *k* of them is 2 − *k*.

### Teichmüller space {#Teichmüller_space}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **Teichmüller space** *T<sub>X</sub>* of a (real) topological surface *X*, is a space that parameterizes [complex structures](wikipedia://en/509742) on *X* up to the action of [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660) that are [isotopic](wikipedia://en/212250#Isotopy) to the [identity homeomorphism](wikipedia://en/15069). Each point in *T<sub>X</sub>* may be regarded as an isomorphism class of 'marked' [Riemann surfaces](wikipedia://en/173181) where a 'marking' is an isotopy class of homeomorphisms from *X* to *X*. The Teichmüller space is the [universal covering orbifold](wikipedia://en/293504) of the (Riemann) moduli space.

Teichmüller space has a canonical [complex](wikipedia://en/5826) [manifold](wikipedia://en/2073470) structure and a wealth of natural metrics. The underlying topological space of Teichmüller space was studied by Fricke, and the Teichmüller metric on it was introduced by (txt).[^o7]

::: {#o9 .statement kind=statement/theorem cue=named-heading name="Uniformization theorem"}

### Uniformization theorem {#Uniformization_theorem}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), the **uniformization theorem** says that every [simply connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) is [conformally equivalent](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Conformal_equivalence) to one of the three domains: the open [unit disk](wikipedia://en/252738), the [complex plane](wikipedia://en/217628), or the [Riemann sphere](wikipedia://en/30876799). In particular it admits a [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) of [constant curvature](wikipedia://en/3638803). This classifies Riemannian surfaces as elliptic (positively curved—rather, admitting a constant positively curved metric), parabolic (flat), and hyperbolic (negatively curved) according to their [universal cover](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Universal_cover).

The uniformization theorem is a generalization of the [Riemann mapping theorem](wikipedia://en/26215) from proper simply connected [open](wikipedia://en/39358) [subsets](wikipedia://en/27631) of the plane to arbitrary simply connected Riemann surfaces.

:::

## Three dimensions {#Three_dimensions}

A [topological space](wikipedia://en/30450) *X* is a 3-manifold if every point in *X* has a [neighbourhood](wikipedia://en/1529485) that is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) to [Euclidean 3-space](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclidean_3-space).

The topological, [piecewise-linear](wikipedia://en/2174184), and smooth categories are all equivalent in three dimensions, so little distinction is made in whether we are dealing with say, topological 3-manifolds, or smooth 3-manifolds.

Phenomena in three dimensions can be strikingly different from phenomena in other dimensions, and so there is a prevalence of very specialized techniques that do not generalize to dimensions greater than three. This special role has led to the discovery of close connections to a diversity of other fields, such as [knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008), [geometric group theory](wikipedia://en/955164), [hyperbolic geometry](wikipedia://en/241291), [number theory](wikipedia://en/21527), [Teichmüller theory](wikipedia://en/1835661), [topological quantum field theory](wikipedia://en/443757), [gauge theory](wikipedia://en/23912155), [Floer homology](wikipedia://en/2672856), and [partial differential equations](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Partial_differential_equations). 3-manifold theory is considered a part of low-dimensional topology or [geometric topology](wikipedia://en/621732).

### Knot and braid theory {#Knot_and_braid_theory}

[Knot theory](wikipedia://en/153008) is the study of [mathematical knots](wikipedia://en/366808). While inspired by knots that appear in daily life in shoelaces and rope, a mathematician's knot differs in that the ends are joined so that it cannot be undone. In mathematical language, a knot is an [embedding](wikipedia://en/52119) of a [circle](wikipedia://en/6220) in 3-dimensional [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697), **R**<sup>3</sup> (since we're using topology, a circle isn't bound to the classical geometric concept, but to all of its [homeomorphisms](wikipedia://en/13660)). Two mathematical knots are equivalent if one can be transformed into the other via a deformation of **R**<sup>3</sup> upon itself (known as an [ambient isotopy](wikipedia://en/7404467)); these transformations correspond to manipulations of a knotted string that do not involve cutting the string or passing the string through itself.

[Knot complements](wikipedia://en/1350120) are frequently-studied 3-manifolds. The knot complement of a [tame knot](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tame_knot) *K* is the three-dimensional space surrounding the knot. To make this precise, suppose that *K* is a knot in a three-manifold *M* (most often, *M* is the [3-sphere](wikipedia://en/39792)). Let *N* be a [tubular neighborhood](wikipedia://en/3192042) of *K*; so *N* is a [solid torus](wikipedia://en/1251473). The knot complement is then the [complement](wikipedia://en/54347) of *N*,

$$X_K = M - \mbox{interior}(N).$$

A related topic is [braid theory](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid_theory). Braid theory is an abstract [geometric](wikipedia://en/18973446) [theory](wikipedia://en/30746) studying the everyday [braid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Braid) concept, and some generalizations. The idea is that braids can be organized into [groups](wikipedia://en/19447), in which the group operation is 'do the first braid on a set of strings, and then follow it with a second on the twisted strings'. Such groups may be described by explicit [presentations](wikipedia://en/99494), as was shown by (txt).[^o13] For an elementary treatment along these lines, see the article on [braid groups](wikipedia://en/550138). Braid groups may also be given a deeper mathematical interpretation: as the [fundamental group](wikipedia://en/11004) of certain [configuration spaces](wikipedia://en/54981574).

### Hyperbolic 3-manifolds {#Hyperbolic_3-manifolds}

A [hyperbolic 3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1237700) is a [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) equipped with a [complete](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Complete_space) [Riemannian metric](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Riemannian_metric) of constant [sectional curvature](wikipedia://en/285618) -1. In other words, it is the quotient of three-dimensional [hyperbolic space](wikipedia://en/318742) by a subgroup of hyperbolic isometries acting freely and [properly discontinuously](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Properly_discontinuous_action). See also [Kleinian model](wikipedia://en/1589114).

Its thick-thin decomposition has a thin part consisting of tubular neighborhoods of closed geodesics and/or ends that are the product of a Euclidean surface and the closed half-ray. The manifold is of finite volume if and only if its thick part is compact. In this case, the ends are of the form torus cross the closed half-ray and are called **cusps**. Knot complements are the most commonly studied cusped manifolds.

::: {#o16 .statement kind=statement/conjecture cue=named-heading name="Poincaré conjecture and geometrization"}

### Poincaré conjecture and geometrization {#Poincaré_conjecture_and_geometrization}

[Thurston's geometrization conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thurston's_geometrization_conjecture) states that certain three-dimensional [topological spaces](wikipedia://en/30450) each have a unique geometric structure that can be associated with them. It is an analogue of the [uniformization theorem](wikipedia://en/288270) for two-dimensional [surfaces](wikipedia://en/27865), which states that every [simply-connected](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Simply_connected) [Riemann surface](wikipedia://en/173181) can be given one of three geometries ([Euclidean](wikipedia://en/9417), [spherical](wikipedia://en/174026), or [hyperbolic](wikipedia://en/241291)). In three dimensions, it is not always possible to assign a single geometry to a whole topological space. Instead, the geometrization conjecture states that every closed [3-manifold](wikipedia://en/1018257) can be decomposed in a canonical way into pieces that each have one of eight types of geometric structure. The conjecture was proposed by (txt), and implies several other conjectures, such as the [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798) and Thurston's [elliptization conjecture](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Elliptization_conjecture).[^o18]

:::

## Four dimensions {#Four_dimensions}

A **4-manifold** is a 4-dimensional [topological manifold](wikipedia://en/2119193). A **smooth 4-manifold** is a 4-manifold with a [smooth structure](wikipedia://en/1542238). In dimension four, in marked contrast with lower dimensions, topological and smooth manifolds are quite different. There exist some topological 4-manifolds that admit no smooth structure and even if there exists a smooth structure it need not be unique (i.e. there are smooth 4-manifolds that are [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) but not [diffeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffeomorphic)).

4-manifolds are of importance in physics because, in [General Relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/General_Relativity), [spacetime](wikipedia://en/28758) is modeled as a [pseudo-Riemannian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pseudo-Riemannian) 4-manifold.

### Exotic R<sup>4</sup> {#Exotic_R4}

An **exotic** **R**<sup>4</sup> is a [differentiable manifold](wikipedia://en/2119219) that is [homeomorphic](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Homeomorphic) but not [diffeomorphic](wikipedia://en/8564) to the [Euclidean space](wikipedia://en/9697) **R**<sup>4</sup>. The first examples were found in the early 1980s by [Michael Freedman](wikipedia://en/658970), by using the contrast between Freedman's theorems about topological 4-manifolds, and [Simon Donaldson](wikipedia://en/976305)'s theorems about smooth 4-manifolds.[^o21] There is a [continuum](wikipedia://en/1574901) of non-diffeomorphic [differentiable structures](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Differentiable_structure) of **R**<sup>4</sup>, as was shown first by [Clifford Taubes](wikipedia://en/6893920).[^o22]

Prior to this construction, non-diffeomorphic [smooth structures](wikipedia://en/1542238) on spheres—[exotic spheres](wikipedia://en/1114931)—were already known to exist, although the question of the existence of such structures for the particular case of the [4-sphere](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/4-sphere) remained open (and still remains open to this day). For any positive integer *n* other than 4, there are no exotic smooth structures on **R**<sup>*n*</sup>; in other words, if *n* ≠ 4 then any smooth manifold homeomorphic to **R**<sup>*n*</sup> is diffeomorphic to **R**<sup>*n*</sup>.[^o23]

### Other special phenomena in four dimensions {#Other_special_phenomena_in_four_dimensions}

There are several fundamental theorems about manifolds that can be proved by low-dimensional methods in dimensions at most 3, and by completely different high-dimensional methods in dimension at least 5, but which are false in four dimensions. Here are some examples:

- In dimensions other than 4, the [Kirby–Siebenmann invariant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kirby–Siebenmann_invariant) provides the obstruction to the existence of a PL structure; in other words a compact topological manifold has a PL structure if and only if its Kirby–Siebenmann invariant in H<sup>4</sup>(*M*,**Z**/2**Z**) vanishes. In dimension 3 and lower, every topological manifold admits an essentially unique PL structure. In dimension 4 there are many examples with vanishing Kirby–Siebenmann invariant but no PL structure.
- In any dimension other than 4, a compact topological manifold has only a finite number of essentially distinct PL or smooth structures. In dimension 4, compact manifolds can have a countable infinite number of non-diffeomorphic smooth structures.
- Four is the only dimension *n* for which **R**<sup>*n*</sup> can have an exotic smooth structure. **R**<sup>4</sup> has an uncountable number of exotic smooth structures; see [exotic **R**<sup>4</sup>](wikipedia://en/2180637).
- The solution to the smooth [Poincaré conjecture](wikipedia://en/23798) is known in all dimensions other than 4 (it is usually false in dimensions at least 7; see [exotic sphere](wikipedia://en/1114931)). The Poincaré conjecture for [PL manifolds](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/PL_manifold) has been proved for all dimensions other than 4, but it is not known whether it is true in 4 dimensions (it is equivalent to the smooth Poincaré conjecture in 4 dimensions).
- The smooth [h-cobordism theorem](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/H-cobordism_theorem) holds for cobordisms provided that neither the cobordism nor its boundary has dimension 4. It can fail if the boundary of the cobordism has dimension 4 (as shown by Donaldson). If the cobordism has dimension 4, then it is unknown whether the h-cobordism theorem holds.
- A topological manifold of dimension not equal to 4 has a handlebody decomposition. Manifolds of dimension 4 have a handlebody decomposition if and only if they are smoothable.
- There are compact 4-dimensional topological manifolds that are not homeomorphic to any simplicial complex. In dimension at least 5 the existence of topological manifolds not homeomorphic to a simplicial complex was an open problem. In 2013, Ciprian Manolescu posted a preprint on ArXiv showing that there are manifolds in each dimension greater than or equal to 5, that are not homeomorphic to a simplicial complex.

## A few typical theorems that distinguish low-dimensional topology {#A_few_typical_theorems_that_distinguish_low-dimensional_topology}

There are several theorems that in effect state that many of the most basic tools used to study high-dimensional manifolds do not apply to low-dimensional manifolds, such as:

**Steenrod's theorem** states that an orientable 3-manifold has a trivial [tangent bundle](wikipedia://en/211794). Stated another way, the only [characteristic class](wikipedia://en/363530) of a 3-manifold is the obstruction to orientability.

Any closed 3-manifold is the boundary of a 4-manifold. This theorem is due independently to several people: it follows from the [Dehn](wikipedia://en/570602)–[Lickorish](wikipedia://en/11177096) theorem via a [Heegaard splitting](wikipedia://en/864418) of the 3-manifold. It also follows from [René Thom](wikipedia://en/61633)'s computation of the [cobordism](wikipedia://en/391832) ring of closed manifolds.

The existence of [exotic smooth structures on **R**<sup>4</sup>](wikipedia://en/2180637). This was originally observed by [Michael Freedman](wikipedia://en/658970), based on the work of [Simon Donaldson](wikipedia://en/976305) and [Andrew Casson](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Andrew_Casson). It has since been elaborated by Freedman, [Robert Gompf](wikipedia://en/42024874), [Clifford Taubes](wikipedia://en/6893920) and [Laurence Taylor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Laurence_Taylor_%28mathematician%29) to show there exists a continuum of non-diffeomorphic smooth structures on **R**<sup>4</sup>. Meanwhile, **R**<sup>n</sup> is known to have exactly one smooth structure up to diffeomorphism provided *n* ≠ 4.

:::
