# Matrix analysis

::: {#o1 .subject cue=lead name="Matrix analysis"}

In [mathematics](wikipedia://en/18831), particularly in [linear algebra](wikipedia://en/18422) and applications, **matrix analysis** is the study of [matrices](wikipedia://en/20556859) and their algebraic properties.[^o2] Some particular topics out of many include; operations defined on matrices (such as [matrix addition](wikipedia://en/125276), [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) and operations derived from these), functions of matrices (such as [matrix exponentiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_exponentiation) and [matrix logarithm](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_logarithm), and even [sines and cosines](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sines_and_cosines) etc. of matrices), and the [eigenvalues](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eigenvalue) of matrices ([eigendecomposition of a matrix](wikipedia://en/13576645), [eigenvalue perturbation](wikipedia://en/10465001) theory).[^o3]

:::

## Matrix spaces {#Matrix_spaces}

The set of all *m* × *n* matrices over a [field](wikipedia://en/10603) *F* denoted in this article *M*<sub>*mn*</sub>(*F*) form a [vector space](wikipedia://en/32370). Examples of *F* include the set of [rational numbers](wikipedia://en/19727024) $\mathbb{Q}$, the [real numbers](wikipedia://en/20646438) $\mathbb{R}$, and set of [complex numbers](wikipedia://en/5826) $\mathbb{C}$. The spaces *M*<sub>*mn*</sub>(*F*) and *M*<sub>*pq*</sub>(*F*) are different spaces if *m* and *p* are unequal, and if *n* and *q* are unequal; for instance *M*<sub>32</sub>(*F*) ≠ *M*<sub>23</sub>(*F*). Two *m* × *n* matrices **A** and **B** in *M*<sub>*mn*</sub>(*F*) can be added together to form another matrix in the space *M*<sub>*mn*</sub>(*F*):

$$\mathbf{A},\mathbf{B} \in M_{mn}(F)\,,\quad \mathbf{A} + \mathbf{B} \in M_{mn}(F)$$

and multiplied by a *α* in *F*, to obtain another matrix in *M*<sub>*mn*</sub>(*F*):

$$\alpha \in F \,,\quad \alpha \mathbf{A} \in M_{mn}(F)$$

Combining these two properties, a [linear combination](wikipedia://en/55632) of matrices **A** and **B** are in *M*<sub>*mn*</sub>(*F*) is another matrix in *M*<sub>*mn*</sub>(*F*):

$$\alpha \mathbf{A} + \beta\mathbf{B} \in M_{mn}(F)$$

where *α* and *β* are numbers in *F*.

Any matrix can be expressed as a linear combination of basis matrices, which play the role of the [basis vectors](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Basis_vector) for the matrix space. For example, for the set of 2 × 2 matrices over the field of real numbers, $M_{22}(\mathbb{R})$, one legitimate basis set of matrices is:

$$\begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad \begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix}\,,\quad \begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,$$

because any 2 × 2 matrix can be expressed as:

$$\begin{pmatrix}a&b\\c&d\end{pmatrix}=a \begin{pmatrix}1&0\\0&0\end{pmatrix} +b\begin{pmatrix}0&1\\0&0\end{pmatrix} +c\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\1&0\end{pmatrix} +d\begin{pmatrix}0&0\\0&1\end{pmatrix}\,,$$

where *a*, *b*, *c*,*d* are all real numbers. This idea applies to other fields and matrices of higher dimensions.

## Determinants {#Determinants}

The **determinant** of a [square matrix](wikipedia://en/166022) is an important property. The determinant indicates if a matrix is [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Invertible) (i.e. the [inverse of a matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Inverse_matrix) exists when the determinant is nonzero). Determinants are used for finding eigenvalues of matrices (see below), and for solving a [system of linear equations](wikipedia://en/113087) (see [Cramer's rule](wikipedia://en/166008)).

## Eigenvalues and eigenvectors of matrices {#Eigenvalues_and_eigenvectors_of_matrices}

::: {#o8 .definition cue=heading name="Definitions"}

### Definitions {#Definitions}

An *n* × *n* matrix **A** has **eigenvectors** **x** and **eigenvalues** *λ* defined by the relation:

$$\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \lambda \mathbf{x}$$

In words, the [matrix multiplication](wikipedia://en/125280) of **A** followed by an eigenvector **x** (here an *n*-dimensional [column matrix](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Column_matrix)), is the same as multiplying the eigenvector by the eigenvalue. For an *n* × *n* matrix, there are *n* eigenvalues. The eigenvalues are the [roots](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Root_of_a_polynomial) of the [characteristic polynomial](wikipedia://en/218268):

$$p_\mathbf{A}(\lambda) = \det(\mathbf{A} - \lambda \mathbf{I}) = 0$$

where **I** is the *n* × *n* [identity matrix](wikipedia://en/59718).

Roots of polynomials, in this context the eigenvalues, can all be different, or some may be equal (in which case eigenvalue has [multiplicity](wikipedia://en/19211790#Multiplicity_of_a_root_of_a_polynomial), the number of times an eigenvalue occurs). After solving for the eigenvalues, the eigenvectors corresponding to the eigenvalues can be found by the defining equation.

:::

## Matrix similarity {#Matrix_similarity}

Two *n* × *n* matrices **A** and **B** are similar if they are related by a **similarity transformation**:

$$\mathbf{B} = \mathbf{P}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{P}^{-1}$$

The matrix **P** is called a **similarity matrix**, and is necessarily [invertible](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Matrix_inverse).

## Matrix norms {#Matrix_norms}

Since matrices form vector spaces, one can form axioms (analogous to those of vectors) to define a "size" of a particular matrix. The norm of a matrix is a nonnegative real number.

::: {#o21 .statement kind=statement/axiom cue=named-heading name="Definition and axioms"}

### Definition and axioms {#Definition_and_axioms}

For all matrices **A** and **B** in *M*<sub>*mn*</sub>(*F*), and all numbers *α* in *F*, a matrix norm, delimited by double vertical bars || ... ||, fulfills:[^o22]

- [Nonnegative](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nonnegative):

$$\| \mathbf{A} \| \ge 0$$

with equality only for **A** = **0**, the [zero matrix](wikipedia://en/1168888).

- [Scalar multiplication](wikipedia://en/244373):

$$\|\alpha \mathbf{A}\|=|\alpha| \|\mathbf{A}\|$$

- The [triangular inequality](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Triangular_inequality):

$$\|\mathbf{A}+\mathbf{B}\| \leq \|\mathbf{A}\|+\|\mathbf{B}\|$$

:::

### Frobenius norm {#Frobenius_norm}

The **Frobenius norm** is analogous to the [dot product](wikipedia://en/157093) of Euclidean vectors; multiply matrix elements entry-wise, add up the results, then take the positive [square root](wikipedia://en/29208):

$$\|\mathbf{A}\| = \sqrt{\mathbf{A}:\mathbf{A}} = \sqrt{\sum_{i=1}^m \sum_{j=1}^n (A_{ij})^2}$$

It is defined for matrices of any dimension (i.e. no restriction to square matrices).
